# Chapter 44 — Standing Room

On the Tuesday nearly three weeks after Maud, Cael did not go down to the Ironyard at all, because there was nobody on the evening card he needed to see and because Lira had asked him to hold a lamp.

She was at the kitchen table with her sleeves pushed up and her hands flat on the boards, under the light, and she wanted to look at them properly. The bars Maud had laid across the backs of them had gone from black to plum, and from plum to a green like pond water, and were now a yellow that only showed when the lamp came close. She turned them over and back. She made two fists and opened them, slowly, and watched the knuckles as if they belonged to somebody she was thinking of hiring.

"Closer," she said.

He brought the lamp closer.

"There." She touched the second knuckle of her left hand with one finger of her right. "That one still talks when I close it hard. The rest have stopped." She closed it hard, to be sure, and her mouth went thin, and she opened it again. "A week. Then it's quiet."

"You've been using them every night."

"I've been using them every night because that's where the middle lives." She did not look up. "Maud said a year. I'd like it to be less than a year. So I'm not resting them, I'm teaching them, and they can complain while they learn like everybody else." She took her hands off the table and put them in her lap, out of the light. "Put that down before you burn the cloth. What are you doing in that book?"

He had the Log open at the back, at the two columns from the night of Bede. The *Habit* column had five lines in it now, and a cost against each. The *Shape* column had five lines and no costs, because shapes could not be bought out, and under each he had been trying for a fortnight to write how he would stand so that nobody was ever in the place it left open. Four of the five had something under them. The fifth did not.

*The right is only ever my feet,* it said, and under it there was nothing at all.

He turned the book round and showed her.

Lira read it with her chin on her fist. The heavyset man's wife came through with the kettle and looked over her shoulder at the page, as she did at anything written in the house, and went out again, satisfied that it was not a bill.

"Your feet are a beat slow going right," said Lira.

"I know they are."

"And you've put it under *Shape*." She tapped the column. "Your hip only goes left. That's how the Wind was made in you; it came out of my left hip and my barn master's before me, and you can't argue with it. Fine. But your feet aren't the Wind. Your feet are only feet." She looked up at him. "Nobody built them slow going right. You did, by never asking them. That's a habit."

He opened his mouth and found that he had nothing ready to put in it.

"I'm not saying I can fix it," Lira said. "I'm saying you've filed it in the wrong column, and you of all people ought to mind that." She stood, and took the lamp out of his hand, and set it on the table where it belonged. "I'm going down to the alcove. Don't wait up. And don't move it till you've had a better look at it."

He did not move it. He sat in the kitchen after she had gone with the book open in front of him, while the heavyset man read his carter's bill by the range and the sister's hen made its small complaints from the coal box. He looked at the line for a long time, and in the end he did a thing he had never done to a page in the Log before. He drew a small question mark in the margin beside it, in pencil, so that it could come out again.

Two streets away, on an ordinary Tuesday card, the Ironyard's door was taking money.

---

The man at the door wanted four coppers for standing room, and Reydan paid it.

He paid it in coppers, because he had learned on other trips that silver at a door like this one made the door remember you, and he had not come to be remembered yet. The doorkeeper was an old fighter with one ear thicker than the other, sitting on a stool with a tin box on his knee, and he looked at the coat first and the face second, as doorkeepers did everywhere. The coat was plain and good. The face, Reydan had been told by people who meant it kindly, gave very little away even when he wanted it to. The doorkeeper looked at both, dropped the coppers in the box and jerked his head at the room, and turned to the carter behind him.

Reydan went in and found a place to stand.

He had come down the river road that afternoon on the back of a wool wagon, three days from the city where he had lived since he was eleven, with one bag and the price of a room. He had found the room above a saddler's and left the bag in it. He had eaten something at a stall without noticing what it was, because his attention had already gone ahead of him to the building at the bottom of the hill, the way it always did on these trips. The trips were the only time it ever did that any more.

The place he chose was at the end of the south rope, where the lamplight was good and the floor could be seen whole, and far enough back that nobody would have to step round him to see past. He folded his arms and settled his weight, and looked at the room before he looked at anything in it.

It was better than he had expected.

He had seen a good many rooms like it, in more cities than he liked to count. Most were yards or sheds or the backs of taverns, and they were run by whoever had the loudest voice, and they kept their records on a slate that got wiped when it rained. This was an old foundry, by the stone of the floor, and the stone was dense and black and swept. The ceiling was low and the lamps had been hung by somebody who had thought about where shadows fell. There was a long wall by the slate covered in chalk names in a careful hand, with small private marks beside them: rings, dots, short stitches. A narrow man with chalk on his cuffs stood in front of the wall with a pencil he never used, reading it the way a farmer reads the sky.

And at a table by the rope, with a great book open in front of her and a second book under her elbow, sat a woman in her fifties with spectacles and grey hair pinned flat. When the first bout was called, she stood up to write it.

Reydan noticed that. He noticed that nobody else in the room seemed to find it strange. He noticed that when she said the words she said before every bout, the two fighters both answered her aloud, and that she waited for the answer, and that the room went quiet while she waited. He did not catch every word. He caught *the rule in this room*, and he caught the quiet, and he put both away to look at later.

On the east bench, among the carters and the stall-keepers and the girls who had come to shout, sat a young man in a coat Reydan knew. It was a river academy's coat, two or three years out of the house by the wear at the cuffs, and the young man sat in it with his hands flat on his knees and watched the empty stone between bouts as if something were still happening on it, and nobody sat near him. Reydan looked at him for a moment with something like fellow feeling. There was one in every room, he had found, who sat like that and watched the floor when nothing was on it. Usually it was him.

The first bout was two Coppers. One was a Stone, a solid man of thirty or so with a grinder's patience, who set himself in the middle and let the other come. The other was a Blade, a woman, quicker and younger, with a losing look about her that Reydan recognized. It was the look of a fighter who had lost three in a row and come back to stand on the same stone anyway, because the stone was where you found out.

He did not watch the strikes. He had stopped watching strikes at about fourteen, when a master at the academy had stood behind him at a sparring floor and put two fingers on the back of his neck and turned his head, gently, from the blades to the feet. *The blade tells you what happened,* she had said. *The feet tell you what's about to. And between the exchanges, when nobody's looking, the feet tell you what the fighter's afraid of.*

So he watched the Blade's feet between the exchanges.

In the first rest she stood square, flat, and shook out her sword hand. In the second she stood with her weight on her back foot and her front heel just off the stone. She had been hit on the forward step in the second exchange, a solid knock on the hip from the Stone's forearm, and her body had decided, without asking her, never to be caught going forward again. It would cost her the bout if she let it. The Stone would see it in a while, if he was any good.

The Stone did not see it. In the fourth exchange the Blade found a gap that came of the Stone's own slowness, not of anything Reydan had seen in her feet, and put the flat of her sword against his ribs. The narrow man at the wall made a mark. The woman at the table wrote standing. The room made the noise rooms made for a tidy finish, and Reydan did not make it with them. He noted the gap, and noted that the woman had not known about her own back foot, and that she had won anyway. That was worth knowing too, about a room: a fighter could win here while still carrying a fault she had not found.

---

The second bout went wrong, and Reydan watched it go wrong with the same attention he had given the first.

It was a Force against a quick lad of nineteen or twenty whose Path Reydan could not name from the rope, something light and fast that wanted distance. The lad was good. He kept the Force at the end of his reach for two exchanges, darting in and away. The room liked him; there were girls on the second bench who shouted his name. In the third he came in once too often on the same line, half a step deeper than he had come before. The Force had been building all that time, slow and deep, and had been waiting for exactly that half-step. He put the whole of what he had built into the lad's chest.

The lad went off the floor backward and landed on the stone on his shoulder, hard, with a sound Reydan felt in his own teeth.

Several people near the front looked away. One of the girls on the second bench put her hands over her mouth. Reydan did not look away. He watched the lad lie there and try to breathe. He watched the Force stand back at once with his hands open, the way the woman at the table's rule presumably asked, and he watched the narrow man come off the wall at a walk, not a run, and crouch by the lad and say something, and wait. After a long moment the lad got up, holding his arm against his body, and walked to the rope on his own feet, and the room let its breath out.

The fall had told Reydan more than the clean finish.

It had told him that the lad's half-step deeper on the third entry had not been a decision. His body had grown confident, and his body had gone a little further than his head had agreed to. The lad would not do it again for a month. And then, Reydan thought, he would do it again, the first night he felt safe, and somebody would be waiting for it. People on the bench thought a loss like that was a thing that happened to you. He had never thought so. A loss like that was a fact about you that somebody else had found first, and a fall was only the price of being told.

He had learned that young, and expensively, in a room much grander than this one.

His father's house had a training yard, because his father was a guild officer of the middling sort who believed in such things, and at ten Reydan had been put in it with a hired man who was paid to make him better. At eleven, when he Kindled with a Pressure Path that the Arbiter wrote down as burst-compression and the examiners called promising, the academy took him. He had been there, in one way or another, ever since. He had risen through every examination his tier asked of him, cleanly and on time. He had sparred every morning of his life against people who were paid to find what was wrong with him and say so, and he had been told what was wrong with him so many times, and so precisely, that at twenty-two there was very little left that anybody could find.

That was the trouble. He did not think of it as a boast. He thought of it the way a man might think of a well that has gone dry: nobody's fault, and no less dry for that.

The guild bouts had stopped meaning anything around his nineteenth year. It had not happened all at once. Opponents had begun to be ill on the days they were drawn against him. Trainers had begun to speak to their prospects quietly before a draw went up. Then, three autumns running, the two academies in his city had redrawn their invitational so that he and the other house's best boy sat in brackets that could not meet before the final. On the third autumn the other house withdrew its boy before the final, with a letter about an injured wrist. Everybody knew the wrist was well. Nobody said so.

Reydan had not been angry about it. He had tried to be, for a while, because it seemed the right thing to feel, and found that what he felt was mostly hunger.

So he had started taking these trips. He would hear of somebody, the way you heard of anybody: a name passed in a guild hall, a story told at a supper, a champion in some small town who had beaten everyone for a year. He would go, and pay at the door, and stand at the back, and watch. Mostly the stories had been smaller than their telling. There had been a salt-town Copper with heavy hands and a good heart, who had beaten everyone within fifty miles because there was nobody within fifty miles to show him where his ceiling was. There had been a Blade in a river port who was quick and clever, and had never once in her life been made to wait. He had watched each of them for one bout and known by the second exchange, and gone home the next morning without speaking to anybody. He did not resent the trips. A trip that came to nothing was cheaper than a story ignored that turned out true. But he had begun to think, lately, that he would stop taking them, and that thought had frightened him more than any opponent had for years.

---

The story about the boy had reached him in a guild hall in the spring, and it had been absurd.

A boy with no Path, it said, a boy the Compact's own registry had looked at and written down as nothing, had put a Bronze Rank 1 on the ground in a yard in a district at the bottom of a hill somewhere down the river. The boy had done it, the story said, with his bare hands and nothing else. It had been told by a man who had heard it from a man who had been on the river, and by the time it reached Reydan it had grown a crowd of a thousand, and a sword, and a broken arm that nobody could afterward account for.

He had distrusted every word of it on principle. Stories about circuit fighters grew in the telling, the way fish grew in the telling, and an impossible one grew fastest of all. But he had learned on the trips that the principle was not enough. Every so often an impossible story was only a true story told badly. The only way to know which was to go and look, and he had been looking for three years, and he was very tired of the salt-town Coppers.

So this time he had done a thing he had never done before a trip. He had gone to the keeper of the unsanctioned book in his own city, an old man who sold lamp oil by day and kept the circuit's records by night, and asked him to write down the river for one line. The old man had looked at him strangely. He had written all the same, to a keeper he had never written to in his life, and had sent it with the courier. A short letter had come back eleven days later in a narrow, careful hand, with the whole of the line copied out at the foot of it.

*Iron-equivalent. Cael. No Path designation.*

Reydan had read it standing in the lamp-oil shop with the smell of the oil all round him. The story had said *no Path*, and the story had been told by fools. But a keeper's line was not a story. A keeper did not write *Iron-equivalent* in a book that other keepers read because a crowd had shouted. And *No Path designation* in a keeper's careful hand was not a boast or a legend. It was simply what was there, set down plainly, by somebody whose whole life was being right about such things. He had read it twice, and then gone home and packed one bag.

Now he stood at the end of the south rope in the room where the line had been written, and watched the woman at the table close her book on the second bout and stand the pen in its pot. He had come here to find out whether the story was only told badly, and he had seen two bouts and not the boy. But he had seen the room, and the room was the first half of the answer. It had a keeper who stood to write, and a rule that both fighters said aloud, and a narrow man who walked rather than ran to a fallen boy because running would have frightened the crowd. It was not a small pond. Whatever the boy was, he had been measured here by people who knew how to measure.

The crowd began to move between bouts, toward the slate and the stalls by the side door. Reydan unfolded his arms and went the other way, along the rope, to the wall.

The narrow man saw him coming. Reydan watched him do it: the eyes going to his feet first, and then his shoulders, and then his hands, and only then his face, reading him in exactly the order the academy masters had read their pupils on the first morning. The man did not stop reading when Reydan stopped in front of him. He held the unused pencil against his chest and waited to be told which kind of problem this was.

"You keep the card," said Reydan.

"I'm Dace. I keep the card." The man looked at the coat. "You're not from the hill."

"No. From up the river." Reydan let a moment pass, because he had found it was kinder to say a thing once and plainly than to work up to it.

"I want to fight the unranked one."

Dace looked at him for a while.

"He's Iron-equivalent now," said Dace. "He's not unranked any more. He's got a line in the main-floor book, in the keeper's hand. He earned it on this stone."

"I know that. I want to fight him anyway." Reydan nodded at the table, where the keeper was talking to a man with a pie in his hand. "Somebody in this room sent that line up the river to the keeper in my city. I read it in a lamp-oil shop the week before last. That's why I'm here and not somewhere else."

Something in Dace's face moved a very little. Reydan saw him put the courier and his own letter together, and find that he did not entirely like the sum.

"You've not seen him fight," said Dace.

"No. I've seen your room." Reydan looked round it once, at the lamps and the stone and the girls on the second bench, who were still talking about the lad's shoulder. "I watched two bouts. Your Stone doesn't know the Blade had stopped going forward. Your quick lad will go a half-step too deep again in about a month. And your keeper stands up to write." He looked back at Dace. "That's enough to tell me the line is worth something. It isn't enough to tell me about him. That's what I came for."

"What's your Path? And your tier?"

"Pressure. Burst, not the long kind. Iron-tier, rank eight." Reydan said it as he would have said his street. "Twenty-two, if your keeper wants it for her book. I trained at an academy. I'm not here for a purse. I'll take whatever the floor pays and put it back in your box if you'd rather."

Dace did not answer that. He looked at the wall instead, at the chalk names and the rings and the stitches, and Reydan watched him read it for a long moment the way he must have read it a thousand times, weighing what a thing would cost before he wrote it down.

"Two weeks," said Dace. "Main floor."

"Thank you."

"I'll not book him anything before it. He'll have a fortnight with nothing on his card but you." Dace turned the pencil over in his fingers. "Most men in your place would ask me next where he trains in the mornings."

Reydan understood that he was being weighed, and did not mind it. He had come three days down the river to look at the boy, and the man who could tell him where to look was standing in front of him with a pencil.

"I'm not going to ask," he said. "He's never seen me. I've never seen him. Let it stay that way until the floor." He thought about how to say the rest of it, and said it plainly. "Tell him I'm not here to embarrass him, or anyone. I didn't come to prove a story wrong. I came because it was the first one in three years that was worth the road."

He let that stand a moment, and then gave Dace the rest.

"I'd like it to stay that kind of fight."

---

He went out past the doorkeeper into the cold, and up the hill through the district, by streets he did not know, toward the saddler's and the room.

It was a poor district; that was plain from the doors and the windows, and from the way the people still out at that hour walked in the middle of the street. But it was not a frightened one. A pie stall was shutting at the corner of a square with a pump in it. The woman packing it up said goodnight to two carters by name. A boy went past with a can of milk and a dog and did not look at Reydan's coat at all. When he looked back from the top of the hill, the foundry roof was dark below him except for one high window at the north end, where a lamp was burning, small and steady, though it was late for anybody to be up.

He thought about the room as he walked, and the keeper's careful hand, and the line.

He had no idea what the boy was, and he knew it. The story said no Path; the line said no Path designation; and those were two different things, and he did not know which one was true, or whether either was. A fighter with no Path would be a curiosity, a boy who was very good with his hands and his eyes. He would be beaten in two exchanges by any trained Iron with a Path to spend, however good his eyes were. A fighter with a Path the registry could not name would be something else again. Reydan had no words for what. He found that he did not want to guess, and that the not-wanting surprised him. He had spent his whole life being told what things were before he met them. It was, he thought, a long time since he had walked toward a fight knowing nothing at all.

He could judge the room, and he had. He could judge the line, and he had judged it worth three days on a wool wagon. The boy he could not judge yet, and would not, until the boy stood at the other mark under those lamps and did whatever it was he did.

At the saddler's he climbed the stair, and lay on the narrow bed in his coat, and looked at the ceiling. He realized after a while that he was smiling, and that he had not done so on any of the trips before, not once, not even on the way there.

# Chapter 45 — The Alcove Wall

Dace came for him at first light on the Wednesday, which Dace never did.

Cael was alone in Lira's alcove with his back against the wall, working the short right-hand steps he had been working since the kitchen table, a dozen and then a dozen more, slow, with his eyes shut so that he could feel how late the right foot was against the left. The lamp in the north window was out, because Lira had come up the hill a little after two and he had heard her stop on the landing and go on, and the straw post hung still on its rope with the frost of the night still grey along its binding. Somewhere out in the main room a sweeper was pushing a broom across the stone, a long dry sound that came and went.

Then the light at the arch changed, and he opened his eyes, and Dace's narrow shadow was lying across the floor.

Dace did not come in. He never came into an alcove that was in use; he said it was a fighter's room while the fighter was in it, and he stood at arches the way other men stood at doors. He had his pencil in one hand and nothing in the other, and he looked at Cael for a moment the way he looked at the wall before he wrote on it.

"A man came in last night," Dace said. "On the Tuesday card. Stood at the south rope for two bouts and spoke to nobody, and then came and spoke to me."

Cael waited.

"His name's Reydan. He's from up the river, two cities on." Dace said it carefully, giving each part its own place. "Iron-tier, on a guild card. Rank eight. Pressure Path, the burst kind, not the long kind. Twenty-two. Trained at an academy. He wants you."

Cael stood away from the wall.

"Wants me how?"

"On the main floor. I've given him two weeks from yesterday. Tuesday fortnight." Dace turned the pencil over. "I'll not put you on anything before it. You've a clear card till then."

The sweeper's broom went by the arch and on. Cael found that he was standing very still, as he stood at a mark, and made himself breathe out.

"Why me?"

"Because of a letter." Dace looked at the floor for a moment, which was not like him either. "You remember I told you about the courier. The keeper up the river, asking for one line by name. I wrote him back. I copied it out for him, the line, the whole of it, in my own hand, because a keeper had asked and I've never yet refused a keeper a line." He looked up again. "He took it to this man. This man read it, and packed a bag, and came down the river on a wool wagon to see if it was true. He told me so himself. So you've got me to thank for him, if thanks is what you want to give."

"It's the line," said Cael. "It would have gone anyway."

"It might. It went because I sent it." Dace let that stand; he did not seem to want it taken off him. "He said to tell you something. I'll give it you the way he said it, near enough. He said he's not here to embarrass you, or anybody. He came because yours was the first story in three years worth the road. And then he said, *I'd like it to stay that kind of fight.*"

Cael turned that over. It was an odd thing to send ahead of you. It was not a threat, and it was not quite manners.

"What did you say?"

"Nothing. He didn't wait for anything." Dace rubbed his thumb along the pencil. "I'll tell you the other thing, because you'll hear it in the market by noon and I'd sooner you heard it straight. I asked the carters this morning. The wool-wagon man has carried him before, to two other towns, and stood at the back with him in one of them. The talk on the road is that he was in a training yard at ten, his father's, and at an academy from eleven. And that he never fights a man the same way twice." He paused. "Not twice, the carter said. If you've seen him do a thing once, he's done with it."

"Has anybody here seen him at all?"

"Not a soul. If he's ever stood on a floor within three hundred miles of us, no carter on the road knows of it." Dace looked past him, at the straw post, at the chalk line Lira had left on the floor. "Cael. You could tell me no. He asked for you; that doesn't oblige you to anything. I'll walk up the hill and tell him so myself, and nobody in this building would think less of you for it."

"I know."

"But you're not going to."

"No."

Dace nodded, as if a figure had come out the way he had known it would. He turned to go, and then turned back to the arch.

"One more thing. I've written up the river again this morning, to that keeper. I've asked him for anything his city has on this man. Anything written down." Dace's mouth went thin. "I've kept this card twenty years and I've never once asked another keeper for a thing. Not a line, not a word. We don't share; you know that. I'm asking this once, because I sent him your line, and that makes it a trade whether I like it or not." He tapped the arch with the pencil, once. "If it comes, you'll have it the day it comes."

His shadow went off the floor, and the broom came back along the main room, and Cael stood in the alcove with his hands at his sides.

---

He did not go back to the steps at once. He sat down on the bench against the back wall instead and opened the observation notebook on his knee, at the front, where the first volume's names had been copied over in his smallest hand. He read down them the way another person might have counted coins.

Feryn, Bronze, rank two, a Pressure of the long kind, who had built his weight so slowly you could watch it come. Darrow Innes, Bronze, rank one, the win that had put his name in Vell's book. Sarel, Bronze, rank one, the worst loss he had ever had, underlined twice. Talis, Copper, rank four, and his ground that shook. He had stood across a floor from every one of them. Iron rank eight sat under all of the Bronzes, on paper. It was a step below the line where the guilds began to write *Bronze*, and if he had been a clerk with a ledger he would have said that this man was less than Feryn, and less than Sarel, and that Cael had beaten worse.

He sat with the notebook open and did not believe a word of it.

The rank was a measure of what an examiner could see in a hall on a given day. He knew exactly how much that was worth; he had been looked at by an Arbiter for eleven seconds once, and written down as a single word. Feryn and Sarel had come up on circuits and in yards, the hard way, with gifts and gaps both. He had beaten the ones he had beaten by finding the gap their gift had grown round. A man who had been in a paid yard at ten and an academy at eleven did not have gaps like that. Every gap he had ever had, somebody had been paid to find and close. What was left would be very small, and very well hidden, and the man would know where it was better than anyone.

And the second thing was worse.

He turned to the back of the notebook, to his own method written out in its first form, the year before. It was the bench, and the hooks, and the three layers, and the four months he had watched Keth. Every fight he had ever won above his weight, he had won on a bench first, watching. That was what the grey book was for. That was what *he* was for, if he was honest about it.

There was nothing to watch, and there would be nothing. No bout of the man's had been fought on any floor within three hundred miles, and nobody in the district had seen him so much as plant a foot. Even if some carter had stood at the back of a shed in some town on the road and seen everything, it would not have mattered, because the man threw away whatever had been seen and came at the next one new.

He had never walked into a fight owning nothing. He tried to imagine it, and could not, and that frightened him more than the rank.

He tried, sitting there, to think how a file could be built on a man who threw away everything that had been seen of him, and the longer he tried the less sense the question made. A file was a record of what a fighter did again; that was all it had ever been. Keth chose his count fresh every cut, and even Keth had a join that opened at the end of his second every time, because a join was a shape and a shape could not be chosen away. Perhaps this man had shapes too, somewhere under all the masters' work, things he did because of how his Path was made in him and not because he had decided to. But there would be no bench to find them from. If they were there at all, they would have to be found on the floor, under the lamps, inside the exchange itself, by a boy with the man's hands already on him.

The dock partner came in under the arch with his staff over his shoulder and his breath going up white. He looked at Cael's face and then at the empty floor.

"She's not down?"

"Not yet. She was late at the lamp." Cael shut the book. "Would you go with me for half an hour? Fast. As fast as you go with her. No warning."

The broad man considered him with his broken nose and his patient eyes. "You've got the look," he said. "Somebody's asked for you."

"Yes."

"Then half an hour." He took the staff off his shoulder.

They went fast, and it was no good.

It was not the dock partner's fault. The man came in his combinations, three and four blows run together, the way he came at Lira, and Cael had stood in front of those combinations a hundred times and knew them in his skin. But this morning he was not standing in front of the dock partner. He was standing in front of a man he had never seen, whose blow would come from nowhere with nothing before it, and every time the staff moved, his body tried to stand in front of that man instead.

He felt it happen on the fourth combination. His weight, which had always gone forward into a step as easily as water goes downhill, hung back. It was only a half-beat, only his heels staying down on the stone a moment longer than they ought to, as if they were waiting to be told something by somebody who had not yet arrived. But the staff came round in the half-beat and took him on the shoulder, and he had not been hit by the dock partner on the shoulder in a month.

It happened again on the sixth. And on the ninth he felt it begin and caught it, and stepped in hard to prove to his own feet that they could, and stepped straight into the second blow of a pair.

"You're waiting for something," said the dock partner, standing off.

"I know."

"It's not me you're waiting for."

"No," said Cael. "It isn't."

---

He gave it a heading of its own in the Log before supper. A fault that showed itself against a blow he had only imagined was a fault that would show itself twice as plainly against a real one, and he had learned the hard way that the only thing worse than writing such a fault down was not writing it.

*Hesitation, the first time I've had to write the word, and it's in my feet, not my head. Against the dock partner at speed, imagining a burst with no wind-up, the weight hangs back on the heels for a half-beat, as if waiting to be told something by somebody who isn't there; three times in thirty minutes; caught it once, and stepped in too hard when I did. Is it fear or is it arithmetic? At this distance I don't know that they're different things. Watch it, and don't argue with it until I know what it's for.*

But that was later, at dusk. Before dusk came the market, and the market had heard by noon, exactly as Dace had said it would.

He went up for bread a little after midday with the dock partner's bruise still warm on his shoulder, and the whole row seemed to have got there before him. The pie woman, who had said *Cael* to him every day for three weeks, said it again, and then added nothing, which was new. Two carters at the pump stopped talking when he passed. That was not new; but this time one of them called after him, quite cheerfully, "Up the river, is it?" and the other hit him on the arm.

The paper-stall man had moved his slate line twice since Keth, and had let Cael run up nearly a mark in ink and paper. He took Cael's coppers for a new pencil and gave him his change. Then, as Cael turned to go, he said with great care, not looking up from his stock, "And you're feeling well, these days? In yourself?"

Cael looked at him.

"Quite well," he said.

"Good. Good." The man squared a stack of paper that was already square. "Only a man likes to know. That's all."

Cael was most of the way to the baker's before he understood it. The man had nearly a mark of his on the slate, and nothing to show for it but a name and a face, and a fortnight from now the face might not be worth what it had been worth yesterday. It was not a question about his health. It was a wager, put to him politely, by a man asking whether to keep extending the credit.

He laughed out loud in the street, which made a woman with a basket look round.

It went on like that the whole length of the row. Nobody said the visitor's name to him, and nobody needed to. The chestnut man gave him two chestnuts for the price of one and would not take the other copper. The mending-stall woman looked at him over her iron spectacles as he passed, and said *lad* in a voice that meant several things at once, one of which was plainly *I hope you know what you're doing*. A boy he did not know ran past him on the market steps and shouted *up the river!* as if it were a game, and was gone before Cael could see his face. By the time he reached the baker's door he understood that the district had decided something about the fight before he had decided anything about it himself, and that what it had decided was that the fight was partly theirs.

At the corner by the triangle boards the betting man had a fresh square of chalk on his slab, with the visitor's name across the top in capitals and no name of Cael's under it yet, only a long line of figures down the side that he kept rubbing out with the heel of his hand and writing again.

"I can't price you," he said, when he saw Cael reading it. He sounded aggrieved. "Nobody's seen him. How'm I to price a man nobody's seen? I've got practitioners in here all morning telling me what Iron rank eight means, and I've got the whole row telling me you're ours. And none of them has laid eyes on him." He rubbed out another figure. "Give me a week. I'll have a line by Monday if it kills me."

He walked home at dusk by the long way, round by the river gate and up past the ropewalk, because he wanted the cold and the time, and because every face on the market row had already looked at him once that day and he did not think he could stand to be looked at again. The river was high and brown and loud under the gate, and the ropewalk was shut, with the long shed dark and a single lamp in the forewoman's window at the end of it.

She was sitting at the kitchen table when Cael came in at dusk, with a carter's bill turned face down in front of her and a stub of charcoal in her fingers. On the back of the bill she had ruled fourteen squares in two rows of seven, the way Dace ruled a week on his wall. Most of them already had something in them. She did not look up when he sat down across from her. She turned the bill round so that it faced him and pushed it over the boards.

"I've done this since the dock man told me your heels stuck." She put the charcoal down. "Two weeks is nothing against what Dace says this man is. So I'm not wasting any of it. No wasted mornings. Not one. If you're ill, you're ill in the alcove where I can see you."

"Now tell me what's different," said Lira, under the hen. "Not the rank. I know the rank; anybody can say the rank. Tell me what you're actually afraid of."

"I've never fought anybody I couldn't watch first," he said. "Not once since I came down the hill. So the one thing I'm good at, I can't use until I'm standing in front of him, and by then it'll be his tempo, not mine."

Lira listened with her chin on her fist. Then she took up the charcoal again and wrote, in the square for the morning after next, very small, *right foot*.

"That's the second morning," she said. "You'll not like it."

"I told you about my heels."

"The dock man told me about your heels. You've told me now as well, and you told me on the first day, which is new." She looked at him across the pot with something that was not quite a smile. "I'll put that on the bill too, if you like. *Told me the first day.* I'll frame it."

The heavyset man's wife, without asking anybody, took Cael's bowl and filled it to the brim and set it down in front of him, and went back to her own.

Lira looked at the bowl.

"And you'll eat," she said. "Properly, this time, from the first day to the last. Before Brom you ate half of everything that was put in front of you for a week and then sat down in the alcove without meaning to."

"I didn't sit down without meaning to."

"You sat down so suddenly the post swung." She wrote a small *E* in every square along the bottom row, one after another, with great firmness. "There. Now it's on the bill."

Brom came in late, with rain on the shoulders of his coat and the cold coming off him, and the fourth bowl was on the table before he had the coat on its nail. He sat down and looked at the bill. He read it slowly, from the front, the way he read everything, and stopped at the question marks.

"I'll have the middles," he said. "All of them. Take those off." He began to eat, steadily, and spoke between spoonfuls. "I've been round the washouts' benches since three. The tannery yard, the sailmakers', the place behind the chandler's where the dockers go. Nobody's fought him. Two had heard the name. One had heard it in a guild hall, years ago, in a city up the river, and wouldn't say more, and went red when I asked." He ate. "So there's somebody. Washouts know washouts. I'll find him tomorrow, or I'll find the man who knows him."

"Dace says nobody here's seen him."

"Dace asked the carters. Carters see the road." Brom wiped his bowl with a heel of bread. "Washouts see the inside of halls, and then they come down the hill with a bag and never talk about it. I know. I came down a hill with a bag myself."

He was quiet for a moment, looking at the bread in his hand.

"Twelve years, the carters say. A yard at ten and the academy at eleven." He set the bread down. "I had a yard. At home, at Velmere, from when I could walk till I was fourteen. Everybody who hit me in it was paid to, and everybody who was paid wanted me to do well. Every morning somebody stood behind me and told me what was wrong, and every afternoon somebody else made me do it again till it wasn't." He turned the cup in his big hands. "That's what paid improvement is. People think it makes you strong. It doesn't, much. It makes you have fewer holes. Every hole you've got, somebody's paid to find it before anybody else can."

"I know," said Cael.

"I know you know. You've had me in your book since the autumn." Brom's voice did not change. "The Bronzes in your book came up on floors like this one, with their gifts and their holes all mixed up and nobody to tell them which was which. You beat the ones you beat by finding the hole the gift grew round. This man's had twelve years of being told. If there's a hole left, it's small, and he knows exactly where it is, and he's had a man paid to stand in it with him." He drank. "Don't take any comfort from the number on his card. On a card he's under the Bronze line. On a floor I'd not want to say."

Nobody said anything for a while. The sister had stopped telling the hen story and was watching Brom with her mouth a little open, because he did not usually talk so much at supper, or at all.

"There's the other thing," said Brom, more slowly. "And then I'll stop."

He looked down the table at the household: the sister, the heavyset man's wife, the heavyset man behind his bill.

"Dace has given this to the main floor. An outside Iron, who came down the river on his own money to ask for you by name. Dace isn't lighting every lamp in the place to be kind." Brom spoke carefully, choosing each word as if it would cost him. "Everybody on this hill who cares about a fight will know about this one a week before it happens. They'll remember how it went for years after they've forgotten every other card this season. Win or lose, it'll be the night they think of when they hear your name." He looked at Cael. "So it's not only about winning. It's what people decide you are, after. You've been building that since you came down the hill without ever meaning to. Now it gets weighed in one night, in front of everybody, by a man who's never seen you." He picked up the spoon. "You knew that already. I'd rather it was said once, at the start, at this table, so that none of us needs to say it again."

The kitchen was very quiet, and the range ticked as it cooled.

"Well," said the heavyset man from behind his bill, after a while, "I'll be there," and turned the page.

Lira picked up the charcoal and rubbed out the question marks beside every *B*, one by one, with her thumb.
---

He sat at the crate desk late with the Log open at the back, under the lamp, and did not write for a long while.

The hesitation was already in the book, under its heading, where he had put it before supper. He turned past it to the two columns. The question mark was still there in the margin, in pencil, beside *The right is only ever my feet*. He looked at it for a long time.

If Lira was right, if the slowness on the right was a habit and not a shape, then it could be trained, at a price. And a man who threw bursts with nothing before them would not care which side Cael had been born slow on. He would only find it, in the first exchange, if it was there to be found.

He did not move the line. Not yet. But under the question mark he wrote, small, *L. has the right foot. Second morning.*

Then he turned to a clean page and wrote the man's name at the top of it, the only name he had, and the tier and the Path and the age. And under it he wrote everything he knew, which took four lines, because that was all there was. *Paid for since ten; academy at eleven; never the same way twice; asks for that kind of fight.*

He looked at the four lines for a long time. It was the thinnest page he had ever opened on anybody, and it would have to carry him further than any of them.

At the very foot of it, after a while, he wrote one line more.

*Thirteen days.*

# Chapter 46 — No Warning Beat

Lira did not say good morning. She came under the arch at first light with her staff in her hand and her hair tied back hard, and before Cael had finished turning round from the post she was already low and already coming.

He got his forearm up, and that was all he got. The staff went past the forearm and took him across the ribs on the left side, not hard, but square, and he went back a step into the wall.

"That's one," said Lira, standing off. "You saw me come in. You saw me pick it up. You turned round to say something, and you were going to say it before you did anything else, and I hit you while you were deciding what it was." She reset, with the staff low across her body. "He'll not say good morning either."

"You might have warned me."

"That's the whole of the lesson. I'm not going to." She was already moving again.

There was no beat before it. That was what she meant, and it took him the first hour to feel how much she meant it. Every opponent he had ever stood across from had told him, in some small way, that something was coming: a breath, a shoulder, the fourth hook, the moment when a body stopped gathering and committed. He had built his whole way of fighting out of those small tellings. Lira took them away, all of them, and came off a still stance with nothing before it, the way she had learned to come in the north-window nights, so that her first blow arrived out of the same stillness that had been there a heartbeat before. When he got a hand to it, it was only because his hand had been in the right place already, by luck.

By the twentieth time she had hit him eleven times, and he had stopped counting.

"Stop trying to see it," she said, standing off with her breath going up white. "There's nothing to see. You're waiting for the thing you always wait for, and it isn't there, and so you wait longer. That half-beat you told me about, in your heels. That's what it is. Your feet are waiting for a telling that never comes." She tapped the stone with the end of the staff. "So don't stand waiting. Be moving when it comes."

"Moving where?"

"Anywhere. Round. Off the line. It doesn't matter at first." She was quiet a moment, and then said it more slowly, looking at her own hands on the staff. "Maud taught me that, with the backs of these. I stood still to catch her, every time, and every time she was waiting in the middle of my standing. When I met her moving there was no middle to wait in." She looked up at him. "It's the same thing. A man who fires with nothing before it has to fire at where you are. If where you are keeps changing, he has to choose. Make him choose."

So he moved. He stood in front of her the way he had never stood in front of anybody, not still, not watching from a fixed place, but going round on short steps, left and right and back, with his weight never set long enough to be a target. It felt like trying to stand up in a rowing boat. His eyes, which were used to settling on one place before he did anything, had nowhere to settle. She hit him less, but not much less, and twice when he went right she was there before him, because his right foot was still a beat slow and she knew it.

"Right foot," she said, the second time. "That's tomorrow."

"You said tomorrow last night."

"I did. Today you're learning to move at all." She came again with no beat before it.

Near the end of the hour she came at him from the left with nothing before it, and he used the hip.

He had not decided to; it was the old reflex, the one that had kept him whole since the Cinder House: a blow coming from the left and too close, and the hip dropping and loosening and taking him front-left round it, half a body, clean. He felt the burst go and was glad of it for the length of a breath. Then he was in the lock, two *ands* of bright stillness at the end of the burst, standing exactly where it had put him; and Lira was already there. She had not followed the burst. She had stepped once, without hurry, to the place the burst always ended, and the end of her staff was resting on his collarbone before the second *and* was out.

She left it there a moment, so that he would feel where it was.

"Bede counted two," she said. "He sat on a bench all season with a clerk's book and counted two. Do you think a man who's had masters paid to stand behind him since he was ten can't count two?" She took the staff away. "Your hip's a door. You go through it, and then you have to stand still in the doorway while it shuts behind you. That's the shape of it; you wrote it down yourself. Against him you go through that door when you know. When you've read him and the knock's told you and there's nobody at the other side. Not because you're frightened."

"What do I do when I'm frightened?"

"Use your feet. Your feet don't have a doorway." She went back to her mark. "They're slower, and they're stupid, and they're yours, and they can go anywhere. Even right, once I've finished with them."

He stood a moment with the place on his collarbone where the staff had rested. For a year the hip had been the thing he reached for when the world came at him too fast, and he had thought of it as the best thing he owned, because it was the quickest. Now he saw that quickest was not the same as best against a man who would be waiting at the end of it. The lock had been a price he paid for a read, ever since the trials. Against Reydan it would be a price he paid for nothing, unless he had bought the read first.

At the end of the hour he sat down on the bench against the back wall with his shirt stuck to him and his ribs and forearms singing, and she stood in the middle of the alcove opening and closing her left hand.

She went out under the arch. He sat on the bench and watched the place where she had been, and found that he had been holding the knock shut all hour without once thinking about it, the way he had held it shut through the whole of her bout with Maud. He sat on a while longer with his ribs and forearms singing and thought about what she had said, *be moving when it comes*, and about Maud standing in the middle of her own footprints while Lira went round and round her. He kept the tally on the back of his hand in charcoal, as he had in the autumn, a stroke for every knock and a ring round the ones that answered.

The knock did not go anywhere. When it answered clean, the answer arrived all at once and without words, and he had to put the words to it afterward: *there; heavy; going forward*. When it smeared, there were no words to put. "I'm sorry," said Brom. "I'd not have come if there'd been anybody else."

"There isn't. There's me." Ansel looked at Cael properly then, for the first time since the door. "You're the one. With no Path."

"Yes."

"I don't care about that. I want to tell you something, and then I'd like to go home." He leaned forward a little over the cold stew. "Don't try to work him out. Not early. That's what I did; I'd been taught to read a man and I went in reading, and he let me read, and while I was reading he was done with me. Whatever you've got for working people out, he's ahead of it. He was ahead of mine at nineteen." He sat back. "Survive first. Think second."

He stood up and left two coppers on the board beside his bowl, though Brom had said he was buying, and Brom did not argue.

"That's all of it," said Ansel. "I'd like you not to ask me again." He went out into the dark without his coat done up, and they heard his feet go away up the eel-market steps, quick, and then they were gone.

Brom sat looking at the two coppers for a long time. Then he picked up Ansel's bowl, and his own, and ate them both, cold, steadily, to the bottom, and wiped the second with bread.

"He paid for his own," he said, when he had finished.

"You told him you were buying."

"I know." Brom put the coppers in his pocket rather than leave them on the board. "He didn't want it said, after, that he'd sold it for a bowl of stew. That's what that was." He stood up. "I'll give them back to him when he'll take them. That'll be a while."

Cael sat at the crate desk that night a long time with the Log open and the stub of the knock tally still grey on the back of his hand, listening to the house settle round him, the sister's hen shifting in the coal box below and the heavyset man's slow tread on the stair, and did not write anything at all until the house was quiet.

They were four words, and he had spent a year building a method, a notebook, a whole way of being in a room, and the four words said most of it, plainly, and said it backwards.

*Survive first. Think second.*

He had always thought first; that was the method. He sat on a bench and thought for four months, and then stood up and survived on what he had thought. But against a man he could not watch, the bench would have to happen on the floor, under the man's hands, and the only way to get the time to think at all was to be alive and standing at the end of each exchange. Survival was not the opposite of the method. It was what would buy the method its time.

Lira knocked on his door frame on her way to bed, still in her shirt with her staff over her shoulder, and he showed her the line.

She read it, and was quiet, and then said, "That's you, said badly."

"It's me said backwards."

"It's you said by somebody who paid for it." She handed the Log back. "You'd have got there. It would've taken you four pages and you'd have used the word *architecture*. He did it in four words because he lay on a floor in front of his whole house and had three years to think about it." She went to her own door. "Use it. It doesn't matter where it came from. Good advice doesn't care who bled for it."

Her door closed. He sat and looked at the line, and then, under it, wrote what she had said about meeting it moving, and the half-beat, and *right foot, tomorrow*. Then he wrote one more line, and looked at it, and did not cross it out.

*He hasn't talked about that hall in two years. He talked about it tonight because we asked, and he still couldn't say the name. I owe him something for that, and I don't know what.*

# Chapter 47 — It Compresses Inward

On the second morning Lira gave him his right foot, as she had promised at the kitchen table, and he did not like it, as she had promised too.

She began by making him stand on a chalk cross in the middle of the alcove floor with his weight even. "I know it's late."

"Then why is it late?"

He did not know. He had thought for a year that his right foot was slow because he was made that way, the way the hip went left because the Wind had been made in him that way. But Lira had taken that from him at the kitchen table, and he had nothing to put in its place.

"Because you step with it," she said, after the tenth. "You pick it up and put it down somewhere else, like a man crossing a stream on stones. Your left doesn't do that. Your left never steps; the hip takes it. You've been going left on the Wind so long that you've forgotten how a body goes anywhere without one." She came round behind him and put the end of her staff against the back of his left knee, lightly. "When it comes from the right, don't step. Drop."

"Drop where?"

"Into this knee. All of you, at once, as if somebody had cut a string." She pressed the staff a little. "And as you go down, roll the weight across. Off the left and onto the right, like a barrel rolled off a cart. Your right foot doesn't have to go anywhere. It's already there. He showed her, and it was terrible. He dropped and forgot to roll, and sat down on the stone. By the end of the hour he had done it perhaps sixty times, and perhaps four of them had looked like the thing she meant.

What made it hard was not the knee, though the knee would have its say later, and not the roll. It was that his body had never in its life been asked to do this and did not believe it could. Going left, it had a year of the hip behind it, a year of a borrowed thing that knew the way already, so that his own legs had only ever had to follow where the Wind took them. Going right, there was nothing to follow. That evening, after Lira's hour, he did the redirect three more times against Brom's slow burst, in the lamplight, with the shoulder still remembering the first three. Then he sat on the bench with his eyes shut and the place behind his breastbone aching, and thought about the number on his hand.

He had believed, for the whole of the autumn, that the knock was a thing that answered about half the time, the way a well gives so many buckets a day. He had believed it because it was true. But it had been true only in the places he had happened to use it, under the conditions he had happened to be in, and he had never once made the conditions change on purpose to see where it broke. Brom had made them change, one at a time, and it had broken exactly where he said it would. And now he could see the break, he could work there. It was the same thing he did to everybody else, and he had never thought to do it to himself.

*Nine in twenty, moving,* he wrote that night. *Of the eleven that didn't answer, six went out with my foot still coming down. The read isn't bad in motion. My knocking is. Plant, arrive, then ask.*

Under it, smaller, he wrote: *Six redirects done. Six to go.*

# Chapter 48 — Declined to Be Interesting

On the sixth morning the drop went right thirty times in a hundred, and Lira said nothing about it at all, which he had learned to take as the best thing she could say. His left knee had gone stiff on the stairs and stayed stiff till noon. His forearms were the colour of a plum gone over. When he went up to the market for bread at midday, the betting man had his line at last, chalked large across the slab under the visitor's name, and it was two lines, not one.

"Practitioners," said the betting man, tapping the top figure, which was long against Cael, three and four to one. "Anybody with a card in his coat who knows what rank eight means. They'll not touch you." He tapped the lower figure, which was short, nearly even. "The row. The stalls, the carters, the women at the pump. They've no idea what rank eight means and they don't care. You're theirs." He looked at the two figures with the frank dislike of a man whose trade was making two crowds agree. "I've never had a line split like it. I can't make it one number. Every time I shorten the top, the row comes and lays money and the bottom gets shorter still."

Cael looked at the slab for a long moment. It was the first time he had seen what the district thought he was written down in figures, and it was two different things at once.

On the sixth afternoon Cael planted his foot before he knocked, every time, and arrived before he asked, and the knock began to come back to him.

It was slow work, slower than the standing had been, because there was no way to plant and arrive and ask without stopping, and stopping in the middle of a circling man was exactly the thing a fight would never let him do for long. So he learned to make the stop small. He learned to let the left foot come down as Brom's weight came round, and to let the knock go out in the instant between the foot touching and the next step beginning, a gap so short that he could not have measured it with a pulse. When he caught the gap, the answer came back nearly as clean as it had at rest: *inward*, *nothing*, *inward*, and once *going left*, as Brom threw the slow push at the wall instead of at him to see if Cael would notice. When he missed the gap, it was smear, or it was the stone floor answering instead of the man.

Thirteen in twenty.

He held the back of his hand to the light from the arch and looked at the rings, and Brom looked at them over his shoulder and said nothing, which from Brom was a kind of praise.

"Of the seven that missed," said Cael, "four were me going early again. I can feel it now, after. I couldn't yesterday."

"Feeling it after is the first half." Brom sat. "Tomorrow you'll feel it before. Then it'll stop." He leaned his back against the wall. "Your foot's still loud. But it's loud on the right beat now."

Cael sat down beside him with the band thickening behind his eyes, thinking about the gap. A day ago he would have said the knock needed stillness, and he would have been right. Now he thought it did not need stillness so much as a moment of it, a single arrival, and that a man moving round a floor arrived somewhere with every step if he let himself. The whole art was in letting himself.

They were sitting like that when Dace's shadow came across the floor from the arch.

It had been written for a house, not for a fighter; the first page said so. The last page was the clerk's conclusion, for the house, and it was blunt. A good plan against him would work for an exchange, perhaps two. Then he would have read it, and thrown it away, and the opponent would need a new plan, and then another. Sooner or later every opponent ran out of new plans before he ran out of ways to answer them. The clerk advised the house to keep its promising boy away from him for at least two years.

*Lost once. To a man who gave him nothing and waited. Overcommitted in the fifth, looking for something to answer. Won the rematch in two. — L.: Maud did it to me. It works on people who've never been made to wait.*

# Chapter 49 — Ansel's Round

Ansel came to the arch on the eighth morning, before Lira, with the frost still white on the steps behind him and his coat buttoned to the throat, and stood there with his big quick hands in his pockets as if he had not yet decided whether he had arrived.

Cael was alone, working the drop against the straw post with his eyes half shut. He felt the light change at the arch and turned, and saw who it was, and stood still.

"The dock man says you can't get anybody fast," said Ansel. He did not come in. "He says she's quick, your girl, but she's a Wind, and she knows you too well, and the big one's slow on purpose. He says you've had nobody in front of you all week who moves like a man who means to finish you." He looked at the straw post, not at Cael. "I've been lying awake since the stew. Six nights. I'd like to stop."

"You said not to ask you again."

"You didn't. I'm asking you." Ansel took his hands out of his pockets and looked at them, front and back, the knuckles that had been broken and set. "One round. Three exchanges. On my terms, or not at all."

"Tell me the terms."

"My speed. Not what I am now, after two years of the cooper's back room and a bag of sand. What I was that autumn. As near as I can get it." He said it flatly, a man reading out a list he had written in the dark. "I'll not hold back to be kind and I'll not hold back because you're a boy. You'll use whatever you've got. Anything. I want to see it." He paused. "And not in here."

"Where?"

"Out there." He tipped his head at the main room behind him, at the long side floor that ran under the east benches, where the newcomers worked in the mornings and the Tuesday cards were fought. "On the stone, in front of whoever's on the benches. Not in a hole in the wall where nobody can see." His mouth moved, very slightly. "I've not stood on a floor where anybody could watch me since I came down the hill. I sweep the cooper's and hit sand in the dark. If I'm going to do this, I'll do it where it counts."

Cael studied him for a while, the buttoned coat and the flat coast face that would not let anything show, and understood that the terms were not for him.

"There's one more thing," he said. "On my side."

Ansel waited.

"There's a thing I do, close in, that some people mind." He chose the words with care, because they would have to be true without being the whole of it. "At about a forearm, if I ask for it, I can tell where a man's weight is going, and whether he means a thing or only looks as if he does. Only that much. It doesn't tell me what he is, or what he's thinking. Only the weight." He met Ansel's eyes. "I'd like your leave to use it. If you'd rather I didn't, I won't."

Ansel looked at him with something in his face that Cael could not read.

"You're asking," he said.

"Yes."

"He didn't ask." Ansel's voice did not change, but something in his shoulders did. "He read every part of me in a minute and a half and he never asked me for a thing. Not that it was his to ask. A floor's a floor." He put his hands back in his pockets. "Ask it all you like. You've my leave. I'd like to see if it does you any good."

They fought at the end of the side floor, under the east benches, an hour after dawn, in front of perhaps a dozen people.

It was not a bout. Vell's table was empty and her books were locked away in the back room, and nobody would write a line about it anywhere. But word had gone round the alcoves the way word went round everything in the Ironyard. By the time Ansel took his coat off and folded it on the end of the bench, there were six of Keth's newcomers on the second bench, and the tall lad and the girl who kept her eyes open, and two stall-keepers who had come in for warmth. The dock partner stood at the west post with his arms folded on his chest. Lira had come down under the arch to find the alcove empty and followed the noise. She stood at the rope beside Brom with her staff upright against her shoulder, and did not say anything, and her face was very still.

Ansel stood at the north mark in his shirt with his sleeves rolled, rolling his shoulders, slowly, one and then the other. He looked smaller with his coat off. He also looked like somebody Cael had not met before.

"Begin when you like," said Brom, from the rope. Nobody else had any right to say it.

Ansel began.

He came in from the mark without any warning at all. It was not the stillness Lira had been teaching, the stance with nothing before it; it was simply fast, faster than anything Cael had stood in front of in a fortnight, a short-stepping rush that ate the floor between the marks in three strides, and then his hands were working. He threw them from very close, short and hard, left and right and left, a hook into the ribs and a straight at the face and another hook, all inside the space of one breath, the quick close work a coast house had sent up the river to do it credit.

Cael did not try to work him out.

It was the hardest thing he had ever made himself not do. Every part of him that had been built on a bench wanted to watch the hands, and find the order, and look for the shoulder that went before the hook. He let it all go by and moved instead. He went round on short steps the way Lira had made him go round all week, never settling, never giving Ansel a place to aim at for longer than a heartbeat. When the first hook came from his right he dropped into his left knee and rolled across onto his right foot, low and over, and the fist went over his head through the space where his head had been.

Somebody on the second bench said *oh*.

He had dropped like that four hundred times in the alcove against Lira, and it had never once felt like this. In the alcove the drop had been a thing he did on purpose, slowly at first and then less slowly, while she watched his knee. Here it had happened the way a hand comes up to a face when something is thrown at it, with no part of him deciding, and he came up out of the roll on his right foot with the stone cold under him and the sound of the benches arriving late, as if they had seen it a moment after he had done it.

The second combination caught him. It was the straight, at the face, which he slipped, and then a short hook under it into the left ribs that he never saw at all, and his breath went out of him in one piece. He went back three steps and Ansel let him go, and stood at the middle of the floor breathing easily, watching.

The third combination he felt coming before it came.

He had planted his left foot at the end of a step, and arrived, and let the knock go out in the space before the next step began, the way he had been learning to do for six days with a man circling him. Ansel was a pace and a little more away. At that distance the knock had always been poor. But Ansel was coming forward, closing the pace as Cael knocked, and the answer came back clean through the closing: *there, coming, heavy on the right*. Cael went left before the right hand had started, and the hook went by in the air, and he was out on Ansel's side with the open ribs in front of him.

He did not hit them; he had thought about it all night, and decided, and he stood off.

Ansel turned and looked at him, and something went across the flat face for the first time, a kind of surprise.

"End of the exchange," said Brom.

In the second Ansel did what Cael had been waiting for him to do.

He did not rush, but came out from his mark slowly, on his toes, and stood at a pace with his hands up and loose, and let Cael come to him. When Cael came, with a short straight at the shoulder to see what it would bring back, Ansel moved enough to let it miss and no more, and watched what Cael did. Cael tried a second thing, a feint at the ribs and a step to the left. Ansel moved again, exactly enough, and watched.

Cael felt it then, in his stomach, and knew what it was. Ansel was doing what had been done to him. He was standing in front of Cael and reading him, the way he had been read in a hall up the river three years ago, letting Cael bring his things one by one so that he could look at each and put it away. He had been lying awake six nights, and he had spent them on this: on what it had been like to be on the wrong side of it, and on what it would be like to be on the right side, once, with somebody else.

And Cael understood that this was the thing he had to learn, the thing in the clerk's fourth page, and that Ansel had brought it to him on purpose.

So he stopped bringing things.

He stood off at a pace and a half and moved. He went round on his short steps, left and right and back, with his hands up and his weight never settling, and he did not throw anything. He did not feint, or try the hip, or step in, or do any of the dozen things a year of watching had taught him to do to find out what a man was. He only stayed alive, and stayed out of reach, and gave Ansel nothing new to look at.

It went on for a long time. It was the longest he had ever stood on a floor without trying to win.

It was terrible. Every heartbeat of it his body was telling him to go and find out. Twice he caught his own weight beginning to go forward of its own accord, toward a gap he had seen in Ansel's guard, and stopped it, and felt the stopping in his whole frame like a cart dragged up short. The benches did not understand it. He heard somebody on the second bench say *what's he doing*, and somebody else say *nothing*, and the first say *well that's no good*.

Ansel understood it.

Cael watched him understand it, not in the flat face, which did not move, but in the feet. For a while they were patient; Ansel stood at his pace and watched and waited for Cael to bring him something, the way a man waits at a stall for the stall-keeper to come back. Then they were less patient. Ansel's weight began to come forward a little, onto the balls of his feet, a little further at each step. Then he tried a thing of his own, a quick straight at the shoulder to draw something out. Cael moved enough to let it miss and no more, and brought nothing back. Ansel tried another, a hook to the ribs. Cael dropped and rolled right and was gone, and brought nothing back.

And Ansel, who had been sent up the river three years ago because he was the quickest thing on the coast, and who had not been allowed to be quick on any floor since, ran out of waiting.

Cael felt it go before it went. He knocked from a planted foot at a pace and the knock came back with a kind of urgency in it that it had never given him before: *there, coming, all of it, now*. Ansel came in with everything, the whole of the quick close work at once, left and right and left. It was too much and too far, because he had waited too long and wanted it too badly, and his weight went a hand past where it ought to have stopped.

Cael was not in front of it. He had gone left at the knock, on the hip, a read burst, front-left round the outside of the first hook, half a body. In the lock, the two *ands* of bright stillness at the end of it, he saw what he had known he would see. Ansel's whole frame had gone past him with nothing behind it, and the back of his ribs was open, and his feet were wrong.

Cael put a short straight strike into the air a finger's width from Ansel's ribs, and held it there.

Ansel stopped. He stood with his weight gone over his front foot and Cael's fist beside his ribs, and looked down at it.

"End of the exchange," said Brom, quietly.

Ansel walked back to his mark and stood there with his hands on his hips and his head down, breathing, for a long time. Then he looked up, at Cael, across the floor.

"That's what he found," said Ansel. "In the hall. That's what it was."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I wanted to know." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "I've been telling myself for three years that he was too fast, and that's true, he was. But it's not what beat me. I beat me. I couldn't stand there and be read without trying to read him back, and while I was trying I gave him everything." He let out a breath. "One more. My speed. And don't stand off this time. I want to see what you do when it's real."

The third exchange was fast and short and ugly, and Cael lost the first half of it.

Ansel came at full speed, as he had in the first, with nothing held back, and Cael met it with everything Lira had put into him in eight days. He dropped and rolled right under the first hook. He knocked from a planted foot at a pace and got *there, heavy left* and went right on his feet, a beat slow still but not a whole beat. He took a short hook on the forearm that numbed it to the elbow, and another on the shoulder he had spent nine redirects on, and felt the shoulder tell him so. Once he knocked and got smear, a confused rush of weight that did not know which way it was going. Because he had decided before the round that smear meant *don't go*, he did not go. He stood off and took a straight on the forearm instead of walking into the hook behind it, and was glad, after, of the bruise.

At the end, Ansel came in once more with the whole of his quick work, and Cael knocked and got it clean, *right, coming, all of it*. He dropped and rolled right, under it and over, and came up off the right foot into the open side with a short strike that he stopped against the cloth of Ansel's shirt over the ribs.

Ansel's last hook had stopped too. It was resting, very lightly, against the side of Cael's head above the ear, where it would have landed a moment later if either of them had meant it.

They stood like that for the length of a breath, each with a fist against the other, in the middle of the side floor under the east benches, while the twelve people watching made no sound at all.

"Even," he said.

"Even," said Cael.

Ansel did not go to his coat straight away.

He stood in the middle of the side floor in his shirt, with the sweat going cold on him and the newcomers on the second bench looking at him with the frank interest of people who had just seen something and did not yet know what. "Who are you?" said the tall lad, finally, because nobody else would.

Ansel looked at him for a moment.

"Ansel," he said. "I was Bronze, on the coast. Three years ago they sent me up the river to an invitational and I drew a nineteen-year-old called Reydan." The tall lad's mouth was open.

"Faster," said Ansel. "And it wasn't the fast that did it." He put his coat on and buttoned it, one button at a time, to the throat. "It was that he'd let me show him everything first. I'll tell you about it sometime, if you'd like. Not today."

"I saw."

"You stood off," she said. "In the second. For as long as I've ever seen anybody stand off in this building. And you didn't go."

"I wanted to."

"I know you wanted to. I watched you want to. Twice your weight went." She looked at him with something in her face he had not seen since the night of Maud. "You didn't go. He wrote it before he iced the arm, sitting on the bench in the alcove with the Log on his knee and the forearm going stiff. *Ansel, with his leave. At his speed, a pace and closing. ---

The defending stage began that afternoon, and the knock fell to pieces again, exactly as Brom had said it would.

It was not Brom's slow burst that did it. It was the other things. Brom circled, and gathered, and sent the slow push the way he had all week, but now between the pushes he struck as well. They were plain blows from his big forearm, slow and heavy, at Cael's head and ribs and shoulder, and Cael had to meet them or get out of their way while he knocked. Every blow that came at him pulled the whole of his attention to it, for the length of the blow, and the knock in that moment went out thin or not at all. Twice he knocked at the very moment he was blocking, and the answer that came back was only his own arm.

Ten in twenty.

He had expected it, because Brom had told him to expect it, and it still went into him like cold water. He understood, sitting there with the band stacking, that this was what every stage would do. Each time Brom took something away, the knock would fall, and each time they would have to build it up again from wherever it landed, and there was no way to skip the falling.

"Half," said Brom. "From four in five, moving. That's the price of having something in front of you that wants to hurt you." He sat down on the bench. "It's the same price every man with a read pays. Mine fell to nothing the first month I fought in the boat shed. I could read a man standing in front of me plain as a sign over a shop door, till he hit me. Then it was gone till I'd stopped being hit." He rubbed his jaw. "You'll get it back. Not as fast as moving. It's harder."

Cael sat with the band stacking behind his eyes and the forearm Ansel had hooked throbbing in time with it.

"Six days," he said.

"Six days. Plenty." Brom stood. "Tomorrow."

---

Lira came under the arch on the ninth morning and did not begin at once.

She stood just inside the alcove with the staff in her hand and looked at him, and he waited, because he had learned that look and it meant she was going to say something she had decided on the way down the hill.

"The knock," she said. "Brom's taught it all week. Standing, moving, defending. All on him."

"Yes."

"I watched you yesterday with Ansel. It caught him coming at a pace, and he's quicker than Brom will ever be." She turned the staff in her hands. "And I'm coming at you every morning with nothing before it, which is the whole of what the man on Tuesday does. And you've been holding that thing shut every morning, all week, because I told you not to go into me, the night of Maud."

"You did."

"I know I did. I meant it then." She looked at him levelly. "You have my leave. In here, in the mornings, while I'm drilling you, till the floor. Not on the stairs, and not at the table, and not after Tuesday unless I say so again. And you tell me every time it answers. Every time, out loud, so I know what you've had."

He stood very still.

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to. You keep saying that to me as if I didn't know." She came to her mark. "It's no use to me having you train against a man with no beat while you're keeping the one thing shut that might catch it. That's not fairness. That's waste." Her face went short. "And I'm not made of glass. Ready?"

He was not ready, and she came anyway, from the right with nothing before it, and he dropped and rolled and was gone under the staff before he had decided to.

He knocked on the next one. He planted his left foot at the end of a short step and let the knock go out toward her as she began. It came back cleaner than anything Brom had ever given him: *there, coming, all of her, left*. It was quick and small and alive, like a bird going off a sill.

"Left," he said, and was already gone right.

"Yes," said Lira. She did not stop. "Again."

He knocked on thirteen of the twenty she sent him that morning, and eleven of the thirteen answered, and he called every one of those aloud. The other seven he did not knock at all, because his feet had already gone, and a knock would have shut him in place to ask. He found that the drop and the knock would not share any better than the hip and the knock had, and wrote it in the Log at noon. *Knock, shut, then go: still true. The drop is a go. Don't knock while I'm already dropping. Choose.*

In the afternoon, defending, with Brom striking slow and heavy between the pushes and Cael meeting the blows and knocking in the gaps between them, the knock gave fifteen in twenty.

Brom looked at the rings on the back of Cael's hand for a long time.

"From ten," he said. "In one day."

"Lira gave me leave this morning."

"I heard." Brom looked at the arch, where Lira's staff could be heard faintly from the alcove beyond, working the post alone. "That'll be it. You've had a morning of somebody who doesn't give you a beat, and you've had it with the thing open." He stood up. "That's three stages. Tomorrow we finish the defending. Then tired." He was quiet a moment. "Tomorrow's the ninth session. I'll come at you harder than I have. I'll come as near his speed as Iron Skin lets me."

"Yes."

"You're not frightened of it."

"I'm frightened of everything," said Cael. "It's only that I've stopped minding."

Brom looked at him for a while, and then laughed, which he did not often do, a big slow sound that filled the alcove.

"Good," he said. "That's about right."

# Chapter 50 — Session Nine

On the tenth morning the mending stall had pennants.

Cael saw them on his way up for bread after Lira's hour, with his forearms singing and his left knee stiff on the market steps. They hung from a string across the front of the stall like washing: small triangles of cheap red cloth, a dozen of them, with a short bar of white stitched across each one. It took him a moment to see that the white bar was meant to be a rope, and the red was the Ironyard's lamps, or the stone, or simply red, because red sold. The mending-stall woman sat under them with her iron spectacles on her nose and a needle going, stitching a thirteenth.

"Two coppers," she said, without looking up. "Three for five. Had a carter buy six before breakfast for his nieces." She bit the thread. "Don't look at me like that, lad. I've mended the Ironyard's shirts for thirty years and never once made a copper off a bout. A woman's allowed one week."

He bought one, without knowing why, except that it seemed rude not to, and put it in his coat beside the Log, where it stayed.

The row had changed again in the four days since the betting man's split line. Two of the food stalls by the pump had begun to bake in larger batches, and the pie woman had a boy helping her who had not been there before. Outside the Ironyard's side door, when Cael passed it, two men he knew by sight from the eel market were standing with Dace in the cold, talking with their hands, and Dace was listening with his pencil against his chest and the face he wore when he was being asked for something he had never given anybody. Cael heard *inside the doors*, and *three hours on their feet*, and *a copper a bowl*, and slowed without meaning to.

Dace saw him over the men's shoulders and lifted the pencil an inch, which meant *wait*. The two men went off toward the eel market looking pleased with themselves, and Dace came over to him with the expression of a man who has just sold something he had sworn never to sell.

"Stew," he said. "Inside my doors. On the night." He looked at the side door as if it had betrayed him. "Twenty years nobody's eaten inside that building but the rats. I've thrown men out for bringing in a pie." He turned the pencil over. "But I've got five hundred people who think they're coming on Tuesday, and room for four hundred if they all breathe in. They'll stand three hours. If they go out to eat they'll not get back in, and then they'll stand in the street and shout, and the street will be full of people shouting the night the whole hill's watching to see if we can run a thing properly." He put the pencil back against his chest. "So the eel-market men can have the corner by the slate. A copper a bowl, and they sweep up after, and if I find one bowl on Vell's table I'll throw the pot in the river."

"You've never let anybody."

"I've never had to." Dace looked at him. "You'll learn this, if you stay in any building long enough. You keep your rules for twenty years so that when you have to break one you know exactly what you're breaking, and how much, and you do it once." He went back in at the side door.

Brom came down at noon with his sleeves already turned back, and did not sit.

"Defending again," he said. "Same as yesterday. I'll come at you with the push and I'll strike between, and you'll deal with the strikes and knock for the push. But I'm going to come faster. "You may. May I?"

"You may." Brom almost smiled. "We're very polite."

*Inward*, and he was already turning, letting the push go past him at the slant.

*Inward*, and the drop, right, under it.

A strike, met on the forearm Ansel had hooked, which complained.

*Nothing*, and he stood, and the strike came instead of the push, and he met it.

*Inward.*

It was the longest he had ever stood in front of Brom without either of them stopping, and somewhere about the fifth push he stopped noticing the band behind his eyes, or the forearm, or the cold coming in under the arch. There was only the next gap. He had felt this before, on floors, in the middle of a bout that was going well, a narrowing of everything down to the width of one man and one moment. He had never felt it in the alcove, in a drill, with nothing riding on it but a number on the back of his hand.

By the eighth he had found a rhythm of a kind. It was not comfortable and it was not clean, but it was a rhythm: strike, met; gap, knock; push, gone. By the twelfth his shirt was stuck to his back and the band behind his eyes had stacked twelve deep and was beginning to fill the room, and the forearm had stopped complaining and gone numb, which was worse. He had called ten of the twelve right, and knew it without looking at his hand. It came after a strike. That was all Cael would be sure of afterward, and he was sure of it because he wrote it down within the hour, before he could make it into a better story. There was no time, and Cael knew that afterward, too. There was no space between the strike and the gathering for his foot to plant and arrive and ask. He did not plant, and he did not knock.

And he did not need to.

It was as if the gathering and his answer to it were one movement, the way a foot finds the last stair in the dark before you know you have reached for it. *Second possibility: something already in me doing a thing I've never found it doing. One of the three, or the three together, under a pressure and a closeness that hasn't happened again. *Filed as anomaly. Monitor.*

The hip went left. The knock gave him when and not where. The redirect cost three a session and he had three left in his shoulder for the eleventh day. Those were things he had, with prices he knew. It would not be there on Tuesday. Brom was right. He would not walk out there hoping for it.

"You were here," she said. "The whole hour. I thought you'd be somewhere else."

"So did I."

"Good." She blew out the lamp. "That's the right place to be."

He thought about Brom's hands on his chest with nothing behind them. He thought about the clerk's nine pages, and Ansel buttoning his coat to the throat, and the drop that had gone into his legs. Four days. Somewhere up the hill above the saddler's, a man who had been paid for since he was ten was lying in a narrow bed, or not lying in it, and thinking about whatever such a man thought about four days out. # Chapter 51 — The Night Before

The tired stage began on the eleventh day, and Lira made sure he was tired for it.

She did not cut the morning short. She gave him the whole hour and then another half on top of it, at full speed, from the right and the left and the front with nothing before any of it. She sent him to the stone with the drop and up again with the roll until his left knee was shaking at the bottom of every drop and his breath was coming in pieces. At the end she stood back and looked at him the way she looked at a horse, and said, "That'll do. Go and give him what's left," and went up the steps without another word.

What was left was not much. He sat on the bench in the alcove at noon with the bread Brom had brought going down in lumps, and felt it: the particular emptiness of a body that has been spent to the bottom and not yet filled again, where every movement has to be asked for twice. His eyes were slow and his feet were stupid. When Brom stood up and set himself in the middle of the floor, Cael got up after him the way an old man gets out of a chair.

"This is the one," said Brom. "This is how you'll be in the third exchange. May I?"

"You may. May I?"

"You may."

It was worse than defending. Defending had pulled his attention to the blows; this pulled his attention to nothing, because there was nothing in him to pull. The knock went out thin and came back thinner. Twice he planted and arrived and simply forgot to ask, and stood there with the gap going by while Brom gathered and sent. Once he knocked and got *inward* clean and could not make his legs do anything about it, and the push took him in the chest and sat him down on the stone, and he sat there a moment looking at the beams before he got up.

Twelve in twenty.

"Three in five," said Brom. "Tired." He did not sound disappointed. "That's where you are. Now we know."

Then, because it was the eleventh day and he had kept the last three for it, they did the redirect. He had thought, after the tired stage, that he would not be able to; but the redirect did not want quick feet or a clear head. It wanted a plant and a turned shoulder and the nerve to let a push run through him instead of stopping it, and he had that much left. Ten went through at the right angle. Eleven went through a little high, and he felt it in the shoulder's groove like a wheel in a rut. Twelve went through clean. Then the bill came, all of it, along the whole groove at once, and he sat on the bench and let it come.

"Twelve," said Brom.

"Twelve. That's the lot." He did not open his eyes. "The shoulder's done till Tuesday. I'll not ask it for anything till I'm on the floor, and on the floor it'll give me three."

"Three's what you said a week ago."

"Three's what it was a week ago. I've only found out it's true."

*Tired: 12, 16.*

He did not come in. He stood the way Dace stood at arches, with the light of every lamp in the building behind him and his face in shadow, and his big hands at his sides.

"You can win this," said Brom.

"I know."

"I don't mean get through it." Brom's voice was flat and careful, as if he had been carrying the words a long way and did not want to drop any. "I don't mean lose it well and write down what it taught you, the way you've done with everything since I sat down on your bench. You've got good at that. You're the best I've ever seen at it. I don't mean that." He let a moment go. "I mean win."

Cael looked at him for a long time.

He thought of the thirteen days since Dace's shadow came across the alcove floor: Lira's staff from the right with nothing before it, and the drop that had gone into his legs. Twelve redirects and a groove worn through his shoulder. Sixteen in twenty, tired. Ansel buttoning his coat, and the clerk's fourth page, and the half-second in the ninth session that he could not call. A man on the west bench who managed what he leaked. He had always gone into a fight leaving room for a loss that would teach him something, a way out at the back of every plan. He looked for it now, out of habit, and found that it was not there. He had not taken it away. It had simply stopped being there at some point in the last few days, while he was busy.

"I know," he said.

It came out different. He heard it come out different, and so did Brom.

Brom stood in the arch a moment longer. Something in his big shoulders let go, very slightly, the way they let go after the hold when the breath went out through his nose.

"That's what I came down to hear," he said. "I'll be at the north rope. She'll be at the south. If you look for either of us you'll find us, and if you don't look you'll know we're there." He stepped back out of the arch. "Be on your own with it now. I'll walk her up the hill."

He went, the way he always went, without any ceremony, and Cael heard his feet go away along the main room and Lira's voice meet him at the far end. Then he was alone in the alcove with the lamp, and the straw post, and the chalk cross on the floor where Lira had made him drop four hundred times, and the noise of the building going quiet round him.

He sat on the bench against the back wall with the Log open on his knee and did not write anything for a long time.

The lamp burned down a little as he sat. Out in the main room the sweepers went over the stone, and the eel-market men's handcart went out at the side door with its pots knocking, and voices went past the arch saying *tomorrow*, and went on. He turned the pages back, past the fortnight's tallies and the clerk's account and Ansel's four words, and past the two columns, with *Going right* crossed out of *Shape* and written into *Habit* with its cost. He went past the three entries with their notices at the head, Wind and Pressure and Iron, and further, to the eight unasked instances carried from the old book, and the farmer, and *copies of real people, taken on real days*. A year and more of it. He had come down the hill with a bag and a direction, and this was what he had to show for it, and it was not nothing.

He began with his own body, because his body was the purse he would be paying out of all tomorrow night, and he wanted to know what was in it.

*Forearms: yellow mostly, the left still blue where Ansel hooked it. Ribs: quiet unless pressed. Left knee: stiff in the morning, sound by noon; it took all the dropping. Right foot: mine. Shoulder: spent twelve, rested two days, will give three. Lira halved the last two mornings and kept the speed. Legs lighter than they've ever been. Tired underneath, the good kind.*

Then the things he had, one under another, short, so that he could carry the whole list in his head onto the floor and never need to open the book.

*Wind-adjacent. Left only; the fan. Half a body. The lock, two* ands*, and he'll count two; a man paid for since ten counts two. Three an exchange at the most. Read bursts cheap, a thin line; reactive bursts full price and a lock to stand in. Chain of two needs the fill, and the fill shows. Rule: the hip only when I know. When I don't know, the feet.*

*The feet. The drop and roll, right, low. No doorway. Slower than the hip; quicker than they were. In my legs since the sixth evening.*

*Iron-adjacent. The knock. Four in five, at a forearm, in every condition Brom could make. One in five lies. Gives me when; doesn't give me where. Spend it on the pulling in and nothing else.*

*Pressure-adjacent. Three redirects in the shoulder, and no more. Every one is paid for in the side and the breastbone, and I'll need those for the next exchange. Only buy a place I mean to stand in. The giving face: three on the beat, if he has a beat to ride. He may not.*

*The gaze. Runs the whole fight. No ration tomorrow. Blind past ten degrees at full depth; he'll find that or he won't.*

He looked at the list for a long time. Then he turned to the front of the book, where the fragments were, and counted them the way he had counted them on the first page a year ago, one finger on each.

Three integrated fragments, one anomaly.

That was the whole of it. Three things he had, with prices he knew, and one thing that had happened once in an alcove and charged him nothing and would not come when called.

He turned to the back again, to the page headed *Anomalies*, and read the entry through. Under it there was a space he had left without knowing why. He knew why now. He wrote one line in it, in the field he had always kept at the foot of every entry for whatever did not fit anywhere else.

*Note: Session nine. Could not reproduce. Still don't know what that was.*

He did not add anything to it. There was nothing to add.

Then he turned to a clean page and wrote the man's name at the top of it, and the plan under it, in a hand that was steadier than he had expected it to be.

*Reydan. Iron-tier, rank eight. Pressure, burst. What I can feel: the pulling in, at a forearm, four times in five. What I can't see: anything else. What I know of him from the clerk: he builds a read of a man, uses it, and the moment it fails he drops it and builds a new one. He's never kept a broken read in his life. Every new one has to be built from what's in front of him at that moment.*

*If I can force him to abandon three consecutive reads, his fourth response will be pattern-broken. That's when I move.*

He read it back once; it was true, as far as he could see, and it was more than he had had on the first morning, which had been four lines and a number.

He closed the book.

He did not go up the hill straight away. He sat on in the alcove with the lamp low and the Log shut on his knee, and did not think about the plan, or the knock, or the man. He thought about nothing in particular for a long time: the chalk cross on the floor, the straw post on its rope, the cold coming in under the arch. Fear came and sat with him, and he let it, the way he would have let a dog lie down at his feet, and after a while it was only another thing in the room.

When he went out at last the main room was dark except for the lamps the sweepers had left at the doors. The stone gave back his footsteps. Above it, on every beam and hook, the hundreds of lamps hung cold and black and waiting to be lit again.

Somewhere up the hill, above a saddler's, a man who had been paid for since he was ten was lying in a narrow bed. He had walked out of the building tonight without looking back, at an even pace, the pace of somebody who had never in his life needed to hurry.

Cael went up the steps into the cold, and home.

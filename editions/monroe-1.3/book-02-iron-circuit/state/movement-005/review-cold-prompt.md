# Chapter 30 — Main Season

Dace put the main season up on the Monday, as he had said he would, and he did it standing on an upturned crate with a fresh stick of chalk and the whole floor watching him.

He began at the top left corner of the long wall by the slate and worked right, and he did not stop until he ran out of wall. Cael had seen the season boards before, from the bench, in his first year, and had not understood then what he was looking at; he had thought it was only more names. It was more than that. On an ordinary week Dace kept one card a night, five or six bouts deep, and a main-floor night when he had something worth lighting the lamps for. Now he put up two cards on most nights and three on the busy ones, and an afternoon card on every day but the chapel day, and a main-floor night twice a week. The rings and dots and stitches of his private grammar went in beside the names as fast as the names did, so that by the time he climbed down the wall looked less like a schedule than like a field somebody had sown in a hurry.

"That's the season," said Dace to nobody, and dusted his hands on his trousers. "Anybody who wants to be on it and isn't, the list's in my head. Anybody who's on it and wants to be off it, the list's shorter."

The floor laughed, and then it went to the wall, all at once, the way pigeons go to a dropped crust.

Cael did not go. His knees would have carried him; they had carried him up the market steps and down again every day since the fourth, and the swelling had gone down to a softness at the sides that only complained on stairs. But there were forty people between him and the wall, and he did not need to stand in front of it to read it. He sat on the end of the east bench with the grey book on his knee and read the wall from where he was, which meant he could read the forty people as well.

His own name was on it three times in the next fortnight. Two were side-floor afternoons, against men he had watched and written down. The third was an evening card, the second slot, against a Copper-formal Shield who had asked for him by name. Dace had said that would happen: *there's men on it who'll want you on their card now that didn't on Tuesday morning*. Cael had not quite believed it, and he found that he believed it now, looking at the white chalk, and that it made him less comfortable than he had expected, not more.

At the top of the wall, in the corner where Dace always put the line, a name stood by itself on the first main-floor night of the season with no opponent chalked against it yet. *Keth.* Under it Dace had drawn a small square and left it empty: the box for whoever Keth would fight, and the whole floor seemed to know without being told that the box would stay empty until Keth himself had said who he wanted in it. Cael looked at the empty square for a while. Then he looked down the floor to the newcomers' ring, where Keth was on the edge of a bench with his practice blade across his knees, not looking at the wall at all, watching a girl's feet.

He had a page on that man, though he had never meant to have one; it had grown anyway, a line or two at a time, through a whole season of dawns, and he did not open it now.

He kept the season's numbers, because a thing that happened twice as often was a thing worth counting.

By the end of the first week he had a page on it. The Assessed purse stayed what it had always been, twelve marks split eight and three and one, but there were twice as many Assessed bouts, and the evening cards paid half again on top for the crowd. The main floor was another matter. A season night on the main floor with two rated names under the lamps could pay out more than a side floor paid in a month, before anybody had counted what changed hands at the betting man's crate.

The district followed the board, a half-step behind it. The chestnut man moved his brazier from the side door to the main door on the nights Dace had marked for lamps, and back again on the nights he had not, and was always there an hour before the doors opened. The pie stall at the top of the row baked a second batch on main-floor nights and sold it cold the next morning at a third off. The fruit woman began to carry a little slate of her own, with the week's cards copied on it in a hand that went downhill.

And Dace was better at the board than the betting man was.

Cael had not meant to find that out. He had only noticed that Dace marked some bouts on his wall with a small hooked tick, the *watch this one* mark, and that the betting man chalked his prices on the crate by the side door each afternoon, and that the two did not always agree. So for a fortnight he wrote them both down, side by side, and then he wrote down who had won. Where Dace's tick and the betting man's price disagreed about which fighter the room should be watching, Dace was right eleven times and the betting man four.

He showed it to nobody, because it was not his business, but he liked it, and he underlined it, and he thought of a man who could keep a district's faces in his head like a room full of benches and seemed to know which of them would get up.

The other thing that doubled was Vell's queue.

He had never thought about it in his first year: that the more people fought, the more people lost, and the more people lost, the more of them came to the keeper's table afterward with a reason. They came in the mornings, mostly, when the room was half empty and they had slept on it. Vell heard every one of them with her pen down and her spectacles on. Cael began, without meaning to, to keep a tally of them in the bottom corner of a page, in the small hand he kept for things that were nobody's business. Money, mostly: somebody had laid coin and lost it and wanted the result to be the reason. Then grudges between gyms, which came to her table dressed as complaints about the result. And once in a while, rarely, a real question about the rules, which Vell answered at length and with what looked very like pleasure.

By the end of the first week the corner said *money 9, gyms 3, rules 1*.

He showed Lira the *Carrying* column on the Tuesday, because he had promised to show her first, and he had already waited four days longer than he liked.

He did it in the kitchen before the household came down, with the lamp between them and the frost still on the window over the basin. She had come in from the dock partner's hour with her hair damp at the temples and her staff on her shoulder. She stood it in the corner and sat down with her bread before she saw that the Log was open on the table at her place, turned round to face her.

She looked at him. Then she looked at the page.

He had turned it to Tuesday's page, the bout night's page, where the narrow column ran down the edge under its heading, with one line in it, very small, in pencil.

*L., through the wall. Didn't.*

Lira read it, and read it again, and did not touch it.

"When?"

"The teaching day. That night." He made himself say all of it, in order, as he would have said it to the book. "I wasn't trying. I was sitting at the desk with my head aching and nothing to aim at, and it opened on its own, a crack. You were on the other side of the wall. About two paces. I could feel that somebody was there." He stopped. "I shut it. As fast as I've ever shut anything."

"What did I feel like?"

He had not expected that question, and he gave her the true answer because he had no other ready.

"Light. Quick. Never still for long." He looked at the table. "Like a bird on a sill that keeps shifting its feet."

Lira was quiet, and then she breathed out through her nose, not quite a laugh.

"That's me, is it."

"That's what it said."

"It's not wrong." She turned the bread over in her hand and did not eat it. "You could have not told me. I'd never have known."

"I'd have known."

"Yes." She looked at the column again, at the one word on the end of the line. "*Didn't.*" She said it as she said the name of a step she was teaching, as if trying it for weight. "That's the right word. You didn't. It's not *couldn't*, and it's not *won't*. It's the thing you did instead." She pushed the book back across the table to him, gently, with two fingers. "Thank you for showing me."

"It might happen again. I don't know how to stop it opening. Only how to shut it."

"Then when it does," said Lira, "tell me the same day. Not four days after." She began on the bread at last. "I'd rather know I've been heard than be kept from it. Being kept from things is what Fenmark did." She chewed, and swallowed, and pointed the end of the loaf at him. "And if you ever find out what I sound like when I'm cross, don't tell me that. I don't want to know."

He laughed, and she did too, and the heavyset man's wife came down the stairs into the middle of it and looked at them both with deep suspicion and put the porridge on.

Afterward, alone at the crate desk with the Log open, he looked at the column for a while. He had ruled it for himself, for the cost of carrying a thing shut. It had not occurred to him until that morning that the things he carried shut might be about other people as well, and that some of them might want to know.

The first thing the new sense taught him, once he began asking it anything at all, was that it did not care how hard he tried.

They worked in the alcove before the sweepers, as they had for the crosses, with the lamp on its hook and the cold coming up out of the stone. Brom stood three paces off with his arms loose, and Cael sat on the floor with his legs crossed and his hands open on his knees, as he had sat on the teaching day, and waited for the door to come open. It came, on the seventh try that first morning, and he felt the big warm weight in front of him. Then Brom took one slow step backward, to four paces, and it went.

"I had you at three," said Cael.

"I know. You leaned." Brom came back to three. "When I stepped away, you went after me. Not your body. The other thing. I felt you reach."

He tried again, and again, for most of that week, and at first it went the same way every time. The door opened at three paces, and Brom stepped back, and something in Cael that had been trained for a year to follow a thing that moved went after him. The moment it went, it was aiming, and the door shut.

He began to understand on the fourth morning, and it was from his knees that he learned it. They had been bad on the stairs, and he had come down to the alcove tired and sore and in no mood to try for anything, and had sat down on the stone as a man sits who does not mean to get up for a while. And the door opened at three paces and stayed open. Brom stepped back to four, and it stayed; to five, and Cael did not go after him, because he was too tired to go after anybody. He only sat there, and let the big man move away across the alcove, and the weight on the front of him went with Brom as the warmth of a fire goes when you walk away from it. It thinned, but it did not stop.

At six paces Brom stopped, with his back nearly to the arch.

"Still?"

"Still," said Cael, and as he said it the door shut. Talking shut it. Everything shut it.

The band came round his head a minute later, hard, and he sat with his palms over his eyes while it settled.

"Six," said Brom, from the arch. He sounded pleased in his flat way, the way he had sounded about the bruise. "Twice what you had on the first day."

"I didn't do anything."

"I know. That's what did it." Brom came and sat on the bench. "You went still. Properly. Not the way you go still on a bench with your book, with your eyes going like a pair of ferrets. Still like a stone in a field."

Cael thought about it with the band round his head, and found it was true, and found that he did not like it. Everything else he owned had grown when he pushed it. The Wind had gone from a hand's width to half a body because he had pushed it every morning for a winter. The giving face had found its ceiling because he had pushed it until it broke. This would not be pushed: when he reached, it got shorter, and when he stopped reaching, it got longer. The more he wanted from it, the less it gave, and the only way to buy distance was with stillness.

He wrote it in the Log that evening, under the day-one entry, and he wrote only what was new.

*Range: bought with stillness, not effort. Reaching shortens it. Six paces once, sitting, tired, not trying. Costs the same band, a little worse.*

Then, because it was the thing he was worst at, he added a line under it. *Every other thing I have, I learned by pushing. This one I'll have to learn by stopping.*

The Copper-formal Shield had asked for him by name, which meant he had been watched, and Cael had been watching back for most of a month.

The man fought out of Orvet's gym in the tannery lanes. He was broad through the chest, a little older than most on the Assessed cards, with a round iron-rimmed shield he carried on the left arm as if it had grown there. He was good, patient behind the rim and hard to get round, and when he had a man where he wanted him, near the rope with nowhere to go, he came off his back foot in a shield-rush that put his whole weight behind the rim and his short club behind that. It had put three men on the stone in the bouts Cael had watched.

And before it, every time, his lead shoulder dropped.

It was very small: a finger's width, the rim of the shield dipping a little with it, as a man's shoulder dips when he sets it against a door he means to shove. Cael had seen it first on a side floor three weeks ago and written it down, with the date, and a question mark after it. He had seen it again eleven days later and written it down again, with that date, and taken the question mark off. Both entries sat on the Shield's page in the grey book, one under the other. Under them was a third line, in ink: *Drop, then rush. Two of two. Wait for three.*

The bout was in the middle of the afternoon, on the side floor by the north wall, and the room was thin, the way the Ironyard was thin at that hour even in the season. There were fifty or sixty on the benches, carters between loads, the newcomers from Keth's ring, a few of Orvet's people at the west post in a knot round Orvet himself. Orvet was already shouting, with a red neckcloth on and a grey beard that wagged when he shouted, and Cael had long since stopped hearing him except as weather.

At the betting man's crate by the side door, a man in a good coat with academy stitching across the shoulders was counting coins into the betting man's palm. Cael knew him; he had last seen him at Vell's table a few months back, with a folded paper in his pocket, being told what the keepers would say about him from here to the sea. The man looked across at Cael once while he paid, and then away.

Cael noticed it, and put it with the faces, and went to his mark.

His knees were good, not new but good; two weeks of the stairs and the alcove crosses had brought them back to something he could trust for a short floor and four exchanges if he did not ask them to do anything clever. He had decided, walking down, what he would ask of them, and it was not much. The Wind on a read, or not at all. The Pressure left where it was; the Shield did not hit hard enough to need it answered, and he did not want it on anybody's page. And the new thing on his skin, he would not open at all. On a floor, against a man who was moving, it would be worth exactly nothing, and it would take the other two down with it when it went.

"Begin," said the junior keeper at the side table, a young man of Vell's who wrote the afternoon cards in a hand very like hers.

The first exchange was a survey, and Cael paid for it. He let the Shield come to him, as the man liked to, and went round the rim to the left and the right to see how fast it followed. It followed fast, and once he was slow getting out, on the left, where the knee was weaker, and the iron edge of the rim came across his forearm with a crack he felt in his teeth. It was nothing that would last, but it was a fact about the man's left side that he had not had from the bench, and he took it and filed it while his arm was still ringing.

The Shield did not rush in the first exchange; he was watching too.

In the second he came forward, and kept coming, and began to walk Cael back toward the rope.

It was well done, and the man did not hurry it. He came forward a half-step at a time with the rim up and his club low behind it, and each half-step took a little more of the floor away, so that Cael had to choose, each time, between giving ground and going round. Going round meant the rim, which was fast. Giving ground meant the rope.

He gave ground. He gave it slowly, on short steps the knees could sign for, and he watched the shoulder.

Two more half-steps, and his back heel touched the chalk of the boundary. Behind it, a hand's width away, was the rope, and the benches behind that, and faces. Somebody on the near bench drew a breath. At the west post Orvet's voice went up a tone, telling his man something about his feet that his man did not need telling.

The Shield's weight settled on his back foot.

Cael did not look at the club, or the rim, or the man's face. He looked at the lead shoulder, at the place where the shirt met the strap of the shield, and he waited, and he did not breathe more than he needed to.

The shoulder dropped. A finger's width, as it had in both entries; the rim dipped with it.

The hip went on the read. It went as it went now when he had paid for it with watching first, cleanly, cheaply, with the half-breath held. The floor went short, front-left, half a body along the chalk and out of the line, and he was in the lock, two *ands* of bright stillness with the world going by in front of him.

The world was the Shield, coming off his back foot with his whole weight behind the rim, into the place where Cael had been.

There was nothing there, and the rim went on into the rope, which took it, and bowed, and two people on the near bench went backward off their seats with a yelp. And the Shield's whole left side was open to Cael where he stood in the lock: the arm out straight behind the rim, the ribs under it, everything a man shows when he has put all of himself into one direction and there was nothing at the end of it.

The lock let go.

Cael put his short straight strike into the ribs under the shield arm. He threw it with nothing from his middle in it, only the hip and the shoulder and the step, and it was enough. The Shield grunted and came off the rope sideways, and went down on one knee on the chalk with his arm wrapped round himself.

"End of the exchange."

The room made its noise. At the west post Orvet was shouting something long and aggrieved about the rope.

Cael went back to his mark and went over himself. The hip line was there, thin, the read price, a pencil line and not a brushstroke. The forearm rang where the rim had caught it, his knees were fine, and his breath came back into its count in four. He had spent one burst and nothing else, and he still had his whole afternoon in him.

Across the floor the Shield got up, slowly, and stood with his hand flat on his side for a while. He looked at Cael, and he was not angry; he looked like a man going back over a sum he had got wrong, trying to find the line where it went bad. Then he looked down at his own left shoulder, as if it belonged to somebody else.

He knew, Cael thought. Not what it was. But he knew that something he had done had been read, and that it was his.

The third exchange was short.

The Shield came forward again, more carefully than before, and he walked Cael back again, but not so far. Twice he set his weight on the back foot for the rush. Both times Cael saw the shoulder begin to go down, and both times the man caught it and held it up by main force. So the rush that came after was a rush with no shoulder in it. It was slow and stiff and short, a man pushing a door with his arms alone because he no longer trusted his back. Cael did not need the hip for that; he stepped off the line on his own feet, two short steps the knees agreed to. The rim went by at the length of an arm, and he put the same strike into the same ribs, a little harder.

The Shield went down on both knees this time, and stayed there a breath, and then lifted his club hand off the floor, open.

"Called," said the junior keeper. "Hand up. Third exchange."

Vell came over from her table while the young man was still blotting, which she did not usually do for an afternoon card, and read the line over his shoulder.

"Write *atypical*," she said.

"It says *atypical*."

"Then write it again under mine." She wrote it herself in the day book she carried on her arm. "*Cael. Win. Third exchange.*" The pen moved. "*Atypical movement pattern.*" She looked at Cael over her spectacles. "Clean," she said, which was all she said, and went back to her table.

The Shield came across the floor to shake hands, with his arm still held in against his side. He had a broad plain face with a white scar through one eyebrow, and he did not let go of Cael's hand straight away.

"What did you see?"

Cael thought about it, because the man had asked a fair question, and he had a right to an answer of some size. But the answer was on a page in a grey book, with two dates on it, and the page belonged to the book.

"Something you'll find yourself," he said, "if you go and look at how you stand before you go in."

The Shield considered that, and then nodded slowly, as Ulric had nodded in the spring.

"I'll look," he said, and went off toward the west post, where Orvet met him with both arms going.

The academy-coat man was no longer at the betting man's crate. Cael looked for him along the benches and did not find him, and noticed that he had looked, and wrote it down that evening in the corner of the Shield's page, under the result: *The man from Vell's table, the Orvet complaint. Laid coin on the Shield. Watched me while he paid.* It was nothing, and he wrote it down only because he wrote everything down.

The purse was twelve marks, eight his. He paid his rent out of it on the way up the hill, a week early, to the heavyset man on the step, who took it without counting and said, "Win?" and nodded at the answer as though it had been a weather report.

At the crate desk he wrote the bout plainly, a line an exchange, as he always did. *First: survey. Rim fast on his left; my left slow; took the rim on the forearm. Second: walked me to the rope. Waited. The drop came. One burst on the read, front-left; lock; ribs. Third: he held the drop up by force, and the rush died without it. Two steps, no burst. Ribs. Hand up.* Under the second exchange he wrote the cost: *one read burst, thin line.* Under the third he wrote the thing he liked best about the afternoon, which was that he had not needed the hip at all.

*He corrected his tell in one exchange,* he wrote. *It cost him his rush. Fixing a tell can cost more than the tell.*

Then, under that, the date, and a small tick beside the Shield's page: three of three.

There was a letter on the desk when he came in. He had seen the hand on it from the door, square and slow, with the tail of the *k* in *Hesk-ward* drawn the long way as it was in every letter he had had since he came over the hills. He opened it last, when the bout was written, as a man saves the best thing on the plate.

*Your letter came on the Thursday, after,* Hesk had written. *I read it at the table and knew from the first line you'd be writing me another, because a lad who expects to win writes about the bread. So I've had two days to make up my mind you lost, and I'm sorry if you didn't, and gladder than I can say about the boy. You found him. I could tell from how you wrote it. My father used to say a man who knows what he's afraid of before and tells you after is worth two who don't. The race has ice at the edges now and the wheel runs slow. Mind the knees. Whatever they are, mind them. H.*

Cael read it twice. He had not told Hesk about his knees; he had told him nothing at all yet about the bout. He sat a while wondering how a man at a kitchen table four days off by post had known to mind them. Then he understood that Hesk had not known anything; he had only known Cael, and guessed what a boy who lost to a man like that would have paid for it.

He put the letter in Hesk's notebook, at the back, with the others, and turned down the lamp.

# Chapter 31 — Two Entries, Dated

Three days after the Shield, he went down early to try the new thing in a room with people in it, because he had run out of ways to put it off.

He had known for a week what he would find. A sense that one big man shifting his weight on a bench could knock flat was not going to do well among a crowd. But knowing a thing and having it in the book were different, and he had promised the book a crowd. So he chose the quietest crowd he could find, which was the Ironyard on a season morning before the first card, when the benches held thirty or so people who had come to sit rather than to shout. Fighters were wrapping their hands, two of Keth's newcomers were arguing about a parry in low voices, somebody's grandfather was asleep with his hat over his face, and Vell was at her table with her tea, going through yesterday's lines.

He took the end of the east bench, put the grey book on his knee, and did not open it. He set his feet flat and let his hands go loose, and looked at nothing in particular, at the middle of the floor where the light from the high windows lay on the stone in long pale bars. Then he did the thing he had learned in the alcove, which was not a thing at all: he stopped asking.

It took a long time to come, and when it came, it came all at once.

It was not thirty people. That was what he had hoped for, somewhere under his sense, though he had told himself not to: thirty small warm weights laid out round him on the benches like coins on a table, each in its place. What came instead was one weight, enormous and shapeless, pressing on the whole front of him at once and the sides and the back. It was the warmth of a full room felt with the skin instead of the face, and there was nobody in it. There was only *people*, all together, the way a river is only *water*, and when he tried, very gently, to find one of them in it, the old man with the hat, who was nearest, there was no old man to find. There was only more of the same.

He did not know what else to do with it, so he held it, and counted his own breath to know how long. Ten. Fifteen. The weight did not change and did not tell him anything. Twenty.

At twenty-two a man's voice went up at Vell's table, sharp and loud, and the whole room turned toward it, and so did Cael, and the door slammed.

The band came round his head before he had even found the man with his eyes. It came harder than it had in the alcove, as if twenty-two breaths of a crowd had cost what an hour of Brom cost. He sat with his thumb and finger pressed into the corners of his eyes and let it settle. The voice at the table went on, louder.

He wrote it down with the band still on, small, in the corner of a page, so that he would not lose it.

*Thirty in a room: one weight, no one in it. Held twenty-two breaths. Got nothing. Loud voice: shut. Band, bad.* And under that, because it was the true size of what he had: *The fragment cannot yet count past one.*

Then he looked up to see what the voice was.

It was the man in the academy coat.

He stood in front of Vell's table at the distance a man stands who wishes to be overheard, which Cael had seen him choose once before. He had a paper in his hand again, and this time it was not folded in a pocket but out, flat, held where people could see it.

"That result was bought," he said. "Tuesday afternoon. The north side floor. The unranked boy and Orvet's Shield."

Vell did not look up for a moment, and when she did, Cael saw her know the man, and saw something go across her face that was not quite weariness, but more like the face of a woman who opens her door to a hawker she sent away last spring and finds him back with a new tray.

"Bought by whom?"

"I don't say anybody paid anybody." The man had practised this too; Cael could hear it. "I say the odds were false. Everybody in this building knows he does something nobody can see. The Arbiter can't follow it. The registry can't put a name to it. He's got no Path on his card." He lifted the paper a little. "How is a man meant to lay an honest coin against a fighter whose whole ability isn't written down anywhere?"

"You laid one."

"I laid three marks on the Shield. Like anybody would."

"Then you lost three marks." Vell put her pen down. "That's a complaint about betting. Betting's always been a gamble against what you don't know. Every fighter on that wall has things he's never shown, and every coin on that crate is laid against them. Tuesday's bout was fought under the terms both men agreed before *begin*, and neither man has complained of it." She looked at the paper. "You weren't one of them."

"This isn't about the bout. It's about the circuit." The man did not lower his voice; he raised it, very slightly, so that it went past Vell to the benches. "I've written it down this time. All of it. I've a house on the river that's guild-registered, and I've people who know people, and there's those who'd want very much to hear that a fighter with no Path is being put on cards in an unsanctioned circuit with nobody over him at all."

The room changed.

It did not go quiet; it went still, the way it had gone still the last time, as people go still at a table when somebody has picked up a knife. Two of Keth's newcomers stopped arguing, and the old man under the hat lifted the brim with one finger. And Cael felt something he did not like, which was every face in the near benches turning, one after another, from the man at the table to him.

Vell said nothing for a moment, and when she spoke it was without heat.

"You stood at this table a few months back with a paper in your pocket," she said, "and I told you then how fast a keeper's word goes down the coast. You've brought the paper out this time. That's braver. It's not wiser." She folded her hands on the book. "If a Compact man walks into my ledger because of you, every table from here to the sea will have heard your name by spring. You'd be the man who did it twice. I'd think hard about whether that's what you want written beside you, when half your purses come from tables like mine."

The man's jaw worked, and he looked round the benches for help, and did not find any, and Cael saw him decide to go on anyway, because he had come too far down the room to go back up it.

"Then show me," he said. "Show me it was straight. Show me what he did."

That was the moment, and Cael heard himself speak before he had properly decided to.

"I can show you."

Every face that had turned to him turned again. He did not stand up; his knees did not want it, and standing would have made it a speech, so he held up the grey book from where he sat.

"I keep the bouts I fight. Every one, the same night. I keep the bouts I watch, too, before I fight them." He kept his voice even; he had heard Vell keep hers even for a year and thought he knew how it was done. "Tuesday's in here. So's the Shield, from before. Argue with the page, if you like. It was written the same night, before anybody had lost anything on it."

---

Vell looked at him for a long moment over her spectacles, and he could not read what was in it.

Then she held out her hand.

He got up, then, because there was no other way to give it to her, and went across the floor with the book. He opened it to the Shield's page before he put it down, and turned it round so that the lines faced her, and stepped back.

She did not read it at once, but opened her own book first, the day book, and turned back through it to Tuesday, and found the north side floor's afternoon card, where the junior keeper had written and she had written under him. Then she laid the two books side by side on the table, square to its edge, his small hand beside her large square one. The man in the academy coat came a step closer to see, and so did half the near bench.

"Read it out," said Vell. "Your page. Slowly."

So he read it, as he had read the Log to Brom's face in the alcove, a line at a time.

"First exchange. Survey. His rim's fast on his left. I took it on the forearm." He had the mark still; he did not show it. "Second. He walked me back to the rope. I waited for the drop. It came. One burst, front-left. His rush went into the rope. One strike, his ribs, under the shield arm. He went to one knee."

Vell's finger went down her own line. *Second exchange. North rope. Shield to one knee.* It stopped, and waited.

"Third. He held the drop up, both times. The rush came without it. Two steps off the line, no burst. Same ribs. Hand up."

*Third exchange. Hand up.* Vell's finger stopped again.

"What's the drop?" she said.

Cael turned back a page, to the two entries above Tuesday's, and put his own finger under them. "His lead shoulder. Before the rush. It goes down a finger's width and the rim goes with it." He moved the finger to the first line. "I saw it the first time three weeks before. That's the date. I wasn't sure." He moved it to the second. "Eleven days after, against the tannery boy. That's the date. Then I was."

Vell looked at the two lines for a long time, not at Cael, or at the man, or at the room. She looked at the dates, and at the ink, and turned the book a little toward the window to see the second date better, and then turned it back.

"This was on your page," she said, to the book, but loud enough that the benches heard, "before Dace ever put the two of you on his wall. Two entries. Dated."

"Yes."

"And if he hadn't done it a third time?"

"Then I'd have been wrong," said Cael. "There's a line through half the pages in it. Most of it's guesses with dates on, until something comes along and tells me whether to keep them."

Somebody at the back made a small sound, almost a laugh, and stopped it.

Vell closed the grey book and held it out to him without looking, and he took it. Then she looked up at the man in the academy coat, and spoke to him and not to the room.

"Two records," she said. "Kept by two people, in two hands, on the same afternoon, neither knowing what the other wrote. One of them three weeks old before the bout began." She laid her palm flat on her own book. "They say the same thing in the same order. The boy read your Shield's shoulder because the Shield showed it to him twice, in front of you, on my floor. There's nothing in that the registry needs to be told about. It's called watching." She picked up her pen. "The bout stands. The claim's answered. You can take your paper wherever you like."

The man stood there with the paper in his hand and looked down at it, as if it had changed while he was not watching, and Cael saw that it had, though not a word on it was different. In front of forty people it had become a page of complaint about three marks.

He folded it, and put it in his pocket, and went out at the side door without looking at anybody, and the cold came in where he had been.

The benches let out their breath, somebody said *well*, and the old man let his hat fall back down over his face.

Vell wrote a line in the day book, small, at the foot of Tuesday. Then she looked at Cael, standing in front of her table with his grey book in both hands, and her face did a thing it did very rarely, which was to look tired in front of people.

"Next time," she said, quietly, so that only he heard, "let me ask for it."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. You were right." She uncapped her ink. "But it's my table. If a book's going to be opened at it, I want to be the one who opened it. Then nobody can say the fighter brought his own witness."

He understood that, and was ashamed for a moment that he had not thought of it, and then decided it was a thing worth having learned. He nodded, and went back to the east bench, and the band round his head, which he had forgotten, came back and reminded him.

---

Dace found him at the end of the morning, at the back of the floor by the door to the locked room where Vell kept her shelves.

Cael was not doing anything there; he had gone because it was the quietest place in the building, and because the band still had one hand round his head, and because there was a stretch of plain wall beside the door that nobody ever leaned on but him. Dace came along the wall from the slate, chalk on his cuffs, and leaned on it beside him, the way he arrived at most things, from the side, so that you saw him before he said anything.

"I've had fighters through that door since before you were born," said Dace, "and not one of them could tell me what happened in his own last bout without a quarrel. You read yours out like a carter reading a bill of lading."

"Vell settled it."

"Vell settles them every day. She settles them out of her own book and her own head, and her head's good enough for most." Dace looked out at the floor, at the cards being chalked for the afternoon. "Today she had two books. That's a different thing. That's harder to argue with than anything she's got on her own."

Cael did not answer, because he could feel that Dace had not come to tell him that.

Dace was quiet for a while, and when he spoke again his voice was the same, flat and easy, but he had dropped it a little.

"You know what the Compact would give for a book like yours?"

"A book about one boy's bouts?"

"A book with dates in it," said Dace. "And names. And what each man does with his left shoulder before he goes in, and how long it took the boy who wrote it to find out. Kept the same way every night. Cross-checked against the keeper's." He did not look at the door. Cael saw him not look at it. "Good thing we don't share."

He said it lightly, like a joke between men leaning on a wall, and it was not a joke.

"You've thought about it," said Cael.

"I think about it every time Vell takes her book home under her arm." Dace rubbed his chalky thumb on his trousers. "Every fighter on that wall is on it because the registry's ladder either threw him off or never had a rung for him. This floor is the one place in the river towns where what a man can do is written down by people who don't answer upstairs. If a book like yours went up the hill, it wouldn't be a record any more. It'd be a list."

He was quiet again, and Cael waited.

"There was a lad here," said Dace, "before you came over the hills. Five, six years back. A Stone, from out past the north gate. I've never seen better feet on a Stone. Guild scout sat on my bench three nights running and then offered him a place. A real one, a house on the river, a station in the spring, his name on a letter." He shrugged. "He went. I'd have gone, at his age. Anybody would."

"And?"

"And the house had his whole history inside a season. Every bout he'd fought on my floor, who he'd beaten and how, what he'd been paid. Not from my book. I never gave it. From people in the crowd, and the betting slates, and a keeper up the river who didn't know any better." Dace looked at his own hands. "The first time he asked them for anything, a better slot, a bigger share, they read him his own past back to him. Every unsanctioned bout, by date. Just so he'd know how it would sound if anybody upstairs ever wanted to hear it."

Cael felt the cold of the wall through his coat.

"He came back," said Dace. "Two years on. Fights for me still, sometimes. Good as ever." He paused. "Careful. He was never careful before. Now he looks at a room before he walks into it, as if he's counting the doors. He won't sit on the end of a bench where anybody can get behind him." He pushed himself off the wall. "So I don't share. Not with the guilds, not with the Compact, not with another keeper unless Vell asks. Not my wall, not my head, not the good ones."

"I won't let it go anywhere."

"I know you won't. I've watched you a year." Dace was already turning back toward the slate. "Only keep taking it down the way you do. And remember whose hands it's for."

"Mine."

"Yours," said Dace, "and the people in it."

He went off down the wall, rubbing chalk off his fingers. Cael stood where he was a while longer with the grey book against his chest.

He had thought of the book, for a year, as the most private thing he owned, after the Log, and he had never once thought of it as dangerous. He thought now of the Shield's page, and the river-academy man's, and Keth's, with all its small notes about a finger off a grip. He thought of the two entries, dated, that had just ended a man's complaint in front of forty people. The same two lines in the wrong hands would tell a stranger exactly how to beat a Shield from Orvet's gym, and exactly when Cael had learned it, and from where. A record did not care whose hands it was in; it said the same thing to everybody.

That evening he went through the grey book from the front, and wherever he had written a full name beside a fighter who was not in Vell's ledger, he turned it into a mark of his own. He did not cross out a single fact; he only made it so that the book would make sense to one reader in the world, and to nobody who picked it up off a table.

---

The letter from the salt end came to Vell that same week, and Cael was at the table when she opened it.

He was there because she had asked him to carry the main-floor book in from the back room before the evening card; she had come to trust his hands with it the way she trusted them with her lamp. He set the red-taped book down beside the day book and stood back. She was slitting a letter with the end of her pen, a keeper's letter, folded in three and sealed with a thumbprint of candle wax, in a hand he had seen once before on the back-room table.

She read it once, quickly, and then again slowly, and then she put it down flat and laid her hand on it and sat very still.

"Fetch me the third shelf," she said. "The second book from the left. The one with the green tape."

He fetched it. She opened it to a page near the back without looking for it, as if her fingers had known the page all along, and ran down it to a line, and stopped.

He read the line upside down. It was a bout of two years ago, on the main floor, on a season night: a Shield, an Iron formal from a house up the river, against a man Cael knew. Everybody in the district knew him: a Shield too, broad and quiet, from the brickworks end, about thirty, who taught Shield work to newcomers in a hired yard for a copper a lesson and was on Dace's wall most weeks in good slots. The line said he had beaten the Iron formal in the second exchange. Under the result, in Vell's hand: *Iron formal guarded left throughout.*

"I wrote that," said Vell. "I wrote *guarded left* and I never asked why."

She turned the letter round and pushed it toward him. It was from the salt-end keeper, the one who had written to ask what Vell meant by *pressed*. It was short. The Iron Shield from up the river had been fighting at the salt end this season, and losing, and after a bad loss he had sat late in the keeper's kitchen and told her a thing he had carried for two years. On the night he fought at Ardenmere he had had two fingers of his left hand cracked under the wrap, from a fall on a dock the week before. He had told nobody, because he needed the purse, and he had held the shield all night with three fingers and kept his left side shut so nobody would find out.

*He asked me to tell you,* the keeper had written. *I think he wanted it off him. I've checked my own lines for his name and found nothing hanging. You'll want to check yours.*

Vell took her hand off the letter.

"He wasn't beaten," she said. "He was beaten one-handed. And I didn't see it." She got up and went into the back room, and he followed her with the lamp. The pine board hung where it always hung, beside the door, with its column of white-painted names. She found the Iron formal's name and touched it, and then went back to the green-taped book and put her finger on the line under it. "The brickworks Shield went in at Iron-equivalent on that win. One link, from a nail. A good link, I thought." She turned pages, and turned them, and her finger went down the years. "And four lines hang off him since."

Cael counted them with her. Four fighters had each been rated Iron-equivalent in the last two years, wholly or partly, by beating the brickworks Shield on this floor. A Blade. A Stone woman from the north gate. A young Wind not long out of Keth's ring. A Force man who had moved up to the evening cards on it.

"What happens to them?"

"The same as happens to him." Vell was already uncapping the red ink. "He goes back to where the book would have put him if I'd seen his opponent's hand. That's Copper-equivalent, high range. And everyone who went up by beating him goes back to what they'd have been without it."

At the north end of the floor somebody laughed, and the sound came down the room very bright and far away.

Lira came in at the side door when they were back at the table, with her staff, and saw the green book open, and saw Vell's face, and came across at once. She looked at the letter and then at the red ink. Cael watched her go a little pale.

"Is Wendel—"

"Wendel hangs off a different nail," said Vell, without looking up. "So do you. So does the boy." She ruled her first red line under the brickworks Shield's name, very straight. "Go and warm up."

Lira let out her breath, and was ashamed of it, Cael could see, and went.

---

Vell told them all on the same night. She would not let it go a second.

She did not do it at her table, and she did not do it from her book. She closed both books at the end of the evening card and put them under her arm. Then she went out across the floor to find each of them where they were, as she had told Cael in his first week she always did: standing in front of them.

He watched, because she had not told him not to, from the east bench, with the grey book shut on his knee.

She went to the brickworks Shield first. He was at the water barrel with two of his newcomers, a towel round his neck, laughing at something. When he saw her coming with the books under her arm he stopped laughing, though he could not have known why. She stood in front of him and said it, and Cael could not hear the words from the bench, but he could see the man's face, which went still, and then red, and then still again. He saw him look at the floor for a long time. Then he said something short, and Vell said something shorter, and the man nodded twice and turned back to the barrel. His newcomers looked at each other.

The Force man took it worst. He had a slot on Thursday's evening card on the strength of the line, and he knew before she had finished that he would not have it now, and he said so, loudly, so that half the room heard. *Two years.* *Two years and you've only now seen it.* Vell stood in front of him while he said it all and did not move or answer until he had stopped. Then she said one thing, and he turned away from her, and kicked the leg of the bench, and sat down on it with his head in his hands.

The young Wind cried, though she did not make a sound about it. She stood with her arms folded and her chin up while the tears ran, and nodded at everything Vell said, and when Vell had gone she went to the far wall and stood with her face to it until it stopped. Two of the girls from the wall came and stood near her without speaking.

The Stone woman from the north gate was not there. Vell asked Dace where she lodged, and wrote it on a slip, and put it in the book for the morning.

The brickworks Shield came to the east bench afterward, while Vell was writing at her table, and sat down at the other end of it from Cael with the towel still round his neck. He did not seem to want anything. He only sat.

"She said it was her fault," he said, after a while. Cael did not think he was talking to him particularly. "She said *I wrote it down and didn't ask, and that's a keeper's fault and not yours.* Stood there and said it in front of my lads." He wiped his face with the towel. "I've been charging them a copper a lesson on that line. *Iron-equivalent.* I've had it painted on the board at the yard."

"Will you take it down?"

"Tonight." The man looked at his own big hands. "And then I'll have to fight my way back up to it, if I can. Which I might not." He got up. "Two years I thought I'd done a thing I hadn't. I don't know which is worse. Being told, or not having been."

He went out. Cael watched Vell at her table. She had the red ink out and the main-floor book open, and she was ruling the lines one after another down the page, and beside every one, in her square hand, two small letters: *t.f.*

He had seen nine of those lines in an old book in his first week, and thought them the cleverest thing he had ever seen in a ledger. He had done the sum then, admiring it: a dozen corrections in thirty years, each one said aloud to the face it cost. He had not watched one happen, or seen a young woman crying at a wall, or a man kick a bench, or the keeper standing still in front of him while he said it. He had not known a record's honesty would have a sound, and that the sound would be somebody's voice going up.

When the last line was ruled he went across.

"Was it worth it?" he said. He did not quite know why he asked. "For a link two years old."

Vell blotted the page and did not look up.

"The four of them climbed two years on a step that wasn't there," she said. "Every bout they fought since, somebody was paid wrong, and somebody laid coin on a line that was a lie, and somebody else got told they were behind a person who was really level with them. That's two years of small wrongs, and I made all of them, with one word I didn't ask about." She closed the book. "Tonight four people hate me. Tomorrow the book's true again." She put the pen down, square to the edge. "The Registry's a lie about who people are. Mine has to be the other thing. It can't be the other thing if I'll only fix it when it's cheap."

She stood, and took both books under her arm, and stopped beside him.

"Go home, Cael," she said. "And next time you think a record of yours is only your own business, remember the girl at the wall."

# Chapter 32 — A Trade

Lira cooked most nights, plainly, in her one iron pan, for whoever was at the table. But perhaps four times a year she cooked properly, for company, and every time she told the whole house about it two days in advance, as a town crier tells a street about a hanging.

"Thursday," she said, at breakfast on the Tuesday, to the table at large. "I'm cooking Thursday. Upstairs. For three."

The heavyset man's wife looked at her over the porridge pot with the expression of a woman who has seen Lira cook before.

"Which three?"

"Me. Him." Lira pointed her spoon at Cael. "And Brom."

There was a small silence, and the sister put down her cup, and the heavyset man, who had been reading a carter's bill held at arm's length, lowered it an inch.

"The big one," said the sister. "The one who did the legs."

"The one who did the legs," Lira agreed. She went on eating. "He's coming to supper. I've asked him. I asked him yesterday at the corner and he said yes as if I'd asked him the time, and I'm cooking, and that's all."

Cael, who had not known any of this, looked at her across the table, and she did not look back; she had the face she wore on a mark before *begin*, the one that had decided a thing and did not wish to discuss it.

He asked her about it that evening on the landing, quietly, so that the sister would not hear through the floor.

"You asked him."

"I asked him." Lira was mending a strap and did not look up. "He's eaten yesterday's bread on that wall every day for a month, and he's paid a week ahead at the carters' inn, and he's never once in your hearing said anything about anybody feeding him." She bit off the thread. "And you sit with him every morning and come home with your arms blue and you've stopped telling me what he says unless I ask. So I'm going to sit across a table from him and find out for myself."

"You said you'd find out what he was on your own time."

"Thursday's my time." She held the strap up to the lamp and looked along the stitches. "And I'm cooking. If he's rude about it, that'll tell me something too."

On Thursday the smoke reached the attic before Cael was halfway up the hill.

He could smell it from the corner of the row: onions, and something under the onions that had gone past brown and was arguing about black. When he came in at the front door the hall was blue with it, and the sister was standing at the foot of the stairs with a dish-cloth over her nose and mouth, flapping the street door to and fro to let it out.

"She's burning the house down," said the sister, through the cloth. "Your girl. She's burning it down a pan at a time."

"She's not my—"

"She's burning it down," said the sister, and flapped the door.

In the kitchen the heavyset man's wife was standing back from the range with her arms folded, as a farmer watches a neighbour plough a field crooked and has decided not to say anything until the furrow is finished. Lira was at the range with her single iron pan, the one she would not let anybody else in the house touch, and her sleeves pushed to the elbow, and her face red from the heat and from something else. The pan held barley and mutton and a great many onions, some of them very dark.

"It's meant to be like that," said Lira, before he could say anything.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to." She stirred. "The dark ones are for taste. Everybody at the barn did them like that. Go and lay the table."

"What table?"

"The one on the landing." She pointed upward with the spoon. "I've carried it into my room. Don't let the sister see you."

So he laid the little table from the landing, in Lira's room, with the two plates and the two bowls the house would lend and a third plate of his own that did not match. He put the three cups where they would go and moved the lamp twice, and found he was more nervous about it than he had been about the Shield, and could not have said why.

Brom came at the hour exactly, with frost on his coat and a jar under his arm.

He stood in the doorway of Lira's room and looked at it: the narrow bed, the little table with its three odd plates, the staff in the corner and the spare one behind it, the window with the alcove lamp a small far light below. He looked at all of it the way he had looked at Vell's book on his first day, from the front, as if he meant to read it to the end.

Then he held out the jar.

---

It was a big stone jar with a wooden lid tied down with string, and something moved in it, slowly, when he set it on the table.

"What is it?" said Lira.

"Pickled," said Brom.

"Pickled what?"

Brom considered the jar as though he had not, himself, entirely settled the question. "A trade," he said.

"A trade for what?"

"For a thing I did for a carter." He untied the string. "He said they were the best he'd ever had. He's from the north. I've found they mean something different by *best* up there."

Lira took the lid off and looked into the jar for a long time.

"Some of those are eggs," she said.

"Some of them," Brom agreed.

"What are the rest?"

"I was hoping you'd know."

She did not know, but she took one out with a fork and turned it in the lamplight, and then another, and set them on the edge of a plate in two small rows according to some private judgement of her own. Then she went down for the pan.

It was a good supper; Cael would think so afterward, and he thought so at the time, which was rarer. The barley was soft and the mutton was not, quite, and the dark onions tasted, as Lira had promised, of something, though it was not clear what. Brom ate two bowls of it without a word and then a third with several, all of them in praise. He said it was the first hot meal he had eaten sitting at a table since he left the river city, and he said it plainly, as a fact, without asking anybody to be sorry. Lira went pink and said that it was mostly onions. They balanced the bowls on their knees because the little table would not take the bowls and the pan and the jar at once, and the jar would not leave.

After the second bowl Brom sat back on the trunk Lira had given him to sit on, with his cup in his hands, and looked round the room again.

"You've made a home of it," he said. "The two of you. Here and through there. An actual one."

"It's a room and a half and a table off the landing," said Lira. "Don't go soft on it."

"I'm not going soft. I'm saying what's here." He lifted his cup a little toward the staff in the corner, the spare one behind it, the measuring stick that lived by the stairs, the grey books on Cael's shelf through the open door. "Two years on the road. Three cities. Inns, mostly, and a boat shed for most of one winter, and one bad month on a loft floor over a stable. I've never had a room that looked like somebody lived in it." He drank. "I don't know what that says about me. I haven't worked it out yet."

"It says you keep moving," said Cael.

"Maybe." Brom looked into his cup. "Or nowhere ever made me want to leave a coat on a chair."

He said it the way he said everything, flat, as if it were the price of bread. But it sat in the room for a moment afterward, and Cael saw Lira see it. She did not say anything, but got up and took the jug off the floor and filled Brom's cup, which was not empty, and sat down again. Brom looked at the cup and then at her, and nodded once, and that was all.

They talked about nothing for a while after that, which was the best part. Brom told them about the carter and the thing he had done for him, which turned out to be lifting the back of a loaded cart out of a ditch on the north road on his own while the carter held the horse. Lira told them about the sister's hen, which had laid an egg on the coal-box lid that morning in full view of the kitchen, as if to make a point. Cael told them about Dace's tick and the betting man's prices, eleven to four, and Brom laughed his short laugh through his nose, and Lira wanted to know why Cael had never told her, and he said he had only just finished counting. Through the floor they could hear the sister telling somebody about the smoke.

It was in the lull after that, while Lira was sorting the jar a second time, that Cael tried it.

---

He had not planned to, and he would think about that afterward, and be honest with himself about it, and not like what he found.

But the room was very quiet. He was sitting still, with a full stomach and his cup in his hands and his back against the wall, and there was nothing on earth he wanted to aim at. Across the little table, an arm and a half away, sat the one strong signature he had spent a fortnight learning. It was the exact opposite of the morning in the Ironyard: one man instead of thirty, a quiet room instead of a crowd, sitting instead of standing, and nothing riding on it. He thought, *this is the other end of the scale*, and that was the last thing he thought before the door came open.

It came open easily, which surprised him. He had sat for half an hour on the alcove floor some mornings to get it, and here it simply opened, the way a door opens that was not quite latched, and Brom was there, a great warm weight on the front of him across the table.

And Brom was not resting.

Cael had expected him to be. Everything his eyes told him said Brom was resting: the loose shoulders, the cup held in both hands, his legs out under the table, a big man at his ease after three bowls of barley. On the alcove floor, with Brom standing still to be found, the weight had been loud and plain, a man holding his read open on purpose. This was not that; this was quieter. But under the ease, under the cup and the loose shoulders, Brom's read was still going.

It was low, so low that Cael could not have said how he knew it was there, except that he had sat a bench's length from it for twelve days and felt it on his own skin like a hand. It was turned down the way a fire is turned down at night, raked over with ash so that it will last till morning, giving out almost nothing, never once quite out. It lay on the room. It lay on Lira, sorting the jar. It lay, very lightly, on him.

It never stopped, Cael understood. Not at supper, not with a cup in his hands, not in a room with two people he had come to eat with. Brom had told him once, on the floor, that he did not know where to be hard without it. He had not understood until now that it had been like this every hour of every day for two years. Brom could not put it down any more than a man can put down his own hearing.

He held it. Six breaths. Seven. It was the longest he had ever held anything, and it cost him nothing yet, and he knew the bill was coming.

At eight Brom lifted his eyes from his cup and looked straight at him across the table.

"You're doing it," he said. "Now."

Cael let it go. The door shut. "Yes."

"I can feel it." Brom did not sound angry. He sounded the way he had sounded about the bruise, interested, and a little pleased, and something else. "Not much. Like somebody standing too close behind me in a queue." He drank. "Your range is terrible."

"I know. An arm and a half. Sitting. In a quiet room." The band was arriving; he felt it gather behind his eyes and closed them. "That's all of it there is."

Lira had stopped sorting the jar. She looked from one of them to the other, the fork with something pickled on the end of it held up in the air.

"What," she said, "are the two of you doing at my dinner table that I can't see?"

"Yes," said Brom and Cael together.

She looked at them both a moment longer, and then she took the thing off the end of her fork and threw it at Brom, and took another out of the jar and threw that at Cael, and went back to sorting.

The band was bad, and he sat through the worst of it with his eyes shut and his palms over them. When he opened them again Brom was watching him, and had been for some time.

"It doesn't go out," said Cael. "Yours. Even now. Even here."

Brom turned his cup on his knee.

"No," he said, after a while. "Not since the boat shed. Before that I could shut it, if I went somewhere quiet and tried. "I don't notice it. It's like breathing. I only notice it when it finds something." He looked at Cael. "Nobody's ever known it was there. Two years of rooms full of people, and not one of them could tell. You're the first."

"I should have asked."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry. I didn't mean to. That's not an answer, I know." Cael made himself say the rest. "It's a thing I'll be able to do to people. Without them knowing. I hadn't thought about that until just now."

Brom was quiet for a while.

"Ask, then," he said. "Next time. I'll mostly say yes." The corner of his mouth went in. "It turns out I don't mind. I thought I would." He drank. "Being noticed. It's company, a bit."

"She's very good," he said.

"She's the best there is."

"I know that now." Brom looked at his own boots. "My feet are terrible."

"They're terrible," Cael agreed, "and so were mine," and Brom looked at him sideways, and the corner of his mouth went in, and they sat a while longer in the cold and watched Lira begin with the dock partner as if the last hour had been nothing at all.

# Chapter 33 — Section Twelve

The second thing the new sense would do, once he stopped asking it for the first, was walk.

He found it out on the market row, on a grey morning in the second week of the season, with Brom beside him and a loaf under each of their arms. They had come up from the baker's door by the long way, slowly, because Brom had said that if Cael was going to try it on his feet he had better try it somewhere he could not fall down a stair. Cael had been trying it for a week, in the alcove and on the walk home and once, foolishly, on the boarding-house stairs, and every time it had gone the same way: he would open it standing still, and take one step, and it would go out like a candle carried too fast across a room.

On the market row it went out three times in the first twenty paces.

"You're asking it too much," said Brom.

"I'm not asking it anything. I'm walking."

"You're asking it *who*." Brom shifted his loaf to the other arm. "Every time somebody comes near us, you want to know who it is and where they're going and what they've got in their basket. I can feel you want it. And it can't tell you that while you're moving. Mine couldn't for a year." He nodded down the row at a woman coming up it with a basket of turnips. "Ask it something smaller."

"Like what?"

"Like *is there anybody*."

So Cael tried that. He let the door come open, standing still, by the water trough at the corner of the row, and then he walked on, at a slow even pace, the pace he would have used to carry a full bucket, and he asked it nothing at all except that one thing. *Is there anybody.*

The woman with the turnips came past on his left, close, a pace off.

*Yes.*

That was all it gave him: no shape and no weight, nothing he could have told apart from anybody else. There was only a small bright *yes* on the left side of his skin as she went by, as clear as a knock on a door, and then, as she went on behind him, the *yes* went on too, thinning, and was gone.

He kept walking. A man with a barrow: *yes*. Nothing for five paces: *no*. Two boys running, very fast, past them both: *yes, yes*, there and gone so quickly that he only knew they had been there after they had passed. A dog: nothing at all, which surprised him, and which he wrote down later with a question mark.

He got almost to the top of the row before a cart came out of a side lane with its iron tyres banging on the cobbles, and he looked at it, and the door slammed.

"Thirty paces," said Brom. "That's a long way for the second week."

"It only said yes and no."

"It only said yes and no," Brom agreed. "That's what it says when you walk. The rest you have to stand still for." He turned the loaf over and looked at the crust. "It'll say more, later. But you've got to let it say the small thing well first, or it'll never trust you with the big one."

On the way down again he found the other thing it would do, and it was Brom who found it for him.

They had stopped at the corner where the row met the square, to let a carter's dray go by. Cael was standing still, and the door was open, and there were three people near them waiting for the dray too: a girl with a basket of eggs, an old man with a stick, and a young man in a blacksmith's apron with his sleeves rolled up.

"Two of those are kindled," said Brom, without looking at any of them. "One isn't. Which?"

Cael stood very still.

He had felt the girl with the eggs and the old man and the smith all at once, as three small weights, and he had not thought to ask them anything. Now he did, gently, one at a time. The old man was there, warm, a weight on the air and nothing else; a person, plainly, but only one thing. The girl was there too, and then, as he attended to her, there was something more under her, inside the first weight, like a second coin under the first in a closed hand. The smith was the same as the girl: a weight with something in it.

"The old man isn't," said Cael. "The other two are."

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"I know what Path they are and about how far up it," said Brom. "Stone, the girl, low Copper. The smith's a Force, not much past his Kindling. Yours won't tell you that. Mine wouldn't either, at the start. But it'll tell you whether there's anything in there at all."

The dray went by, and the three of them crossed, and Cael felt them go, kindled, unkindled, kindled, and then the band came round his head and he stopped feeling anything.

That week, in the alcove, he found the third thing, which was smaller still. When the girl with the sacking in her hair from Keth's ring came in to use the post, and began to warm up, the something inside her weight changed. It was not louder, exactly; it was awake, the way a dog lying down is different from a dog that has heard a step, though both are dogs and neither has moved. When she stopped and sat down to rewind her hands, it went down again.

*Kindled or not,* he wrote, that night, *close, standing still. Working or resting, close, standing still. Walking: yes or no only, thirty paces once. Nothing else. That's the whole of the vocabulary.*

Assessor Havel drew the Ardenmere file for its quarter's review on a Monday, from the middle of a stack of four, and this time he had been waiting for it.

He had not let himself know that he was waiting, because he was not a man who looked forward to files, but he had kept the date of its turn in the back of his notebook, the one with the cardboard cover, in pencil, small, among other dates that meant nothing. He had watched the rota come round toward it all season, a desk at a time, the way a man watches weather come up a valley. When the supervisor read out the morning's stacks along the long table and put a thin grey folder from the river side on top of a dyer's renewal, Havel felt something in his chest that he did not care to name.

He did the dyer's renewal first, because it was on top, and closed it.

The quarter's review was a newer thing than the visit. It had come down that spring with a printed sheet, half a page, which the registry wanted clipped to the inside of every passive monitoring file at its turn. The sheet asked an assessor seven questions, in boxes, and wanted a tick or a cross in each, and initials at the foot. *Residence confirmed. Contact log current. Guild affiliation unchanged.* He had filled in perhaps two hundred of them, and could have filled them in asleep.

He filled in the first five in a quarter of an hour. Residence: the keeper's book, his own visit, nothing since to say otherwise. Contact log: his own entry, and Coss's few, and nothing new. No guild. No incident. No travel outside the district on any record that had come up to the floor.

The sixth box said *Handling markers confirmed against manual*.

He had ticked that box two hundred times without opening the manual, as everybody did. A handling marker was a line in the classification block, entered by somebody with a right to enter it, signed and dated in the right-hand column. To confirm it against the manual meant to read the line and see that it was the kind of line the manual allowed, and every line on every file he had ever drawn had been the kind of line the manual allowed. Every one had initials beside it.

He looked at the classification block on the first leaf, at its three lines. The registry's, with its stamp. His own, the green slip, with *H.* and a date some weeks gone. And between them the line with nobody's name, in the short printed form the registry used, a designation and a figure, and an empty column at its end.

He could tick the box, and nobody would ever check it. He had stood in this room every Tuesday for four years and been told, in the supervisor's flat voice, that the sheets were for the registry's comfort and not for anybody's reading.

Or he could do what the box said.

He sat there for some time with the pencil over it. It occurred to him, distantly, that this was the first time since the yellow room the thing had been offered to him with a reason attached. Only a box on a printed sheet that told him, in the registry's own words, to go and look.

*Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4.*

A Priority Level 4 designation, said the manual, was reserved for practitioners of Gold tier or above under active security review. A clerk's carry from another file, perhaps, or an old marker for a class that had since been folded into another, or a mistake in the short form that meant something dull. This was not dull. This was two things on one leaf that could not both be true by the manual's own words. A Level 4 needed a Gold practitioner and a signature, and the file had neither. And it sat there in its proper place, in its proper form, as quietly as the registry's own stamp.

He thought of the four old files in the archive, the only others of that class there had ever been, which his training year had read. Every one of them had carried markers, and every marker had been signed, and dated, and explained, in a hand anybody could find in the duty records. Not one of them had carried this.

He wrote the query twice.

The first draft came out of him fast, in a quarter of an hour, and he read it back and was frightened by it.

*Contact of record, Ardenmere file. Every word in it was true. He could have defended each sentence to the supervisor line by line. The second took him most of the afternoon, though it was shorter.

*Contact of record, Ardenmere file (Hesk-ward, C.), river side. Quarterly review. A Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4 designation appears in the classification block. I am unable to confirm it against Section 12 of the manual for the review record. Requesting guidance on its origin and continued application. H.*

He read it back four times. It asked for nothing a contact of record could not give with one line. It did not say *wrong*, and it did not ask *who*. It said only that a box on a printed sheet could not be ticked, which was true, and was the registry's own problem and not his. If anybody two floors up ever wanted to know why a junior assessor had asked about a marker above his grade, the answer was clipped to the inside of the file. The sheet had told him to.

He knew, sitting there with it, that it was still a question with his initials on it, going up. He had decided against this once, in the yellow room, on a careful man's reasons. He was not sure the reasons had changed. What had changed was that he had now read Section Twelve, and he could not unread it. A correct man, he had thought then, would raise a line with no name on it, and a careful man would wait until he knew what he was raising. He knew now.

He put the query in the inner-post tray at the end of the room for the afternoon round, with the review sheet unticked in its sixth box and clipped inside the closed file beside him, and went back to a carter's licence.

---

The answer came before the lamps were lit.

It came down in the last round of the afternoon, in an inner-post envelope with his name on the front in a careful upright hand, every stroke of it the same weight. He knew the hand from the contact log, from a year of very short entries. He opened the envelope at his desk while the man by the door hummed, and unfolded the slip inside, and read it.

*Follow standard procedure. Do not attempt to reclassify the monitoring level. File regular reports.*

That was all: no greeting, and no signature but an initial. There was no word in it about origin, or Section Twelve, or the empty column, or anything he had asked. He had not asked to reclassify anything; he did not have the grade to try. But the slip told him not to, in the flat closed voice of a door shut quietly and firmly by somebody who did not mean to open it again.

He read it twice, and noticed, as a careful man notices such things, that it had come back the same afternoon. Queries to a contact of record waited a week at least, and a fortnight in the season. This one had been answered within three hours of leaving his desk. He did not know whether that meant the question mattered more than he had thought, or that somebody wanted it finished before it could start to matter. He did not know which of those he would rather it meant.

He did what the slip said. He put it inside the file, behind the review sheet, as procedure required for any instruction on a monitoring file. In the sixth box, instead of a tick or a cross, he wrote in his smallest hand, *Queried; instructed per contact of record*, and his initials. That was true, and it was all the box would hold. He closed the file and set it on the pile for the morning's return.

He sat in the emptying room a long time after that, until the hummer had gone and the lamp on his desk was the only one lit.

He had learned, in four years, how to tell a question from a challenge. He knew the exact place where a junior officer's curiosity stopped being diligence and became the other thing. He knew that the slip in the file was that place, written down. He would not send another query, or take the volumes down again. He would do as the slip said: standard procedure, regular reports, the level left exactly where somebody had put it. He knew, too, that he would do it well, because he did everything well, and nobody reading his returns for the next ten years would ever find a line in them that the slip had not allowed.

But the slip had told him what to do with the file. It had said nothing at all about what he was to know.

He took the notebook with the cardboard cover out of his inside pocket and opened it to the page where the earlier slip was still folded, the one that said *file thin for class*. Under his note from the yellow room, *Not raised. Kept*, he wrote the date, and then, slowly, the way a form would have wanted it if there had ever been a form for this:

*Quarterly review. The line is* Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4. *Manual, Section 12: Gold tier and above, under active security review. Officer's signature, date and review date required. None here. Subject: no tier, no incident. Queried contact of record in the plainest words I had. Answered the same afternoon:* Follow standard procedure. Do not attempt to reclassify the monitoring level. File regular reports. *Complied.*

He read it back. It was true and it said no more than he knew.

Then, under it, smaller, because it was not a fact and the rest of the page was:

*Who enters a Level 4 and doesn't sign it? And who answers a question about it in three hours without answering it?*

He looked at the two questions for a while. They were the only thing in the whole matter that belonged to nobody but him. They were not in the file, and the slip could not reach them, because they had never been asked anywhere a slip could follow. They would stay in a cardboard cover in his inside pocket, where they would ask nobody anything, and do no harm, and not go away.

He thought, for no reason, of the boy on the pump step holding out a pear in the cold.

He put the notebook away. He took the first draft out of his other pocket, the frightening one, and looked at it once more, and put it back to burn. Then he blew out the lamp, and went home through the Ranked streets, which were wide and named and very quiet, and did not look back at the river.

In his room he knelt at the grate before he lit the lamp, and put the first draft on the cold coals, and set a match to the corner of it.

It burned quickly, the way thin office paper does. He watched the edge go brown and then bright along the lines of his own level hand. He read them going, without meaning to: *cannot be correct*, and *under the manual as written*, and, last, curling up black at the very end of the sheet, *who*. Then that was ash too, and the grate was dark again, and there was nothing in the room but a smell of burnt paper and a young man on his knees in his coat.

He had done the right thing; he was nearly sure of it. The plain query had asked what a review sheet allowed, and been answered, and he had obeyed the answer, and the file was correct. A careful man and a correct one had both been satisfied, and it had taken him only a season and a burnt page to find out how.

He got up, and lit the lamp, and made his supper, and did not take the notebook out of his coat again that night. He could feel it there all evening against his ribs, small and flat, with its two questions in it.

# Chapter 34 — Presence

The Stone from up the river was the widest person Cael had ever seen fight a Wind, and he stood on the south mark under the lamps as if the floor had been laid round him.

He was not tall, perhaps a hand shorter than Lira, and twice as broad, with forearms like the stone they named his Path for. When he set his feet he set them the way a gatepost is set, a little apart and a little turned out, so that you could see the weight go down through them into the floor. He was Iron-equivalent in Vell's book, and had been for three seasons, and Dace had chosen him for exactly that. Cael understood it as soon as he saw the man stand: a provisional rating was a claim, and a confirming bout was the claim tested against somebody who would not give it to you. Nobody on Dace's wall gave anything to anybody less than this man did.

It was the main floor, the Thursday evening card, the third bout. The lamps were lit but not all of them, sixteen or so, a season Thursday and not a big night; there were perhaps two hundred on the benches. The four girls from the wall were in a row by the water barrel, with the dock partner behind them. Brom was at the rope beside Cael at the north post, with his coat on and his arms folded on the top strand, and he had said nothing since they came in.

Cael had decided, walking down, not to open the new thing at all. It was not much of a decision; in a room of two hundred it would have been a flood with nobody in it, and a band round his head for the rest of the night, and he would have learned nothing. But there was more to the deciding than that. It was Lira's night, and he did not want to be anything at the rope but a person standing at it.

His eyes did not agree. As soon as Vell said *begin* they went down into the Stone's feet and began counting, and he found he could no more stop them than stop his breath. So he let them, and told himself he would not write any of it down.

The first exchange was Lira finding out what the man was, and the man letting her.

She went round him. She went round him at the speed she used for nothing, for walking to the pump, and he turned to face her the whole way, not fast, never fast, a gatepost turning on its hinge. When she came in she came in light, testing, a staff-end at the shoulder and away. The Stone did not block it, but let it land on the meat of his upper arm and did not move, and she was gone again before he could do anything about it, though he did not seem to want to.

"He's letting her look," said Brom, very quietly.

"Why?"

"So he can look back." Brom did not take his eyes off the floor. "He wants to feel her hands. Watch his forearm. The left."

Cael watched it, and in the second exchange the Stone began to send things at Lira's hands.

They were not hard strikes but short pushes off the forearm, flat, at the height where a Wind would catch them on the staff and turn them and send them on. That was what Lira did, every time: caught, turned, sent, with the step that did not stop riding through it. It was beautiful to watch from the rope. The Stone's pushes went round her like water round a post, and twice her staff came back off the catch into his ribs before he had his forearm home. The benches liked it, and a sound went along them each time, the sound of people seeing a thing they had come to see.

But the Stone did not mind the ribs. He took the staff there without moving and sent another push, and another, at the same height, at the same hands. Cael, who had been reading feet for a year, saw the man's weight go a little further forward each time, a finger's width at a time. He was not trying to land them. He was timing the catch.

"There," said Brom.

On the fifth push the Stone did not push. He let his forearm go into Lira's staff as if to be caught, and as her hands closed on the catch, he set his weight. It was the place Brom had told her about on the walk up the hill, the place in the middle where her hands had to turn the catch into a send. For less than half a beat the staff was held and not yet sent, and the Stone leaned into it with everything that made him a Stone.

The staff went back into her own shoulder, and Cael heard it from the rope. Lira went two steps back across the stone, off her line, and the step that did not stop stopped.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

Lira came to the north post for the reset and did not look at either of them. She stood with her back to the rope and her left hand flat against the shoulder where her own staff had gone into it, and breathed. Cael could see the place already going dark under the cloth of her shirt, and he could see something else too, in the set of her back, which he had not seen there since the night of Dravin: she was frightened.

"He found it," she said, to the floor in front of her.

"He found it," said Brom.

"He's going to do it again."

"Every time you catch," said Brom. "As long as you're catching."

Lira was quiet. In the middle of the floor the Stone stood on his mark with his weight down through his heels, not even breathing hard, looking at nothing, as a gatepost looks at a field.

"I can't fix my hands in a reset," said Lira.

"No," said Brom.

She turned her head and looked at him then, a quick hard look over her shoulder, and Cael thought for a moment she was going to say something sharp. She did not; something went across her face, very fast, the look of a person who has walked into a room she knows and found the furniture moved. Then she laughed, one short breath through her nose, not the real one, but nearer to it than anything Cael had expected.

"Then I won't catch," she said.

She went back out to her mark before either of them could answer, and rolled the bruised shoulder once, and set her feet.

Cael did not understand what she meant until the third exchange was half over, and then he understood all of it at once.

She did not catch anything. The Stone sent his pushes at her hands, at the height and the time he had found, and her hands were not there. She was not there. She was round his left side, and the push went into air, and the Stone turned, a gatepost on a hinge. When he turned she had moved the angle again without moving a step, turning on her heel, the way she had taught Brom on a chalk line at first light. She was in front of him and outside his forearm, where a push could not reach her without his whole body following it.

It was the feet. She had stopped fighting him with her hands, where she was Copper, and was fighting him entirely with her feet, where Brom had felt her past it on the first morning. She would not stand where a catch was needed; she made the Stone come to her, and every time he came, he came a little off his line, a little turned, his weight a finger's width the wrong way. And when it was the wrong way she sent the staff into it off the step that did not stop, without ever having caught a thing.

It was slower than the other way. It cost her more ground and more breath, and twice the Stone nearly had her against the rope. But he could not find her hands, because she was not using them, and Cael watched the man begin to understand that. He watched him stop pushing, and stand, and wait for her to come in. She would not come in. He watched him decide he would have to go to her, and come off his heels to do it.

That was the end of him, though it took two exchanges more to finish.

A Stone off his heels was a Stone that could be moved. In the fourth Lira moved him twice, both times with the staff in behind his lead knee as he came round, and the second time he went down onto one hand on the stone and the benches came up off their seats. In the fifth he came off his heels a third time, slower now, heavy, and she turned on her left heel outside him and he went past the place she had been. "Called," said Vell. "Hand up. Fifth exchange."

The four girls from the wall made a noise that went up into the beams, and the dock partner put both his arms in the air.

"I knew it was there," she said, at last. "That's all. I didn't fix it. I just knew where it was, so I didn't stand in it."

"That's most of fixing a thing," said Brom.

She looked at him, and then, without saying anything, she leaned very briefly against his arm with her good shoulder, the way you lean on a wall at the end of a long day. Brom stood quite still and let her, for perhaps a breath. Cael found that he had not written down a single thing all evening, and found, too, that he did not mind.

The first time the new sense lied to him, it was on an ordinary night, and the lie was nobody's fault but his.

He had begun to do a thing at the end of every evening card, in the quiet after the last bout, while the benches emptied and Dace wiped his wall. He would sit on the end of the east bench, very still, with his hands on his knees, and let the door come open on its own, and see what was in the room. He called it the sweep, privately, after the officers' sweeps, and was aware of the joke. Mostly there was nothing in the room but the sweepers and Vell. Sometimes there were a few late fighters at the water barrel, small *yes*es at the edge of his range, kindled or not. It cost him a little each night, a short band and a quarter-hour of aching eyes, and he paid it because the only way to learn the thing was to use it when there was nothing riding on it, and the end of a card was the only time he could find.

On the fourth night of the sweep, at the very edge of his range, along the west wall where the lamps had already been put out, there was somebody.

He felt it come in and his whole body went still in a different way. It was a kindled weight, a long way off, at the limit of what he could reach sitting; and it was not resting, and it was not working. It was held low, so low and so steady that it was almost nothing, a thin even pressure lying against the west wall in the dark, not moving, not changing. It was exactly the way, he thought, with his heart beginning to go, a person would hold himself who did not want to be found.

He held it, and did not dare look. Ten breaths, fifteen, and it did not change by a hair.

Then he stopped and thought, very deliberately, about what he actually had. He had *kindled*. He had *at the west wall, low*. He had *not moving*. That was the whole of it. Everything else, the not wanting to be found, the hiding, the dark, he had put there himself, between one breath and the next, because he had been afraid of exactly that for a year and his fear had been waiting at the door with its coat on.

He let the door shut, and stood up, with the band coming, and walked down the room along the west wall, not quickly, to see.

It was a man asleep.

He was a fighter, a big Force from the salt end whom Cael knew by sight. He had fought on the second card and lost, and had sat down on the end of the west bench in the dark to wait for a friend, and had gone to sleep there sitting up with his chin on his chest and his arms folded and his mouth a little open. He was snoring, very faintly. His Path was ticking over in him the way a mill-wheel ticks over at night on the last of the race, not working, not stopped. Brom's had been like that at supper, only lower and older, the fire banked. This was not banked; it was only asleep.

Cael stood and looked at him for some time, a man asleep on a bench, and felt the band settle in behind his eyes, and was ashamed in a small sharp way that he would remember.

He looked at it, and then wrote under it the thing he would need to remember the next time his heart went.

The third time was a week after the market, and it was close enough that he could not pretend about any of it.

Lira had hired a yard for the afternoon, as she did twice a month in the season when the alcoves were full, at the top of the tannery lanes, where the old drying-yards had been let out to practitioners by the hour. The district called those lanes the practitioners' quarter, half as a joke. Lira's was the last at the top of the lane, with a broken pump in one corner, and she had the dock partner there and a borrowed post. She wanted Cael on an upturned crate in the corner with his eyes on her feet, calling the step that did not stop each time it stopped.

"Neither do I."

"Can you read them back?" said Brom. "More than *there*. If you sat still long enough, near enough. Could you get anything else?"

"I don't know. I've never had long enough. They're never near enough for long."

Brom set his feet on his cross again, slowly.

"Then that's what we do," he said. "Not walk up to them. Not that. Sit still where they'll come, and see who gets tired first." He held up his forearm for the next blow. "You're the most patient man I've ever stood across from, and I've stood across from a boat shed full of them. Let's see what that's worth against somebody who's patient on purpose."

He was steady all the rest of that morning, as he always was. But twice, between blows, Cael saw him glance at the arch of the alcove, at the dark of the empty floor beyond it, as he had never once glanced before. It was the look of a man counting the doors.

# Chapter 35 — Even Pressure

The query came up to Coss in the last inner-post round of a Monday afternoon, and he knew whose it was from the envelope.

He knew the hand: every letter on the front was the same height as its neighbour, level as a ruled line, the hand of a young man who had been told once that neatness was a kind of honesty and had believed it. Coss had read that hand on a routing card some weeks ago, on a short return from the river side, and had gone down to the long room at the end of the day to look at a file he had no reason to look at. He had not expected to see it again so soon. He had not expected to see it at all.

He opened it at his desk with the door shut.

It was very short, and he read it standing, and then sat down and read it again.

*Contact of record, Ardenmere file (Hesk-ward, C.), river side. Quarterly review. A Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4 designation appears in the classification block. I am unable to confirm it against Section 12 of the manual for the review record. Requesting guidance on its origin and continued application. H.*

He knew what Section Twelve said, having read it once, years ago, the way a man reads the rules of a game played in another country, and had never needed to read it again. Level 4 was the weight the registry put on a Gold-tier practitioner it had reason to fear. He had seen it on perhaps two files in eleven years, both of them sealed, both with a name in the right-hand column that he had only ever seen in the duty records of the floors above him.

So that was the line. The short printed form between the registry's stamp and the young man's green slip, with nothing in its column, was *that*. He sat with the slip of paper in his hand and felt the knowledge go down through him, slowly, the way cold goes down through a man who has stepped off a bank into deeper water than he thought.

He thought, for a moment, of a table in the district a year and more ago, and a pie between them, and a boy of fourteen with his hands under the edge of it, asking him whether he knew what a flag was. He had told the boy the truth, which was that he did not. He knew now what this one was called. He found that knowing its name was not at all the same as knowing what it was, and that he would have given a good deal, sitting there, to be back at that table not knowing either.

And under the cold there was the thing that frightened him, which was not the line at all.

It was that the question had been asked, and not by a man with a reason, or a grudge, or a rank. A careful junior with four years in had been sent to the manual by a printed review sheet, a half-page of boxes the registry handed out by the hundred, and had found it in an afternoon. Coss had kept it shut in his own head for over a year, and he had told himself in all that time that the line was buried, that nobody below a certain floor would ever go looking. Nobody had needed to look; the registry's own paperwork had walked a young man straight up to it and put the manual in his hands.

If a review sheet could do that once, it would do it again. Not this quarter, perhaps, or the next. But some quarter, at some desk, a man with more rank than Havel, or less sense, would tick down to the sixth box. He would go to the shelf, and he would not write *I am unable to confirm*. He would write *who*.

Coss put the query face down on his blotter.

He knew what he would write before he picked up his pen, and had known it, if he was honest, from the first line. He did not let himself think about it any longer than it took to write it, because thinking about it was the thing he was most afraid of.

*Follow standard procedure. Do not attempt to reclassify the monitoring level. File regular reports.*

He initialled it and read it through once. It answered nothing, and he had meant it to answer nothing. It was the shortest true thing he could find to send a careful man that would end the matter on the young man's side of the floor before anybody else came by and saw it lying there open. He told himself, folding it, that he was doing it for Havel, and it was true. A junior who asked *who* about a Level 4 would be known on every floor of the building by the end of the season, and Coss would not wish that on anybody.

It was not the whole truth, and he knew that too. He sealed it and put it in the out-tray for the last round, and sat looking at the empty tray after it had gone.

*Supplementary note. Ardenmere file, river side (Hesk-ward, C.). That last part was what it was for. He made himself look at that, sitting at his own kitchen table where his daughter did her sums.

It was not diligence, or not only. It was a man making sure that when this came up properly some day, at a level where somebody could demand an answer, there would be a sheet in the chain with *Coss* at the foot and a date that came before the asking. *I saw it. The first was the day of the grey slip, and the second the day he had read Havel's return in the long room. He looked like a man reading a rota, which was what he was doing, and Coss understood that this was exactly what he had looked like every day for four years, and was the whole of his skill.

There was nothing all that month. There was no acknowledgement, which every note got, a slip with a stamp that said *received*. There was no *Noted*, which was what his last query had got, in the spring, in a hand he did not know. There was no instruction, and no summons, and no visit from anybody he had never met. He could not have said how he understood it, because nothing had been said to him. But he had worked in the building eleven years, and he knew the difference between a paper that had been lost and a paper that had been read. A lost paper made a small noise somewhere: a clerk asking after it, a routing card come back marked *not found*. This had made no noise at all. Somebody had read his note, and had decided, somewhere above any floor he could see, that it would not be answered. That was the answer.

There was one thing left that he could do about it, and it was to do nothing. He did not write again, or go down to the long room to look at the file. When the river-side returns came up on the Thursday tray for information, he put his thumb on the routing card and signed nothing, as he had always done. If anybody ever went through his log they would find one line, one note, one date, and then nothing at all, which was the most a man could say in that building without saying anything.

It cost him more than he had thought it would.

It was not a large cost, and nothing in his days changed. He went in at the same hour and came home at the same hour, and his daughter drew her maps, and his returns were as clean as they had ever been. But there was something under all of it now that had not been there before, like a draught under a door in a house he had lived in for years. He had trusted the paper; that had been the whole of his working life: that the forms meant what they said. A line in its proper place said somebody with a right to put it there had put it there. He thought of the hip in the lock, two *ands* of bright stillness, and of the canvas post at the Cinder House in the cold, and of a man on a bench at an estate wall for a month. He thought of nothing.

The room emptied. The sweepers finished the near end of the floor and went to the far end. Dace began putting out the lamps along the east wall, one at a time, with the snuffer on its long pole, and the room went dimmer by halves.

It had not moved, as far as he could feel, so much as thinned, the way the warmth of a fire thins when the man holding his hands to it takes one step back. It thinned again. It went down to nothing at the very rim of what he could reach, and lay there for one more breath, barely, the faintest thing he had ever felt.

Then it was not there.

He sat on, not knowing whether it would come back, and did not let the door shut, in case. The band was a full ring now, and his eyes were watering, and he kept the door open for perhaps a hundred breaths more on nothing at all. Then he let it go.

"Gone," said Brom, at the other end of the bench.

"Gone."

They sat there a while longer without saying anything. The sweepers came back down the floor toward them with their brooms and looked at them, two people sitting at opposite ends of an empty bench in an empty room, and went round.

"Well," said Brom at last. "We lasted."

"So did they."

"So did they." Brom stood up, slowly, the way he did, a big man unfolding. He rolled his shoulders. "Did you get anything? Anything but *there*?"

Cael thought about it with his palms pressed into his eyes.

"No," he said. "Level. Hard under it. The same as the other three times." He made himself say the rest, all of it. "They stood exactly where I can only just reach, the whole time. They went when the lamps started going out. I don't know if either of those means anything." He took his hands down. "I'm not allowed to guess. I've been wrong once already."

Brom looked down at him for a long moment.

"I've never sat still that long in my life," he said. "Not at the wall. Not ever." He held out his hand and pulled Cael up off the bench. "I thought I was the patient one."

They went out at the side door into the cold. Lira was waiting under the lamp at the corner, with her coat buttoned to the chin and her staff on her shoulder, and she did not ask anything. She only looked at Cael's face, and took his arm on the side away from the lanes, and walked.

He wrote it that night with the band still on, in the smallest number of words he could manage.

*Back bench, B. at the other end. A whole card, then all the sweeping. They were there at the far edge, west side, the whole time. Level. Hard under it. B. got the same, barely. Couldn't get under the level, either of us. They thinned at the edge of my range and went as the lamps went out. Learned about them: nothing.*

He looked at that, and then wrote one more line under it, because it was not nothing, and the book was for true things.

*Learned about me: I can sit still longer than I knew. Not as long as they can. Yet.*

# Chapter 36 — Different Words

The pulse came out of failing at the other thing, which was how most of what he owned had come.

He had spent a week after the back bench trying to make the read last while Brom moved, because he wanted it to last; he had sat a whole card and a whole sweeping still enough to feel somebody at the far edge of a room. Somewhere under that he had begun to want, very badly, a version of the thing that would be worth something on a floor, against a man who did not stand still to be found. So every morning in the alcove he opened the door sitting, and Brom stood up, and took one slow step, and the door went out. He opened it again, and Brom lifted a hand, and it went out. Brom walked a slow circle round him at three paces and it went out on the first stride, every time, forty times, sixty, while the band came and went and came.

"You're holding it," said Brom, on the sixth morning, from somewhere behind him.

"That's what it does. It's a door. I hold it open."

"Mine isn't a door when I move." Brom came round in front of him and crouched, a forearm off, frowning, looking for the word. "When I'm still, it lies on everything, like you've got. When I'm moving, it doesn't lie on anything. I don't hold it. I—" He put out one big hand, slowly, and tapped Cael's knee once with a knuckle, the way a man knocks once on a door to see whether anybody is at home. "Like that. "Every time you move?"

"Every time I need to know. A hundred times an exchange, by now. Two years ago it was once, and half the time I got nothing back." Brom stood. "I don't know if yours can do it. It's only the edge of mine. But you keep trying to carry a lamp across the room without it going out. Try lighting a match instead."

So the next morning he tried it.

He did not open the door, but stood, with Brom a forearm off in front of him, and kept himself shut, and did not want anything. Then, when Brom shifted his weight, he did a thing he had no word for yet. He did not open the door and hold it; he threw it open and shut it again in the same instant, hard, like a knock. And in the instant between the opening and the shutting he asked it one thing, sharp, the way you would call a single word into a dark barn: *there?*

Nothing came back but a blur, a smear of warm noise across the front of him like a lamp seen through a wet window, and then the band, small and quick, a pinch behind the eyes.

"Again," said Brom.

The fourth time something came back.

It was not much, and it lasted no longer than the knock itself. But in that instant, while he was stepping, with his own weight moving and Brom's moving, there was a weight in front of him and a little to his right, and it was awake. It was hard on its leading side and soft on its trailing side, the way a body is when it is going somewhere. He knew where Brom was and which way he was leaning before his eyes had told him. Then it was gone, the knock was over, and there was only the room, and Brom, and his own aching head.

"That," said Cael.

"I felt it." Brom had gone still. "Like somebody tapping on my back. Once."

"It answered. While I was moving. For—" He did not know how long. "For as long as it takes to knock."

They did it all that morning, and the next, and the next, and he kept a tally on the back of his hand in ink because his head would not hold one. Most of the knocks brought back nothing but the smear, the wet-window blur that told him there was something warm in front of him and nothing else, which he could have had from his eyes for free. Perhaps one in three brought back the other thing, the weight and its leaning, sharp and certain for an instant. At a forearm it worked, at a pace it worked worse, and at two paces he got nothing but noise. Each knock cost a little band of its own, and they stacked, so that by the twentieth his head was a drum and he had to stop.

"It's not what mine does," said Brom, on the third morning. He was watching Cael's face as he said it. "Not really. Mine never knocked. I just learned to let it run while I went. Yours knocks." He thought about it. "It's yours, I think. The knocking. Not mine."

Cael wrote it in the Log that night, under all the rest, and wrote only what was new.

*Pulse. Not a held door: one hard knock, one question,* there? *Survives my moving and his for the length of the knock. Close only: a forearm good, a pace worse, two paces nothing. One in three answers clean (where, and which way the weight is going); the rest is smear. Small band each time; they stack; twenty and I'm done.*

And under it, smaller: *Mostly noise. But it's the first version of this thing that could ever stand on a floor with me.*

---

He asked Vell about the oldest books at the end of a quiet evening card, in the week the season turned and the cards began, very slightly, to thin.

He had waited a long time to ask, though he had not forgotten. *The oldest ones use different words for things*: it had sat in the grey book since his first fortnight, on a line by itself, with *Ask again. Not yet* written under it. He had kept to the *not yet* for most of a season, partly because Vell was not a woman to be pressed, and anything pressed on her she would put off on principle. And it was partly because he had wanted to be somebody she would say yes to before he asked. After the Shield, and the man in the academy coat, and the night of the red lines, he thought he might be.

She was finishing the day book at her table, with the floor nearly empty and the sweepers not yet in.

"There's a thing you said in the back room, my first fortnight," he said. "About the books at the bottom of the cupboard. That they don't talk like yours. You told me it would take an afternoon."

Vell did not look up, but finished the line she was writing, and blotted it, and laid the blotter square.

"I did," she said.

"I've got an afternoon. Any afternoon. Whenever suits."

She sat back and looked at him over her spectacles for a long time. He thought she was going to say *sometime* again, as she had in the back room in his first fortnight, and he had decided on the walk down that if she did he would say *all right* and mean it.

"Nobody opens those," she said. "Not even me, if I can help it. I've had practitioners at this table ask me. Old men wanting to see their grandfathers' bouts. A scholar from up the river who wrote me three letters. I've said no to all of them." She turned her pen in her fingers. "They're old, Cael. They were old when my keeper had them. Every time one's opened a little more of it goes, and nobody's ever going to make any more of them."

"I know."

"I know you know. That's not why I'm telling you." She put the pen down. "You carried my lamp, that first time, and didn't touch anything. You stood at this table a few weeks ago with your book and read it out in front of forty people, and every line was dated. And you stood on that bench the night of the red lines and watched me do it, and asked me after whether it was worth it." She looked at him. "That's three things, and I don't need four."

She got up, and took the day book under her arm.

"Not an afternoon," she said. "Tonight. Before I change my mind."

The back room was as he remembered it. There was the long wall of her own books with their bows of cotton tape all at one angle, the wheeled ladder, and the pine board by the door with its white names. The cupboard at the end was painted the same dull brown as the wall. Vell hung the lamp on its bracket by the cupboard and did not take it down.

"Hands first," she said.

There was a basin he had never noticed, on a stand in the corner behind the cupboard door, with a jug beside it and a clean cloth folded on the rail. The water in the jug was cold, and had been drawn that morning; he could see the date chalked on the stand. He understood, washing his hands in it, that the basin had no other purpose in the world: somebody filled that jug every day for books that were opened perhaps once a year.

"Dry," said Vell. "Properly. Between the fingers." She watched him do it. "Now listen, because I'll say it once and then I'll watch you, and if you get it wrong you'll go out and I'll do the rest myself."

He listened.

"The lamp stays where it is. On the bracket. Not in your hand, not on the shelf, not on the table, not nearer to an open page than the length of my arm." She held out her arm to show him the length. "Heat takes ink faster than years do. My keeper had a book go brown in an evening from a lamp held close, and he never forgave himself."

"Yes."

"You turn a page from the outer corner. Only there. Never from the middle of the edge, never from the spine." She showed him with her own hand on the air, a light lift at the corner, as you would lift the edge of a sleeping child's blanket. "If it tears at the corner, it tears where there's nothing written. And you keep the other hand under the page while it turns, flat, to carry it."

"Yes."

"And one book open at a time. Always. If you want another, this one's shut and back on its shelf before the next one comes down." She looked at him. "If a draught comes in at that door, or you knock the table with your knee, or you sneeze, it's one book that suffers. Not three."

He said the four rules back to her, in order. She nodded once, and opened the cupboard.

The room was very quiet, the quietest he had been in for weeks, with nobody in it but the two of them and the books, and he felt the thin thing on his skin begin to come open by itself, the way it did now whenever he was still. Vell was a pace from him, and had been Bronze once, and would be a strong clear weight on the front of him if he let it come. He had not asked her. He let it close.

Before the oldest ones she showed him the others, because she said he would not see what the old ones were without them. It was the closest he had ever come to hearing her give a lesson.

Her own books first, along the wall. She took down one from near the end of the row, from her first years, and opened it on the table, and then one from ten years ago, and one from last season, and laid them side by side, open, square. Three books open at once: he looked at her, and she said, "Mine. I'll be careful with mine as I like." He looked at the three hands.

They were all hers, and they were three different women. The earliest was stiff, every letter made separately and held, like a fighter standing in her stance a little too correctly to be comfortable in it. The middle one had loosened: the letters joined, and leaned, and the lines went across the page at one even pace. The last was so even that it hardly looked written at all; it looked as if it had been there before the paper. He could see thirty years in it, the way he could see a fighter's years in her feet.

"You can tell," said Vell, watching his face.

"Your early hand's careful. Your last one's sure."

"It took me ten years to stop *keeping* the book and start just writing in it." She shut the three, one after another, and put them back. "Now his."

His were in the cupboard, on the upper shelves: thirty-odd volumes, she said, she had never counted them properly because it seemed rude. They were in a rounder hand than hers, with bigger loops and a habit of writing the result first, in the margin, before the names, as though he had always wanted to know how a thing came out before he cared who had done it. Under the round hand, in the earliest of them, was another, cramped and sloping, with *Copper* spelt with two *p*s. The one-armed keeper.

"And under that," said Vell, and went down on one knee in front of the cupboard, stiffly, with her hand on its edge, "these."

The bottom shelf held the books nobody could number.

---

He could see why: they were not the same height as one another, or the same thickness, or the same shape. Some were bound in boards and some in leather gone black and some were only bundles of pages sewn through the fold. Two had lost their covers altogether and were tied between thin slats of wood with tape. Several had been mended, and mended again: he could see three different threads in one spine, a coarse grey, a fine brown, a white that had gone yellow, each from a different keeper in a different year who had decided this paper was worth an evening's work. A couple of them had grown together along their edges with age, so that they would never be two books again without tearing.

Vell drew one out, the nearest, with both hands, slowly, as a man draws a sleeping cat off a cushion. She set it on the table under the lamp, at the length of her arm, and opened it at the outer corner.

The ink had gone the brown of old tea, and the hand was small and sharp and used short forms he did not know, whole words cut down to two letters and a stroke. It took him a moment to see that it was a ledger at all.

It was. That was the strange thing, once he saw it: it was a keeper's book, laid out the way Vell's were laid out, a line to a bout, the same order. Two names. The outcome. A short note. A line of witnesses at the foot of each night.

But the witnesses were not one name. Vell signed her own book as its witness, one name, hers, at the foot of the day. Here there were six at the foot of one night, seven at the foot of another. Some were signed and some were only marks, crosses and small drawings, a fish, a wheel. Six or seven people had stood at the edge of a floor, whatever floor it had been, and put their names to what they had seen, all together.

"They witnessed it in a crowd," he said.

"In a crowd," said Vell. "It took a long time for it to come down to one. My keeper said the old circuit thought one witness was the same as none."

He turned a page, from the outer corner, his other hand flat under it.

There were other things missing, things he had not known he would miss. Vell's book had a column, narrow, at the side of every line, where she wrote a fighter's Path and his formal tier if he had one, and left it empty if he had none. There was no such column here, and no Path written anywhere on the page; a fighter was his name, and what he had done. Where a keeper wanted to say more, he said it in the note, and what he said was what he had seen.

Vell read one for him, unfolding the short forms with her finger under them. *Hits from further than his arm.* Another: *Does not move when struck.* Another, beside a woman's name: *Quick. Quicker on the left.* There was no tier, no rank, no name for what anybody was, only what each had been seen to do on a particular night by six or seven people standing at the edge.

He turned the pages one at a time from the corner, and Vell let him.

Past the middle of the book, on a page no different from the forty before it, he came to a line with a hole in it.

The name at the head of the line was gone. Damp had got to that corner of the page long ago and taken it, all but two letters at the end, which could have belonged to any name in the world. The opponent's name was whole, the outcome plain, a win, and the witnesses seven. The note in the line said, in the short forms, that the fighter's abilities matched nothing the keeper knew.

And in the margin beside it, in a smaller hand than the line, cramped, the ink a little darker as if the keeper had pressed harder, there was a note.

*Assessed per pre-registry terminology as [UNBOUND]. Abilities not matching any guild standard. Competed without incident.*

He read it three times.

He did not move his face; he had learned that in the market square and at Vell's table and on a bench a bench's length from Brom, and he was very glad of it now. Vell was standing at his shoulder, and he did not want her to see anything in his face, because he did not know what was in it.

There had been words before the registry. Somebody had called them *terminology*, as if they had been as formal once as the square print on a Compact file. And one of those words had been written, by a keeper, in a margin, beside a fighter whose abilities fitted no standard that keeper knew. Then the keeper had written that the fighter fought, and that nothing had come of it. *Competed without incident.* Nobody had stopped the bout, or taken the fighter away. The next line on the page was somebody else's.

He felt something in him lean toward the word, as the hip leaned toward a burst it had not been asked for. There was a column in Vell's book out on the floor, narrow, at the side of every line, with his own name beside it and nothing in it at all. Every other fighter's was filled. His had been empty for more than a year, and he had never minded, and he did not know why he was thinking of it now.

He made himself stop, because he knew this feeling; he had felt it in the Log on the night of the teaching day, with two things lying side by side as neatly as a lock and a key. The neatness had been the reason not to trust it then, and it was the reason now. One word, in one margin, beside one fighter whose name was gone. That was all it was. He had no evidence that it was about anybody but the person whose name the damp had taken.

He took the grey book out of his coat, with his hands still clean and dry, and opened it on his knee below the table, away from the old page. In the smallest hand he owned, smaller than the tally in the corner, small enough that he would need the lamp to read it back, he copied the first sentence. He left the brackets off, because they were the keeper's and not his.

*Assessed per pre-registry terminology as UNBOUND.*

He shut the grey book and put it back in his coat, and then went on turning the pages from the outer corner, four more, five, past nights of bouts between people whose names meant nothing to him, so that his hands would be doing something ordinary while the rest of him settled.

"Those margins are full of words nobody uses any more," said Vell, behind him. "I know perhaps half of them."

He nodded, and did not ask about the other half.

---

She shut the old book herself, and put it back on its shelf with both hands, and stood, slowly, with her hand on the cupboard door.

"Why do you keep them?" he said. He had wanted to ask it for a season. "Not yours. These. Every time one comes off the shelf you lose a bit of it, and you've told me yourself nobody reads them."

Vell did not answer at once, but sat down on the edge of the table, which he had never seen her do, under the lamp, with her hands folded in her lap.

"I was Bronze once," she said. "Did you know that?"

"I'd heard."

"Kindled at fourteen, Copper the same day. I climbed on my own for four years, in yards, and at eighteen the guild house up the river took me on and put me on its letters. Iron at nineteen. Bronze at twenty-one. They used to bring the younger ones to watch me practise." She said it without any pride in it at all, as she would have read out a result. "I had a few years of that. Not many. Then I made a thing."

"A technique."

"A way of turning a blow that the house hadn't taught me. I found it myself, on my own, in a yard, the way some of the best things get found." She looked at the cupboard. "I used it in a bout, a house bout, and won with it, and the house ruled that it wasn't in the forms and the bout didn't count. I said it should. They said that a practitioner of their house fought in their forms or didn't fight under their name. I said the forms were wrong. I was twenty-two, and I was right, and it made no difference at all." She turned her hands over. "They gave me a month to stop using it. I didn't stop. So they took the pin."

He waited.

"I could have gone to another house. I'd have had to stop there too. They all fight in the forms." Vell looked at her own book on the wall. "So I came down here, and found a keeper who needed somebody with a steady hand. That was the man in the cupboard. He took me on because I could write and because I'd been Bronze and could see what I was looking at. And I found out that what I wanted had never been the pin. It was for somebody to write down, somewhere, that I had done a thing, and that it had happened, whatever anybody's forms said about it."

"And that's what these are."

"That's what these are." She put her palm flat on the cupboard door. "A fight happened. A person did a thing on a floor in front of people. None of it needs anybody's stamp to have been true. A seal on it afterward doesn't make it any truer. It only makes it official, and those were never the same thing." She looked at him. "So somebody had better keep a book that nobody up the hill can tell what to say."

He looked at the cupboard, and at the long wall of her books, and at the pine board by the door, and he thought of the girl crying at the wall on the night of the red lines.

"The Compact does falsify things," he said. He did not say it as a question.

Vell looked at him steadily. There was no surprise in her face at all.

"I know," she said.

They were quiet for a while, and out on the main floor he could hear the sweepers come in, and the long sound of the first broom.

"Twenty years I've thought it," said Vell at last. "I can't prove a word of it. I haven't got a page in any book I could put in front of anybody that says so. I've only watched results that I saw with my own eyes go up the hill and come back down as something else, and other results that I never saw at all come down with seals on them. Enough of both, for long enough, that I believe my eyes and not their paper." She got up off the table. "That's not evidence. Don't you go writing it down as evidence."

"I won't."

"Good." She took the lamp off its bracket, now that every book was shut. "That was the handling. You know how to touch them now. The reading's another thing. That's a long afternoon, a lot of long afternoons, and we'll do it properly some day, from the first page." She held the lamp up. "Not tonight. I'm old and it's late."

He carried the lamp out ahead of her across the dark floor, and waited while she locked the door.

---

He walked home by the long way, round by the river wall, because he did not want to arrive anywhere yet.

It was very cold, and very clear, with the stars out hard over the river and the water going by black under the wall. The gulls were asleep in a long row on the coping, heads under their wings, and did not move for him. Down on the water a barge went by with one lantern at its stern, very slowly, and a man on it was singing under his breath. At the corner by the eel market the chestnut man was banking his brazier for the night with a shovel, and he lifted the shovel to Cael without a word. Up in the lanes somewhere a woman was calling a child in by name, twice, and then a door shut. None of it would go in any book. It was only the district, being itself in the dark.

He walked with the word in his coat.

He did not take it out, and did not need to; he could feel it there, in the smallest hand he owned, in the grey book against his ribs, the way you feel a coin you have been given and not yet decided what to spend on.

He made himself go through it as he would have gone through a bout, from the start, without adding anything. Long ago, before the registry, an old circuit had had a word for something. A keeper had used it once, in a margin, beside a fighter whose name was lost and whose abilities fitted no guild's standard. The fighter had fought, and nothing had come of it. That was what the page said. That was all the page said.

Everything else was his. The pull toward it was his, and the empty column in Vell's book that it had made him think of. So was the feeling, which he did not trust, of a key lying beside a lock. He had been wrong once already this season about a man asleep on a bench, because he had taken what the read gave him and added a thing of his own to it, and the thing he added had been what he was afraid of. He did not know what he was afraid of here, or what he was hoping, and that was reason enough to add nothing.

He would tell nobody.

He thought about that for a long time, on the river wall, in the cold, because it was not like him any more. He had told Lira about the read through the wall, and about the watcher on the same day she asked. He had told Brom the whole of the Log. He had stood in Vell's back room and said the Compact falsified things out loud. He had spent a season learning that the things you keep shut cost you, and that some of them were about other people, who had a right to know.

But this had not happened to anybody he knew. It was one word in a margin, and he did not know what it meant. If he said it aloud, to Lira at the kitchen table or to Brom between crosses, he would have to say why he had copied it. Then he would have to say what he thought it meant, and he did not think anything yet. He only felt it, and a feeling said aloud becomes a claim, whether you mean it to or not. He had a book for claims, and this was not one.

So he would carry it. He would put it in the column for things carried shut, if he put it anywhere, and he would pay what it cost to carry, and he would not open it until he had something to open it with.

When he got in, the house was dark except for the kitchen, where the heavyset man's wife had left the lamp turned low for him, as she did. He went up past Lira's door, which was shut and quiet, and sat at the crate desk without lighting his own lamp. In the dark he could see the practice lamp in the north window over the alcoves, very small.

He opened the Log by feel to Tuesday's page, the bout night's, and found the narrow column at its edge, and the two lines in it. He did not write the word there. But he put the point of the pencil at the foot of the column, and after a while he made a mark there, one small stroke, that meant nothing to anybody but him.

Then he shut the book.

---

It was a week after that, on a night with no card and the house quiet, that he took Keth's pages out and read them through from the beginning.

He had not meant to; he had sat down at the crate desk to write up a side-floor afternoon and found that he had nothing to say about it; it had been a clean bout and a short one, a Blade from the salt end who had stood too square. So he had turned back through the grey book idly, the way you turn back through a letter you already know, and come to the page with Keth's name at the head and *No reason for this page* underneath, and kept going.

There were a great many pages, more than he had known. There was the first, from the morning Dace had pointed him out, with the little finger off the grip and the cuts that finished long. Then the chosen count, the three cuts and the three counts and *The only one I've seen*. Then dawn after dawn in the newcomers' ring, a line or two each, in pencil, never more, on mornings when he had gone down early for no reason he had ever written down. *Keth shows the parry slow, then fast, then real.* *Keth watching feet again.* *Keth chained three cuts for the tall lad and the third was late.* There were pages from the side floors, from main-floor nights. There was a note from the night of the Brom bout about Keth on the end of the east bench, looking at Brom's chest.

Three months of it. He had never once sat down to watch Keth. He had only, every time Keth was in the room, let his eyes do what they did and let his pencil follow, and then turned the page and forgotten it.

He laid the pages side by side under the lamp and read them as he would have read a fighter he meant to meet.

It took him most of an hour to see it, and when he saw it, it had been there for weeks.

Keth chose his count fresh every cut. That was the first thing anybody would write about him, and Cael had written it first, and it was true. But when Keth chained his cuts, two and then three in a run, as he did against anybody good, the choosing stopped for a moment between the second cut and the third. Cael had it on eleven pages, from eleven different weeks, written down as eleven different small things. *Third late.* *Feet stuck on the third.* *Didn't turn for the third, cut it square.* *Second finished long and he couldn't get the angle back.* Eleven times he had written it down, and every time he had thought it was that morning's fault, or the newcomer's, or Keth's tiredness.

It was not any of those; it was the same thing. At the end of the second cut in a chain, Keth's blade finished long, the way it always did, the way of a man who had learned on a bigger blade. For an instant after it finished, the angle was set, and the feet had not yet caught up, and there was nothing Keth could choose. Then the third cut came out of whatever the second had left him. It was the smallest of seams, a sliver of a beat, and it opened at a range that was no use to anybody standing in front of him.

But it was there. It had been there eleven times. And Keth did not know it, Cael thought. He had watched Keth teach that chain to newcomers with his own hands, slowly, and he had never once slowed down there, at the seam. Nobody slows down at a place he cannot see.

He sat back from the desk.

He had not been studying Keth; he would have said so to anybody, and believed it. Keth was the line at Iron, a man who taught twelve-year-olds for nothing at dawn and crouched beside the ones who fell. He had asked only to be told after, and called it the price. And he was the man Cael had very nearly decided, against all his own rules about the people in the grey book, that he liked. He had no plan to fight him. He had no plan for him at all.

And without a plan, without ever once deciding to, he had built the most complete picture of any fighter the grey book held. Three months of habit had done it; his eyes had done it while he was telling himself he was only watching the newcomers.

He did not write the seam down as a seam. He sat a long while with the pencil over the page, and then, under the eleventh entry, he wrote only: *Between the second and the third. The same every time. Look again.*

He did not tell Lira, or Brom in the alcove in the morning. He did not quite know why not, and he made himself look at that too, because it was a thing he would have asked of anybody else. It was not a secret, the way the word in his coat was a secret. It was only that, until he had looked again, it was a thing he had found out about a man he liked without the man's leave. He did not yet know what he meant to do with it.

He shut the grey book. Through the window, down the hill, the practice lamp in the north window over the alcoves was lit. Somewhere in the district Keth was asleep, probably, a man who chose his count fresh every cut, and did not know there was a place where he could not.

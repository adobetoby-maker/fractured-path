# Chapter 1 — The Fifth Exchange

The feint went high, and Cael's eyes went with it.

He knew it was wrong while it was still happening. Ulric's right hand came up toward his face with the whole of a trained shoulder behind it, quick and bright and obvious, and some old part of him that had learned to fight by watching hands took the bait before the rest of him could vote. His chin lifted half an inch. His weight came onto his heels to make room for a blow that was never going to arrive. And the real one, which had been waiting under the feint the way a second letter waits under the first in an envelope, came round low and flat and caught him across the outside of the left thigh.

It was not a big blow. Ulric did not throw big blows in the second exchange; Cael had watched him four times, and he never did. But it landed clean on the long muscle above the knee, and the leg went stupid for a moment, as a leg does when you have slept on it wrong, and the stone floor of the Ironyard tipped very slightly to the left and then thought better of it.

He did not go down. He took two steps back on a leg he could not feel and kept his hands where they were, and Ulric let him go, because Ulric was a guild man and guild men took their points and reset.

At the rope behind him, by the iron post nearest the lamps, Lira had both arms folded on the top strand. He did not look at her. He did not have to. He knew exactly what she was doing with her face, which was nothing at all, and he knew what she would do with her right hand in a moment, which was to lay two fingers flat against her own thigh, where he had been hit, and leave them there while she counted three. It meant *you know why*. She had invented it in the spring for occasions exactly like this one, when he had done something so plainly foolish that a signal for it would be quicker than an argument afterward.

He did know why. He had built the whole of this bout on not watching Ulric's hands.

There was a page in the observation book about it, the newest page, written over four evenings in four different moods. He had watched Ulric fight four times before he let Dace put the two of them on a card, and each time he had watched for one thing and made himself ignore the rest. The first evening had been rhythm only: how many of his own breaths, sitting still on the cheap bench at the back, fell between one committed strike of Ulric's and the next. It was four when the man was fresh, and three when he was pressed, and never two. The second evening had been weight, which meant feet, ankles, hips, and the slow tilt of a body loading itself before it let go of anything. The third had been breath and face, the exhale and the jaw. Only on the fourth evening had he let himself watch everything at once, to see which of the things he had seen would still be there when they were not alone.

And on that page there were three columns, as there always were now: what he was sure of, what he thought likely, and what he had seen once and thrown out. Under the first heading, in a hand that had been very pleased with itself, he had written: *The hands lie. They are paid to. Watch the back heel.*

So he had come out for the second exchange and watched the hands.

"End of the exchange," said Vell, from her table by the fourth post.

The Ironyard let out its breath. It was a mid-week card and a middling crowd, perhaps a hundred and sixty on the benches and along the walls, and it made the sound a middling crowd makes when it has seen a boy take a hit he ought not to have taken: half pleased, half sorry, wholly interested. Somebody near the betting corner called out a price. Somebody else told him to be quiet.

Cael walked back to his mark on the leg, and it held, and on the way he made a small, deliberate change in where he was putting his eyes. He brought them down. Not to the floor; to the knees, and the hips above them, and the place on the stone where Ulric's back heel would sit. He let the hands become shapes at the top of the picture, the way the beams of the roof were shapes at the top of the picture, there and not interesting. It was like taking off a coat that was too warm. Everything got simpler at once.

At the rope Lira took her two fingers off her thigh.

---

In the third exchange he gave ground.

He gave it on purpose and he gave it untidily, a step and then half a step and then a step again, letting his guard look a little late on the left so that Ulric would find it there. He let Ulric drive him two paces toward the north side of the rope, where the lamps hung lowest and the light was best, and he did not answer anything that came at him with more than he had to. Twice Ulric's lead hand tested him and found him slow. Once the back hand came through on its proper count, three breaths after the last, and Cael took it on the forearm and gave another half step for it, and heard somebody on the benches say, not quietly, that the boy was tired.

The boy was not tired. The boy was waiting for a guild-trained Blade practitioner to finish deciding what he was.

That was the part of it he liked least and needed most. Every fighter who came across a rope at him had brought a picture of him in with them, made out of rumour and the chalk on Dace's board and whatever they had heard in the eating houses, and for the first exchanges they fought the picture. He could not stop them doing that. What he could do was paint it for them, a little at a time, until it was a picture he could step out of. *Slow on the left. Gives ground. Watches hands.* Ulric had seen him do the last of those things with his own eyes, and had been paid for it with a clean point on the thigh, and nothing persuades a man of a weakness like having been paid for it.

By the end of the third exchange Ulric was standing taller. His guard had come up an inch and settled, the way a guard settles when the man behind it has stopped expecting surprises.

In the fourth, Cael watched the heel.

It was where the page had said it would be. Every time Ulric meant a strike, rather than merely offered one, the weight went back into his rear heel a half-beat before anything else moved, as if he were leaning on a door before he opened it. The hands could say anything they liked above it. The heel did not lie, because a heel cannot be made to lie without moving the whole body, and moving the whole body costs too much. Under the heel, three times out of four in the bouts Cael had watched, and twice already tonight, there had been the other thing, the thing he had come for: just before a committed downward cut, Ulric's right shoulder dropped. It went perhaps a finger's width, a small sag like a man setting down a bucket, and then the cut came. Somewhere, years ago, a guild instructor had probably told him about it. Somewhere, years ago, he had probably listened, and fixed it for a month, and then forgotten.

Cael did not use it in the fourth. He let it go by twice and stepped out of the cuts the slow way, with his feet, and let Ulric see that he had had to work for it.

"End of the exchange."

He had his breath. His thigh had come back to him in pins and needles and then in a dull, honest ache that would have opinions about stairs by evening. His left hip was sound, because he had not spent anything from it yet. Across the circle Ulric was rolling his right shoulder, loosening it, and Cael watched him do it and thought, quite calmly, *there you are.*

"Begin."

Ulric came in on the four-breath count, fresh again after the rest, and the first strike was a feint and the second was a feint and Cael let both of them go past the top of his picture without lifting his eyes. Then the heel went back. Then the shoulder dropped.

The burst started in his left hip.

It always started there, though he had not yet asked himself why. There was a drop, and a loosening, as if the joint had been held up all day on a string and somebody had cut it; and with the drop his breath stopped, halfway in, and stayed stopped, because the burst would not go on a moving breath and never had. Then the floor went short. That was still the only way he had to say it. The yard of stone between where he was and where he would be simply stopped being a yard, and he was across it before his legs had finished agreeing to go, half the width of his own body to the left, with the cold wash of Ulric's cut going down through the place where his shoulder had been.

In the spring it had been a hand's width. Lira had measured it on the straw every morning since, with a stick.

And then the landing, which was the bill.

His weight came back into his heels all at once, like a sack dropped off a cart, and he was locked. For half a breath, not more and never less, he could not turn or lean or shift a grain of his weight from one foot to the other; he could only stand where the burst had put him and look. It was a strange half-breath. He had never got used to it. The whole Ironyard went very clear and very slow, so that he saw the sweat on the back of Ulric's neck and the lamp behind him and the line of the cut still finishing, still going down and away into nothing, with all of a guild man's weight committed to it and no way left to call it back. He saw Ulric's right side open from the armpit to the hip like a door left off the latch. He saw all of it, and he could do nothing about any of it, and then the lock let go.

He went in through the door.

It was not an elegant strike. It was the short straight one from the hip that Lira had given him a year ago and drilled into him since, the one strike he owned outright, and it went in under Ulric's arm at the bottom of the ribs while the cut was still travelling. He felt it land the way you feel a key turn. Ulric made a sound with no word in it and folded round the place, and his feet, which had been so certain, got in each other's way. He dropped to a knee on the stone, and put his left hand flat on the floor, and after a moment he lifted his right one, open, toward Vell's table.

"Called," said Vell. "Hand up. Fifth exchange."

It was a small noise the crowd made, after that. It was a crowd that had expected the boy to lose and had then remembered, a moment too late, that he usually didn't. The betting corner muttered among itself. Somebody laughed. Over it all came the long line of strain down the outside of Cael's left hip, from the crest of the bone to the inside of the knee, which had been waiting politely for the bout to finish and now introduced itself.

He went across to Ulric before he did anything else and held out his hand. Ulric looked at it from one knee. Then he took it, and came up on it, and let go of it as soon as he was up, and said, "You were watching my hands."

"In the second."

"And then you weren't." Ulric rubbed his side. He had a long, plain, careful face, a face that had been told for six years that it was going somewhere. "I'd like to know what you were watching instead."

"I know," said Cael, and Ulric looked at him a moment, and understood that this was all he was going to get, and went to his corner with his jaw set.

---

The east bench was empty, which was why he liked it.

It was not really a bench, or not only one; it was a recess in the Ironyard's east wall where some earlier use of the building had left a gap the width of a doorway, and somebody had put a plank across two bricks in the bottom of it, and a year of fighters had worn the plank pale and smooth by sitting on it to think. There was a lamp on an iron hook driven into the beam above it. Nobody owned it and nobody had named it, and everybody called it the east bench as you call a chair by the fire your father's chair, without either of you ever deciding.

He had twenty minutes, near enough, before the bout started turning into a story about the bout. He had measured that across the year the way he measured everything, by writing down what he remembered at different distances after a fight and comparing it afterward with what Lira and Vell remembered, and twenty minutes was where his memory stopped being a record and started being a version. It was less after a hard hit, because pain was a busy editor. Tonight he had one clean hit and a hip, and he sat down on the plank with the leg straight out in front of him and wrote quickly.

He wrote the exchanges first, in order, a line each, plain as a list of goods. Then under them he wrote what had been right on the page and what had been wrong. *The heel held: five bouts now, no exceptions. The shoulder-drop held: first seen bout two; three of four; twice tonight before I used it. Rhythm held: four fresh, three pressed.* Then, because he made himself write the bad things in the same size of hand as the good ones, he wrote: *Second exchange: I watched the hands. The hands did what hands are for. One clean hit, left thigh, my fault. Fix: not a fix. A habit. Look at the page before the bout, not just before the plan.*

He sat back against the brick and looked at that for a while.

A habit was worse than a mistake. A mistake you made once and wrote down and did not make again, if you were careful. A habit was a thing you had been doing so long it had worn a groove in you, and it would go on finding the groove when you were tired or frightened or pleased with yourself, and the only cure he had found for it so far was to catch it every single time until the groove filled in. He had caught this one in the second exchange and not in the first. He put a mark beside it to say he would be watching for it.

Then, in the margin, small, because it belonged to the other book and he would copy it across tonight: *W, once. Fifth. Half body. Lock as usual. Hip after: a long line from the bone to the knee, worse than the time before, because I went further.*

Vell found him there with the ledger under her arm.

She did not come into the recess. She stood at the edge of it with the lamplight on her grey head, holding the book against her side with one hand spread over the cover, and looked down at him the way she looked at a fighter who had come to her table to argue: patiently, and as if she had all evening, though she never did.

"I've written you," she said, and opened it, and turned it a little toward the lamp so that he could see.

*Cael Hesk-ward, assessed-Copper, def. Ulric (Copper formal, Blade). Fifth exchange. Atypical movement pattern.*

There it was again. *Atypical.* He had stopped counting how many lines in her book ended with it, and she had stopped explaining it, and somewhere in the last few months it had become less a comment and more a sort of surname.

"He'll want a return," said Vell. "He won't get one this side of midwinter. It takes a guild man longer to mend his pride than his ribs."

"I didn't do anything to his pride."

"No. You did it to his training." She closed the book. "That's eleven Copper formals this year, by my count, and I'm the one who counts. Every one of them came in thinking six years of guild teaching was a wall you couldn't climb, and every one of them went home wondering who had moved it. You don't crow and you don't explain. You only keep doing it." She tilted her head. "It's a particular kind of insult, that. I'm not saying stop. I'm only telling you so you won't take it hard when one of them leaves your hand out in the air."

"He shook it."

"This one did." She was already turning away toward the floor, where Dace was waiting with two names and a question. "Go and write. There's a basin of hot water at home with my name on it, and three more lines between me and it."

He watched her go, and then he did what he did most nights after a win, which was the money.

It was a standard purse for an assessed bout: twelve marks between the two of them, put up before the card. Eight went to the winner and three to the loser and one to the ledger, and Vell had once explained the three to him, unasked, as the most important figure in the whole circuit. A beaten fighter who went home with something in his pocket came back the next month to try again. A beaten fighter who went home with nothing went somewhere worse, where nobody kept a book, and sooner or later somebody there did not get up. The one paid for Vell's ink and Vell's evenings and the plain fact of a record. Dace took nothing from the purse at all, only his share of the door, and Cael had marked that in his first month as the cleverest thing in the building: the man who chose who fought whom had no money riding on who won.

Eight marks, then. Rent on the two back rooms was nine marks a month for the pair. One good win a month kept a roof on them; two kept the roof on and the stove lit and the paper stall paid and something put by. Hesk's pouch, the old leather one with the drawstring, sat on the shelf at home almost as full as the day it had come over the hills in his bag, and he had come to think of it less as money than as a letter that happened to weigh something. He ran the figures through anyway, every time, because a sum you had checked was worth twice one you had assumed.

---

Lira stood in the dark beyond the edge of the lamp for a full minute before she let him know she was there.

She did it most nights. She would never have called it spying; it was more that there was a particular way he looked when he was writing straight after a bout, and she liked to see it before he knew to arrange his face. He sat with his bad leg out along the plank and the book on his good knee, and his whole body was turned toward the page as though the page were a fire on a cold night. His lips moved now and then. His left hand, which was not writing, kept going to his hip and pressing, and coming away, and going back.

She knew that hip better than he did. That was the plain truth of it, and it frightened her a little whenever she let herself think about it.

She had been the one to find the lock. It had been in the weeks after his ribs knit, when he had first asked her to fight him properly and call a stop every time the thing came, so that he could write it down while it was still exact. On the fourth or fifth morning she had seen it, from three paces, and had not known what she was seeing; on the sixth she had been sure. At the end of every burst there was a half-breath when he was not anybody. He was only something that had landed. His eyes went on working, she could see them working, but the rest of him might as well have been one of Dace's iron posts, and if she had been anybody else, anybody who had come across a rope at him meaning harm, that was the half-breath she would have used.

She had told him so, plainly, the way you tell a training partner a thing. He had written it down word for word. Then he had gone very quiet for a day.

Tonight Ulric had not seen it. Ulric had been too busy falling over. But the circuit was a small place and a talkative one, and every month there were more people on the benches who had come to watch the Hesk-ward boy in particular, and some of them were not there for the entertainment. One day, somebody would come across the rope who had watched him four times with a page of their own and three columns, and they would wait for the landing, and she did not know what would happen then. Neither did he. That was why he had gone quiet for a day.

She shifted her training staff on her back, where she had forgotten it, and it knocked against the wall, and she stayed where she was.

Her own leg ached a little. Not from anything that had been done to it; from what she had been doing to it, every evening for a month, in the alcove after her partner went home. He was a Bronze washout from the docks who sparred for a mark an hour and was worth every copper, and when his hour was done he went, and she stayed. She stayed another hour, sometimes more. She worked the step until the floor went short under her a hundred times in an evening, and then she worked the thing after it, which had no name yet, which was only an idea she had had about going on from a step instead of stopping. Nobody had asked her to. Nobody was paying her to. Cael did not know, because he was always at the east bench or the market or the boarding-house table with his books when she did it, and she had not told him, and she was not entirely sure why.

Part of it was that he would ask what it was for. And she would have to say.

She knew her own ledger by heart, and it was short. *Wind Path, Copper.* That was what the Arbiter had said when she was fourteen, and the registry had written it down, and nothing anyone did in a warehouse full of lamps would ever make it say anything else unless a guild sponsor put his name on a letter and sent her to an evaluation station to be looked at properly. And no guild in Valdris would put its name on a letter for a girl Fenmark Academy had sent home. She had found that out the year after, one door at a time. So she had a formal tier that would not move, and she had Vell's book, which would, if she gave it reasons. Copper-equivalent, high range, said Vell's book this month. She beat Iron-equivalents in practice twice a week, and practice did not count.

None of which was his to carry. He had enough on his plank.

She watched him write the last line, and close the book, and sit for a moment with his hand flat on the cover, as he always did, as though checking it was still shut. Then she came forward into the light, and he looked up, and she watched him put his face in order and decided, as she did most nights, not to mind.

"Good fight?"

"Tidy," said Cael. "He had a shoulder. Somebody tried to fix it for him once and it didn't take."

"You watched him four times."

"Four's usually enough now."

"It used to be six." She sat down at the far end of the plank, close enough to see the page if he opened it, far enough that she would have to ask. That was an old arrangement between them, and neither of them had ever said it out loud. "How's the leg?"

"Better than it deserves. I watched his hands in the second."

"I saw."

"I know you saw. You did the fingers."

"I did the fingers," Lira agreed, and found she was smiling, and let it stay. "You'll fix it."

"I've written it down to fix."

---

They walked home the long way, by the rope-walk and the dyers' steps, because the short way went past the eating houses and the eating houses would be full of people who wanted to tell him how the fifth exchange had looked from the cheap seats.

It was a year and some weeks now since he had come into Ardenmere through the west gate with a bag and Hesk's leather notebook and nothing else he could have sold. In that time the district had stopped being a place he was finding his way round and become a place he lived. He knew which lamps the lamplighter skipped on wet nights and which dogs were bluff. He knew that the cooper on the corner of the dyers' steps sang to his barrels in the mornings and argued with them in the afternoons, while his wife sat in the doorway shelling peas and took the barrels' side. He knew that the Ironyard had not always been where the circuit lived. In his first seven months it had lived up the hill at the Cinder House yard, in the open, with the crows on the eaves; but the Sunday after Darrow Innes the yard had held three hundred people and the lane had held a hundred more, and within the month Dace had asked Vell, for what he swore was the twelfth year running, to bring her table down to his floor, where there was a roof, and lamps, and room for everyone. This time she had said yes. Her shelves had been in his back room for longer than Cael had been alive, she said, because his back room had a lock and a dry wall, so it was only her table that had moved.

Darrow had not come back up the river. A Fenrow carter had brought word instead: *not yet, and he's still not in a hurry.* Feryn's grey coat had gone down to the salt flats in a parcel tied with tarred string, and nobody had written to say whether it fitted.

The boarding house was a narrow brick building at the blind end of a short row, two streets from the Ironyard, with a front step worn into a dip and a man on it most evenings. The man was large and slow and unhurried in every particular, and kept the house as he might have kept a field, by walking round it every day to see what had grown. He nodded to them as they came up the step, and said "Win?" to Cael, and Cael said "Win," and the man nodded again and went back to looking at the street. The kettle on the hob in the back kitchen belonged to the whole house, and somebody had always just used it.

Their two rooms were at the very back, on the first floor, with a door between them that they kept shut at night and open in the day. Cael's held a bed, and a desk he had built himself out of a broken packing crate with more care than skill, and a shelf.

The shelf was the thing he looked at first every evening. At one end stood the old Log, the brown one he had bought at the paper stall in his first weeks, full to the last page now and fat with use. Hesk's three letters still lay inside its front cover, where they had always lain. Inside its back cover, in his best hand, was the line about the fence at the edge of the marsh, which he did not need to open the book to read. Beside it was Hesk's own notebook, the leather one, which he still read most weeks though he could have recited it. Then came the new observation book, a plain grey board volume with twenty-three entries in it. And at the far end, set a little apart, as though it might not get on with the others, was the slimmest book the paper stall had sold, which he had bought the month before for the fragments alone.

It had two entries in it. He had chosen it small on purpose, so that it would never let him write more than he knew.

He sat down at the crate desk with the lamp and copied the margin note across, and then he turned to the column he had been adding all month, a third column beside each entry, ruled in pencil, headed *deployment range*. It had taken him the better part of the time since his ribs knit to get a method he trusted. Asking for the burst outside a fight had told him almost nothing, because it came clumsy and late when it was asked for and clean when it was needed, and it had taken him weeks to see that the difference was the finding, not the nuisance. Sparring gently had been worse, because a gentle fight gave him gentle answers that a real fight then broke. In the end it was Lira who had said *fight me properly and I'll stop you every time it comes*, and they had done that, morning after morning, at full speed, with her calling *stop* the instant the floor went short and him writing for thirty seconds while she stood with her staff on her shoulder and waited.

Through the open door he could hear her moving about her own room, the clink of her washbasin, the bed creak as she sat on it to take off her boots.

"You finished the column," she called.

"Nearly. The Wind one."

"And the other?"

"Half." He looked at the half. "Every real fight shows me the middle of it. To see the top I'd need somebody who could hit hard enough to find the top, and I'm not asking a Bronze to flatten me for the sake of a column."

She came and stood in the doorway in her stockinged feet, with one boot still in her hand.

"Can I ask you something? Not about the testing."

"Yes."

"Do you ever mind it," said Lira, "that I know the whole of it? Not that there's something. The whole thing. Where they came from. One of them's mine. Some weeks that little book's been open on my knee more than on yours." She turned the boot over in her hand. "I wonder whether you'd sleep easier with a corner of it nobody else had been in."

He thought about it properly, because she had asked it properly. He thought about the first months, when he had carried it alone and told Hesk five things fewer than the truth, and had sat on a wall for two hours with a stranger's look in him rather than go and get her.

"No," he said. "It would be lonelier. I've had lonelier. I didn't like it better."

Lira stood a while longer with the boot. Then she nodded, once, as though he had confirmed a figure she already had, and went back into her room. A moment later he heard the other boot hit the floor.

# Chapter 2 — The Ladder

By the middle of the next morning the thigh had gone from an ache to a colour, and the colour was purple going on green, and he limped down the market steps on it with the particular dignity of someone who has decided not to limp and is limping anyway.

The fruit woman saw him from the bottom step. She sat where she always sat, behind her baskets at the foot of the stairs, with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes on the far end of the row, and she did not look at him as he came down, which was how he knew she had seen. In a year he had never once heard her name. She gave short weight to anyone who called her *auntie* and full weight to everyone else, and she had told him once, in a cold week, that a person who wanted her name could earn it by buying apricots for forty years.

"Pears," said Cael, and pointed, because pointing was what she liked.

She weighed them. She put her thumb on nothing, which was her way of being friendly.

"Which one is it this week?"

"Left leg."

"Last week it was the right arm." She tipped the pears into his hand. "One of these mornings you'll come down those steps on two good legs and I won't know who you are."

"I'll try."

"Don't try too hard." She had already looked away, back up the row, where a woman was haggling with the bearded fish cousin over something that had clearly been a good fish yesterday. "A boy that never limps isn't learning anything."

He went up the row eating a pear and thinking about that. It was the kind of thing people in the district said to him more and more, as if word had gone round that the Hesk-ward boy collected sayings and would write them down, which was true. Hesk had said much the same in his own way in the workshop at home, with a cracked housing in his hands: *a thing that's never failed has never been loaded*. Lira said it every morning in the alcove with her staff, in one word, which was *again*. Here was the fruit woman saying it over a basket of pears. He had begun to suspect the whole of Valdris was saying it, in a thousand different accents, and that most people simply did not stop to hear it.

At the top of the row Red Cap was selling chalk.

He was selling it out of a cloth bag at a copper a stick, to the fighters who drew their own plans on the Ironyard's floor before a bout, and to the bookmakers, and to anybody else who looked as if they might want to write on something. Where the chalk came from was a question Cael had decided not to ask. Red Cap saw him and held up a stick and said "For you, two," which was a joke, and Cael bought one for one, which was the answer to it, and Red Cap grinned with the gap in his teeth and said that the whole of the east lanes had the fifth exchange off by heart already, and half of them had it wrong.

"Which half?"

"The half that weren't there." Red Cap tucked the copper into his cheek, a habit Lira had been trying to break him of for months. "They're saying you went sideways a whole yard. I told them half a yard. Was it half a yard?"

"Less."

"I'll tell them less," said Red Cap, satisfied, and went off up the lane shouting *chalk*.

Cael turned the stick over in his fingers as he walked, and turned something else over with it. It was a question he had carried for months without spending, the way he carried most of his questions now. He had learned that a question asked before he had done his own looking bought a worse answer than the same question asked after, because the person answering could tell how much work you had done and pitched the answer to match.

He had done the looking. He could have drawn the ladder from memory on a slate. A newcomer went into Vell's book as *unrated*, with nothing after it, though they came with a cart full of honours. After two bouts under her eye they became *Assessed*, and she put a metal beside the word, and never after one bout, in all the year he had watched. Above that came the rungs proper, and the rungs wore the Compact's own names with a tail tied on, *Copper-equivalent* and *Iron-equivalent* and *Bronze-equivalent*, and stopped there, because anyone who could climb past Bronze-equivalent was being paid better by somebody with a roof that did not leak.

The joint he could not see was the one between the two systems. Her rungs said *Copper* and *Iron* as though those words were hers to use, and the whole building treated them as though they were. Somewhere there had to be a place where her word was fastened to the Compact's, or it was all only a woman with a book, saying things.

---

He asked her in the slack hour after noon, when the main floor was empty but for the sweeper and the lamps were out and the light came down grey and level from the high windows along the north wall.

"Where does your *Copper* get fastened to theirs?"

Vell was at her table with the day's book open and nothing in it yet, drinking tea from the chipped cup she had carried down from the Cinder House. She considered him over its rim for long enough that he began to wonder whether he had asked it wrong.

"Most people ask me why they're not higher," she said. "That one's been in your pocket a good while. I can tell by the shine on it." She closed the book, and stood, and took the key on its string from round her neck. "Come and see."

The back room was behind a plain door in the wall behind her table. He had seen it shut a hundred times and open never. Inside, it was narrow and high, with one barred window up near the ceiling, a desk under it, and a wheeled ladder on a rail that ran the length of the shelves. The shelves went all the way up. The books on them were tied, every one, with a length of cotton tape in a bow, and the bows had been tied by the same hands so many times that they all sat at the same angle, like birds on a wire.

But it was not the books she went to. It was a board on the wall beside the door, a plain pine board with a column of names painted on it in white, and a date beside each, and some of the dates struck through and painted again.

"My fixed points," said Vell. "Everybody in this district, and some outside it, who holds a formal tier from a real Arbiter at a real station, and has fought under my eye, so that I know what that tier looks like on them. Copper formal. Iron formal. Two Bronze. They're the nails in the wall. Every rung in my book hangs off one of those nails by a string, and I can show you the string."

She took down this year's book, the one she had written his line in the night before, and found it without searching, and put her finger on Ulric's name.

"Him. Copper, from a guild station, sealed and entered. He's on the board." She moved her finger to the line above it. "You beat him. That's one link. Last night you hung one string's length from a Copper seal the Compact put on him itself." She looked up. "That's how my word gets fastened to theirs. I don't ask their Arbiter what you are. I ask it what *he* is, and then I watch you fight him."

"And if I'd beaten someone who'd beaten him?"

"Two links. Further from the nail. More give in it." She turned back three pages and showed him a line where somebody's rating had a small number written in the margin beside it, a *3*, in pencil. "That's how far out she hangs. Three strings. I'll give a rung on three, if the fights are clean, but I watch it, and when she fights somebody nearer a nail, I see whether she holds."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then the string was bad somewhere. A link I thought was sound wasn't." Vell turned further back, through a year and then another, until she came to a page with a short red line ruled under a name, and a date beside it, and two letters, *t.f.*, in her square hand. Below it, every line that hung from that name had the same: the red rule, the date, the two letters. There were nine of them. "When a link rots, I go back to it and come down the chain, and every line that hangs off it gets corrected. Every one. And then I find each of them and tell them, standing in front of them, what I've done and why." She tapped the letters. "*Told, face.* I don't change a figure in a book they'll never read and hope they won't notice."

He looked down the column of red rules and small letters, and tried to imagine nine people, one after another, being told by a grey-haired woman with a cup of tea that they had been Iron-equivalent for a year by mistake.

"How often?"

"About a dozen times. Thirty years." She closed the volume and tied its tape, and the bow sat at the same angle as all the others. "It's the worst part of the work. It's also the reason anybody believes the rest of it. A book that won't say it was wrong is a book you can buy."

He did the sum standing there, because he could not stop himself. Thirty years of books; a page an evening in season; somewhere in the tens of thousands of results, and a dozen corrections, every one of them said aloud to the face it cost. Hesk would have signed his name to an instrument that kept that kind of error. And the *t.f.* was the clever part, the part that made it hold. A record that admitted its mistakes to the people they hurt left nobody anything to whisper about.

---

He did not have to ask about the rule. She told him, while she put the book back, as though it had been next on a list.

"You know what we say before a bout. Nobody dies in the circle." She slid the volume into its gap with her palm flat on the spine. "People think that's me being kind. It isn't. Or it isn't only. The day somebody dies on Dace's floor, the Compact gets something it's never had from us, which is a reason it can write down. A body is a reason. And once they've a reason, they'll come in with seals and take the ledger for evidence, and that's this room gone. The circuit will start again somewhere, under a new name, with a new keeper and a new book that knows nobody." She turned. "So I stop bouts. Three, in thirty years. Three times I've walked into the circle and put my hand up and said *done*, and three times the person I did it for walked out of the building on their own feet."

"How did you know when?"

She took a while over that, and he waited for her.

"You watch where they mean to fall," she said at last. "A fighter who's losing still has a plan for the floor. You can see it. When the blow comes they're already choosing which shoulder, which hand, how to come up. They're bargaining. And then there's the other one, who's stopped bargaining. Their body isn't arranging anything any more. It's only standing there, quite still, waiting for something it already knows about, the way a dog waits at a door." She shook her head. "It's quiet. That's the thing nobody tells you. The one about to be hurt is the quiet one."

He thought of the yard in the spring, and of her standing over a man in the raked groove, counting aloud at the pace she had counted at all her life, and three hundred people going still to hear her.

"Darrow was quiet," he said. "On the dirt. You counted him out."

"Darrow was empty." She did not seem surprised that he had asked; she seemed, if anything, to have been waiting for it. "That's a third kind, and you don't see it often. He wasn't bargaining and he wasn't waiting. He'd asked his body for the thing it had never once refused him, and it hadn't got it to give, and he knew that before I did. He lay there looking at the sky like a man who's found his well dry and is working out which way to walk for water." She turned her cup on the desk. "I counted because the rule says I count. If I'd thought he was hurt, I'd have stopped it on three and nobody in that yard would have argued. I knew by two that he wasn't. I've never seen a man so tidy about being beaten." She looked up at him. "It was you I was watching, after. You were the one with the face like a bridge had gone."

He did not know what to say to that, and she did not seem to need him to say anything. She drank the last of her tea, which must have been cold for a quarter of an hour, and made a face at it.

He kept that word for word. That evening he wrote it on a page near the back of the observation book that had no heading yet, a page for things that lived underneath technique and underneath Path, in the plain animal of the body. There were three things on it already: the hum of a planted foot in the ground, a held breath before a feint, and the way his own neck nodded before he struck. Now there was a fourth, and it had come out of thirty years of watching he could never have done himself, handed to him across a desk as if it were nothing.

There was one more thing he wanted, and he was not sure he had the right to it, and he asked it anyway, because she had brought him in here and shut the door.

"Ulric's guild has him on paper as six years trained, Copper formal, a credit to his station. Your book has him on its back on your floor with his hand up. If somebody from his guild came in here and said your book was wrong about him, that their paper was the real thing and yours was a story, what would you say?"

Vell looked at him for a long time without any weighing in it.

"The Compact's Registry is a lie about who people are," said Vell. "My ledger is the truth. These fights happened. These results are real. Nobody gets to say otherwise."

She said it the way she said *begin*, flatly, setting it down where it went.

Then, because he was still looking at her, she went on in an ordinary voice. "Not a lie about what a sigil said to them at fourteen. That's true enough, as far as it goes. A lie about *who they are*. It says a word and stops, as if the word were the person. My book doesn't stop. It goes on writing down what they did, every month, for as long as they come through that door." She opened the back-room door and held it for him. "You'd know a bit about a word that stops."

He did. He went out past her into the grey light of the main floor without saying so, because there was nothing to add that was not already in her face.

---

Dace caught him by the elbow before he had gone ten steps and steered him toward the big slate by the door.

"She's had you an hour," said Dace. "Now you can see the part that actually keeps the roof up."

Dace was a narrow man of fifty or so with chalk dust in the creases of both cuffs and a pencil behind his ear that Cael had never once seen him write with. He did everything quickly and said what he meant. If you wanted him in the Ironyard you looked for him in front of the slate wall by the big door, where he could usually be found with his head on one side, frowning at it as if it owed him money.

The wall was black slate from the floor to above a tall man's reach, and it was covered from end to end in chalk: names in white, cards in columns, and over and among the names a scatter of small marks that Cael had seen every day for months without ever knowing what they meant. There were little rings. There were dots. There were short crosses between pairs of names, like stitches.

"Tell me what you think they are," said Dace, "and I'll tell you when you're cold."

Cael looked. He had a year of the room in his head to look with.

"The rings are people who are hurt," he said slowly. "There's one beside the Stone woman from the tannery lanes, and she's been wearing a wrap under her sleeve all week."

"Warm."

"Hurt and not telling anybody. Healing. You put the ring there so you won't pair them with anybody who'd find it." He went along the wall. "The crosses are pairs you won't make. Those two are brothers. That one owes that one money." He frowned at a cross between two names he did not know. "That one I don't know."

"Their mother," said Dace. "She comes to every bout either of them fights, and if they ever fight each other she'll have to pick which of her sons to stand behind, and I won't do that to a woman who brings me apples." He tapped a dot. "And these?"

The dots were the hardest. They sat beside names in no order he could find, sometimes one and sometimes two. Cael watched Dace's hand hover near them as he talked, and saw that the hand never rubbed one out without writing a name somewhere better.

"Debts," he said. "Somebody took a bad slot when you needed it filled. You owe them a good one."

"Hot." Dace looked pleased, in the narrow way he looked pleased about anything, by moving one eyebrow. "That's the whole book. Not who can beat whom. Vell does that. Mine is who can stand to be in a room with whom, and who's hurt, and who's owed. None of it's written anywhere else."

"It's written there."

"It's written in chalk." Dace drew his thumb through a dot, lightly, and it smeared. "Chalk wipes. That's what I like about it."

A woman came to the wall while they stood there, a solid Copper-equivalent with a good record and a better opinion of it, and asked for a pairing on the next big card against one of the brothers. Cael looked at the board before Dace could speak. There was no cross between her name and the brother's, and no ring beside either. But there were two dots beside the other brother, and none beside her.

*He'll give the slot to the brother he owes,* Cael thought, *and give her the next card, and she'll be angry.*

Dace gave the slot to the brother he owed, and promised her the next card, and she was angry, and went away to tell somebody about it.

"You had that before I opened my mouth," said Dace.

"I had a guess."

"Get two more and call it a habit." He took the pencil from behind his ear and turned it over and put it back. "Vell's told me about your rule of three. I'll give you one of mine for nothing, since you're here. This board answers to the people on those benches, and to nobody else. Not a guild, not a district office, not a man in a grey coat with a pin. If the Compact wants to know who fights in Ardenmere, it can pay at the door and sit on a bench and find out the slow way, like everybody else has to."

He said it lightly, but Cael had seen the corner of Dace's desk through the door of the little room where the door money was counted. On it, under a lump of slag from the old foundry that served as a paperweight, there was a letter on thick cream paper with a printed head, and it was the third such letter Cael had seen there in a year. He had never seen one answered. He had seen one used, on a wet evening in the spring, to light the lamp over the north benches when the spills ran out.

"They write to you," he said.

"Every season. *A registered venue. Licensed bouts. A modest annual fee, in return for the protection of the Guild's good name.*" Dace said it in a dry high voice, like a clerk reading out a will to people who were not in it. "I've got the Guild's good name. It's under a lump of slag on my desk. It's very useful when the spills run out." He turned back to the slate and squinted at a dot. "The day I take their licence is the day they get a copy of this wall. Not once. Every week, by law, with the rings on. And then a woman who's six weeks out of a cracked wrist is a line in a clerk's book in the Ranked core, and the clerk doesn't care whether she gets put in front of a hard fighter or not." The dot came off under his thumb and went back on, smaller. "So they don't get the wall."

---

Stedd lost that afternoon, in the second bout of a thin card, and Cael learned more from the way he did it than from anything Vell had said all day.

He was a Copper washout of forty or so, soft in the middle and slow on his feet, with a bald patch he made no effort to cover and a laugh like a barrow on cobbles. He had not won in Vell's book since before Cael first walked down the hill. He fought a young Force practitioner who was too quick for him from the first exchange, and he went down in the third with his breath knocked out of him, and lay on the stone a moment looking at the beams. Then he got up, slowly, the old way, a hand and a knee and the back last. He shook the young one's hand. And then, without dusting himself off, he walked straight to Vell's table and stood in front of it.

"Fair," said Stedd.

That was all. Vell nodded and wrote, and Stedd went to the water barrel.

And on his way the room nodded to him. Cael watched it happen and could hardly believe it. Fighters on the near benches lifted their chins as he passed. One of Dace's sweepers leaned on her broom to say *well fought*. A Fenrow bookmaker who was famous for acknowledging nobody took one finger off his slate. A man who had just lost in the second bout of a Thursday card crossed the Ironyard like a man coming home with a good harvest.

"He's not won since before I came," said Cael to Vell, when he could.

"He's never once told me I was wrong." Vell blotted Stedd's line. "Eleven years of bouts. Win or lose, he walks to this table first, before he's even got his breath, and tells me it was fair. Some of those weren't, as it happens. I've called a few against him I'd call different now." She put the blotter down. "That's what's worth anything in here. You can't win it in a night, and nobody can give it you. You turn up when you said, you fight clean, and you take what I write without making it anybody else's trouble. Keep doing that for eleven years and people will step aside for you at the water barrel. It's the only kind of rich this building recognises."

He watched for it afterwards, and it was everywhere. The benches nearest Vell's table belonged to the long clean records, whatever their rung, and nobody had ever made it a rule or ever broken it. The bookmakers had the northwest corner and kept to it, and their noise was allowed right up to *begin* and not one breath past it.

And then, near the end of the afternoon, two strangers came in through the big door together, and he saw the thing he would write down first.

They were both fighters; he could tell by the way they stood. One wore the stitched shoulders of an academy training coat and boots with thin soles cut for sprung wooden floors. The other wore a patched jacket and the kind of boots you could walk to Fenrow in. They stopped inside the door side by side, and each of them looked, first, at one thing.

The guild boy looked at the slate, at the names, at how big the chalk was and whose it was.

The circuit girl looked at Vell's table, to see whether Vell was at it, and what she was writing.

It took less than a breath. Neither of them knew they had done it. And there it was, the whole difference between the two worlds he lived between, in one turn of two heads: one of them walking in to see who could be beaten, and the other walking in to see who was keeping the account.

He wrote it all down that evening at the crate desk, in the grey book and not the slim one, because none of it was about him. The fixed points on the pine board, and the strings that hung from them, and the small pencilled number for how far out a rung hung. The red rules and *t.f.* The rule, and the three stopped bouts, and the quiet body. Dace's rings and dots and stitches, kept in chalk because chalk wipes. Stedd's *fair*. The two heads turning at the door.

Then, under it all, he wrote something that was not a fact, and boxed it, so that he would know later that it was only what he thought.

*Nobody made them build it honest. People the registry wouldn't let in built this out of nothing but a book and a slate, and they could have built it to sell. Every day somebody in that building chooses not to, and that's all that holds it up. The record is only as straight as the hand that keeps it.*

He looked at it for a while, and then put a small star beside the box, the first star in the new book, and sat with the lamp until the oil began to smell.

# Chapter 3 — Two Coats

There was a page near the middle of the grey book that had no fighters on it at all.

He had started it in the old Log, in his first autumn, after a man in a grey coat with a pin in his collar had walked the market one afternoon from end to end asking about lodgers without papers. When the old Log filled, the page had come across into the new book with everything else that was still alive, and it had grown. At the top, in capitals, it said *OFFICIALS*. Under that it was a list, kept in the same three columns as everything else, of what he had seen of the Compact in Ardenmere with his own eyes, and what he had only been told, with a small *h* beside each of those.

There was the pin man, and the dark coat off the west cart who had asked for him by his whole name. There was Coss, who had sat across a pie from him and told him about a daughter. There was the woman Ilsev, at a table, with a little black book with a strap. And under them, as the months went on, there were the sweeps.

They had come through the district a few times a season when he first arrived, and nobody had paid them much mind. Then, half a year ago, they had begun to come more often, and nobody in the district could say why, and everybody in the district had an opinion. He had written down the dates. Some months there were three. One month there had been five. He had looked at the column of dates for a long time one evening and then made himself stop looking, because there was a thing he wanted to write beside it and he had no evidence for it at all, and a thing with no evidence did not go on the page, however loud it was in his head.

What did go on the page was how they moved. *Always two. One of them writes. Better boots on the one who doesn't. Mid-morning, never early, never late.* He had seen four sweeps whole, from start to finish, and parts of several more, and each one had walked the district as if it were following a line painted on the cobbles that only the officers could see.

It was a quiet morning when the fifth came. He and Lira were in their alcove at the north end of the Ironyard, the one with the straw post in its corner that she had brought down from the Cinder House on a borrowed handcart, with its grey canvas and its chalk columns and Red Cap's fist still printed in the straw at the height of a small boy's shoulder. They were doing slow work, because of the thigh. She would come at him at half speed with the staff, and he would move his feet, and she would tell him which of his feet had been wrong, which was usually both.

Red Cap came in at the big door at a run, saw them, and stopped running, which was how Cael knew before he said anything.

He came across the floor at a walk. He was very bad at walking when he wanted to run; he did it with his arms stiff, like a boy carrying two full buckets. At the alcove he leaned on the post and said, to nobody, in his ordinary voice, "Two coats at the river gate."

"How long?" said Lira.

"Just now. Coming up the tannery lane." He was already turning to go, because he had other people to tell. "Pie boy's gone for Dace."

Lira looked at Cael. She did not say anything. She did not need to; they had talked about this, in the abstract, a dozen times, in the evenings with the door open between their rooms, and she had always said the same thing, which was that the worst thing he could do on a sweep morning was look like a person who had something to do on a sweep morning.

"I'm going to watch," he said.

"From where?"

"From where they'd expect a boy to be." He picked up his coat from the hook. "The market. Then home."

She held his eyes a moment, measuring. Then she nodded, and put the staff on her shoulder, and turned toward the empty floor of the alcove. "I'll be here, making a noise," she said. "Dace likes a noise on sweep days. It sounds like nothing's wrong."

---

He went down to the market by the dyers' steps, at a stroll, and sat down on the bottom stair beside the fruit woman's baskets with a bag of dried pears he had not needed, and waited.

The fruit woman did not look at him. She said, to the far end of the row, "You're early for your pears."

"I'm late for my pears. I bought them yesterday."

"Then you're sitting here eating yesterday's pears on my step." She sniffed. "Suit yourself. You're no worse than a cat."

The market had not changed, and it had changed completely. The stalls were all open; nobody had pulled a shutter or a cloth. That was the first rule of a sweep morning, the one every stallholder in the east lanes knew in their bones and would never have said aloud: you did not close, because closing was a thing the procedure could write down. But the market had gone quiet in a way he could hear. Prices were still being called, but nobody was calling them very loud. The bearded fish cousin, who on an ordinary morning would talk a widow into three fish she did not want, was selling one fish at a time, for the exact coin, and putting it straight into the hand without the usual argument. At the paper stall the man with ink on his cuffs had taken the account book off his counter and put it under the counter. The baker had stopped giving anyone credit, even the fighters she gave credit to every week of their lives, and the fighters had stopped asking.

Nothing was hidden. Everything was simply small.

And the children had gone.

He noticed it the way you notice a clock has stopped: not at the moment, but a while after, when the silence has been going on long enough to have a shape. On an ordinary morning there were children everywhere in the market, under the stalls and on the steps and darting between legs with messages and stolen apricots. Now there were none. The lane at the top of the row, which he could see from the step, was as empty as a church on a Monday.

Then the two coats came round the corner by the well, and he saw why. Half a street behind them, peering out from the mouth of an alley they had just passed, were three of the smallest boys from the east lanes, who had vanished in front of the officers and reappeared, as if by magic, the moment they had gone by.

The officers walked side by side, at the same steady pace, as if they were measuring the street. Both wore the long grey coat with the collar pin, a little silver mark that caught the light. The one on the left was younger and thinner and carried a flat leather case in his left hand and a pencil in his right. The one on the right was older and walked half a pace slower and carried nothing at all, and his boots were better. Cael looked at the boots first, because the boots told you which of two officials you had to watch; the one with good boots did not take notes, because somebody took them for him.

They came down the row. The left-hand one looked at each stall as they passed it, and sometimes wrote, and once asked the bearded cousin a question Cael could not hear, and the bearded cousin answered it with his whole face, shaking his head and pointing back at the river as if it were the river's fault. The right-hand one looked at nothing in particular, and therefore at everything.

When they reached the bottom of the steps, the right-hand one's eyes passed over the fruit woman, and her baskets, and the boy beside her eating pears.

They did not stop. Why would they? A boy with a limp, on a market step, on a weekday, eating fruit. Cael let the look pass over him as he would let a breeze go over him, and went on chewing, and counted his breath while the look was on him, and it was four breaths, which was not long. He was surprised to find his hands were steady.

"Are they gone?" said the fruit woman, without moving her eyes from the far end of the row.

"Going. Toward the boards."

"Then pass me the pears," she said, "since you're not eating them."

He passed her the pears, and she ate one, and he got up and went the other way, by the back lane behind the paper stall, at the same stroll he had come down at.

---

He knew where they were going next because they always went there next.

That was the strangest thing on the officials page, and the most useful. Every sweep he had ever seen had walked the same road: in at the river gate, up the tannery lane, down the market row, along to the three notice boards at the triangle where they read every card and every notice and wrote some of them down, and then across to the boarding-house row behind the Ironyard, where most of the district's fighters lived, and then out by the north gate. The road never changed. It did not change when the north gate was half blocked by a brewer's dray, or when the market row was flooded with a burst gutter; the officers simply walked through the dray's horses and the flood. The road had been written down somewhere, by someone in a room a long way from Ardenmere, and the people who walked it did not know there were four better streets.

So he went home by the back lanes and got there first, and went up the narrow stairs to the two back rooms, and did not go to the window at the back. He went to the small window on the landing at the front, which looked down the length of the row from the end. He had found it in his first week in the house and had sat there with his book a hundred evenings since, so that if anybody looked up from the row they would see what they always saw, which was a boy on a landing with a book.

The heavyset man was on his front step, as he always was. He had his arms folded on his chest and his hat pushed back. He was watching the end of the row with exactly the face he used for weather.

The coats came round the corner a quarter of an hour later.

They worked down the row house by house. At each door they stopped, and knocked, or did not need to because somebody was already on the step, and the young one opened his case and asked a question, and wrote. At two doors they went in for a little while and came out again. At the house next but one to Cael's, a woman came out with a baby on her hip and talked very fast and showed them a paper, and they read it and gave it back and moved on.

Then they were at his step, and the heavyset man unfolded his arms.

Cael could not hear what was said. He did not try to; he kept his eyes on the page in front of him and let the edges of his sight do the work, as he did at the rope when he wanted to watch a fighter without the fighter knowing it. He had not hidden. That was the thing he kept turning over. If he had been anywhere else, if he had run to the cooper's roof or the river or the far end of the district, there would have been an empty room behind a name in a book, and an empty room was a question. When he came down the hill to the Ironyard, an hour after the coats had gone out through the north gate, the slate wall by the big door was black from end to end.

"I didn't see anything."

"They didn't come in here."

"They never have. Not in twelve years." Dace put the chalk in his pocket, and dusted his hands, and for a moment, looking at the wall, he looked older and more tired than Cael had ever seen him. "That's six months of this, now. Four last month. They didn't use to come four times in a year." He shook his head. "I don't know what they want. I don't think they know. I think somebody in a room somewhere has written *more often* on a paper, and two men in grey coats are walking it."

"How do you hold it?" said Cael. "All of it. I copied the whole wall two days ago, sitting down, with a pencil, and I still didn't have the mangle."

Dace looked at him sidelong, as if deciding whether the question was worth the breath, and seemed to decide that it was.

"Faces," he said. "I can't keep a list in my head any better than you can. Nobody can. But I can keep a room." He nodded at the benches, empty now in the grey light from the high windows. "The Shield boy told me about his mother's mangle sitting on the third bench from the barrel, with his elbows on his knees, looking at the floor because he was ashamed of it. Cael looked at the empty benches and tried to see it, and could not. He went off toward Vell's table. Cael stood a while longer in front of the wall, with its names restored and its rings and dots and stitches, and the smear near the bottom where a hand had leaned, which Dace had not bothered to rub out.

*Chalk wipes,* he thought. Last week it had sounded like a joke.

He wrote it up that evening, on the officials page, with Lira sitting on the end of his bed eating bread and reading over his shoulder, which she was allowed to do with this page because there was nothing on it that was his.

*Fifth sweep watched whole. Two coats, left one young, writes, case in left hand, pencil in right. Right one older, better boots, carries nothing, looks at nothing, sees everything. Mid-morning. River gate, tannery lane, market, triangle, the row, north gate. Same road as every other time. The road doesn't bend for drays or water.*

*The district: nothing shut. Everything small. Exact coin, ledgers under counters, no credit. Children: none on the road in front of them, the whole way; three behind, out of an alley the moment the coats had passed. Board at the Ironyard wiped before they reached the triangle, rebuilt from Dace's head inside an hour. One ring he had and I didn't (a mangle, h).*

*The row: the lodgers' book on the step. Read one page, top to bottom, same speed. Didn't stop at mine. Nothing written that I saw.*

"You were on the landing," said Lira. "With a book."

"With a book."

"I'd have been on the cooper's roof." She chewed. "Or under the bed."

"I nearly went to the roof." He turned the pencil over. "Then I thought about what they'd have seen if they'd looked up at the window and there wasn't anybody in it. Or if they'd asked the man on the step where the two at the back were this morning, and he'd had to say he didn't know."

Lira stopped chewing.

"He looked in," she said. "The young one. On the way past the big door. I was making a noise on the post the way Dace likes, and he stopped in the door and looked at me for about as long as it takes to say *good morning*, and then he went on." She put the bread down. "He looked at me like I was weather. Like I was a girl hitting a sack, which I was." She was quiet a moment. "I'm in their book too, you know. Same as you. *Wind, Copper*, and under it whatever Fenmark wrote when they sent me home. I've never seen it. I don't want to." She picked the bread up again and did not eat it. "When I first came down from the academy I couldn't walk past a collar pin without my hands going cold. For a whole winter. I used to cross the street."

"You didn't cross the street this morning."

"No," said Lira, and looked surprised, as if she had only just noticed. "No, I hit the post."

He wrote the last part slowly, because it was the part that mattered, and he wanted to get it right in case he needed it some morning when he was frightened.

*They don't find you by looking for you. They find you by looking where the paper says you are and seeing whether you're there. So be there. Be exactly where the paper says, doing exactly what a person would be doing, on the day they come to read the paper. Don't be hidden. Be findable, but only where they look, and only as much as they look for.*

He underlined *only where they look*.

"That's horrible," said Lira, after a while. "It's also right. That's the worst kind." She tore her bread in two and gave him half without looking at him. "Does it bother you, being in their book? Your name on that page with his pencil going down it?"

"Everybody's in their book," said Cael. "That's what the book's for." He looked at the line on the page about the pencil, which had gone down at the same speed all the way. "What I want to know is whether my line's in some other book as well. One I've never seen. And I can't find that out by looking out of a window."

She did not say anything. She sat on the end of the bed beside him in the lamplight, and ate her half of the bread, and after a while she leaned her shoulder against his, as she did when she had no answer and did not intend to pretend she had one.

# Chapter 4 — The Keepers

The man who wanted to argue with Vell sat on the bench nearest Dace's slate for most of an hour before he did it, and Cael, who had come in to watch the morning card's first bout, ended up watching him instead.

He was a Blade practitioner of perhaps twenty-five, Copper by his registry and Assessed-Copper by Vell's book, and he had lost the evening before to a circuit fighter with a third of his training and twice his patience. Cael had not seen the bout. He had heard about it four times on the way down the hill, and all four versions agreed on the one thing that mattered, which was that it had been clean. The man had been put on the stone in the third exchange by a sweep he should have seen coming, and he had got up, and shaken hands, and gone home.

And now he was back, in a good coat with academy stitching across the shoulders, with a folded paper in his hand.

Cael knew him a little, as you know anybody you have watched for a year without ever speaking to. He had come down from a guild-registered house on the river two seasons ago, and he paid that house a fee every quarter for the right to say he trained there, and the house paid him back in a coat with stitched shoulders and a line on his papers and not much else. It did not find him bouts. Guild houses never did; they had more members than matches, and the matches went to the members whose fathers had paid the most fees. So he came to the Ironyard, like a dozen others in academy coats, because Dace would put him on a card twice a month and Vell would pay him for losing as well as winning. Cael had watched him at the slate the week before, trying to talk Dace into a better slot, and had watched him in the eating house afterward paying for one bowl of soup with exact coin and then counting what was left in his purse under the edge of the table. It was a thin purse for a man with such good shoulders on his coat.

He did not look at the paper. He held it, and turned it over, and held it again, as a man holds a ticket for a coach he is not sure he wants to catch. His lips moved. Every so often he looked along the benches toward the door, and Cael followed the look the third time it went, and found what it was for: a younger man in the same academy coat, sitting near the back with his arms folded, who had come in with him and was waiting for something to happen.

*He's got a witness of his own,* Cael wrote, in the margin of the morning's page. *Somebody from his facility. He's going to do this in front of him, not in spite of him. Prediction: he'll wait until the benches are full, so that it's a public thing. He'll go to Vell, not Dace. He'll open with the biggest word he's got.*

He put the pencil down and waited to be told whether he was right.

The benches filled toward the hour, as they did. By the time Vell came out of the back room with the day's book and her tea there were seventy or eighty people in the near rows, and fighters stretching along the walls, and the bookmakers in their corner chalking up the first bout's odds. Vell sat down. She opened the book. She uncapped her ink.

The man stood up, put the paper in his inside pocket, and crossed the floor.

He did it well. He had clearly practised the walk: shoulders back, chin level, not fast, not slow. He stopped in front of Vell's table at exactly the distance a man stops at who wishes to be overheard.

"Last night's result," he said, "was corrupted."

*Corrupted.* It was the big word, and it had come first. Cael inked a small tick in the margin, and another under it, and noticed that he was pleased with himself, and did not much like that, and noted it.

---

Vell did not look up.

She finished blotting the line she had been writing, which was the date and the card, and laid the blotter down square to the book's edge. Only then did she raise her eyes, and when she did they were neither cold nor kind. They were simply open, the way a shop is open, ready for whatever was going to be brought in.

"By whom?"

"There was coaching from the benches. A man was calling corrections to my opponent all through the second and third exchanges. Feet, guard, when to step. I could hear him."

"Where was he standing?"

"By the west post. Grey beard. Red neckcloth."

Cael watched Vell know who it was. She knew from *west post*; *red neckcloth* only confirmed it. And then, knowing already, she did the thing he had seen her do at the Cinder House and had never been close enough to understand. She turned back through the book, unhurried, to a page some years old, and ran her finger down it until she came to a line, and turned the book a quarter round on the table so that the people on the nearest bench could see that there was a line there, though they could not read it.

"Orvet," she said. "Runs a gym in the tannery lanes. Shouts at fighters. It's in my book, four years back, on a complaint very like yours, and I ruled on it then. He shouts at his own people, and he shouts at other people's, and he shouts at the bookmakers when they get the odds wrong. Nobody pays him to. It's a habit, not a service, and it isn't against anything I've ever written down." She did not turn the book back. "You're saying he coached your opponent."

"I'm saying he did."

"Hm." Vell looked past him, along the near bench, and found a face. "Dessa."

It was Dessa, the Stone fighter Cael had met twice in his first months and beaten twice, both times by less than the benches thought, who sat on the near bench most mornings with her hair tied tight and said less than anybody in the building. She looked up from the strap she was mending.

"You were on the west bench last night, second bout."

"I was."

"One question, and loud enough for the room. Who was Orvet shouting at?"

Dessa thought about it honestly, which with her always took a moment, and then she said, in a voice that carried to the bookmakers' corner, "Both of them. He told the girl to keep her weight back. Then he told this one to stop dropping his elbow, which was good advice, and he didn't take it."

Somebody near the back laughed, and then several people did, and the man in the academy coat went a slow dark red from the collar up.

Vell set her pen down.

Cael had learned what that meant. It was the moment at which she stopped being patient and became merely clear. "You lost to a fighter who'd prepared better than you," she said, "under terms you agreed before the first *begin*, in front of a full bench, with a man in a red neckcloth shouting advice at both of you, and you didn't take yours. That's what happened. That's the line I wrote. It stands."

The man drew a breath. Cael saw his hand go to his coat, to the pocket where the paper was, and stop short of it.

"My facility is guild-registered," he said. "I can make this a formal complaint. To the Compact. They'd have an inspector down here in a month."

The benches went still. It was not a loud stillness. It was the stillness of seventy people who have watched a man pick up a knife at a dinner table and are waiting to see what he means to cut.

Vell looked at him. She did not look frightened, or angry, or even very interested. She looked as she looked when a fighter tried a move she had seen a hundred times before, and she said, without raising her voice at all:

"Do that, and you won't fight in any circuit from here to the coast. The keepers talk."

She let it lie a moment, and then she went on, more gently, which was worse.

"Not because I'll tell them to. Because they'll ask. Every keeper between here and the sea will want to know why a Blade from a guild house brought a Compact inspector into a ledger over a lost bout. And when they've heard, they'll write you *unrated*, and they'll never write you anything else, and they'll be polite about it." She picked her pen back up. "Go home. Come back when you can lose a line in my book and still look at it. Most people manage it, in the end. It's cheaper."

He stood there. He looked, once, toward the younger man in the academy coat at the back, and the younger man was looking at the floor.

Then he went, without the paper ever leaving his pocket.

Vell dipped her pen and wrote the first bout's names under the date. Cael looked at the water clock on the wall behind Dace's slate. It had taken a little under four minutes.

He did not go to her table until the card was done and the benches were emptying, because he had learned that Vell answered questions best when the room had stopped watching her answer them.

"You're writing down how I do it," she said, before he could open his mouth. She did not look up from the day's last line. "I can see your pencil from here. You've written *one* and *two* and *three* and a ring round *four minutes*."

"You do it the same every time."

"Near enough. Thirty years is a lot of practice at a small number of arguments." She blotted. "There aren't many ways for a man to tell you he lost unfairly. When you've heard them all, you stop needing to think. You only need to do it in front of people, so they can see it's the book deciding and not me."

He had written it as four steps, on the page, with a box round them. First, strip the claim down to a question about a fact, so that it could not stand up on its adjectives. Second, go to the record, with your hands, where people can see you do it, even when you know the answer, because the going is what makes it the record's answer and not yours. Third, if it is needed, call one witness by name and ask one question that anybody can hear. Fourth, put the pen down and say it plainly, once, and pick the pen up again.

He showed it to her. She read it, and pushed it back.

"You've left out the fifth," she said.

"What's the fifth?"

"Don't enjoy it." She capped her ink. "The day you enjoy it, you start looking for it."

Then she sat back in her chair with her hands folded on the closed book and looked not at him but at the big door, where the late light was coming in long across the stone, and said, in a different voice, as though it had nothing to do with anything:

"Dace says there were two grey coats on the row yesterday."

"There were."

"That's five this season, by my count. I keep it in the back of the book now, in pencil, so I can rub it out if I ever feel foolish." She did not look at him. "When I first sat at a table in this district, they'd come through twice a year to read the boards and buy a pie. I've had more sweeps through these streets in the last six months than in ten years before them, Cael. I'm not a woman who frightens. But something's got them counting, and when people start counting, they generally know what they're hoping to find."

He kept his face where it was. He had a fair idea what had got them counting. It was on the officials page in a column of dates with nothing written beside it, because there was no evidence for the thing he wanted to write, only the shape of it, and the shape was a boy who had come in through the west gate a year ago with a word on him that nobody had survived before.

"That's interesting," he said, which was true, and was about a tenth of what was true.

Vell looked at him then. It was a long look, longer than the answer deserved. He watched her see that there was more behind it, and watched her decide, as she generally did with him, not to go in after it.

"Keep your address straight in the lodgers' book," she said. "Keep your name spelt the way the registry spells it. Pay your rent on the day. If anybody wants a reason to trouble you, make them work for it." She stood, and put the book under her arm. "I'd hate to have to write *unrated* beside you, after all this."

"You won't have to."

"I know. That's why I've only told you once."

He wrote, that evening, under the box with the four steps and the fifth that Vell had given him, a line about the keepers. It had struck him, watching the guild man's face, that Vell had no seal and no law and no clerk behind her, and that it did not matter, because she had something that worked the same way and was harder to argue with. There was a keeper at Fenrow and a keeper at the salt end and keepers in towns he had never heard of, every one of them with a book, and every one of those books was, in a way, open to all the others. Nobody had written it down as a rule. Nobody needed to. A man could quarrel with one table and find, the next month, that a hundred tables had heard. *It isn't a law,* he wrote. *It's a circuit of its own, made of keepers instead of fighters, and it runs on the same thing the floor does. She did not want anything else; she only wanted to know whether a thing her husband had told her across forty years of suppers had been a story or a fact. Vell had told her to come back in a week.

"There was a keeper with one arm," said Vell, holding the lamp's hood while Cael lit it. "Before my keeper. I've heard of him. If her man fought for him, he's in one of these."

It was. "Different how?"

She cooked the way she fought, all at once and with total commitment, in a single iron pan she had bought from a widow and would not let anyone else wash. She did not cook often. When she did, she cooked enough for four, and they ate it for two days, and on the second day she always said it was better than on the first, and it always was.

"Your keeper sent a guild boy home with the whole coastline round his neck," she said, without turning round from the stove. "The baker's girl says Vell stood up to do it."

"She didn't stand up."

"I knew she didn't. She never has to. That's what makes it frightening." She stirred. "Renn's telling everybody about the elbow. He wasn't even there. He's very pleased with the elbow."

He sat down at the end of the table in the back kitchen with the grey book, and did not open it, and watched her cook. Then, because it had been sitting in him all evening, under the dispute and the cupboard and the sweep, the way a stone sits in a boot, he said it.

"I want to run the landing."

Lira did not stop stirring, but her shoulders changed. He saw her hear it.

"Properly?"

"Properly. Full pace. You don't pull off it. You hit the lock every time I land. Every time, as hard as you'd hit somebody who meant it." He turned the closed book on the table. "I've been testing everything else round it for a month, and it's been sitting there in the middle of the page with nothing beside it but your sentence. Somebody's going to wait for it soon. She took the pan off the heat and turned round and leaned against the stove with the spoon in her hand.

"How many?"

"Ten. That's what I can afford in a morning, near enough. It'll cost me most of a day after."

"It'll cost you most of two." She looked at him a long moment, and he watched her do the arithmetic, the same arithmetic he had done, of what ten bursts did to a hip and a chest and a breath, and then add something to it that was hers and that he could not see. "All right. Tomorrow. Early, before Renn comes in to tell everybody about the elbow again."

"Thank you."

"I haven't done anything yet." She pointed the spoon at him. "And I've got a condition."

"Go on."

"I count." She turned back to the pan. "Every landing, I count the lock with my own breath, out loud. You can't count it from inside. You think you can. You can't; you're too busy being a post." She put the pan back on the heat. "And if I say stop, we stop, and you don't argue with me till you've eaten something."

"That's two conditions."

"Then I've got two," said Lira. "And a question." She put a plate in front of him and stood over him with the spoon, which made the question feel like a third condition. "Where do I hit you?"

"Wherever you'd hit somebody you meant to hit."

"That's the collarbone, mostly. Or the ribs, on the side you've got the old bruise." She considered him. "I won't do the hip. You'll need the hip."

"Hit what you'd hit. If I only get hit where it's polite, I'll learn what's polite."

She looked at him for a while with the spoon in her hand. Somewhere in the front of the house the heavyset man said something to somebody about onions, loudly, in the voice of a man who has opened a window and wants it known.

"All right," said Lira. "The collarbone, mostly. You'll be sorry."

"I'll be sorry and I'll know something."

# Chapter 5 — Ten

The Ironyard before the sweepers came was a different building from the one he fought in.

At night, with the fourteen lamps lit and three hundred people breathing in it, it was close and warm and loud, and the low ceiling seemed to sit on everybody's head. At first light it was cold, and the lamps were dark on their chains, and the only light came grey through the high north windows and lay on the stone in long pale bars. The stone itself looked older in that light. You could see the marks of whatever had stood on it when it was a foundry floor, the round worn places and the long scars, before anybody had thought of fighting on it at all.

Lira was there before him. She had chalked a cross on the floor of the alcove, a stride out from the straw post, and she was standing beside it with her staff across her shoulders and her hands hooked over it, like a farmer with a yoke.

"You land there," she said. "Every time. I want to be able to tell the difference between you landing somewhere new and you landing badly. I'll come from wherever I like."

"That's fair."

"I didn't ask if it was fair." She looked at his thigh, which he had wrapped, and then at his face, which he had not. "Ten, you said. Not eleven because you've had a good idea on the ninth."

"Ten."

"And I count."

"You count."

She took the staff off her shoulders and turned it once in her hands, and went back three paces, and set herself. Not in her ordinary stance. In the one she used for the girls from the wall when she wanted to show them what it looked like to mean something: lower, heavier, the staff held short. It was the stance of a fighter who intends to hit you hard and has decided not to feel sorry about it until afterward. He had seen it from the rope a hundred times. He had very seldom seen it from in front.

"Go when you like," said Lira.

He went from the far side of the alcove, at a run, the way he would come off a feint in a bout, and dropped his left hip and held his breath and let the floor go short, and was on the chalk cross, and locked.

She was already moving. "That's what we're here for."

"I know. I could hear you."

"Could you see it coming?"

"Three," she said. "Go on."

The third time he tried landing soft.

It was the first of his ideas, and he had liked it best in the dark the night before, lying on his back with his hip aching. If the lock came from the landing, perhaps it came from landing *hard*: the weight thumping back into the heels all at once, as he always felt it, like a sack off a cart. If he landed with his knees already loose, already bent, ready to give, perhaps the half-breath would have nowhere to catch.

He came off the far wall and dropped and went and held his breath and came down on the chalk with his knees soft as a cat's.

And locked, exactly as hard, with his knees bent.

It was worse, if anything. A man locked upright could at least be hit standing. A man locked with his knees bent was a man in a chair with no chair under him. Lira's staff came across at his shoulder and he watched it come from a crouch and could do nothing about it, and it knocked him half round, and he heard her say "*two-and*" from what seemed a long way off, and then he was free and sitting down on the stone.

"Three," said Lira. "Same."

He got up. "Four. Shorter."

That was the second idea. If the lock was the end of the burst, then a shorter burst ended sooner, and if it ended sooner, perhaps he would be through the lock and out of it before the staff could finish its trip. He took the run again and dropped and went, and this time he cut the burst off early, threw his weight down before the floor had finished going short, so that he landed a long pace in front of the chalk instead of on it.

Lira's staff went through the air behind him, where the chalk was, and touched nothing.

He looked. He had cut the burst short, and so he had not gone as far, and so he was standing a long pace closer to her than the chalk was. Close enough that she could have reached him without stepping at all if she had known he would be there.

"Four," he said. "Five. Breath."

Breath was the third idea, and the one he had least hope for and most curiosity about. The burst would not go on a moving breath; he held it in, every time, half a lungful. Perhaps the lock was only the breath catching up, and if he let it out as he landed, instead of after, he would let the lock out with it.

He landed and let his breath go in a long rush, and the lock did not care. On the second *and* he folded over and pulled in air like a man coming up out of a pond.

# Chapter 6 — Six Fields

On the third evening after the trials it rained, and the card at the Ironyard was put off because the roof over the north benches leaked onto the bookmakers, and Cael sat at the crate desk with both books open in front of him and found he could not tell where one stopped and the other began.

He had been meaning to look at it for weeks. He had kept the slim book apart from the grey one since the day he bought it, on purpose, so that what he saw of other people and what he knew of himself would not get mixed up in the same columns. But over the weeks the edges had blurred anyway. He would be writing up a bout in the grey book, Ulric's heel or Dessa's count, and a line about the burst would come into his head, and he would write it in the margin where the pencil already was, meaning to copy it across. Sometimes he did. Often he did not. And the other way about: in the slim book, under the Wind entry, there was a line about how Lira's partner from the docks dropped his guard on the third swing of a combination, which had nothing to do with anything in that book and had got in because the slim book was the one that happened to be open.

The trials had made it worse. He had written them up on the evening of the second day, lying on his bed with the grey book on his chest because it was the one within reach, and so now the most important thing he had learned about himself all year, that the lock was a price and not a fault, lived on page forty-one of a book that was supposed to be about other people, between a sweep and a scheduling board.

He found four other lines like it, once he started looking. A note about the hollow behind his breastbone sat at the bottom of a page about Ulric's heel. A line about the giving face of the Pressure, which he had used against a Copper formal in a hot week and which had taken the man's guard down like a door off its hinges, was jammed into the margin of a page about Dace's rings. One of Lira's sentences about the lock was in both books, in slightly different words, and he could not now remember which he had written first, or which was nearer to what she had actually said. That last one bothered him most. He had told himself, when he bought the slim book, that it would never hold a word it had not earned. And here was a sentence of Lira's living in two places in two shapes, so that if he ever needed to stand on it he would not know which of them would hold his weight.

He sat and looked at it while the rain came down the window in sheets.

There was a reason it mattered, beyond tidiness, and he made himself say it to himself plainly, because he had been not saying it for weeks. The two books needed two different kinds of honesty. The grey book could afford to be wrong. If he wrote down a tell that was not a tell, the next fight would correct him, usually painfully, and he would cross it out and write the correction beside it, and the book would be the better for having been wrong. Somebody across a rope would always check it for him.

Nobody would ever check the other book. There was no opponent for it. It was the only record anywhere in Valdris of what he was, so far as he could tell what he was, and if he wrote something in it that was not so, the mistake would not be corrected by anything; it would simply sit on the page looking like a fact until the day he trusted his weight to it. The grey book was a tool. The slim one had to be held to Vell's standard instead, the standard of the cupboard and the red rules, where an empty line was allowed and a false one was not.

He picked up the slim book and turned it over in his hands. *The fragment book*, he had been calling it, to himself, since he bought it. But *fragment* was the notice's word, not his. The notices had used it on him the way the registry had used *Shattered*, telling him what he was before he had found out. The book was not a list of pieces. It was about what he could do, and what each thing cost him, and how far he could trust it.

He wrote on the inside of the front cover, small: *The Power Log.*

He looked at that a while. It was not a beautiful name. But it was accurate, and tonight he wanted accurate more than he wanted beautiful.

---

Before he designed anything, he went to the shelf and took down the oldest book on it, the first notebook, the one he had carried out of Denvash, and turned to the back, and found the page where he had copied Joren's declaration.

He had copied it before he ever left home, because it was the only Path declaration he had ever been shown whole and he had wanted to keep its shape. *PATH DECLARATION — COPPER RANK 1*, it said at the head, and under that the ability's name in square brackets, and then one sentence that said exactly what Joren could do and exactly where it stopped. Everyone who had a Path got those. The Arbiter gave them, each time a rank came, and they were private, and people read their own declarations so often that they could recite them like their own names. They were a set of edges with the measurements written on.

And they went somewhere. That was the other thing about them, and the thing he had thought about most, in his first months, lying awake on his cot at Torvin's with the wheezing man whistling on the next one. A Copper practitioner got a declaration at Rank 1, and another when Rank 2 came, and so on up to ten, each one a little wider than the last, each one telling him exactly how far his fence had moved. At Rank 10 there was a door. You took a guild sponsor's letter to a registered Arbiter station and stood in front of it and were looked at, properly, and if the station agreed, you came out Iron, at Rank 1 again, with a new declaration and a new set of edges, one metal further up the hill. Everyone he had ever fought had that ladder inside them, whether they had climbed three rungs of it or nine. Lira had it. She had told him once where she stood on it, and then said that it did not matter, because without a letter nobody was going to let her through the door at the top.

Then he took down the brown Log as well, and opened it at the back, where he had copied his own notices letter for letter as they came, and laid the two books side by side.

His were not like that. They had a heading, and a name that was not a name, *[unnamed]*, and then a row of fields with a word in each. *Duration. Integration. Tier equivalent.* And the words in the fields mostly said that nobody knew. *Undetermined. Partial. Unknown.* There was no sentence at all. Nothing said what he could do, or where it stopped. Whatever wrote the notices did not tell him anything about his edges; it only filled in a form, and most of the form was blank.

Very well, then. He would fill in his own.

He took out the steel straightedge with the inches cut into it, which had cost him the best part of a loser's purse at the paper stall, and ruled the first clean page of the Power Log. He ruled it into three columns, as he ruled everything now: *Claim*, *Evidence*, *Ruling*. The middle column was the one that mattered most, because it was the one that kept him honest; and anything in it that he had not seen with his own eyes, but had been told, would have a small *h* beside it, for *heard*, as it had done in every book he had kept since his first month.

Then, down the side, he wrote the six things he wanted to know about every entry, in an order he would never change, so that half a year on he could open the book anywhere and know at once where to look.

*What it is.* As near as he could say.

*Source.* Where it came from, and when, and how much of that he actually knew.

*Conditions.* Which days it answered, and which days it sulked.

*Deployment range.* What it had actually been seen to do, measured, with no guessing in it at all.

*Costs.* Everything it charged him, item by item, because a thing written down without its price was half written.

*Open questions.* Last, with the most room. He gave it half a page on its own, ruled and empty, because the honest proportion between what he knew and what he did not was still about one to three, and he wanted the page to look like that when he opened it, so that he would never be tempted to think otherwise.

At the top of each entry, before any of it, he would copy the notice's own fields exactly, in its own words. He did not trust the notices to tell him much. But they were the only other witness he had, and a witness was a witness.

---

He began with the Wind, because it was the one he knew best and he wanted to make his mistakes on the easier one.

*WIND-ADJACENT.*

*From the notice: Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial. Tier equivalent: unknown.*

*WHAT IT IS. Claim: a burst out of the way of a committed strike, sideways or slantwise, faster than my legs can carry me. Evidence: every morning on the straw since my ribs knit; eleven Copper formals; Ulric, fifth exchange. Ruling: so.*

*SOURCE. Claim: Lira. Evidence: three months of watching her step every morning in the grey half before it ever came. The notice said Wind, and hers was the only Wind I had watched. She says it doesn't feel like anything was taken from her (h). Ruling: so, as far as it goes. How it came, I don't know, and I won't write a guess on this page.*

*CONDITIONS. Claim: the worse the moment, the cleaner it comes. Asked for on a quiet morning, it comes late and clumsy, like a word on the tip of the tongue. Under a blow it is already done before I know I wanted it. Evidence: the testing; Darrow's third exchange, when it came unasked in front of three hundred people. Ruling: so. Untested: whether that can be trained the other way.*

*DEPLOYMENT RANGE. Claim: half my body's width, to the left, the front-left or the back-left, in the time it takes a committed strike to fall. Evidence: Lira's stick on the straw every morning; a hand's width in the spring, half a body now. Ruling: half a body. It may still be growing.*

He stopped there and looked at *to the left* for some time. He had written it without thinking, because it was true, and only now, seeing it in ink, did it look odd. Not once, in all the mornings, had it taken him right. He did not know why. He put a small mark beside it and went on.

*COSTS.*

*(1) Breath. Claim: half a breath held for every burst; it won't go on a moving breath. Two close together and the next exchange is short of air, and short air makes poor choices. Evidence: every burst. Ruling: so.*

*(2) The lock. Claim: half a breath at every landing, locked, no turning, no leaning, no shifting a grain of weight. Two of Lira's* ands*. Evidence: ten trials, nine bursts in one hour. Tried to shorten it five ways: landing soft, bursting short, breathing out, leaning, a second burst. None shortened it. The short burst only moved it nearer to her. Ruling: it does not shorten. It is not a fault. It is a price.*

*And a price buys something. In the lock there's no choice to make, so nothing can drag my eyes off what's coming. Half a breath of only looking. One committed strike, read whole, start to end, with the answer loaded before the lock lets go. Trial ten: an exchange. Rule: the burst stays in my hip if anybody close enough to hit me has a hit I haven't priced.*

*(3) The hip. Claim: the left, from the crest of the bone to the inside of the knee; one burst is nothing; three in an evening and I limp in the morning; it adds up. Evidence: every week since the spring. The fruit woman can tell which day it was (h, and correct). Ruling: so.*

*(4) Ceiling. Claim: three in one exchange before it starts coming late. Evidence: twice in the testing, never in a bout. Ruling: probable. Not pushed.*

*(5) Depletion. Claim: after nine in an hour, a hollow behind the breastbone like a cold stone made of nothing, and the burst gone, not refused, gone, for most of two days. Evidence: the trials, once. Ruling: so, once. Don't do it twice to find out.*

*OPEN QUESTIONS. Can three become four with work, or is three the wall? What* undetermined *means in the notice, and whether it will ever say anything else. Whether the half body will grow. Lira says it has a side it doesn't like (h). I haven't found one yet. I want to.*

He read it back from the top, slowly, with his finger under each line, the way Hesk checked a drawing before he cut metal. It held. There was nothing on the page he could not point to a morning for. The open questions took up more room than the deployment range, which was exactly right.

He looked longest, when he had finished, at the line he had not written himself: the notice's own words at the head of the page. *Duration: undetermined.* He had copied them as he would copy a witness's answer, letter for letter, and they sat above his careful fields like a stranger's signature at the top of his own letter. He did not know what *duration* meant, for a thing that lasted only as long as a strike took to fall. He did not know whether *undetermined* meant that nobody knew yet, or that it had not yet been decided, or something else that a form would say and a person would not. He had never been able to ask. Whatever filled in the notices did not answer questions; it only filled in forms. So he left the words where they were, exactly as they had come, and put a small ring round *undetermined*, which was what he did in the grey book beside any word an opponent had used that he meant to come back to.

---

The Pressure was harder, and he had known it would be.

It was harder because it had two faces and he had only learned the second one by accident, and because he had less time with it altogether, and because the one time it had mattered most it had not been his to call at all.

He sat back from the desk for a while and listened to the rain, and put it in order in his head first.

The first face was the one he had found at Torvin's trough in the spring, night after night in the yard by the pump, with the water going flat under his hand. It was the taking face. When he called it, a weight came into his forearm and shoulder that was not mass, a heaviness like bones gone full of water, and a blow that landed on the weighted arm did not stay where it landed but spread, and went down through him, and into the floor. He had tested it since on Lira's staff at full swing, on the forearm, on purpose, a dozen mornings. It was slow, though. It needed most of a breath to gather, so he had to see the strike coming a long way off. And it showed: when it came, he settled lower and his feet went wider, and anyone watching could see him decide to be hit.

The second face he had found in a sparring bout with Lira's dock partner, the Bronze washout, a few weeks after his ribs had knit. He had thrown his short straight strike on the beat of the man's own rhythm, the way he had been taught, and something in his middle had gone *with* it, out along his arm, and the strike had landed twice as heavy as it had any right to. The dock man had sat down on the stone and looked up at him in honest surprise. That was the giving face. It only worked on the beat. He had found that out, too, twice, by trying it off the beat, and both times the weight had gone the wrong way, back up his own arm, and turned his left wrist over as if it had been stepped on. The wrist still ached when the weather turned.

And then there was the third thing, which was not a face at all.

He wrote.

*PRESSURE-ADJACENT.*

*From the notice: Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial. Tier equivalent: unknown.*

*WHAT IT IS. Claim: weight without mass. Two faces.*

*Taking: gathered into one forearm and shoulder, or one side; a strike that should stagger me spreads and goes down into the floor, and what's left is a strike I can stand. Evidence: the trough (the water goes flat); Lira's staff, a dozen mornings, full swing, forearm. Ruling: so.*

*Giving: ridden out along my own strike, on the beat of the other fighter's rhythm, it lands far heavier than the strike. Evidence: the dock man, sitting down; four bouts since. Ruling: so, on the beat only.*

*SOURCE. Claim: Feryn. His hand on my shoulder in the fourth exchange, holding a beat longer than the throw needed. Evidence: the notice came that night. Ruling: so. One second of his against three months of Lira's. I don't know why the two are so different. No guess.*

*CONDITIONS. Claim: it has never once come by itself, except the once. I have to mean it every time. Taking needs most of a breath of warning. Giving needs the beat. Evidence: every use. Ruling: so.*

*DEPLOYMENT RANGE. Taking: one limb or one side; tested to Lira's full swing and no further. Giving: one strike at a time; about three in a sequence. Ruling: so, as far as tested. The top of taking is not tested. On purpose.*

*COSTS.*

*Taking. (1) Slow: most of a breath; no use against anything fast; a plan, not a reflex. (2) It shows: weight sinks, stance widens; it tells the room I've chosen to be hit, and every time I use it, somebody learns that. (3) Drains about what three bursts do. (4) Afterwards the arm I braced is sand, two exchanges' worth. Ruling: all so.*

*Giving. (1) Draws from the middle: a hollow under the ribs after each, deeper each time; about three before the hollow is too deep to draw from. (2) Off the beat it misfires back up my own arm: the left wrist, twice; still aches in the cold. Ruling: so. Never off the beat.*

*After practice of either: both arms like wet sand, most of an hour.*

*OPEN QUESTIONS. The top of taking: where the spreading gives out and the whole blow comes through. Not looked for. Why giving only works on the beat. Whether it will ever come without being asked, as the Wind does.*

*And: Darrow's fourth exchange. Both at once, unasked, the only time. The notice said* concurrent function: recorded. *At the trough, reaching for one makes me drop the other. Can it be called? Ruling: not yet. I don't know. I would like to know more than I would like to know anything else on this page, and that is a reason to be careful with it, not a reason to hurry.*

He read it back, and changed one word, and read it back again.

Then, because the brown Log had counted them and he did not want the count to stop just because the book had, he turned to the very back of the Power Log and wrote a single line there, on its own: *Instances carried from the old Log: eight. The step unasked, the last at Darrow's third; the eighth, Darrow's fourth, both.* He would count the unasked ones here from now on, apart from all the rest. A thing that came when it chose was a different kind of thing from a thing that came when he called it, and he wanted to be able to see the difference at a glance.

---

He was still sitting with the two entries open when Lira came in.

She came in late. He heard the street door, and the heavyset man say something, and her answer, and her feet on the stairs, two at a time as always; and he looked at the window and saw that it was full dark and had been for a while, and that the rain had stopped without his noticing. She had said *an hour*, when she went out. She had said it over her shoulder with the staff on her back, as she said it most evenings now. It had been more than two.

She stood in the door between the rooms with her hair wet and her face pink from the cold and looked at him, and at the desk, and at the two books with the lamp between them, and at the steel straightedge.

"You're doing the thing," said Lira.

"What thing?"

"Sitting in the lamp being pleased about ruling lines." She came and leaned on the end of the desk. "You've got a face you only do for paperwork. It's very peaceful. It's a bit like a cow."

"I've given it a name."

She turned the slim book round with one finger and read the inside of the cover. Her eyebrows went up.

"*The Power Log*."

"It's accurate."

"It's very grand. I'd have called it *the little book*." But she was already reading, and he watched her read, which he had not done often; she read the way she fought, leaning in, with her whole attention, and her lips moved very slightly on the words she liked. She read the Wind entry through, and stopped at the lock, and read that part twice, and put her finger on *two of Lira's* ands and left it there a moment without saying anything. Then she went on into the Pressure.

At the giving face she stopped.

"This line. *The dock man, sitting down.*" She tapped it. "He didn't sit down. He fell over. I was there. And he's a Bronze washout, not just a dock man; he'd be cross if he saw that."

"Is that evidence or opinion?"

"It's evidence. I watched." She took the pencil from his hand, before he could stop her, and wrote in the middle column, very small, beside the line: *fell over. (L., seen.)* Then she gave him back the pencil. "There. Now it's true."

He looked at what she had written. It was the first time anybody but him had put a mark in that book.

"You can't do that," he said.

"I just did." She sat down on the end of the bed with a sigh, as though her legs had been waiting all evening to be allowed, and bent to unlace her boots. "You put my name in it. You can't put my name in a book and tell me I can't write in it. That's not how names work."

He did not argue, because she was right, and because he found he did not want to. He looked at *L., seen* a while, in her round quick hand beside his small square one, and then he closed the book with it in.

"You were a long time," he said. "You said an hour."

Lira went on unlacing her boot.

"Was I?" she said. "The dock man stayed. We got talking." She pulled the boot off and dropped it, and looked at the floor. "And I wanted to try something on the straw while it was empty. It didn't work. I'll tell you when it works."

It was not like her to say *when it works* about anything without telling him what the anything was, and he heard that it was not like her. He heard it, and turned it over, and set it down carefully on the edge of the evening without picking it up again, as she had set things down for him a hundred times.

"All right," he said.

She looked up at him then, quickly, as if she had expected him to ask, and found that he was not going to; and something went over her face that he could not read, because it went too fast, and then she was herself again, pulling off the other boot.

"Read me the Wind one again," said Lira. "Out loud. The bit about the lock. I like hearing my *ands* in it."

# Chapter 7 — Fingerprints

The words *to the left* would not leave him alone.

He had put them down in the Power Log without a thought, because they were true, and then he had looked at them, and they had looked back. For two days afterward he found himself turning them over at odd moments: on the market steps, at the east bench, lying in bed with the lamp out and his hip quiet at last. *To the left, the front-left or the back-left.* He had burst perhaps a thousand times since the spring, and he tried, lying there, to remember one of them that had gone right, and could not. He tried to remember one that had gone straight back. He could not remember that either.

It was the kind of thing he would have seen in an opponent in two bouts. He had not seen it in himself in six months.

On the third morning he went down to the alcove early, before Lira, and stood on her chalk cross with the straw post at his shoulder, and tried.

He set himself square, and thought *right*, and dropped his right hip.

Nothing happened. The plainest part was that. He dropped his hip and his body went sideways the way any boy's body goes sideways when he steps, on his feet, at the speed of feet, and that was all. There was no loosening. The floor did not go short. He took one ordinary step to the right and stood there feeling foolish, like a man who has walked confidently into a room to fetch something and forgotten what.

But something had gone out of him all the same. He stood on the stone and felt for it, the way he felt for the cost after every burst, and it was there: a small catch in the breath, as if it had begun to stop and then thought better of it, and a faint warmth along the line of the left hip, the wrong hip, the hip he had not used. It was the burst's toll, or a shadow of it, as though he had been charged a little for knocking on a door that was never going to open.

He tried again. And again. On the fifth try he stopped, because the shadow tolls were adding up and the hip was beginning to take an interest, and he sat down on the bench at the back of the alcove and took out the Power Log and wrote it before he could start explaining it to himself.

*Right hip: five tries. Nothing comes. An ordinary step. But a toll: a catch in the breath and a little of the left hip, each time. Charged for the asking.*

Then, because he did not trust a memory to tell him what he had never done, he went and fetched the brown Log off the shelf at the boarding house at a limping run and brought it back down to the alcove, and went through the back of it, instance by instance, by the grey window light.

The first, the drop under Lira's backhand in the grey half: left leg out behind, turned from the hip. Left. The box drill: front-left. Feryn's third: round the blow, and the little drawing beside it went round to the left. Darrow's third. He read that one twice. *The step. Not asked. Everybody saw.* And under it, in his own hand, written on the cot that night with his ribs singing, where he had drawn it: a stick figure, and Darrow's straight blow coming down the middle, and the figure gone round it on the far side, onto Darrow's left, which, facing him across a circle, was Cael's right.

He sat with the old book open on his knee.

Every burst he had ever called had gone left. Every one he had measured on the straw, every one in eleven Copper bouts, every one this morning. But in the third exchange against Darrow Innes, with no ground left and his heel swept, the step had come up out of the dark unasked, and it had gone the other way, to the right, round the edge of a blow where no step of his could have reached. It had gone where it was needed, not where it was used to going. He had written it down at the time, and drawn it, and never once looked at which side of the paper the figure was on.

*Asked: left only,* he wrote in the Power Log, under the line about the right hip. *Unasked: once right, Darrow's third. Once is an accident. But it was the worst moment I've ever had, and it came cleanest then, and it went the way it had to. I can't call that. I can't count on it. Ruling: for planning, the burst only goes left.*

He was still sitting there when Lira came in with her staff on her shoulder and a heel of bread in her mouth, and stopped in the opening of the alcove and looked at him, and at the book, and at his face.

She took the bread out of her mouth.

"You've found it," she said.

---

It turned out that she had known for nearly a month.

"Not known," said Lira. "I had a feeling, and then I had a guess, and then I had a lot of mornings." She sat down beside him on the bench and finished the bread. "You never go right. You never go straight back, either. Not once, not even when right's closer. I've watched you do it from three paces for half a year and I didn't see it for five months, because when you've seen something do one thing five hundred times you stop seeing that it never does the other." She brushed the crumbs off her knees. "Then one morning I came at you from the left on purpose, the way you'd want to burst, and you went left anyway, straight into me. You paid extra to go the long way round. And I thought, that's odd. And then I did it again the next morning, and the next."

"Why didn't you say?"

"Because you'd have stopped doing it while I was watching. You'd have tried to go right to prove you could, and you'd have done it with your feet, and I'd never have been able to tell whether it was your feet or the other thing." She looked at him. "I wanted to see it a lot of times before I told you. Your rule."

He could not argue with his own rule.

"So you've been building something," he said. "On the straw. Those late hours."

Something went across her face, too quick to read, and was gone.

"Some of them," said Lira. "Get up. I'll show you."

She showed him with the staff, slowly at first. She did not try to hit him. She came at him from his left front, and he moved left, away from it, the natural way; and she came again from where he had moved to, from the front this time, and he moved left again; and again, and each time she came she came from a little further round his left side, so that each time his escape took him a little further round to the right without his ever choosing to go there. It was like being swept with a broom. Every stroke was small, and every stroke moved him a little toward the corner, and by the sixth stroke he found his back to the straw post with Lira in front of him and slightly to his left, and the only open ground anywhere near him was behind him and to his right.

And she came at him.

He reached for the burst out of plain habit, and the burst looked at where it would have to go and would not go there. It was the same as on the chalk cross: nothing came, and a little toll went out. He stepped on his feet, slow and mortal, into the right-rear corner, and the end of her staff tapped him on the ribs as he went, not hard, the way you tap a door to show you could have knocked it down.

"That," said Lira, "is a broom."

He stood with his back against the post.

"Where does it go?" she said. "Your burst. Draw it for me."

He crouched and drew it on the stone with Red Cap's chalk. A dot for himself. Then the lines the burst had taken in a thousand mornings: left, front-left, back-left, and every angle in between, a spray of lines like the ribs of a fan, all of them on one side. He drew the fan as wide as he honestly could. It covered the whole of the left of the circle, and a little of the front, and a little of the back. And it left a quarter of the circle untouched, behind him and to the right, where there was nothing at all.

They both looked at it.

"Two-thirds," said Cael. "Near enough. The rest it won't go."

"The rest you have to walk." Lira squatted beside him on her heels and looked at the empty quarter as if it were a hole in a fence. "Most people won't find it. Most people don't come at you from the same side six times running; they don't know you well enough. But a Wind fighter would. Anybody who's fought a lot of Wind would. They'd feel it the way I felt it, because we know how a step's supposed to go, and yours goes funny." She stood. "I wanted you to know about it before somebody across a rope did. Somebody who wouldn't stop at a tap."

---

He spent the next hour trying to make the burst go right, because he could not stop himself, and because there was a thing he did not understand and could not leave alone.

He tried it slowly and quickly. He tried it standing and from a run. He tried it with Lira coming at him from the left, so that right was the only sensible way to go, in case the burst only needed a reason. He tried it from his right foot, in case it was the foot and not the hip. Every time, the drop of the right hip summoned nothing but a step, and every time the step was charged, a little, as if it had been the real thing. After the ninth try his breath was short in a way nine ordinary steps could never have made it, and his left hip, the one he had not been using, was warm and sore from the crest to the knee.

"Stop," said Lira, at the tenth, and he stopped.

"It won't," he said. "It won't go. It's not that it's hard. It's that it isn't there. Left is there. Right isn't." He stood and breathed. "It's like a word I can only say one way round."

"Then don't say it the other way round." She leaned on her staff. "You don't need it to go right. You need nobody to make you go right. That's a different thing, and it's one you can do with your feet."

"I want to know why first."

"Of course you do." But she waited.

He stood in the middle of the alcove and thought about it, and as he thought about it his eyes went, without his asking them, to her hips. She was standing loose, with the staff grounded, her weight even. There was nothing to see. But he had watched her step every morning for half a year, and three months of mornings before that from a barrel by a drain, and he did not need to see it now to know.

"Do your step for me," he said. "The real one. Ten times. From the post to the chalk."

She looked at him oddly, and did it. She went from the post to the chalk ten times, and each time the floor went short under her, quick and clean and as easy as breathing, much easier than it had ever been for him; and each time, before she went, her left hip dropped. Not her right. Never her right. It dropped the width of a fingernail and loosened, and she was across.

"Left," said Cael. "Every time."

Lira stopped on the chalk.

"What?"

"You drop the left hip. Every step. I'd bet the Log on it. I don't think you've gone off the right once in all the mornings I've been watching you."

She stood very still. He watched her go inside herself and look, the way you look for a thing in your own coat pocket, and find it, and not believe it, and find it again. She did the step once more, slowly, with her eyes on her own hip. She stopped.

"Huh," said Lira.

It was a very small word to come out of her. He had heard her say a great many things in a year, angry and kind and rude and frightened, and he had never heard her sound like this: like somebody who has opened the door of their own house and found a stranger sitting in the kitchen, quite at home.

"I do," she said. "I do drop left. I didn't know." She looked at him. "I learned it from a man in a barn when I was fourteen. Before the academy. He was the first one who ever showed me anything. He used to tie his left hand behind his back to teach, so his right would do the showing, and it never worked, he always forgot and untied it." She laughed, not quite properly. "He was left-handed. Everything he did, he did from the left. And I copied him for a year in that barn before anybody at Fenmark ever saw me. And nobody at Fenmark ever said. Not once."

"So mine goes left because yours does."

"And mine goes left because his did." She sat down on the bench, quite suddenly. "You've got a left-handed farmer in your hip."

He sat down beside her. They looked at the fan on the stone, with its empty quarter.

He had known, since the night the first notice came, that the burst was hers, or had come out of her. He had written it in the Power Log under *Source* three nights ago. But he had thought of it as a kind of shape, a thing that had been copied the way you copy a drawing, clean, with the useful lines in it. He had not thought that it would bring her habits with it, or that her habits would have had a man in a barn before her, with his own habits, tying his left hand behind his back and forgetting.

It was not a drawing, then. It was a real person's way of moving, taken from a real person on real mornings, with everything that had been in it. Even the parts nobody had ever meant to teach.

---

"Right," said Lira.

She stood up off the bench as though she had made her mind up about something, and picked up her staff. Whatever had gone through her about the man in the barn, she had put it somewhere for later; he could see her do it, and he knew the look, because it was the one she wore at the rope before a bout of her own.

"You can't mend it," she said. "Fine. Nobody can mend everything. So we don't mend it. We make sure nobody ever gets you into that corner. That's footwork, and it's boring, and it doesn't come from anybody's farmer, and you can have as much of it as you like." She set herself. "I'm going to broom you. You're going to not be broomed."

That was the rest of the morning, and it was the hardest work he had done in weeks, and none of it had anything to do with the burst.

She came at him from his left, again and again, each time a little further round, sweeping him toward the corner. And he learned to see the broom coming. Not the stroke itself; the stroke was easy to see. He learned to see the shape the strokes were making, three or four strokes ahead, the way you see a fence going up post by post and know where the gate will be before anybody has hung it. When he saw the shape start, he spent his feet, cheaply and early, a small step and another, to keep the left side of the circle open in front of him, so that whenever the real strike came, the fan had room to open. He gave the empty quarter to his ordinary footwork, his slow mortal feet, and kept it as you keep a doorway, by standing in it, not by running through it.

It was dull and it was exhausting and he was bad at it. She broomed him into the post four times in the first quarter of an hour and tapped him on the ribs each time, and each time she said *again*, and he did it again.

By the second quarter of an hour she was brooming him into the post twice in a dozen tries, and then once.

It was in the middle of the third quarter of an hour, with Lira coming round his left side on her fifth stroke and his feet already moving to deny her the sixth, that the quiet came.

He knew it before he knew what it was. He stopped in the middle of a step, with his weight half moved, and his body was aware of it the way a body is aware of a change in the weather, before the head has noticed the clouds. It was not pain. It was not tiredness. It was the hush that came before a whole thought arrived, and he had felt it only three times in his life, and every time something had been written afterward in the dark place behind his breastbone.

Lira stopped too. She had seen him stop like that before. She grounded her staff and stood quite still, three paces off, and did not say a word.

The quiet turned over, and in the dark behind the bone, where nothing had lit since the word, the letters came, as plain as print held up to a lamp:

```
FRAGMENT UPDATE
[Wind-adjacent] — Duration revised: sustained.
Integration: partial.
```

He stood in the alcove with his weight half on one foot and read it.

It was not new. That was the first thing he understood, and the strangest. Every notice he had ever had had brought him something: a fragment where there had been nothing, or two at once, recorded. This brought nothing. It had the same name in it as the first notice, months ago, and the same *partial*. Only one word had changed. Where the old notice said *undetermined*, this one said *sustained*. And it was headed *UPDATE*, a word he had never seen at the top of one of them before, as though the thing that kept the form had gone back to an old page and corrected it.

Three nights ago he had copied *Duration: undetermined* into the Power Log in his best hand, word for word, as the notice's own testimony, the one witness he had besides himself. And now the witness had changed its story.

He did not know how long he stood. When he looked up, Lira was still standing three paces off with her staff grounded, exactly where she had been, and she had not moved, and she had not asked.

"I need a moment," he said.

She nodded, and stayed where she was. She did not come closer and she did not go away. She stood in the alcove with her staff and gave him the moment as if it were a thing she had been keeping in her pocket for him, and when it was over she was still there.

"It changed," he said at last. "The Wind one. Not a new one. The same one, written again. Where it says how long it lasts, the old one said *undetermined*. Now it says *sustained*."

"Sustained," said Lira slowly. "Is that better?"

"I don't know. I don't know what it means. I don't know what *undetermined* meant, either." He looked at his own hands, as though they might have something written on them. "It's never done that. It's never gone back and changed one."

"Did you do anything?"

"I don't know that either."

Lira looked at him for a while. Then she looked at the fan on the stone, and the empty quarter, and the chalk cross, and back at him.

"Well," she said, "I'm not stopping. You've got two more strokes of broom to not be broomed by, and then you can have a sit-down and think about it as hard as you like." She lifted the staff. "If it's changed, it's changed. It'll still be changed in five minutes."

He laughed, which surprised him, and set himself, and she came round his left side.

He denied the fifth stroke. He saw the shape of the sixth starting, a full three strokes before it could have closed, and spent two cheap steps to keep the left open, and when she came for the corner the corner was not there. The broom swept nothing. She tried it again, from further round, with a feint in it, and he saw that one coming as well, and the trap fell apart before its last post was up.

Lira stepped back and grounded her staff. She was out of breath and she was grinning.

"There," she said. "That's shut. Not shut against me. Shut. I know my own broom better than anybody alive, and I can't get you in the corner now, and if I can't, a stranger who's fought you twice isn't going to." She wiped her forehead with her wrist. "It's not sealed. The burst still won't go right. It's guarded. That's better than sealed. Sealed things you stop looking at."

"Thank you," said Cael.

"Don't thank me. Buy me lunch." The water barrel at the far end of the alcove got all her attention after that. "I'm starving. Closing your holes is hungry work, and I've been doing it since dawn."

He bought her lunch.

He bought it at the pie stall at the top of the market, from the woman who was honest about the weight, and they took it to the low wall by the dyers' steps, where you could sit with your back to the sun and watch the river barges come in. Lira ate two pies and half of his. He did not mind. His hands were busy with the Power Log on his knee and a pie in his other hand that he kept forgetting about.

"You're going to write it all down before you eat that," said Lira, "and then it'll be cold, and you'll eat it cold and tell me it's fine."

"It is fine cold."

"Nothing's fine cold except pickles and other people's opinions." She took the rest of his half from him. "Go on, then. Write."

He wrote what had come before it, because what came before a thing was evidence, and evidence did not keep.

Three nights before, he had copied the Wind entry into the Power Log in its new form, field by field, with the old notice's *undetermined* at the head. This morning, before Lira came, he had pressed the burst harder than he had ever pressed it outside a bout: five tries to the right, five tolls paid for nothing. Then, with her, nine more, and then an hour and a half of the closest attention he had ever given to the edges of the thing, where it would go and where it would not, the fan drawn in chalk on the stone and the empty quarter stared at. If he had wanted to set out to make the notice change, if he had sat down with a plan to provoke it, he could hardly have done better than this one morning. And he had not planned it. He had not even wanted it; he had wanted to know why it would not go right.

That made it more interesting, not less. It also made it nothing yet, because it had happened once. One thing that happens once is an accident, until it happens again.

"You've got your *one is an accident* face on," said Lira, through pie.

"It is an accident. Until there's another one."

"And if there is another one?"

"Then I'll look at what came before both of them, and see whether it's the same." He wrote that down too, under the rest, so that if there ever was another one he would have something honest to compare it with. "If pushing at the edges of it is what makes it change, that's the most important thing I'll ever know about it. And I'm not allowed to believe it on one morning."

Lira finished his pie and brushed her hands.

"I'll tell you what I saw," she said. "For your middle column. With an *h*." She was not smiling. "When it came, you didn't look scared. You looked like you do in the lock. Like you'd stopped being anybody for a bit, and you were only eyes." She looked out at the river. "It was a long bit. Longer than two *ands*. I counted, out of habit. I got to eleven."

He wrote it down, exactly, and put *L., seen* beside it, in his own hand this time.

Then he sat with the Power Log open on his knee and the cold pie forgotten on the wall beside him, and watched a barge come in under the lower bridge, low in the water, heavy with something, its crew leaning on their poles and its wake spreading out behind it in long lines toward both banks. He thought about the word *sustained*, and what it might mean for a burst that had only ever lasted the time it takes a strike to fall. He thought about the old word, and about having written it down so carefully only three nights ago, as if it were fixed.

He had thought, without ever quite saying so to himself, that the fragments were things that were done. Fixed things that had arrived and stayed the shape they had arrived in, and that he was only slowly learning to see them properly, the way you learn the shape of a room in the dark. Everything that had changed in the Power Log until today had been him changing: his reading getting better, his measurements getting closer. The thing itself had sat still while he walked round it.

It had not sat still.

"You're doing it again," said Lira quietly. "Going off on your own with it."

"I'm still here."

"I know you are. I'm only checking." She bumped his knee with hers. "Eat your pie. It's fine cold."

# Chapter 8 — Revised

He wrote it up that night, at the crate desk, with the door between the rooms open and Lira asleep on the other side of it, or pretending to be.

He did not write it at once. He sat for a long time first with the Power Log open at the Wind entry and the lamp turned low, looking at the line at the head of the page where he had copied the notice's fields three nights before. *Duration: undetermined.* His own hand, his best hand, careful and square. It was not wrong. It had been true when he wrote it. It had been true, as far as he knew, until about the middle of the morning, and then it had stopped being true, and nothing on the page had any way of saying so.

That was the first thing he had to mend, and it was not a thing about the burst at all. It was a thing about the book.

He had built the Power Log to hold what he knew. Six fields, three columns, an *h* for anything heard, the open questions given the most room. He had built it so that it would never say more than he had seen. What he had not built it for was the thing he had seen today: a fact that was true on one page and then, without his doing anything to it, not true on the next. He had built a book for a world that held still while he looked at it.

So he did not cross out *undetermined*. He left it where it was, and drew a short line under the whole of the notice's fields, and under the line he wrote a new heading, small, the way Vell wrote *t.f.* in her margins:

*REVISIONS.*

And under that:

*Three days after I ruled this book, in the alcove, about the middle of the morning, in the middle of the broom drill. FRAGMENT UPDATE. [Wind-adjacent]. Duration revised: sustained. Integration: partial (unchanged). Old word:* undetermined. *New word:* sustained. *What sustained means: not known. What undetermined meant: not known either.*

He looked at that, and it was right, and it was ugly, and he left it.

Then he went to the back of the book, past the empty pages, to the place where he had begun to keep the things that were not fields: the line about the eight instances carried from the old Log, and nothing else. He wrote under it.

*The fragments are not static. They develop.*

He stopped there, because the next part was the part that mattered, and he wanted to put it on a line of its own where he would not be able to skip it.

*Or I'm developing my understanding of them.*

*I can't tell which. It matters which. If they change by themselves, whether I'm watching or not, then I am keeping a record of something that moves, and the record has to be built to catch it moving. If it's only my seeing that gets better, then the notices are only catching up with me, and that's a different thing, and it changes what I ought to be watching. Today, I can't tell. One revision doesn't tell me. Filed under open questions, at the front of the queue.*

He read that back, and wrote the conditions under it, plainly, as he had written them on the wall by the dyers' steps: the new format three nights before; the five right-hip tries and their tolls; the nine more with Lira; the fan in chalk; an hour and a half of the closest attention he had ever paid to where the burst would go and where it would not. *Should a second revision come, set what led up to it beside this, line for line. He sat back.

He wrote to Hesk the next evening, because Hesk had asked him to find out what the half-breath was worth and tell him, and because he had promised, a long time ago now, on a wall by a pump, to tell the next thing as it came and not five things later.

Then he told him the rest.

*There's a thing I keep, which I've just given a name to. It's the book for what I carry. I copied the notices into it at the top of each page, word for word, because they were the only other witness I had.*

*Yesterday one of them changed. Not a new one. An old one. It went back and corrected itself. Where it had said it didn't know how long the thing lasts, it says now that it's sustained.

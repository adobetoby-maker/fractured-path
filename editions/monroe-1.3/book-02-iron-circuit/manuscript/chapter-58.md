# Chapter 58 — Wind Only

The hill had a way with news, Cael had learned, and the news of the woman from Greyvane went the way of all the rest. It took a thing in, and talked about it for three days at every stall in the market row, and then it put the thing away somewhere and went back to the price of eels. By the Monday the talk at the pump was all about a carter who had put his wagon into the river at the ford and come out with the horse and lost the wagon. A few people still nodded to Cael in the street a little longer than they used to. Most did not.

Cael found he was grateful for it.

His body had spent the week mending in the order it chose, which was not the order he would have chosen. The knees came back first. On the Wednesday he had gone up the market steps one at a time with a hand on the rail. By the Saturday he went up them like anybody, and by the Monday he had forgotten, for most of a morning, that they had ever been locked. The hands came next. The stupid brightness went out of the knuckles a day at a time until he could close both fists on the Sunday, though not hard, and not without telling himself to. The left forearm went through every colour Lira had a word for, plum and then slate and then a green she called *pond*, and finished the week the yellow of an old bruise. It was sore to press and no longer hot.

The right shoulder was the slowest, and the strangest.

The groove from the three redirects hurt the way it always had, a long bright line from the top of the arm to the breastbone, and it eased by the day the way it always did. Under the groove, in the joint itself, nothing hurt very much at all. It was simply quiet. Each morning Brom came early, before Lira's hour, and stood in the doorway of Cael's room and said, "May I?" and Cael said, "You may." Brom laid his big hand flat on the joint and read it, a whole palm's slow listening, and each morning he said the same thing.

"Still quiet."

On the Monday he added, "Less quiet than it was."

That day Cael watched Ulric. He did it out of habit as much as anything, the way he would have watched anybody whose name was on a slate opposite his. He sat on the east bench with the grey book on his knee through two of Ulric's side-floor bouts, a Copper Shield in the afternoon and a young Wind from the salt end after supper. He had not watched him properly since the night he beat him, months ago, before Brom had ever come down the river. Ulric had been a Copper Blade with a heavy slash and a tell: the right shoulder dropped a finger's width before the big cut, every time. Cael had found it in the second of four watched bouts and beaten him with it in the fifth exchange, and Ulric had come up to him afterward, still holding his side, and said, *I'd like to know what you were watching instead.* And Cael had given him nothing.

Ulric had fixed it.

Not all of it. You could not fix a thing that deep in a season; it had gone into him at the post too young. But the drop was smaller now, and it came later, and twice in the Shield bout it did not come at all. Cael watched him find his feet against the young Wind, who was quick and silly and kept trying to go round him. Ulric did not chase. He stood and let the boy come, and turned on the spot, and every turn kept him in the middle of the floor while the boy ran round the outside. He had learned something since the autumn. He had been watching people too.

*Shoulder: smaller, later, sometimes gone,* Cael wrote under the old mark. *Doesn't chase. Keeps the middle. Turns on the spot. He's been somewhere and come back better.* And then, because it was true: *Good.*

On the Tuesday, the morning of the bout, Brom stood with his hand on the shoulder a long time and said, "It's like a room after people have gone out of it, and somebody's begun to sweep." By then the arm would lift to the height of the shoulder and no higher. That was enough to guard with. It was not enough to strike with, and it was certainly not enough to put through a redirect, and Cael had no intention of asking it to.

Quenna took the coach that morning. He did not go to see her off; she had not asked him to, and he thought she would rather he spent the morning on his shoulder. By the evening the hill had already begun to behave as if she had never been there.

---

The Tuesday card was an ordinary Tuesday card, and that was the best thing about it.

There were fourteen lamps on the beams and no more. There was no stew in the corner and no pennant over the side door. The betting man had his trestle back, with his chalk and his two columns, and the columns were the ordinary columns, names and prices, and nobody was arguing at them. Perhaps three hundred people stood round the main floor. Three hundred was a good crowd for a Tuesday, and a week ago it would have seemed like a great many; tonight the building felt half empty and comfortable, like a kitchen after a wedding.

Vell stood at her table with the red-taped book. She looked across at him once as he came in, over her spectacles, and lifted her pen perhaps a finger's width off the page. That was all.

Ulric was already at the south mark when Cael stepped over the rope. He was a square, plain young man, with a blade's thick wrists and a face that had been broken once at the nose and set a little to one side. He was looking at Cael's arms: the left forearm, yellow from wrist to elbow; the right, which Cael was holding a little stiffly at his side. His face went careful.

He came across the stone before Vell called anything, which was not done, and stopped a stride off and spoke low.

"I'll not go for the arms," he said. "If that's what you're thinking."

"I wasn't."

"Then I'm telling you anyway." Ulric looked at the yellow forearm again, and then at Cael's face. "I asked for this a month ago because I wanted to know if I'd learned anything. I don't want to learn it off a man who can't lift his arms."

"I can lift them," said Cael. "To here." He showed him: the right arm to the shoulder and no further. "I'll not hit you hard tonight, Ulric. I can't. I'll show you what I watch instead. You asked me in the autumn."

Ulric looked at him for a long moment. Something went over his broken face that might have been a laugh he did not let out.

"All right," he said, and went back to his mark.

Vell's voice came across the floor.

"Ulric. Blade Path. Copper, on a guild card." The pen moved. "Cael. Iron-equivalent. No Path designation." It moved again. "The rule in this room is that nobody is killed on my floor. Both of you know it. Both of you say so."

"Yes," said Ulric.

"Yes," said Cael.

"Then it's a bout."

---

He had decided before he came in what he would use, and he used only that.

The knock stayed shut, because he did not need it and this was not a night for needing what he did not need. The giving face stayed asleep, because there was nothing in either hand to give with, and the shoulder he did not even think about. What he had was his eyes, and his feet, and the Wind, which had been the first thing he ever had, from Lira, a winter and a half ago in a frozen yard. He meant to spend the whole bout on those three and nothing else, and see how far they would carry him.

The first exchange he gave away. He stood off and let Ulric come, and Ulric came the way he had come at the young Wind, not chasing, taking the middle. His blade was a practice blade from Dace's locked rack, dulled at the edge and heavy at the end. He swung it in short honest cuts that wanted Cael to commit to a side, and Cael would not. He went on his feet, left and right, and once on the hip, left, half a body over, the old fan. He watched the shoulder. It dropped, small and late, before two of the five cuts. Before the other three it did not drop at all.

*Two in five,* he thought. *Then the cuts it doesn't come before are the light ones. He's fixed it on the light ones. The heavy one still has it.*

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

In the second, he watched the middle.

Ulric owned it. He stood in it and turned on the spot as he had against the young Wind, and every turn kept his blade between Cael and the centre of the floor. Cael moved round him, and Ulric turned, and Cael moved again, and Ulric turned again. It was very good. It was the thing Ulric had learned since the autumn, and he had learned it well, and Cael watched it the way he would have watched a new tool in Hesk's workshop, turning it over to see how it was made.

It was made with the feet. Ulric turned on the ball of his right foot and brought the left round after. Every time he did it, the left came round a fraction wide, so that after each turn he stood a hand's breadth further toward whichever side Cael had gone. He was keeping the middle by turning. But he was not quite turning on the spot. He was turning on a spot that walked.

Cael went left, round him, and Ulric turned and walked a hand's breadth left. Cael went left again. A hand's breadth. Again.

He did not hurry it. He went round Ulric five times in the second exchange, slowly, on his feet and once on the hip, and took one heavy cut on the yellow forearm when the shoulder dropped and he was a breath slow reading it. It was not a hard cut. It still hurt like fire. And each time round, the middle of the floor that Ulric owned walked a hand's breadth further toward the north rope.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

Ulric went back to his mark breathing hard, and looked round at where he was, and frowned. He was not stupid. He had seen something, and could not yet see what.

In the third exchange, Cael finished it.

He went left round him, as before, and Ulric turned and walked his hand's breadth. Left again; the breadth again. Ulric was close to the north rope now, two strides off it, then a stride and a half, and he felt it. Cael saw him feel it in the set of his back. He stopped turning and came forward instead, to take the middle back by force. It was the right thing to do, and Cael had been waiting for it since the first exchange. The shoulder dropped, small and late, but it dropped. It was the heavy cut.

Cael went on the hip.

He went left and forward in the fan, half a body over, the oldest thing he had, and the heavy cut went through the place where he had been and pulled Ulric after it. In the lock, standing with his weight caught and his breath held for the two *ands*, Cael did nothing at all. He did not need to. He had only to stand there, at the one place on the floor where Ulric's next step, after a heavy cut that had found nothing, would have to go.

Ulric took the step. He took it backward, the way a man recovers from a cut that has missed, to make room for the next. And the back of his calf met the rope, and his weight was going the wrong way, and there was nothing behind the rope but the north bench and the dock partner's knees.

He went over it. Not hard. He went over it the way a man sits down on a wall he did not know was there, and sat on the stone on the far side, with his blade still in his hand, looking up at Cael over the rope with an expression of absolute astonishment.

Then he began to laugh.

Vell's pen moved.

"Cael. Win. Third exchange. Forfeit by the rope."

The room made a sound that was half a cheer and half a laugh, the sound a crowd makes when it has watched a thing it did not expect and liked it. Cael went to the rope and held out his left hand, and Ulric took it, and Cael pulled, gently, because the left would not pull hard. Ulric came up and over and stood on the stone, still laughing, rubbing the back of his calf.

"You walked me."

"You walked yourself. I only went round."

"The turn." Ulric looked down at his own feet as if they belonged to somebody else. "The left foot. It comes round wide."

"A hand's breadth. Every time."

Ulric stood for a moment and looked at his feet, and his broken face did several things one after another.

"That's what you watch," he said.

"That's what I watch."

"Then I'll go and watch it myself." He let go of Cael's hand. "Thank you," he said, and meant it, and went off along the rope toward the side door, still looking at his feet.

Cael stood on the stone and went through himself, out of habit, and found almost nothing to count: a thin hip line from two bursts, a sting along the forearm where the cut had caught it, and his breath already easy. Nothing else had been asked of anything.

It had been, he thought, the least he had ever spent to win a bout on that floor. He could not remember the last time he had fought anybody for the plain pleasure of watching a thing and seeing it work, with nothing riding on it but a line in Vell's book.

He did not tell anybody that it was his last bout on that stone, and he did not say it to himself either, not in words; he let it be a bout, an ordinary Tuesday bout with an ordinary line in Vell's book, and went to fetch his coat from the rail.

---

The notice came late, after the bout, while he was still awake at the desk.

He was at the crate desk with the binder open in front of him and the lamp turned low, writing Ulric's line in the back pages in a hand that was nearly his own again. *Ulric. Main floor. Won, third exchange. Forfeit by the rope. Wind: two, both left. Feet. Eyes. Nothing else.* He had just written *Nothing else* and was looking at it, thinking that it was a pleasant thing to be able to write, when the room went quiet round him in the way he knew.

It was not the lamp, or the street. It was the particular quiet that came with every notice, and never once when he was waiting for it. A held breath of the whole world, and then, in the middle of the quiet, in front of him and in no place at all, the words.

He sat very still and read it.

He read it the way he read every one of them: twice, slowly, and then once more, field by field, the way Vell read a line before she wrote it. And then he sat back against the wall and let his breath out. He found that his hands, both of them, had gone flat on the desk on either side of the binder, as if to keep it from moving.

It was not like the others.

The other three had come as strangers. Wind had arrived after a winter of Lira's mornings, and he had not known what it was or how to call it, and had spent half a year learning. Pressure had arrived after a hand on his shoulder in a yard, and he had spent months finding out it had two faces. Iron had arrived after three mornings of Brom hitting him for science, and he had spent a whole autumn learning to knock. Each of them had been a door in a wall he had not known had doors. He had opened each one and found a room he did not know, and had to learn its size in the dark.

This one he knew.

He knew the room. He had stood in it for half a second on the main floor a week ago, with Reydan's burst gathered in his right shoulder and pointing home, and he had not known what it was, or where it had come from, or whether he would ever stand there again. Now it had a name, or the half of a name that the notices gave: *Compression-adjacent*. It had a field that said *force absorption*, which was the gathering, and a field that said *damage redirect*, which was the turn, and the sending home. It had *contact range*, which was the shoulder, the burst inside him, and no further. It was the face of somebody glimpsed once in a crowd and now standing in the doorway with their hat off, saying, *Yes. That was me.*

He put his hand on the right shoulder, the quiet one, and held it there.

*So that's what you are,* he thought. *That's what was in there.*

He did not think, *I took it.* He was careful not to think that, because it was not true. He had not taken anything. He had not reached for it on the floor; he had only gone where it was already pointing. And he had not reached for it since. It had come, as all of them had come, unasked, after close and long and costly work against one person. It was found in him afterward, the way you find a splinter in your hand at night and remember the fence. That was all he knew about how they came. It was all he let himself know.

He turned to the back of the binder, past *Habit* and *Shape* and *Carrying*, to the leaves he had glued in himself.

The *Anomalies* page had two entries on it now, on two leaves. The first was session nine, the oldest, in his ordinary hand. The second was the half second, from the bout night, in the bad hand of a boy writing with his left fingers out of a sling. He read the second through. Then he drew a line under it, a single clean line across the leaf, and wrote beneath it, small and exact:

*Closed. Compression-adjacent, incomplete expression preceding integration. See the notice.*

The column balanced. He felt it the way a clerk must feel a long sum come right at the bottom, a small, absurd, enormous satisfaction out of all proportion to the ink.

Then he turned back one leaf, to session nine.

He did not read it through again. He had laid it beside the half-second on the bout night, field by field, and found them different everywhere he could look, and the notice in front of him changed none of that. It said *force absorption*, and in session nine nothing had been absorbed; the push had come apart before it was ever sent. Whatever session nine had been, it was not this. He added nothing to its leaf, and kept the pen away from it altogether, so that the page stood as it had stood since the night before the bout, with its one line at the foot.

One anomaly. Still one.

Then he turned to the front of the binder, to the clean leaves behind the three old entries, and ruled a fourth.

Not with the six fields, though; he had been meaning, since the night he put the pages under the rings, to do the old entries over in a shorter way. The six had turned out, in a year of use, to be four questions, two of them asked twice: what it does, and where, and when, all in one; what it is good for; what it costs; how much of it comes when he calls. He ruled the new entry the short way, four heads, and meant to bring the other three over to match it on the road, where there would be time.

*Compression-adjacent. Reydan.*

*Function, as known once, in a bout: force already inside me, gathered instead of passed, turned, and sent back along the line it came in by. Contact range. The force has to be in the body first; nothing before that.*

*Benefit, guessed: a burst, or any committed strike, turned into return and position. Strongest against exactly the kind of blow I can least get out of the way of.*

*Cost, guessed: the shoulder it went through, all of it, for a week. Assume it's dear until I find out it isn't.*

*Integration: partial. Untried on purpose. Each of the others took weeks before it would come when asked. Expect the same. Begin small. Begin when the shoulder has something to give.*

Under it, outside the heads, smaller:

*Found in me afterward, the same as the others. Not taken. Not called. I went in on it once because it was already pointing, and that's all I did.*

He read it back. Then he turned to the very first leaf of the binder, where a year ago he had first counted what he had, one finger on each entry. He counted again, the same way, with a finger on each.

Wind. Pressure. Iron. Compression.

Four confirmed fragments. One anomaly.

He sat looking at that for a long time, while the lamp burned down.

---

He knocked on the wall. Not the other knock; the ordinary kind, with his knuckles, twice.

Lira came through the door between the rooms in her nightgown with a shawl over it, holding a candle, her hair in a plait over one shoulder and her face full of sleep and alarm.

"What," she said. "What is it. Is it the shoulder."

"It's the shoulder," said Cael. "In a way." He turned the binder round on the crate desk so that it faced her and held the lamp closer.

She read it standing. She read the notice through once, and then the closed line on the *Anomalies* leaf, which he showed her, and then the new entry, all four heads. She read slowly, the candle tilting in her hand so that wax ran down onto her fingers, and she did not seem to notice. When she had finished she stood holding the candle and looking at the page.

"The fourth exchange," she said.

"Yes."

"The thing that went back." She was quiet a moment. "I saw it from the rope. I didn't know what I was seeing. I thought you'd found something new in the redirect. Something you and Brom had kept back from me."

"We didn't keep it back. Neither of us knew."

"I know that now." She looked at him over the candle. "Four," she said.

"Four. And one."

She set the candle down on the desk, carefully, and put her waxy fingers flat on the open page beside the new entry, not on the writing, beside it, the way she had once put her hand in his book when it was a slim stitched thing with two entries in it, and said *It would be lonelier*.

"I'm going back to bed," she said. "You're going to bed too. And in the morning you'll go down to the carters' inn before Brom's up and tell him, because you tell him the same day, and it's after midnight, so it's already tomorrow, and if you don't tell him before breakfast he'll know from your face and be hurt that you didn't."

"I know."

"I know you know." She picked up the candle. At the door between the rooms she stopped. "It was in you all week," she said. "When I was holding the spoon. And neither of us knew."

"No."

"That's a strange thing to think about," said Lira, and went through, and he heard her bed take her weight on the other side of the wall.

He went down to the carters' inn at first light, through a cold so raw the cobbles sweated with it, and Brom was already up, sitting on the bench outside the stable door with his breath going up in the cold, as if he had been expecting somebody. Cael gave him the binder open at the new page. Brom read it from the front, every word, the notice and the four heads and the line underneath, and then went back to the notice and read that again.

"*Contact range*," said Brom. "*Damage redirect*." He looked up. "That's my trick. The thing I found in the boat shed. Hardened at the right instant, and the man went back along his own line." He shook his head slowly. "Only you've no hardness. You did it soft. You did it with the taking face, gathered and turned, where I'd have done it with a wall."

"I didn't do it. It did."

"I know. I'm only saying what it looked like." He shut the binder on his thumb and handed it back. "When do we start?"

"Not yet. Not till the shoulder's got something in it to give." Cael took the binder under his arm. "On the road. When we're walking, and nothing's riding on anything, and there's nobody watching but you."

Brom looked at him for a moment, and then nodded, slowly, the nod of a man agreeing to a term.

"On the road," he said. "I'll bring my hands."

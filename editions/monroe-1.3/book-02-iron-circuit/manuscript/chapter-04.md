# Chapter 4 — The Keepers

The man who wanted to argue with Vell sat on the bench nearest Dace's slate for most of an hour before he did it, and Cael, who had come in to watch the morning card's first bout, ended up watching him instead.

He was a Blade practitioner of perhaps twenty-five, Copper by his registry and Assessed-Copper by Vell's book, and he had lost the evening before to a circuit fighter with a third of his training and twice his patience. Cael had not seen the bout. He had heard about it four times on the way down the hill, and all four versions agreed on the one thing that mattered, which was that it had been clean. The man had been put on the stone in the third exchange by a sweep he should have seen coming, and he had got up, and shaken hands, and gone home.

And now he was back, in a good coat with academy stitching across the shoulders, with a folded paper in his hand.

Cael knew him a little, as you know anybody you have watched for a year without ever speaking to. He had come down from a guild-registered house on the river two seasons ago. He paid that house a fee every quarter for the right to say he trained there, and the house paid him back in a coat with stitched shoulders and a line on his papers and not much else. It did not find him bouts. Guild houses never did; they had more members than matches, and the matches went to the members whose fathers had paid the most fees. So he came to the Ironyard, like a dozen others in academy coats, because Dace would put him on a card twice a month and Vell would pay him for losing as well as winning. Cael had watched him at the slate the week before, trying to talk Dace into a better slot. Afterward, in the eating house, he had watched him paying for one bowl of soup with exact coin and then counting what was left in his purse under the edge of the table. It was a thin purse for a man with such good shoulders on his coat.

He did not look at the paper. He held it, and turned it over, and held it again, as a man holds a ticket for a coach he is not sure he wants to catch. His lips moved. Every so often he looked along the benches toward the door, and Cael followed the look the third time it went. He found what it was for: a younger man in the same academy coat, sitting near the back with his arms folded, who had come in with him and was waiting for something to happen.

*He's got a witness of his own,* Cael wrote, in the margin of the morning's page. *Somebody from his facility. He's going to do this in front of him, not in spite of him. Prediction: he'll wait until the benches are full, so that it's a public thing. He'll go to Vell, not Dace. He'll open with the biggest word he's got.*

He put the pencil down and waited to be told whether he was right.

The benches filled toward the hour, as they did. By the time Vell came out of the back room with the day's book and her tea, there were seventy or eighty people in the near rows. Fighters stretched along the walls, and the bookmakers in their corner were chalking up the first bout's odds. Vell sat down. She opened the book. She uncapped her ink.

The man stood up, put the paper in his inside pocket, and crossed the floor.

He did it well. He had clearly practised the walk: shoulders back, chin level, not fast, not slow. He stopped in front of Vell's table at the distance a man chooses when he wishes to be overheard.

"Last night's result," he said, "was corrupted."

*Corrupted.* It was the big word, and it had come first. Cael inked a small tick in the margin, and another under it, and noticed that he was pleased with himself, and did not much like that, and noted it.

---

Vell did not look up.

She finished blotting the line she had been writing, which was the date and the card, and laid the blotter down square to the book's edge. Only then did she raise her eyes, and when she did they were neither cold nor kind. They were simply open, the way a shop is open, ready for whatever was going to be brought in.

"By whom?"

"There was coaching from the benches. A man was calling corrections to my opponent all through the second and third exchanges. Feet, guard, when to step. I could hear him."

"Where was he standing?"

"By the west post. Grey beard. Red neckcloth."

Cael watched Vell know who it was. She knew from *west post*; *red neckcloth* only confirmed it. And then, knowing already, she did the thing he had seen her do at the Cinder House and had never been close enough to understand. She turned back through the book, unhurried, to a page some years old, and ran her finger down it until she came to a line. Then she turned the book a quarter round on the table so that the people on the nearest bench could see that there was a line there, though they could not read it.

"Orvet," she said. "Runs a gym in the tannery lanes. Shouts at fighters. It's in my book, four years back, on a complaint very like yours, and I ruled on it then. He shouts at his own people, and he shouts at other people's, and he shouts at the bookmakers when they get the odds wrong. Nobody pays him to. It's a habit, not a service, and it isn't against anything I've ever written down." She did not turn the book back. "You're saying he coached your opponent."

"I'm saying he did."

"Hm." Vell looked past him, along the near bench, and found a face. "Dessa."

It was Dessa, the Stone fighter Cael had met twice in his first months and beaten twice, both times by less than the benches thought. She sat on the near bench most mornings with her hair tied tight and said less than anybody in the building. She looked up from the strap she was mending.

"You were on the west bench last night, second bout."

"I was."

"One question, and loud enough for the room. Who was Orvet shouting at?"

Dessa thought about it, which with her always took a moment, and then she said, in a voice that carried to the bookmakers' corner, "Both of them. He told the girl to keep her weight back. Then he told this one to stop dropping his elbow, which was good advice, and he didn't take it."

Somebody near the back laughed, and then several people did, and the man in the academy coat went a slow dark red.

Vell set her pen down.

Cael had learned what that meant. It was the moment at which she stopped being patient and became merely clear. "You lost to a fighter who'd prepared better than you," she said, "under terms you agreed before the first *begin*, in front of a full bench, with a man in a red neckcloth shouting advice at both of you, and you didn't take yours. That's what happened. That's the line I wrote. It stands."

The man drew a breath. Cael saw his hand go to his coat, to the pocket where the paper was, and stop short of it.

"My facility is guild-registered," he said. "I can make this a formal complaint. To the Compact. They'd have an inspector down here in a month."

The benches went still. It was not a loud stillness. It was the stillness of seventy people who have watched a man pick up a knife at a dinner table and are waiting to see what he means to cut.

Vell looked at him. She did not look frightened, or angry, or even very interested. She looked as she looked when a fighter tried a move she had seen a hundred times before, and she said, without raising her voice at all:

"Do that, and you won't fight in any circuit from here to the coast. The keepers talk."

She let it lie a moment, and then she went on, more gently, which was worse.

"Not because I'll tell them to. Because they'll ask. Every keeper between here and the sea will want to know why a Blade from a guild house brought a Compact inspector into a ledger over a lost bout. And when they've heard, they'll write you *unrated*, and they'll never write you anything else, and they'll be polite about it." She picked her pen back up. "Go home. Come back when you can lose a line in my book and still look at it. Most people manage it, in the end. It's cheaper."

He stood there. He looked, once, toward the younger man in the academy coat at the back, and the younger man was looking at the floor.

Then he went, without the paper ever leaving his pocket.

Vell dipped her pen and wrote the first bout's names under the date. Cael looked at the water clock on the wall behind Dace's slate. It had taken a little under four minutes.

---

He did not go to her table until the card was done and the benches were emptying, because he had learned that Vell answered questions best when the room had stopped watching her answer them.

"You're writing down how I do it," she said, before he could open his mouth. She did not look up from the day's last line. "I can see your pencil from here. You've written *one* and *two* and *three* and a ring round *four minutes*."

"You do it the same every time."

"Near enough. Thirty years is a lot of practice at a small number of arguments." She blotted. "There aren't many ways for a man to tell you he lost unfairly. When you've heard them all, you stop needing to think. You only need to do it in front of people, so they can see it's the book deciding and not me."

He had written it as four steps, on the page, with a box round them. First, strip the claim down to a question about a fact, so that it could not stand up on its adjectives. Second, go to the record, with your hands, where people can see you do it, even when you know the answer, because the going is what makes it the record's answer and not yours. Third, if it is needed, call one witness by name and ask one question that anybody can hear. Fourth, put the pen down and say it once, in as few words as it takes, and pick the pen up again.

He showed it to her. She read it, and pushed it back.

"You've left out the fifth," she said.

"What's the fifth?"

"Don't enjoy it." She capped her ink. "The day you enjoy it, you start looking for it."

Then she sat back in her chair with her hands folded on the closed book. She looked not at him but at the big door, where the late light was coming in long across the stone, and said, in a different voice, as though it had nothing to do with anything:

"Dace says there were two grey coats on the row yesterday."

"There were."

"That's five this season, by my count. I keep it in the back of the book now, in pencil, so I can rub it out if I ever feel foolish." She did not look at him. "When I first sat at a table in this district, they'd come through twice a year to read the boards and buy a pie. I've had more sweeps through these streets in the last six months than in ten years before them, Cael. I'm not a woman who frightens. But something's got them counting, and when people start counting, they generally know what they're hoping to find."

He kept his face where it was. He had a fair idea what had got them counting. It was on the officials page in a column of dates with nothing written beside it. There was no evidence for the thing he wanted to write, only the shape of it, and the shape was a boy who had come in through the west gate a year ago with a word on him. The registry's word for him was *Shattered*. It had been written against four people before him. Three of them had been dead within weeks of their Kindling, and the fourth was a line that said nobody knew.

"That's interesting," he said, which was true, and was about a tenth of what was true.

Vell looked at him then. It was a long look, longer than the answer deserved. He watched her see that there was more behind it, and watched her decide, as she generally did with him, not to go in after it.

"Keep your address straight in the lodgers' book," she said. "Keep your name spelt the way the registry spells it. Pay your rent on the day. If anybody wants a reason to trouble you, make them work for it." She stood, and put the book under her arm. "I'd hate to have to write *unrated* beside you, after all this."

"You won't have to."

"I know. That's why I've only told you once."

He wrote, that evening, under the box with the four steps and the fifth that Vell had given him, a line about the keepers. It had struck him, watching the guild man's face, that Vell had no seal and no law and no clerk behind her. It did not matter, because she had something that worked the same way and was harder to argue with. There was a keeper at Fenrow and a keeper at the salt end and keepers in towns he had never heard of, every one of them with a book. Every one of those books was, in a way, open to all the others. Nobody had written it down as a rule. Nobody needed to. A man could quarrel with one table and find, the next month, that a hundred tables had heard. *It isn't a law,* he wrote. *It's a circuit of its own, made of keepers instead of fighters, and it runs on the same thing the floor does. Everybody in it has decided it's worth more to them kept than broken.*

---

She had an errand in the back room before she went home, and she let him carry the lamp.

A widow from the brickworks end had come to the table the week before with a story about her husband, who had died the winter before last. He had told her, she said, that he had fought in Ardenmere as a young man, under a keeper with one arm, before ever Vell's time. The widow wanted to know whether it was true. She did not want anything else; she only wanted to know whether a thing her husband had told her across forty years of suppers had been a story or a fact. Vell had told her to come back in a week.

"There was a keeper with one arm," said Vell, holding the lamp's hood while Cael lit it. "Before my keeper. I've heard of him. If her man fought for him, he's in one of these."

She took the lamp from him and went past the long wall of her own books, past the bows of cotton tape all sitting at one angle, to the end of the room, where there was a cupboard he had never noticed. It was built into the wall and painted the same dull brown as the wall, with a plain iron latch. She opened it.

The books inside were not tied with tape. They were not the same height. Some were tall and thin, and some were fat and square, and two were hardly books at all, only bundles of pages sewn between boards. Their spines were cracked, and some of them had been mended, by different hands at different times. One had a new spine of good brown leather that had clearly been put on long after the pages inside it were written, and somebody had paid a binder real money to do it.

"Mine are mine," said Vell. "Thirty years. These were his, the keeper I learned under, and before that whoever he learned under, and some of them he had from further back still. Forty years of books in this room, if you count the ones I kept with him before he died, and a good deal more than forty if you count these." She held the lamp up to the cupboard. "The one-armed man's will be on the second shelf."

It was. She found the widow's husband in twenty minutes of careful turning. The hand sloped hard to the right and spelt *Copper* with two *p*s and one *e*. There was a young man from the brickworks, three bouts, two lost, one won, and beside the won one, in the third column, a single word that meant *brave*. Vell copied it onto a slip for the widow in her own square hand, and Cael watched her do it and thought that there were people who would walk a long way for a slip like that.

Then she put the book back, and her hand went along the shelf below, to a row of the oldest ones, and rested there on a spine so cracked the boards hung on it by threads.

"These are older again," she said. "Older than this building. Older than the one it replaced, if you believe what my keeper said, and I generally did." She did not take the book down. She only stood with her fingers resting against it. "The oldest ones use different words for things."

"Different how?"

She did not answer at once. The lamp hissed. Out on the main floor the sweeper's broom went along the stone, and stopped, and went again.

"That's an afternoon, not a minute," she said at last. "And these don't care to be opened for a minute. Every time they're opened, a little more of them goes." She took her hand off the spine. "I'll show you properly. Sometime when we've both got the afternoon."

"Soon?"

"Sometime." She shut the cupboard, and dropped the latch, and turned with the lamp. "I showed you the board on the wall because you asked me the right question. I showed you this because you carried my lamp and didn't touch anything. Don't make me sorry for either."

He went out ahead of her into the dark of the main floor, and waited while she locked the door behind them. He did not look back at the cupboard, because she was watching to see whether he would.

But he wrote it down that night, by itself, on a line with nothing else on it, in the grey book.

*The oldest ones use different words. Ask again. Not yet.*

---

Lira was cooking when he got back, which meant the whole back of the house smelt of onions, and the heavyset man had put his head round the kitchen door twice to see whether anything was on fire.

She cooked the way she fought, all at once and with total commitment, in a single iron pan she had bought from a widow and would not let anyone else wash. She did not cook often. When she did, she cooked enough for four, and they ate it for two days, and on the second day she always said it was better than on the first, and it always was.

"Your keeper sent a guild boy home with the whole coastline round his neck," she said, without turning round from the stove. "The baker's girl says Vell stood up to do it."

"She didn't stand up."

"I knew she didn't. She never has to. That's what makes it frightening." She stirred. "Renn's telling everybody about the elbow. He wasn't even there. He's very pleased with the elbow."

He sat down at the end of the table in the back kitchen with the grey book, and did not open it, and watched her cook. Then, because it had been sitting in him all evening, under the dispute and the cupboard and the sweep, he said it.

"I want to run the landing."

Lira did not stop stirring, but her shoulders changed. He saw her hear it.

"Properly?"

"Properly. Full pace. You don't pull off it. You hit the lock every time I land. Every time, as hard as you'd hit somebody who meant it." He turned the closed book on the table. "I've been testing everything else round it for a month, and it's been sitting there in the middle of the page with nothing beside it but your sentence. Somebody's going to wait for it soon. I've seen the people on the benches. Some of them aren't watching me for the fun of it."

She took the pan off the heat and turned round and leaned against the stove with the spoon in her hand.

"How many?"

"Ten. That's what I can afford in a morning, near enough. It'll cost me most of a day after."

"It'll cost you most of two." She looked at him a long moment, and he watched her do the arithmetic, the same arithmetic he had done, of what ten bursts did to a hip and a chest and a breath, and then add something to it that was hers and that he could not see. "All right. Tomorrow. Early, before Renn comes in to tell everybody about the elbow again."

"Thank you."

"I haven't done anything yet." She pointed the spoon at him. "And I've got a condition."

"Go on."

"I count." She turned back to the pan. "Every landing, I count the lock with my own breath, out loud. You can't count it from inside. You think you can. You can't; you're too busy being a post." She put the pan back on the heat. "And if I say stop, we stop, and you don't argue with me till you've eaten something."

"That's two conditions."

"Then I've got two," said Lira. "And a question." She put a plate in front of him and stood over him with the spoon, which made the question feel like a third condition. "Where do I hit you?"

"Wherever you'd hit somebody you meant to hit."

"That's the collarbone, mostly. Or the ribs, on the side you've got the old bruise." She considered him. "I won't do the hip. You'll need the hip."

"Hit what you'd hit. If I only get hit where it's polite, I'll learn what's polite."

She looked at him with the spoon in her hand. Somewhere in the front of the house the heavyset man said something to somebody about onions, loudly, in the voice of a man who has opened a window and wants it known.

"All right," said Lira. "The collarbone, mostly. You'll be sorry."

"I'll be sorry and I'll know something."

"That's you all over," said Lira. She sat down across from him with her own plate, and they ate in the onion smell with the window open, not talking much. Once or twice he caught her looking at him with the face she wore at the rope when she was adding up a fighter she had never seen before.

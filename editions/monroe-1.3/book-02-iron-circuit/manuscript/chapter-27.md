# Chapter 27 — For Science

On the fourth day he went up the market steps without the stick, slowly, with his hand on the rail and his knees giving him their opinion of every tread. At the top he stood in the cold square by the pump and waited to see whether they would let him come down again. They did. He went down and up a second time to be sure, and an old woman shelling the last of somebody's dried beans on the wall watched him do it with deep suspicion.

He gave Lira back her measuring stick that night at the kitchen table.

"You're going," she said.

"In the morning."

She turned the stick over in her hands, looking at the notches, the half-body mark and the hand's-width mark from the spring.

"Don't let him be kind to you," she said. "If he's kind to you you'll only learn what he's like being kind." She stood the stick against the wall by the stairs, where it lived. "And don't come home with your knees like that again. I haven't got the pump water in me twice."

"You could come and see."

"I could." She went up two stairs, and stopped, and leaned on the rail. "I've got the dock partner at first light. He's paid for. And you've had my stick four days, and I've missed it." She looked down at him over the rail. "Tell me after. You're good at after."

He went down the hill in the morning in the full dark, with a lamp, before the frost had been walked on. The row was white and silent. His knees took the slope one careful step at a time and did not complain about anything they had not complained about yesterday, which he chose to count as a kind of permission. At the bottom of the row the baker's window was lit and smelled of the first batch. The Ironyard's side door was barred on the inside still, and he had to knock for the night man, who let him in without a word and went back to his blanket by the brazier.

Brom was not there when he came in at the side door before light. The Ironyard was black and cold, and the sweepers had not come, and the only lamp lit was the one Cael carried. He went across the floor to the second alcove, Brom's, and hung the lamp on its hook, and sat on the bench against the back wall where he had sat for twelve days with his fingers on his wrist.

It felt wrong to sit there. He got up and stood in the middle of the alcove instead, on the stone where the newcomers had stood, and waited.

Brom came in a little after, with frost on his shoulders and his breath going up, and stopped in the arch at the sight of him.

"You're standing where they stand."

"I'm going to be hit where they're hit."

Brom considered that, and seemed to find nothing wrong with it. He took his coat off and hung it on its nail. Under it his shirt was open at the throat, and when he reached up for the nail the shirt pulled, and Cael saw, low on his right side under the arm, a bruise. It had gone the dull yellow-green a bruise goes at about a week, and it was the size of a boy's fist.

Brom saw him see it.

"Yours," he said.

He said it flatly, as he said everything. But he put his hand on the place for a moment, flat, before he started winding his hands, and Cael had the odd feeling that the big man was rather pleased to have it.

"Two more terms," said Brom, winding. "Now that we're doing it and not talking about it. The three from the wall stand."

"Go on."

"When something happens to you, you tell me. Then. Not at the end of the morning when you've worked out what it was. If all you've got is *something*, say *something*." He finished the left hand. "I've stood close to you for a month and the read's told me things it's never told me about anybody. If one of them's happening, I want to know it's happening while I'm feeling it."

"Yes."

"And what you learn about how I do it," said Brom, beginning the right hand, "stays in this alcove. Not to the keeper. Not to the slate. Not to anybody who might stand across a rope from me. It's not a show I'm giving you. It's the thing itself, and there's only one of it."

Cael hesitated, and Brom saw that too, and stopped winding.

"Keth," said Cael. "I owe Keth something. He asked me, before Tuesday, to tell him what's in the middle. The gap. I said I would when I'd found it."

Brom thought about that.

"Tell him what you found about watching," he said at last. "Not what you found about me." He went back to the cloth. "That's yours. You can give it to who you like."

"Agreed. Both of them."

"Then what are you testing?"

Cael had known that would come first, and he had his answer, and it was a plain one.

"What you are from a hand away," he said. "Everything I've got on you I got from a bench. It's true from a bench. I found out on Tuesday what a bench is worth."

Brom nodded once and set his feet.

---

They could not spar the ordinary way, because of Cael's knees, and Brom did not try.

He set them on two chalk crosses a forearm apart and said that nobody was to step off his cross. Cael was to hit him, anywhere above the belt, as hard as he liked, without the thing from his middle; Brom would answer as he answered anybody; and when Cael had been answered he was to come back to his cross and do it again. It was a stupid way to fight and a very good way to find something out. With his feet fixed and nowhere to go, there was nothing in the whole of Cael's body to attend to except the place where his fist met the man.

He hit him on the forearm, and found out at once how little a bench had told him.

From the bench the breath had been a thing he saw: a belly going still under a shirt. From a forearm away it was a thing he heard, low, a short pull of air through the nose and then nothing, a held silence he could have counted on his fingers.

From the bench the hold had been a fist stopping dead on an arm. From his own knuckles it was the feeling of a blow going down out of his hand into a man and not coming back for the length of a beat. It went down the way a stone goes down a well, so that he could feel the whole weight of what he had thrown leave him and go somewhere far below. Then the turn came up out of the well, and it was his own weight returned to him with an angle in it, through the bones of the hand and the wrist and the elbow, and his arm swung wide on a line he had never sent it.

He came back to his cross and did it again. And again. He hit the forearm, the shoulder, the chest, the forearm, while the alcove went from black to grey round the lamp and the cold came up out of the stone.

He learned things in that hour that he could not have learned in a year of benches. He learned that the hold had a texture: when it was new it was soft at the very start, like a hand closing, and only then hard, and when it had seen a thing three times it was hard from the first touch, like a door already shut. The recovery had a texture too. If he hit in it, before the next low breath, the arm he hit was only an arm, and it gave, and it was warm, and he could feel the man's pulse in it.

And every one of these things cost him. He had always known a map of a man who hit back would be bought with bruises. He had not known how many.

Halfway through the hour Brom spoke, between one hit and the next, without moving off his cross.

"You're louder close."

Cael stopped with his fist drawn back.

"On the read." Brom's eyes rested on him as they always did, but there was something working behind them. "At a bench's length you're faint. You're a step upstairs in stockings. Here you're a man walking about in boots." He thought. "Still not on a shelf. Still moving round something in the middle I can't find. But louder." He tipped his head. "And the thing like a Wind's gone quiet. In your hip. It's been dark since Tuesday."

"I haven't asked it for anything since Tuesday."

"I know. I'm telling you what I've got." Brom set his feet again. "That's the term."

Cael thought about that while he hit the forearm twice more and was thrown twice. It was a strange thing, being told what he was from outside, by somebody who had no reason to make it kinder or worse. He had been told what he was by a clerk, once, in one word. He had never been told it by somebody who was measuring.

"Does it change?" he said, coming back to his cross. "When I hit you. Does the middle move?"

"It leans. Toward your arm, a little, every time you throw. Like a crowd leaning toward a door when somebody comes in." Brom shrugged with one shoulder. "Everybody's does that. Yours does it from a place I can't see."

Cael did not write it down, because he did not have to. He knew he would remember it word for word for the rest of his life.

By the end of the hour both his forearms were blue from the wrist to the elbow on the inside, where the returns had come up through them, and his hands had swollen enough that the knuckles had gone smooth. He sat on the bench afterward to write, and found he could not close his fingers properly round the pencil.

"Write it tomorrow," said Brom, who was unwinding.

"I'll lose half of it by tomorrow."

"Then write half." Brom flexed his own left hand, open and shut, and looked at it. "You'll have more tomorrow."

Cael wrote the half with the pencil held in his fist like a child's, in letters three times their size. *Hold: soft first when it's new; hard from the first touch by the third time. Recovery: an arm. Warm. I could feel his pulse in it.* He stopped, because *I could feel his pulse in it* was not a measurement. He left it.

---

On the second hour of the same morning, when his hands had stopped shaking, he asked to do the thing he had written in ink that he would never do.

"What are you testing?" said Brom.

"What the giving face costs me against you." Cael stood on his cross. "I wrote a rule. *Not once.* In ink, in the Log. I kept it on Tuesday, and it cost me ground both times. I want to know whether the rule's right, or whether it's only frightened."

Brom looked at him.

"How much?"

"A quarter. On the beat. Into your forearm, where you're surest." Cael had worked it out lying in bed with the cold cloths, and he had worked it out carefully. "A quarter's less than I've ever thrown. If it comes back the way I think, a quarter of it coming back is a thing I can stand. If it doesn't come back at all, the rule's wrong and I've lost a bout for it."

"And if it comes back worse than you think?"

"Then I'll know that, too."

Brom set his feet.

"Say when."

Cael found his beat, which was easy; it had been easy since the river-academy man. He let it come up through his feet and his breath until he could feel it, steady, and on the fourth count he threw the short straight strike and let the hollow under his ribs give a little of itself into it. A quarter. Less than he had ever let go. It went out along his arm into his fist, a small dense warmth, and his fist went into Brom's forearm on the beat with a weight in it that his arm had not made.

He heard the breath go in.

It came back up his arm at the size he had fed it, and then some more, on its angle. It did not come back as a quarter; it came back as everything he had put in, his arm and his weight and the warm dense thing from his middle, all together, turned and handed to him through the forearm and the elbow and up into the shoulder.

The shoulder took it like a door taking a kick. He went round on his cross; his feet stayed on it, which was the stupid part, and his body went round over them, and kept going, and he sat down on the alcove floor with his legs crossed under him and his right shoulder ringing like a bell somebody had hit with a hammer.

He sat there.

Somewhere above him, Brom laughed.

It was not a large laugh. It was a short one, out through the nose, the sound a man makes when a thing happens that is as funny as he had hoped it would be. But Cael had never heard him make any kind of laugh before, in a month of being near him, and he looked up from the floor in such surprise that he forgot the shoulder for a moment.

Brom had his hand over his mouth, and he took it away.

"Was that the answer?" he said.

"It was the answer I'd written down." Cael got his good hand on the stone and pushed himself up, slowly, with the shoulder going *bell, bell, bell*. "Now I've had it."

"It's the same answer."

"It isn't." He stood on his cross and rolled the shoulder, carefully, and found it went round, though it complained. "I'd written it down. I had the reasons. I'd have bet my purse on it. But I'd never felt it, and a thing you've only worked out can turn out wrong the first time it's dear." He looked at the forearm he had just hit. "Prediction was only a map. Knowledge was the ground."

Brom looked at him; the laugh had gone out of his face, but something was left behind in it that had not been there before.

"A quarter cost you a shoulder."

"A quarter cost me a shoulder. On Tuesday, the whole of it, into your ribs, would have cost me the bout in the first. Probably the shoulder for a month as well." He nodded at the forearm. "The rule was right."

"You paid a shoulder for a sentence you'd already got."

"I had it in pencil," said Cael. "It's in ink now." He set his feet. "Again. Unamplified this time."

---

The second morning his forearms had gone dark blue and he could lift the right arm only to the height of his ear, and they went on anyway, because nobody had broken.

He had come with a question this time, and he said it before Brom asked.

"When you hold a thing and send it back, and when you only hold it." Cael stood on his cross. "From the bench, they look the same until the end. From the knuckles yesterday I thought they felt different. I want to know if they are."

Brom looked at him with interest.

"They are," he said. "I won't tell you how. Find it."

It took most of the hour. Some of the answers threw him; some only stopped him dead and let him go, his fist falling away off a hardness that sent nothing back at all. He knew that much already; what he wanted was to know, from his own hand, before the end came, which one it was going to be.

He found it in the first touch.

When Brom meant only to stop a thing, the hardness was flat. It met the fist like a wall, square, all at once, with nothing in it leaning anywhere. When he meant to send it back, it was not quite flat: there was a tilt in it, in the very first instant of the knock, before the hold had properly begun. It was the smallest lean, like a door that is closed but not latched and gives a little toward the side it is going to swing to, and it was there before the well, in the touch itself.

If he kept his attention entirely in the skin of his own knuckles and nowhere else, he could feel which way the door was hung before it opened.

He said so, badly, because there were no words for it yet, with his hand held up in front of him in the lamplight and the knuckles turned toward the big man.

"A door that's not latched," he said. "You know which way it'll swing before it swings. Because it's already given a hair."

Brom did not answer at once. He stood on his cross with his hands at his sides, and then he did a thing he had not done all morning: he hardened, and Cael felt the tilt, and he did not send. He only stopped, and then he did it again the other way: no tilt, flat as a wall, and then, at the last instant of the hold, the turn came anyway, rough and late, and threw Cael off his cross.

Cael picked himself up.

"You're testing something."

"Yes."

"You said every time. That we'd say."

Brom looked at him, and the corner of his mouth went in.

"So I did," he said. "I'm testing whether I can put the lean in where I don't mean it, and take it out where I do. Whether I can lie to your knuckles." He considered. "I can. Not well. It costs me a beat to do it, and the turn comes out ugly. Anybody who could feel the lean would feel the beat."

"Has anybody ever felt the lean?"

"Not that I know of," said Brom. "I didn't know it was there till you told me."

"Then why did you try?"

"Because you'd found it." Brom looked at his own forearm, turning it slowly in the lamplight. "The first thing I thought, when you said it, was who else could. Somebody with good hands and a fortnight. Somebody across a rope." He let the arm fall. "So I wanted to know if I could hide it. I can, a little. It costs." He looked at Cael. "Now you know that too. That's two things you know about me this morning that I didn't know at breakfast."

"Do you mind?"

Brom seemed to think about it seriously, with his whole attention, as he had thought about *Does it bother you* in the alcove with the cloth half off his hand.

"No," he said. "I mind not knowing more."

Cael wrote that down standing up, at the alcove wall, in the large clumsy letters his hands would allow. *Sending: a lean, in the first touch, before the hold. Stopping: flat. He can fake it, a beat late, ugly. He didn't know it was there.* Then, under it, because it was the strangest part: *He learned something about himself from my hand this morning.*

---

It was in the third hour of that second morning that Cael understood Brom was being careful with him.

He did not notice it all at once. The answers had been getting shorter. They still came, and they still hurt, but they sent him a step where on the first morning they had sent him two, and they turned him in a direction that put his weight over his good knee and not his bad one.

Twice, when Brom felt Cael's legs waver on the cross, the hold simply went on a little longer than it needed to and then let him go without any turn at all. And when Cael had sat down hard on the alcove floor at the end of the last one, Brom had put a hand under his elbow before he could get up on his own.

He stood on his cross and looked at the big man, and Brom looked back.

It took him a while to say anything, because there was a part of him that did not want to. Being sent one step was better than two, and a turn over the good knee better than one over the bad. A hand under the elbow was a kind thing, and he had had very few kind things done to him by people who could hurt him, and there was something in him that wanted to stand on the cross and let it go on. He noticed that part of himself wanting it, and was ashamed of it, and then was not ashamed, because it was only tired.

Then he thought about the column. Twelve days of honest numbers, from a man who had chosen not to lie to it, and every one of them had been the man fighting twelve-year-olds.

"You're going easy," said Cael.

"Your knees."

"My knees are the third term. If they go, I'll say. You'll hear me say it." He held his arms out, the blue forearms, the swollen hands. "This isn't them going. This is me finding out what you are. And for an hour now I've been finding out what you are when you're being careful with somebody, and I don't need that. I've got that already. I watched you be careful with twelve-year-olds for twelve days."

Brom did not answer.

"You said, on the wall. All of it, every time, or I'd get half the truth and not know which half." Cael let his arms drop. "That goes both ways. If you send me a step because of my knees, I'll write down that you send people a step, and I'll be wrong, and I won't know I'm wrong until somebody faster than me sends me two." He thought of the middle column, true and out of date. "I've done that once. Please stop being careful with me."

The alcove was very quiet, and out on the floor the sweepers had come at last, and the long patient sound of their brooms came in through the arch.

Brom looked down at him, and his face did nothing that Cael could have put a hook to. Then he looked away, at the wall, and Cael saw that he was not deciding whether to agree. He was deciding whether he could.

"In the river city," Brom said, "there was a man I trained with. Older. He used to say a partner who's careful with you is a partner who's decided you can't take it." He moved one thumb against the side of his leg. "I hated that. I never wanted to be that." He looked back at Cael. "I didn't notice I was doing it."

"I know. That's why I said."

"Tomorrow, then," said Brom. "Properly. Everything. And if your knees go you say *knees*, one word, and we stop, and I won't argue."

"And if your read tells you something, you say."

"I'll say." Brom bent and picked his coat off the floor, where it had fallen from the nail at some point, and hung it back up, and stood with his hand on it. "Nobody's asked me that before," he said, to the coat. "To stop being careful. They always ask the other thing."

---

He went to find Keth in the dusk, at the far end of the floor, where the newcomers' ring was rubbed into the stone in its ghost circles.

Keth was alone, sitting on the edge of a bench with his practice blade across his knees, watching the sweepers. He did not look round when Cael sat down at the other end of the bench, slowly, with his knees and his arms.

"You look like you've been through a mill-race," said Keth.

"I've been in the big man's alcove."

"I can see." Keth's little finger came off the grip and went back. "You lost well on Tuesday. Everybody says so, the ones who know what they're saying. The rest are still talking about the third." He did not ask about the third. "You said you'd tell me what was in the middle."

"I said when I'd found it." Cael looked out at the ring. "I've found part of it. Not about him. About it."

"Go on."

"It learns." He turned his swollen hands over on his knees. "That's the first thing. The gap after the hit, before the answer. It gets shorter every time it sees the same thing. Three times, and it's half what it was. So a fighter who wants the gap long has to keep showing new things, and most don't, because most fighters have three things they're good at and they use those." He thought about it. "And the second thing is that it's the wrong place to look. I looked there for a fortnight because it was the only place nobody else was looking. But by the time the middle starts, the man's already got your hit. The truth isn't in the middle. It's at the very start of the touch. Before anything."

Keth was quiet a long while. His finger lifted and settled on the grip.

"The start of the touch," he said.

"In the skin. If you keep all of yourself there and nowhere else." Cael shrugged, and his shoulder told him not to. "I don't know if it's true of anybody but him. I've only had my hand on him."

"It's true of me," said Keth. He said it slowly, as if he had not known it until he heard it said. Then he stood and lifted his blade, though there was nobody in the ring to lift it at, and looked down the length of it at nothing. "Thank you. That was worth the waiting." He walked off toward the side door, and stopped, and said without turning round: "Whatever you're doing in that alcove, mind the knees. A man who reads like you needs to be standing up to do it."

Cael sat on the bench a while after he had gone, in the last of the light from the high windows, with his hands turned up on his knees.

He had given away something true and kept something else, and both had been his to give or keep, and he had done it as Brom had asked. It was a small thing, and it felt larger than it was. He thought that for a year he had kept everything he found in a book with his own name on the front, because nobody had ever asked him for anything in it that he could give without giving himself away. Keth had asked. Brom had told him where the line ran between the part that was his and the part that was not. And it turned out that a thing found and given away was not smaller afterward. It was in two places.

At home Lira was at the kitchen table with the lamp, with her own wrist in a fresh cloth and her bad arm gone from plum to green. She looked at his forearms when he took his coat off, both of them, the insides blue from wrist to elbow, and counted under her breath without seeming to know she was doing it. Then she got up and fetched the bucket from the yard.

"Not the knees," she said, putting a cold cloth round his right forearm. "You kept the knees."

"He was careful with them."

"Today."

"Today," said Cael. "Tomorrow he's going to stop."

Lira wrapped the left forearm and tied the cloth off, harder than she needed to. She looked at the knot.

"Good," she said. "That's what you went for." She did not say anything about Brom at all, but she put the bucket where he could reach it, and before she went up she laid two fingers on the back of his swollen hand, very lightly, the way you touch a stove to see whether it is still hot.

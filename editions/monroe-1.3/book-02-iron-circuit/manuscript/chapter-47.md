# Chapter 47 — It Compresses Inward

On her second morning, the Friday, Lira gave him his right foot, as she had promised at the kitchen table, and he did not like it, as she had promised too.

She began by making him stand on a chalk cross in the middle of the alcove floor with his weight even. Then she came at him from his left with nothing before it, and from his front, and from his right. Every time she came from the right she watched his feet and not his face, and every time she said the same thing in the same flat voice: "Late."

"I know it's late."

"Then why is it late?"

He did not know; he had thought for a year that his right foot was slow because he was made that way, like the hip that only went left. But Lira had taken that from him at the kitchen table, and he had nothing to put in its place.

"Because you step with it," she said, after the tenth. "You pick it up and put it down somewhere else, like a man crossing a stream on stones. Your left doesn't do that. Your left never steps; the hip takes it. You've been going left on the Wind so long that you've forgotten how a body goes anywhere without one." She came round behind him and put the end of her staff against the back of his left knee, lightly. "When it comes from the right, don't step. Drop."

"Drop where?"

"Into this knee. All of you, at once, as if somebody had cut a string." She pressed the staff a little. "And as you go down, roll the weight across. Off the left and onto the right, like a barrel rolled off a cart. Your right foot doesn't have to go anywhere. It's already there. You just arrive on it, lower than you were, and a body's width to the right." She stood back. "Low and right, all in one piece. A man who fires at where your head was finds your head has gone down and over. Show me."

He showed her, and it was terrible: he dropped and forgot to roll, and sat down on the stone. He rolled and forgot to drop, and went sideways at the height of a man standing up, a perfect target, and she tapped him on the ear with the staff to show him how perfect. He dropped and rolled both at once and arrived on his right foot so low and so far over that he could not get up again in time for anything, and she stood over him and waited, and said nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said.

By the end of the hour he had done it perhaps sixty times, and perhaps four of them had looked like the thing she meant.

What made it hard was not the knee, though the knee would have its say later, and not the roll. It was that his body had never in its life been asked to do this and did not believe it could. Going left, it had a year of the hip behind it, a year of a borrowed thing that knew the way already, so that his own legs had only ever had to follow where the Wind took them. Going right, there was nothing to follow. Every part of the drop had to be put together from the beginning by a body that was already sure it was bad at it, and every time it began, some old part of him would try to call the hip instead, and find the hip would only go left, and stall.

He had felt like this once before, in Lira's angle-denial footwork a season ago, standing in the alcove with his feet in the wrong places while she counted. He had forgotten how much he had hated it, and how much it had given him.

"That's four," said Lira. She was breathing hard too; she had been coming at him from the right the whole hour, and her left knuckle was talking, he could see it in her mouth. "Four in an hour. It'll be four hundred before it's yours, and it isn't yours till you do it without asking. Tonight, after supper, again."

"Every night?"

"Every night." She leaned the staff against the wall. "My lamp's dark this fortnight, in case you were going to ask what I'm doing about my own hands. They can wait two weeks. They've waited a year."

He looked at her. The lamp in the north window had been burning nearly every night since Maud, past one and two in the morning, and he had lain awake and seen it and not gone down, because the lamp was hers and the hands were hers and she had asked him to leave them alone. He understood that she was giving him something she had not given anybody, and that she would be angry if he said so.

"You don't have to do that."

"I know I don't." She did not look at him. She was opening and closing her left hand, slowly. "It's not a present, so don't thank me for it. I'll have them back the night after the floor and work twice as hard. But I'm not going to stand in front of you every morning half asleep from teaching my own hands at two in the morning, and give you half a teacher." She picked the staff up again. "Go and get your breakfast. Brom wants the middle, and he says he's got something to show you, and he's been insufferable about it since breakfast."

---

What Brom had to show him was a thing Brom could not quite do, and said so before he began.

"I'm not a Pressure," he said. He was standing in the middle of the alcove with his coat on its nail and his sleeves turned back, and his big forearms bare. "I can't throw what this man throws, and nobody in this district can that I know of. So I'll show you the nearest thing I've got, and you'll have to forgive it for being slow."

"Show me."

"You know how I harden." Brom raised his right forearm across his body, as he did against the post. "A low breath, held, while it comes. Then the hold, when it lands. Then I send, if I mean to. Most of the time you only see the send. You've felt the rest from the inside, with your knuckles." He lowered the arm again. "Ansel said there's nothing coming up through this man to watch. I believe him. But I've been lying awake on it, and there has to be something, because that's how a burst works. You can't throw a thing out of you that you haven't first got together. It's only that he does the getting-together so fast and so small that nobody sees it from outside."

He set his feet and breathed in, low, and Cael watched his whole frame tighten, not outward, as a man braces, but inward, everything drawing toward the middle of him, smaller and heavier at once. It lasted perhaps half a beat, and then Brom let it go, all at once, forward, in a short flat push of both hands that stopped just short of Cael's chest and moved the air against his shirt.

"It compresses inward before it fires outward."

Brom said it slowly. "That's the burst. It's got to be. It pulls in first and then lets go, and the letting go is the bit everybody sees. But the pulling in is there, every time, for whatever time it takes him. Mine takes half a beat, and you could see it from the bench. His takes next to nothing." He stood easy again. "So you'll not see it. You've no time to see it. But you might feel it."

Cael understood him before he had finished. "The knock."

"The knock." Brom nodded. "At a forearm, you can tell me whether I'm gathering and which way my weight's going. Whether I mean it. A man pulling in to fire has his weight going one way only, and it's inward, toward his own middle, and nothing else in a fight does that. If the knock can catch that, even once, you'd know he's about to fire before he fires. Not much before. But some."

"May I?"

"You may."

So the second session was that: Brom standing a forearm off, gathering and sending, slowly at first and then less slowly, and Cael knocking at the moment he thought the gathering began, and saying what came back before the push arrived. *Inward*, *nothing*, *inward*, *smear*, *nothing*, *inward*, on and on. The inward answer was like nothing the knock had brought back before, neither *there* nor *going forward*. It was a kind of drawing tight, as though the whole man had become a fist in the making, and it came and went so fast at the edges that he could not have said whether he felt it begin or only felt it already begun. Twice he knocked too late and got the push instead, the weight going outward, already spent. Once he knocked too early and got only Brom, large and still and thinking about nothing, and had to say *nothing* while the gathering began a heartbeat after under his hand.

At twenty he held his hand to the light.

"Fourteen," he said.

"Better than yesterday." Brom shook his arms out. "We'll see what it does when I'm not standing like a gatepost."

Cael sat down on the bench with the band coming up and looked at the back of his hand. Something was bothering him, and it took him a while to find where it was.

"It tells me when," he said at last.

"That's what we wanted."

"Yes. But only when." He turned the hand over. "When you send, there's only one place it ever goes. At me, straight, because we're standing a forearm apart and you're aiming at my chest. So when the knock says *inward*, I know everything, because I already know where it's going. He won't be standing still in front of me. He'll be able to fire it anywhere." He looked at Brom. "The knock catches the pulling in. The pulling in is the same whichever way he means to let it go. The direction doesn't exist yet when I knock. It only exists when he fires."

Brom thought about it with his big face still.

"Yes," he said. "That's right. I'd not thought of it." He rubbed his jaw. "So it gives you the when and not the where."

"Which is something."

"Which is a great deal. And not enough." Brom looked at the arch, where the light was going yellow toward afternoon. "Which is why we're going to do the other thing now. The one with a price on it."

---

"If the read fails," said Brom, "and it will, some of the time, against a real one, you'll be standing in front of a burst you can't get out of the way of. What then?"

"I take it."

"How?"

"The taking face." Cael showed him, standing: the weight going down into the forearm and shoulder over most of a breath, the stance widening, the sink in him that anybody could see. "It spreads a hit. It sends it down through me into the floor."

"Over most of a breath," said Brom. "He'll have hit you, and gone, and thought of his supper, inside most of a breath." He shook his head. "And blocking's no better. Against a burst a block's only a slower way of being hit. All the weight arrives in your arm, and your arm hands it to your shoulder, and your shoulder sits you down. I've watched you block. You block like a door."

"Then what?"

Brom did not answer at once, but went and stood by the straw post, and put his hand on it, and swung it a little on its rope.

"You know how I found my redirect," he said. "In the boat shed, by accident. I hardened at the exact moment, at the exact place, and the man went back along a line he'd never sent his fist on. A hardness laid down at the right instant doesn't only stop a thing; it turns it." He let the post go. "You can't do that. You've no wall. There's nothing in you hard enough to turn a burst back on him. But you've got the other thing, the soft one, the taking. And I think you've been using it wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"You gather." Brom made a fist and drew it in against his chest. "You pull the weight in and hold it and let the hit come into the held place and then spread it out and down into the floor. That's a thing for keeping. It keeps the hit in you and gets rid of it slowly. It's why it's so dear and so slow." He opened the fist. "Don't keep it. Pass it. Let it come in at the shoulder and go out somewhere else, at an angle, through you and on. Plant, take it on the shoulder turned, let it run down through the hip and out past you, and while it's going past, use the push. It'll be shoving you somewhere. Let it shove you in."

Cael stood very still in the middle of the alcove.

"In," he said.

"In, toward him. A man who's just fired has spent it. Whatever he's got left is the next one, and the next one isn't ready." Brom shrugged. "That's the idea. I don't know if it works for you. I've no taking face; I've only read yours in a book. You'll have to find out what it costs."

They found out.

Brom came at him with the slow burst, the gathering and the push, but heavier now, with his weight behind it, and Cael planted and turned his right shoulder to it and let it in. The first time he gathered from habit, and the push sat him down on the stone as it always had, with his shoulder full of sand. The second time he did not gather, but took it on the turned shoulder and let it go, through, down through his ribs and his hip, and out through his back foot into the floor at a slant. For the length of a breath he felt the whole weight of Brom's push run through him like water through a pipe. Then it was gone past him, and it had turned him, and he was half a step closer to Brom than he had been, without having stepped.

He stood there with his mouth open.

Then the cost arrived, all at once and everywhere, in the shoulder first and then in the ribs on that side and then in a place behind his breastbone that he had not known could hurt.

It was not like the Wind's price, which came as a thin bright line in one place, from the hip crest to the knee, so that he always knew what he had spent and where. It was not like the giving face's hollow under the ribs either, or the knock's stripe behind the eyes. It was the whole of a man's push, paid for in one piece by every part of his frame that it had run through on its way out. He could feel the path it had taken by the ache it had left: the shoulder and the side and the hip and the back heel.

"Well," said Brom, watching his face.

"It works," said Cael, when he could. "It costs."

"How much?"

"I'll tell you after the third."

He did the third, and it worked again, a little less cleanly, the push going through him at a slightly wrong angle that he felt in the hip. After it he stood bent over with his hands on his knees, breathing, while the place behind his breastbone told him things.

"Again," said Brom, but quietly, to see what he would say.

Cael straightened up slowly and thought about it, because Brom had asked him straight. His form was already going; he could feel it in the third, the angle wrong, the hip catching. A fourth would go through him crooked, and if he did a crooked fourth he would be teaching his body the crooked one, and his body would remember the crooked one on the floor when he had no time to tell it otherwise.

"No," he said. "Three. A fourth would teach it wrong."

"Three a session, then."

"Three a session. And not every day; the shoulder won't have it." He sat down on the bench very carefully. "Every other day, at most. And that's at your speed. At his I don't know."

He wrote it that evening, in ink, in a new entry under the Pressure fields, in his smallest hand. It was a kind of fact that would matter most at the moment when it was hardest to remember.

*Redirect (taking face, passed, not gathered): plant; take it on the turned shoulder; let it run through and out at a slant; ride the push in. Works at Brom's speed. Cost: shoulder, the ribs on that side, and behind the breastbone, all at once and after, never during. Three clean in a session before the fourth goes crooked, and every other day at most. In a fight, three, or fewer if I've already been hit.*

*This is not an evasion. It is a purchase.*

*Know what I'm buying before I pay for it.*

Brom read it over his shoulder in the kitchen, chewing.

"The good part," he said, "is that whoever you do it to is having a worse day than you."

"And the other part?"

"You've three people's worth of worse days to hand out, and then you're done." Brom swallowed. "Spend them on the right people."

---

On her third morning Lira gave him the right again, and the drop, and the roll. He did it a hundred times and perhaps fifteen of them were the thing she meant. On her fourth it was twenty-five in a hundred, and his left knee, which took all the dropping, had begun to have opinions on the stairs.

The bruises came up in layers, and he began to read them like a page, because he could not help it.

They were a record, if he looked at them properly, and a better one than the Log. The fresh ones on the outside of the right forearm were the drop coming late, every one of them a moment when his knee had hitched and Lira's staff had arrived first. The yellowing ones on the left ribs were the first morning, before he had learned to move at all. There were fewer new ones each day, and they were higher on the arm, which meant the drop was arriving sooner and the staff was finding only the last of him on the way down. He could have charted the whole fortnight from them, if anybody had asked, and he was glad nobody did.

He did not mention any of it, and neither did she. Every morning she looked at him once as he came under the arch, a quick look up and down, the way a farrier looks at a horse led into the yard, and then came at him with nothing before it.

The heavyset man's wife began leaving a covered bowl on the range for him at night, without being asked and without saying so. He found it there after the evening hour with Lira, still warm, with a plate over it. On the second night the sister had written on the plate in chalk, *EAT, L SAYS*, and drawn a hen.

Vell stopped him on the Saturday afternoon, as he came up from the alcove past her table with the band behind his eyes and Brom a step behind. She did not look up from the book, but only lifted her pen off the page, which was how Vell said a person's name across a room, and waited until he had stopped.

"Dace tells me you've a clear card," she said.

"Till the floor."

"Good." The pen went back to the page. "I've had men come down this hill with a story behind them before. Not often. Every one of them wanted a bout the week he arrived, so that he could see what he'd come for and be gone again." She wrote a figure, very small. "This one has asked for two weeks and gone up the hill to wait. He's taken a room over the saddler's, and he walks the river road in the mornings, and he comes nowhere near this building." She looked up then, over the spectacles. "I mention it because it's unusual. I've no opinion on what it means."

"Neither have I."

"No. I'd be surprised if you had." She looked at him a moment longer, at his forearms and at his face. "Are you eating?"

"The whole house is making sure I am."

"Good," said Vell again, and went back to her book, and he was dismissed.

In the third session Brom stood still for the last time.

The knock gave sixteen in twenty, and Cael held up the back of his hand to the light, and Brom looked at the rings and nodded once.

"Four in five," he said. "At rest. Now I move."

He moved the next day, and it fell apart.

It was not that Brom moved quickly. He moved as he always moved, every step weighed first, circling the alcove slowly with his weight low, so that Cael had to move with him to stay a forearm off. But a forearm off was no longer a place; it was a thing Cael had to keep making, every step, and every step he made unsettled the stillness the knock was built on. He planted his foot to knock, as Brom had taught him, and the knock went out, and half the time the answer came back as smear, or as nothing, or as a confused *there* that did not know whether it meant the man or the wall behind him.

Nine in twenty.

He stood in the middle of the alcove after the twentieth with the band stacking behind his eyes and stared at the back of his hand.

"It's gone back past where it started," he said.

"It's gone back to where it is when you move." Brom was not even breathing hard. He sat on the bench and thought. "Plant harder. Don't knock on the way to planting; knock when you've arrived. Half your knocks went out with your foot still coming down."

"I didn't feel them go early."

"I did. You're very loud." Brom stood again. "Tomorrow."

That evening, after Lira's hour, he did the redirect three more times against Brom's slow burst, in the lamplight, with the shoulder still remembering the first three. Then he sat on the bench with his eyes shut and the place behind his breastbone aching, and thought about the number on his hand, and saw that Brom was doing to the knock what Cael did to everybody else on a bench: changing one condition at a time to see where it broke. He had never once thought to do it to himself.

*Nine in twenty, moving,* he wrote that night. *Of the eleven that didn't answer, six went out with my foot still coming down. The read isn't bad in motion. My knocking is. Plant, arrive, then ask.*

Under it, smaller, he wrote: *Six redirects done, six to go.*

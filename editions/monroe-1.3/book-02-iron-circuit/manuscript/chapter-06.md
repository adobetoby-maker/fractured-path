# Chapter 6 — Six Fields

On the third evening after the trials it rained, and the card at the Ironyard was put off because the roof over the north benches leaked onto the bookmakers. Cael sat at the crate desk with both books open in front of him and found he could not tell where one stopped and the other began.

He had been meaning to look at it for weeks. He had kept the slim book apart from the grey one since the day he bought it, on purpose, so that what he saw of other people and what he knew of himself would not get mixed up in the same columns. But over the weeks the edges had blurred anyway. He would be writing up a bout in the grey book, Ulric's heel or Dessa's count, and a line about the burst would come into his head. He would write it in the margin where the pencil already was, meaning to copy it across. Sometimes he did. Often he did not. And the other way about: in the slim book, under the Wind entry, there was a line about how Lira's partner from the docks dropped his guard on the third swing of a combination. It had nothing to do with anything in that book. It had got in because the slim book was the one that happened to be open.

The trials had made it worse. He had written them up on the evening of the second day, lying on his bed with the grey book on his chest because it was the one within reach. So now the most important thing he had learned about himself all year, that the lock was a price and not a fault, lived on page forty-one of a book that was supposed to be about other people, between a sweep and a scheduling board.

He found four other lines like it, once he started looking. A note about the hollow behind his breastbone sat at the bottom of a page about Ulric's heel. A line about the giving face of the Pressure, which had taken a Copper formal's guard down like a door off its hinges in a hot week, was jammed into the margin of a page about Dace's rings. One of Lira's sentences about the lock was in both books, in slightly different words, and he could not now remember which he had written first, or which was nearer to what she had actually said. That last one bothered him most. He had told himself, when he bought the slim book, that it would never hold a word it had not earned. And here was a sentence of Lira's living in two places in two shapes, so that if he ever needed to stand on it he would not know which of them would hold his weight.

He sat and looked at it while the rain came down the window in sheets.

There was a reason it mattered, beyond tidiness, and he made himself say it to himself plainly, because he had been not saying it for weeks. The two books needed two different kinds of honesty. The grey book could afford to be wrong. If he wrote down a tell that was not a tell, the next fight would correct him, usually painfully, and he would cross it out and write the correction beside it, and the book would be the better for having been wrong. Somebody across a rope would always check it for him.

Nobody would ever check the other book. There was no opponent for it. It was the only record anywhere in Valdris of what he was, so far as he could tell what he was. If he wrote something in it that was not so, nothing would correct the mistake. It would simply sit on the page looking like a fact until the day he trusted his weight to it. The grey book was a tool. The slim one had to be held to Vell's standard instead, the standard of the cupboard and the red rules, where an empty line was allowed and a false one was not.

He picked up the slim book and turned it over in his hands. *The fragment book*, he had been calling it, to himself, since he bought it. But *fragment* was the notice's word, not his. The notices had used it on him the way the registry had used *Shattered*, telling him what he was before he had found out. The book was not a list of pieces. It was about what he could do, and what each thing cost him, and how far he could trust it.

He wrote on the inside of the front cover, small: *The Power Log.*

He looked at that a while. It was not a beautiful name. But it was accurate, and tonight he wanted accurate more than he wanted beautiful.

---

Before he designed anything, he went to the shelf and took down the oldest book on it, the first notebook, the one he had carried out of Denvash. He turned to the back and found the page where he had copied Joren's declaration.

He had copied it before he ever left home, because it was the only Path declaration he had ever been shown whole and he had wanted to keep its shape. *PATH DECLARATION — COPPER RANK 1*, it said at the head, and under that the ability's name in square brackets, and then one sentence that said exactly what Joren could do and exactly where it stopped. Everyone who had a Path got those. The Arbiter gave them, each time a rank came, and they were private, and people read their own declarations so often that they could recite them like their own names. They were a set of edges with the measurements written on.

And they went somewhere. That was the other thing about them, and the thing he had thought about most, in his first months, lying awake on his cot at Torvin's with the wheezing man whistling on the next one. A Copper practitioner got a declaration at Rank 1, and another when Rank 2 came, and so on up to ten, each one a little wider than the last, each one telling him exactly how far his fence had moved. At Rank 10 there was a door. You took a guild sponsor's letter to a registered Arbiter station and stood in front of it and were looked at, properly. If the station agreed, you came out Iron, at Rank 1 again, with a new declaration and a new set of edges, one metal further up the hill. Everyone he had ever fought had that ladder inside them, whether they had climbed three rungs of it or nine. Lira had it. She had told him once where she stood on it, and then said that it did not matter, because without a letter nobody was going to let her through the door at the top.

Then he took down the brown Log as well, and opened it at the back, where he had copied his own notices letter for letter as they came, and laid the two books side by side.

His were not like that. They had a heading, and a name that was not a name, *[unnamed]*, and then a row of fields with a word in each. *Duration. Integration. Tier equivalent.* And the words in the fields mostly said that nobody knew. *Undetermined. Partial. Unknown.* There was no sentence at all. Nothing said what he could do, or where it stopped. Whatever wrote the notices did not tell him anything about his edges; it only filled in a form, and most of the form was blank.

Very well, then. He would fill in his own.

He took out the steel straightedge with the inches cut into it, which had cost him the best part of a loser's purse at the paper stall, and ruled the first clean page of the Power Log. He ruled it into three columns, as he ruled everything now: *Claim*, *Evidence*, *Ruling*. The middle column was the one that mattered most, because it was the one that kept him honest; and anything in it that he had not seen with his own eyes, but had been told, would have a small *h* beside it, for *heard*, as it had done in every book he had kept since his first month.

Then, down the side, he wrote the six things he wanted to know about every entry, in an order he would never change, so that half a year on he could open the book anywhere and know at once where to look.

*What it is.* As near as he could say.

*Source.* Where it came from, and when, and how much of that he actually knew.

*Conditions.* Which days it answered, and which days it sulked.

*Deployment range.* What it had actually been seen to do, measured, with no guessing in it at all.

*Costs.* Everything it charged him, item by item, because a thing written down without its price was half written.

*Open questions.* Last, with the most room. He gave it half a page on its own, ruled and empty. The honest proportion between what he knew and what he did not was still about one to three, and he wanted the page to look like that when he opened it, so that he would never be tempted to think otherwise.

At the top of each entry, before any of it, he would copy the notice's own fields exactly, in its own words. He did not trust the notices to tell him much. But they were the only other witness he had, and a witness was a witness.

---

He began with the Wind, because it was the one he knew best and he wanted to make his mistakes on the easier one.

*WIND-ADJACENT.*

*From the notice: Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial. Tier equivalent: unknown.*

*WHAT IT IS. Claim: a burst out of the way of a committed strike, faster than my legs. Evidence: the straw every morning; twelve Copper formals, Ulric the last. Ruling: so.*

*SOURCE. Claim: Lira. Evidence: three months of watching her step before it came; hers was the only Wind I had watched. Ruling: so. How, I don't know. No guess on this page.*

*CONDITIONS. Claim: the worse the moment, the cleaner it comes. Evidence: the testing; Darrow's third, unasked. Ruling: so.*

*DEPLOYMENT RANGE. Claim: half my body's width, to the left, the front-left or the back-left. Evidence: Lira's stick; a hand's width in the spring. Ruling: half a body, and maybe still growing.*

He stopped there and looked at *to the left* for some time. He had written it without thinking, because it was true. Only now, seeing it in ink, did it look odd. Not once, in all the mornings, had it taken him right. He did not know why. He put a small mark beside it and went on.

*COSTS. Breath: half a breath held, every burst. The lock: two of Lira's* ands*, the same every time. Ten trials, nine bursts in one hour, five ways tried to shorten it, and none worked. Ruling: not a fault. A price, and it buys half a breath of only looking. Rule: the burst stays in my hip if anybody close enough to hit me has a hit I haven't priced. The hip: the left, crest to knee, and it adds up; the fruit woman can tell which day it was (h). Ceiling: three in an exchange, probable. Depletion: after nine in an hour, a cold hollow behind the breastbone, and the burst gone for two days. Ruling: once was enough to know.*

*OPEN QUESTIONS. Can three become four with work? What* undetermined *means. Whether the half body will grow. Lira says it has a side it doesn't like (h). I haven't found it yet. I want to.*

He read it back from the top with his finger under each line, the way Hesk checked a drawing before he cut metal. It held. There was nothing on the page he could not point to a morning for, and the open questions had the most room, which was right.

He looked longest at the line he had not written himself, the notice's own words at the head of the page. *Duration: undetermined.* He had copied them as he would copy a witness's answer, letter for letter. He did not know what *duration* meant for a thing that lasted only as long as a strike took to fall. He did not know whether *undetermined* meant that nobody knew yet, or that nothing had been decided. Whatever filled in the notices did not answer questions; it only filled in forms. So he left the words exactly as they had come, and put a small ring round *undetermined*. In the grey book, that ring meant a word an opponent had used that he meant to come back to.

---

The Pressure was harder, and he had known it would be.

It had two faces, and he had only learned the second one by accident. He had less time with it altogether. And the one time it had mattered most, it had not been his to call at all.

The first face was the one he had found at Torvin's trough in the spring, night after night in the yard by the pump, with the water going flat under his hand. It was the taking face. When he called it, a weight came into his forearm and shoulder that was not mass, like bones gone full of water. A blow that landed on the weighted arm did not stay where it landed. It spread, and went down through him, and into the floor. It was slow, though; it needed most of a breath to gather. And it showed: when it came, he settled lower and his feet went wider, and anyone watching could see him decide to be hit.

The second face he had found sparring with Lira's dock partner, the Bronze washout, a few weeks after his ribs had knit. He had thrown his short straight strike on the beat of the man's own rhythm, the way he had been taught. Something in his middle had gone *with* it, out along his arm, and the strike had landed twice as heavy as it had any right to. That was the giving face, and it only worked on the beat. He had found that out twice by trying it off the beat. Both times the weight had gone the wrong way, back up his own arm, and turned his left wrist over as if it had been stepped on.

He wrote it in the same six fields, and kept it shorter than he wanted to, because most of what he wanted to write was guessing. The heart of it went:

*PRESSURE-ADJACENT.*

*From the notice: Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial. Tier equivalent: unknown.*

*WHAT IT IS. Weight without mass, with two faces. Taking: gathered into one arm or one side, it spreads a strike into the floor. Evidence: the trough; Lira's staff, a dozen mornings. Giving: ridden out along my own strike on the other fighter's beat, it lands far heavier. Evidence: the dock man, sitting down; four bouts since. Ruling: both so. Giving on the beat only.*

*SOURCE. Feryn's hand on my shoulder, a beat too long, and the notice that night. One second of his against three months of Lira's. No guess.*

*COSTS. Taking: slow, a plan and not a reflex. It shows, and every time, the room learns I've chosen to be hit. It drains what three bursts do, and the braced arm is sand for two exchanges. Giving: a hollow under the ribs each time, about three in a sequence. Off the beat it comes back up my arm (the left wrist, twice). Ruling: so. Never off the beat.*

*OPEN QUESTIONS. The top of taking, not looked for. Whether it will ever come unasked, as the Wind does. And Darrow's fourth: both at once, unasked, the only time. The notice said* concurrent function: recorded. *At the trough, reaching for one makes me drop the other. Can it be called? Ruling: not yet. I want to know this more than anything else on the page, and that is a reason to be careful with it, not a reason to hurry.*

He read it back, and changed one word, and read it back again.

Then, because the brown Log had counted them and he did not want the count to stop just because the book had, he turned to the very back of the Power Log. He wrote a single line there, on its own: *Instances carried from the old Log: eight. The step unasked, the last at Darrow's third; the eighth, Darrow's fourth, both.* He would count the unasked ones here from now on, apart from all the rest. A thing that came when it chose was a different kind of thing from a thing that came when he called it, and he wanted to be able to see the difference at a glance.

---

He was still sitting with the two entries open when Lira came in.

She came in late. He heard the street door, and the heavyset man say something, and her answer, and her feet on the stairs, two at a time as always; and he looked at the window and saw that it was full dark and had been for a while, and that the rain had stopped without his noticing. She had said *an hour*, when she went out. She had said it over her shoulder with the staff on her back, as she said it most evenings now. It had been more than two.

She stood in the door between the rooms with her hair wet and her face pink from the cold and looked at him, and at the desk, and at the two books with the lamp between them, and at the steel straightedge.

"You're doing the thing," said Lira.

"What thing?"

"Sitting in the lamp being pleased about ruling lines." She came and leaned on the end of the desk. "You've got a face you only do for paperwork. It's very peaceful. It's a bit like a cow."

"I've given it a name."

She turned the slim book round with one finger and read the inside of the cover. Her eyebrows went up.

"*The Power Log*."

"It's accurate."

"It's very grand. I'd have called it *the little book*." But she was already reading, and he watched her read, which he had not done often; she read the way she fought, leaning in, with her whole attention, and her lips moved very slightly on the words she liked. She read the Wind entry through, and stopped at the lock, and read that part twice, and put her finger on *two of Lira's* ands and left it there a moment without saying anything. Then she went on into the Pressure.

At the giving face she stopped.

"This line. *The dock man, sitting down.*" She tapped it. "He didn't sit down. He fell over. I was there. And he's a Bronze washout, not just a dock man; he'd be cross if he saw that."

"Is that evidence or opinion?"

"It's evidence. I watched." She took the pencil from his hand, before he could stop her, and wrote in the middle column, very small, beside the line: *fell over. (L., seen.)* Then she gave him back the pencil. "There. Now it's true."

He looked at what she had written. It was the first time anybody but him had put a mark in that book.

"You can't do that," he said.

"I just did." She sat down on the end of the bed with a sigh, as though her legs had been waiting all evening to be allowed, and bent to unlace her boots. "You put my name in it. You can't put my name in a book and tell me I can't write in it. That's not how names work."

He did not argue, because she was right, and because he found he did not want to. He looked at *L., seen* a while, in her round quick hand beside his small square one, and then he closed the book with it in.

"You were a long time," he said. "You said an hour."

Lira went on unlacing her boot.

"Was I?" she said. "The dock man stayed. We got talking." She pulled the boot off and dropped it, and looked at the floor. "And I wanted to try something on the straw while it was empty. It didn't work. I'll tell you when it works."

It was not like her to say *when it works* about anything without telling him what the anything was, and he heard that it was not like her. He heard it, and turned it over, and set it down carefully on the edge of the evening without picking it up again, as she had set things down for him a hundred times.

"All right," he said.

She looked up at him then, quickly, as if she had expected him to ask, and found that he was not going to. Something went over her face that he could not read, because it went too fast, and then she was herself again, pulling off the other boot.

"Read me the Wind one again," said Lira. "Out loud. The bit about the lock. I like hearing my *ands* in it."

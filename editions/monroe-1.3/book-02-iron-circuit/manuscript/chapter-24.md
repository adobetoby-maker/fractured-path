# Chapter 24 — On Contact

Brom did not move at the word.

Cael had known he would not. A fortnight of watching had told him that much, and so had seven lines in Vell's book. Still, there was a part of him that had been in the Ironyard a year and had learned what *begin* meant, and that part waited, for the length of a breath, for somebody to come. Nobody came; twelve strides of dark stone lay between them under twenty-six lamps, and the man at the far end of it stood on his heels with his arms loose and let them lie there.

So Cael went to him.

He went on his feet, at a walk that was not quite a walk, with the open room on his left and his weight low and his eyes on the place where Brom's breath lived, low in the belly under the wound cloth of his shirt. Six strides. Four. He felt the crowd lean, all at once, the way a field of barley leans when the wind comes into it. At three strides he could hear Brom breathing. It was even and slow, a big man's breath, and nothing in it was getting ready for anything.

The plan was three things, close together, each one new.

The first was the short straight strike he owned, the one Lira had given him a year ago, and he put it into the lead forearm. Brom held that forearm loose across his front, as he had held it against every newcomer for twelve days, and it was the place he trusted most. Cael wanted it trusted, and he wanted the first answer hard.

He heard the breath go in before he had finished stepping. It went low and stayed there, the sack-settling breath, and his fist arrived on the end of it.

It was like hitting the post in the alcove, except that the post moved and this did not. His knuckles stopped against the forearm as if the arm had been bolted to the floor, and for one whole beat of his own pulse nothing happened at all. His fist sat on Brom's arm while Brom's knees gave the width of a finger. Cael felt his own blow going down out of his hand, through the big man, into the stone, the way water goes into the gears of a mill before the wheel turns.

Then the wheel turned.

His own strike came back up his arm. It did not come back the way he had sent it, straight, so that he could have braced for it; it came round, on a line his shoulder had never been asked to hold, and it turned him. It turned him a quarter round on his own feet and carried him a long step and a half to his right, into the quarter of the floor behind his right shoulder where the fan never went and his feet never chose to go. He landed with his ankles crossed and his back half to the man and his right arm humming from the knuckles to the elbow like a struck pan.

He heard the breath go out. Long, through the nose, a sack set down.

There it was: the recovery, the thing he had found live against the dock partner and staked the night on. Brom was soft, soft now all over, and would be until the next low breath came in.

Cael was a long step and a half away with his back turned.

He uncrossed his feet and turned, and squared, and came back in, and it took him what it took him, which was more than half a beat and less than a whole one. When he arrived the breath was already in. He could hear it.

He did not throw. He went round instead, on his feet, to the left, keeping the fan open, and while he went round he did the second thing: a rising strike off the back hand at the shoulder, quicker than the first and lighter, a different shape so the man would have to think about it. It landed on an ordinary shoulder, with an ordinary give in it, and Brom let it land and did not breathe for it at all. There was no knock, no hold and no throw. There was only a big man's shoulder taking a boy's light blow and not caring, as a wall does not care about rain.

He chooses, Cael thought. He had written it down himself, on the fifth afternoon. *So he chooses.* Light hits went on an ordinary arm, and nothing was spent on them, and so nothing was drawn down out of the purse; a light hit was a hit he could stand in front of all night.

So it had to be hard. Every one of the three had to be hard enough that Brom could not afford to let it land soft.

The third he threw with everything he had in his body and nothing out of his middle. It went into the forearm again, because the forearm was where the answers were surest, and he heard the low breath come in for it before he was halfway there. The knock. The hold, shorter this time, much shorter; Brom had seen this one before, a moment ago. And then the throw, and his own blow came back up his arm and turned him again, harder, further. It carried him two full steps toward Vell's table and put him down on one knee for a moment before he got his legs under him, and when he came up his right arm had gone numb from the elbow to the fingertips. The fingers felt fat and far away, like a pair of gloves somebody else was wearing.

He came up, and he understood.

He had measured the recovery against the post first, and the post had come back. It hung on a rope. When Brom threw it, it swung away along its arc and swung back on the same arc at the same rate, because that was what a post on a rope did, whether anybody wanted it to or not; half of the gap he had timed on that rope had been made by the rope. Then he had measured it live against the dock partner, and the dock partner had been heavy. He was a Bronze washout as broad as a door. Brom's answers had sent him a hand's breadth off his line, and a hand's breadth was nothing to a man that size, so he had been there, close, still inside, to put his fourth blow into the two beats.

Cael was not a post and he was not a door. Every hard answer Brom gave him threw him out of reach of the gap it opened. The harder he hit, to make Brom spend, the further the answer threw him, and the longer it took him to come back, and by the time he was back the breath was in. He could make Brom open the purse; he could not be standing beside it when it was open. The only thing that could ever go into that gap was the thing Brom had just thrown away.

The plan had lasted three blows.

---

He had promised Lira he would find out why as fast as he could, and he had, and now he had to do the next thing with what was left, and the trouble was that the next thing was offering itself to him from inside.

It came on the fourth contact. He went in low, testing the knee he had drawn on his coverage map, and Brom turned on his heels to take it on the edge of his lead foot. The foot was hard and sent Cael's shin back past him. But turning for it brought Brom round square, chest and ribs open, for perhaps half a beat, with his lead forearm out of the way and his breath already spent on the foot.

And under Cael's ribs, the hollow stirred.

He knew the feeling better than he knew his own handwriting. It was the giving face waking, the thing from his middle that rode a strike on the beat and put a weight in his fist that his arm alone could never have found. It leaned toward his right hand the way a dog leans toward a door it has heard a step behind. The beat was there, he could feel it in his feet, and the ribs were open. One amplified strike into that place, on that beat, would be the hardest thing he had ever put into anybody, and against anybody else on the slate it would have ended the exchange and very possibly the bout.

*Pressure: locked. Don't open it. Not once.*

He let the hollow lean, and did not answer it. It was harder than he had thought it would be; it was like holding a door shut against a wind, with his shoulder, while trying to do something else with his hands. He put a plain strike into the ribs instead, because the ribs were open whether the giving face was let out or not, and a plain strike was still a strike.

The ribs were not open. The breath had come in while he was deciding. His fist met the hardness and came back up his arm, his good arm, his left, and turned him half a step, and the half-step cost him the ground he had just made.

He had paid for that one twice. Once for the strike that came back, and once for the half-beat he had spent holding the door, which was where the breath had got in.

The second offer was harder to refuse, because it was the better one.

Brom stepped. It was the first step he had taken toward Cael all bout, one short heavy step off the back foot to take away the room Cael was making on his left; and as he stepped, for the length of the step, his whole front was square to Cael and moving forward into him. The breastbone was right there, coming in. It was the line that finished things, the one every fighter on the slate would have given a month's purse to be offered. Cael's body knew it before he did. The short straight strike was already leaving his hip with his weight behind it, and the hollow under his ribs had already risen to meet it, and the beat was exactly, perfectly there.

He stopped it.

He stopped it with the strike already half thrown, and stopping a thing half thrown is not like not throwing it. It left him standing square, with his weight stuck in the middle of his feet and his arm checked short in front of him like a man who has reached for a cup and remembered it is hot. Brom's palm came onto his checked arm. It did not strike. It simply met the arm, flat, with the low breath behind it, and took the force of Cael's own stopping, the whole weight he had just put into not hitting, and sent it back down through him into his heels and out behind him. He went backward two steps, then a third, off the dark middle of the floor toward the pale stone at the edge, without once having been hit.

The door had held, and the rule had held. He had kept both, and both had cost him, and the second had cost him more than the first.

He had never thought about what it cost not to use something. He had a whole column in the Log for what each thing charged him to use, but none for what it charged him to carry while he kept it shut. He did not have time to think about it now. He put it somewhere, the way he put the faces in a crowd, and came forward again.

---

He came forward with a cross-step, left over right, meaning to go round to the open side, and Brom took his forearm as he crossed.

It was not a throw, exactly. Brom met his lead arm in the middle of the crossing, with the breath in and the forearm hard, and turned the arm a little on its way past, no more than a door is turned on a hinge. But Cael's feet were crossed when it happened. The turn went into his crossing step and made a spiral of it, so that his weight, which had been going left, went round, and on round, and past the place where his feet could follow it. The stone tipped. He felt the floor come up toward his right shoulder with all the slow certainty of a thing that has already happened.

The hip went.

He did not ask it to, and he did not read anything. There was nothing to read; there was only the stone coming, and something under the thinking part of him that had kept him alive in the spring dropped his left hip without consulting him. The floor went short. He was half a body to the left, crooked, with his weight caught on the outside of one foot and his breath stopped halfway in, and he locked there, in the slow bright stillness, two *ands* of nothing, and the stone did not arrive.

The line flared from the crest of his hip to the inside of his knee, wide and hot, a brushstroke and not a pencil line.

*That was a reaction.*

He knew it in the lock, where there was nothing to do but know things. The first line of the two lines, the one he had thought would be the harder one, broken inside the first exchange, not by a choice but by a fall. He could feel the price of it as plainly as a coin missing from his palm: twice what a read burst cost, from the bottom of him, crooked. He had a moment, in the lock, to hate the arithmetic of it. He did not have a moment for anything else.

The lock let go.

Brom was there. He had not hurried, because he had not needed to. He had watched Cael go short off the hip and simply walked the half body after him, at a walk, the way a man follows a hat that has blown a little way down a street. He arrived as the lock let go, with his shoulder low. It came into Cael's chest not hard, not a blow at all, more a lean, and it took the leftover of Cael's own crooked motion and finished it for him. He sat down on the stone.

He sat down on the chalk of the boundary line, with the rope at his back and his legs out in front of him, like a child who has sat down suddenly in a field.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

The crowd's noise came back up all at once, as if somebody had opened a door on it. He got up, and his legs were fine. That was the strange part: his legs were entirely fine, and everything else hurt.

On the way back to the north mark he went over himself part by part, as he would have gone over a room he had just walked into, and for the same reason: to know what was in it before anything else happened.

Right arm: numb from the elbow down, the fingers coming back slowly in pins, fat and stupid. Left arm: one returned blow, ringing, but his own. Hip: the bright line, wide, from the reactive burst; it would be there all night now, and the next burst off it would cost more. Breath: ragged at the edges, coming back. Seat: he had sat down on stone. The middle of him, where the giving face lived: untouched, full, leaning, like a dog still sitting by the door.

At the rope by the north post Lira had both hands on the strand. Her face was doing nothing at all. Her fingers were nowhere near her leg.

On the end of the east bench Keth was looking at Brom's chest.

Vell's pen had moved, and was still moving.

He stood on his mark and made himself think, quickly, because he did not have long.

The recovery was real and he could not reach it. That was the first thing, and it was finished. He had built a fortnight on it and it had lasted three blows. The plan had told him what he would do if he found it wrong, and it had been honest about that: find out why, and do the next thing with what was left.

So: what was left. Not the hold. The hold was no use to anybody; by the time it began, Brom already had the hit. Not the recovery. That left the breath before.

*He breathes while I'm still coming.* He had said that to Lira, at the alcove, as a reason not to try for it. Brom breathed, and was hard, and the newcomer's fist arrived on the end of the breath. But the newcomers were slow. Their fists took the time they took, and the breath went in half a beat before the knock because half a beat before the knock was when there was a knock to breathe for.

If he came faster than that, the breath might not be in.

He had speed he had never shown on a slate. He had kept it off on purpose all year, as a man keeps a coin sewn in his coat, because nobody he had fought had made him need it and because a thing never shown was a thing nobody could have studied. On the side floors there had never been room for it. Here there was all the room in the world. He could cross three strides in the time it took most people to decide to cross one, on his feet alone, with no hip in it at all.

He would go through the second door.

"Begin."

---

He went at the word, and it was the fastest he had ever moved on purpose.

It was a low diagonal from the north mark, from left to right across Brom's front, on his feet. He went down and long, with his weight ahead of him, covering the stone in three strides that felt like one. He saw the crowd's faces smear at the edge of his eye like lamps seen through rain. He saw Brom's feet, which had not moved. He saw the lead forearm, loose, and he aimed his short strike at the elbow behind it, where the arm bent, and he was halfway there and still a full stride out when he heard the breath go in.

A full stride out. Not half a beat before his fist arrived, but a whole beat, more, while he was still in the air between one step and the next.

The knock. The hold. The throw. He went off sideways along the line he had come in on, faster than he had come, because Brom had given him his own speed back and added an angle to it, and he had to run three steps to stay on his feet.

He came round, and tried the mirror of it. High this time, from the right, and at the last stride he changed his tempo inside the entry, a half-step stutter that had once made the river-academy man cut at an empty place. The breath went in before the stutter. It went in before the first stride. He heard it as he left the ground.

He came in a third time, and a fourth, and by the fourth he had stopped trying to win anything and was only trying to find out what was happening, and he found it.

Brom was not breathing on the fist.

He was breathing on Cael. On his weight, coming, the moment it came near enough to feel. *Close, whatever you do,* Cael had said to him in the alcove; *it doesn't reach far.* It did not need to. It reached a bench's length, and a bench's length was two strides, and at Cael's best speed two strides were still a beat. The read found him two strides out, and the breath went in on the read, and by the time Cael's fist arrived Brom had been hard and waiting for it for longer than the newcomers' whole blows had taken to land.

And he had not breathed like that against the newcomers, because they were never fast enough to need it. Against them the read and the fist had come together, and he had breathed on whichever came; Cael had sat on the bench with his fingers on his wrist and timed the version of the man who was fighting twelve-year-olds. Somebody, somewhere, in a boat shed or a city by the coast, had tried exactly this before Cael was ever born into it. Some fast Wind with good legs had tried to arrive before the hardness, and had made Brom learn to harden on the read instead of the knock, and Brom had kept the lesson.

The middle column was not wrong; it was out of date. It was a map of the man as he had been before somebody faster came, and Cael had walked onto the main floor with it as though it were the ground.

He was thinking that, and adjusting, when it happened.

He had committed to a low entry, fast, from the left, and on the second stride his lead foot came down across the long lead seam in the floor, the smooth line he had felt through his boot on the mark. It took his weight a hair less kindly than the stone round it, a hair slicker, and to keep his line he put more into the step than he had meant to. For one instant he was neither on one foot nor the other. He was a bridge, all of him in the air between his two feet with his weight in the middle and going forward, the one moment in any step when a body has nothing to stand on but its own speed.

Brom did not meet his arms. He met the line.

The breath, the hardness, and then the answer, and the answer did not come back up Cael's arm. It came down. It took the whole of his speed and the whole of his weight, everything he had put into that low committed step, and turned it at the place where the two of them touched and sent it straight back into him at the hips, and from the hips down through both legs at once, on an angle that nothing below his waist had ever been built to carry. It was not a blow. It was the floor's bill, delivered to his knees.

He went down.

---

He was up again before he had decided to be. His arms did it first, and then his legs agreed, and then he was standing on them, and they began to tell him their new terms.

They were not broken; he knew what broken felt like and this was not it. But both knees had been asked to carry a thing sideways that they had only ever carried straight, and they had carried it, and now they were telling him about it in a low steady voice from hip to ankle. They held, the way an old rope bridge holds, there and working, and honest at every step that it would not hold for ever.

Brom had stepped forward to take the space while he was down. He was a stride and a half away, on his heels again, the breath going out of him long through the nose, and Cael needed room. He needed to be somewhere else, now, before the next breath came in, and he read it: Brom's weight settling forward over the front foot to take the next step, the heel going light. It was a read, clean, a hinge, the first he had been given all night. He set himself over his feet while he watched it, as the drill had taught him, and when the heel came up he dropped his left hip.

The burst fired. He felt it go out of him at its full price, the half-breath, the whole of what it had cost on the chalk the morning of the six marks, a read burst's price exactly. And his legs gave him perhaps a third of the ground.

He went short, and landed crooked, a hand's width to the front-left instead of half a body, with his weight on the wrong part of his foot and his knee buckling in the lock. He had paid for the whole of a thing and been given a piece of it. The burst was not magic. He had always known that and never had to know it. It was a hip and two legs doing something faster than legs could, and two legs that had just been told new terms could not do it any more, and they had charged him for the asking anyway.

He shut it.

He shut it the way he would have closed a book with the ink still wet, carefully, so as not to smudge anything, and with no plan at all to open it again that night. A thing that charged full and paid a third was not insurance; it was a leak.

So that was the arithmetic, and it was very simple now. The Pressure was locked by a rule. The Wind was closed by his legs. What he had left was his eyes, and his two hands, one of them still half numb, and two legs that might last two more exchanges if he was careful with them and one if he was not.

Brom ended the second exchange the way a man finishes a job he has nearly finished already, without any hurry. One more entry Cael could not make fast enough; one more turn at the place where they touched; the stone, for the second time, coming up under his shoulder.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

Her pen did not stop when she said it. It went on for a while after, longer than a result needed, and he heard it from where he lay, very faintly, under the noise of the room, the scratch of a woman writing something down.

He lay there for the length of three breaths, because nobody had told him he could not, and looked up at the lamps.

They were very bright, and there were a great many of them, and from the floor they made the low ceiling into a field of small flames with the dark beams running across it like furrows. He had never seen the main floor from here. He thought that most of the people who had seen it from here had not been in a state to notice the lamps, and that he would like to be able to say afterward that he had.

Then he rolled onto his side and got his hands under him and stood, and the room made a sound for that, which he did not listen to.

His knees chose the size of his steps back to the mark. He let them. They chose short ones, and he took short ones, and found that if he kept his weight low and close and let his feet do only what his knees would sign for, the knees stopped arguing and only grumbled. He had not walked like that since his first winter. He had walked like that across the Cinder House yard to the canvas post, every morning for three months, with no hip in it at all, because there had been nothing in his hip to use. He had been slow, and careful, and he had looked at everything, because looking was all he had.

*He's in there somewhere,* Lira had said, standing on her heels in the alcove with her face gone pink. *I've seen him.*

At the rope she had not moved. He did not look for her fingers.

He stood on the north mark and waited for his breath to come back into its count, and while it came he found that the arithmetic had become very simple. The Pressure was locked, by him, in ink. The Wind was shut, by his legs. The plan was dead and the second door had been bolted by some fast stranger in another city before Cael had ever heard of it. What was left was the boy who had walked to the canvas post. He had two hands, one of them still coming back from numb, and two eyes, and knees that would give him perhaps two more exchanges of short steps if he spent them carefully, and one if he did not. That boy had not had a plan either. He had not known what a read was. He had only known where his next foot was going, and what the man in front of him was doing with his.

"Begin," said Vell.

---

He walked out to meet Brom, and he did not try to win anything.

He found, a little to his surprise, that he could not have tried if he had wanted to; there was nothing left in him to try with. So he did the only thing his knees allowed. He went in a half-step at a time, never more, and touched, and came away. He touched the forearm, lightly, not enough to be worth a breath. He touched the shoulder. He went round to the left, two short steps, and touched the hip, and went back. None of it was anything. Brom let all of it land soft and spent nothing on any of it. Twice the big man stepped in to take the space, and twice Cael gave it to him, a half-step back, on knees that told him exactly how much a half-step cost.

He was not watching for the breath any more. He had stopped listening for it somewhere in the walk back from the floor, without deciding to, the way a man stops listening for a cart that has gone round the corner. He was not watching the purse, or the read, or the lead seam, or anything that had a page. There was only the next thing. Where Brom's lead shoulder was. Where his own left foot would go when it went. How much stone there was between them, and whether it was getting more or less.

It was very quiet in there. The room was roaring, he supposed, though he could not have said.

He looked at Brom's eyes, for no reason, because they were the next thing.

And Brom's eyes went wide.

It was not much. A big man's calm face, and for one instant the look in it of somebody in a lit room when every lamp goes out at once: not fear, not yet, only the blank start of a person whose world has just gone missing round him. Brom's guard was aimed at the stone a little to Cael's left. His weight had begun to go there to meet something, and it hung, half gone, stopped. Cael had seen the big man stand still for twelve days. He had never once seen him stuck.

There was nothing in Cael that had done it. He knew that then and he would know it afterward, whatever else he did not know. Nothing had leaned toward a door, and nothing had gone out of him. He had not reached for anything; he had been looking at the next half-second, and the next half-second had turned out to have a man in it whose read was pointed at an empty piece of floor.

He was one short step away, in plain sight, with lamplight on him.

He took the step, and put his left hand under Brom's lifted arm into the ribs, the short straight strike from the hip with nothing in it but a boy.

It went in.

It went into a body, and that was the whole of it, and it was enormous. No knock, no stillness, no turn; only the soft-and-solid give of muscle over bone under his knuckles, as Ulric's ribs had given, and every man's before. Brom's breath went out of him in a grunt that had no plan in it. He gave a step. It was a short step back, off his heels, and it was the very step Cael had watched him give the dock partner's fourth blow in the alcove, the only one he had ever given.

Then Brom's hand came down on Cael's arm.

It came late, and it came by sight. Cael could feel the difference through his sleeve the way you can hear the difference between a man reading aloud and a man making it up. It was a fighter's hand, fast and trained and very strong, landing where his eyes had sent it. The turn it gave Cael's arm was real, and still more than he could stand against, and it moved him a step. But it was rough. It had been made on the spot, out of nothing, by a man who had not known it would be needed until it was. For the first time all night, Brom was building his answer in front of him.

And then it was over, the way the start of it had been: without any edge he could find. Half a breath later the low breath was going in again, and the hardness was there again under Cael's hand, and whatever in Brom read people had him again, as though it had never let go.

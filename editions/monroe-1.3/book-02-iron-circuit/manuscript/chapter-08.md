# Chapter 8 — Revised

He wrote it up that night, at the crate desk, with the door between the rooms open and Lira asleep on the other side of it, or pretending to be.

He did not write it at once. He sat for a long time first with the Power Log open at the Wind entry and the lamp turned low, looking at the line at the head of the page where he had copied the notice's fields three nights before. *Duration: undetermined.* His own hand, his best hand, careful and square. It was not wrong. It had been true when he wrote it. It had been true, as far as he knew, until about the middle of the morning, and then it had stopped being true, and nothing on the page had any way of saying so.

That was the first thing he had to mend, and it was not a thing about the burst at all. It was a thing about the book.

He had built the Power Log to hold what he knew. Six fields, three columns, an *h* for anything heard, the open questions given the most room. He had built it so that it would never say more than he had seen. What he had not built it for was the thing he had seen today: a fact that was true on one page and then, without his doing anything to it, not true on the next. He had built a book for a world that held still while he looked at it.

So he did not cross out *undetermined*. He left it where it was, and drew a short line under the whole of the notice's fields, and under the line he wrote a new heading, small, the way Vell wrote *t.f.* in her margins:

*REVISIONS.*

And under that:

*Three days after I ruled this book, in the alcove, about the middle of the morning, in the middle of the broom drill. FRAGMENT UPDATE. [Wind-adjacent]. Duration revised: sustained. Integration: partial (unchanged). Old word:* undetermined. *New word:* sustained. *What sustained means: not known. What undetermined meant: not known either.*

He looked at that, and it was right, and it was ugly, and he left it.

Then he went to the back of the book, past the empty pages, to the place where he had begun to keep the things that were not fields: the line about the eight instances carried from the old Log, and nothing else. He wrote under it.

*The fragments are not static. They develop.*

He stopped there, because the next part was the part that mattered, and he wanted to put it on a line of its own where he would not be able to skip it.

*Or I'm developing my understanding of them.*

*I can't tell which. It matters which. If they change by themselves, whether I'm watching or not, then I am keeping a record of something that moves, and the record has to be built to catch it moving. If it's only my seeing that gets better, then the notices are only catching up with me, and that's a different thing, and it changes what I ought to be watching. Today, I can't tell. One revision doesn't tell me. Filed under open questions, at the front of the queue.*

He read that back, and wrote the conditions under it, plainly, as he had written them on the wall by the dyers' steps: the new format three nights before; the five right-hip tries and their tolls; the nine more with Lira; the fan in chalk; an hour and a half of the closest attention he had ever paid to where the burst would go and where it would not. *Should a second revision come, set what led up to it beside this, line for line. If pressing at the edges is what does it, that's the most useful fact this book will ever hold. One morning doesn't entitle me to believe it.*

Then, last, he wrote the thing he had been thinking since Lira sat down on the bench and laughed about her farmer.

*The burst only goes left because Lira's does. Hers goes left because the man in the barn who taught her was left-handed and kept forgetting to tie his hand. So I am carrying a habit two people old, from a barn I've never seen. These are not ideas I've been handed. I'm carrying copies of real people, taken on real days, with their habits still in them.*

*Whatever I ever borrow will come with fingerprints.*

He sat back.

Through the open door Lira said, very quietly, without moving, "Are you writing about my farmer?"

"Yes."

"Good," said Lira, and turned over, and was asleep, or pretending to be, for the rest of the night.

---

He wrote to Hesk the next evening, because Hesk had asked him to find out what the half-breath was worth and tell him, and because he had promised, a long time ago now, on a wall by a pump, to tell the next thing as it came and not five things later.

It was a long letter for him. He told Hesk about the ten trials, and the five ways the lock would not get shorter, and the dance, and the ninth, when Lira would not hit a falling man. He told him about the tenth, and the exchange, and the rule. He told him what Hesk's own letter had said about the part that stays put, and how it had arrived the afternoon after he had found that out for himself on a stone floor, so that reading it had been like hearing the end of a song he had just sung.

Then he told him the rest.

*There's a thing I keep, which I've just given a name to. It's the book for what I carry. I write down what each thing is and where it came from and what it costs and what I don't know, and I've ruled it into three columns the way I rule everything. I copied the notices into it at the top of each page, word for word, because they were the only other witness I had.*

*Yesterday one of them changed. Not a new one. An old one. It went back and corrected itself. Where it had said it didn't know how long the thing lasts, it says now that it's sustained. I don't know what that means. I don't know whether the thing itself has changed or whether something has only got round to writing down what was true already. But I had written the old word in my best hand three days before, as if it were fixed, and it wasn't, and I've had to make the book able to say so.*

*And I found out why the one from Lira only goes one way. It's because she only goes one way. And she only goes one way because the man who first taught her did. I'm carrying her habits, and his. I didn't know you could carry somebody's habits without ever meeting them.*

He stopped there with the pen over the page for a while, because he could feel a question coming up under the last line that he did not want to write, and he did not want to not write it either. He sat with it until he was sure what it was.

*I've been thinking about you and your bench,* he wrote. *About how I set my feet before a bout the way you set a housing on the bench, square, before you load it. I've always thought I learned that from watching you. I think now I didn't learn it so much as take it. I think everybody's carrying somebody. It's only that with me it shows up in a notice.*

*I'm well. My hip complains. Lira eats my pies. Vell has a cupboard of books older than the building and won't let me read them yet.*

*Tell me how the mill's wheel goes at midwinter.*

He signed it, and folded it, and took it down to the carriers' hut at the fish steps himself in the morning, and paid the east bag, and stood a moment watching the clerk drop it in among the others, where it would go over the hills for days in the dark with somebody's rent and somebody's love letter and somebody's complaint about a horse.

On the way back up from the fish steps he found he was counting, the way he counted the purse after a bout. It was not money he was counting. It was the things he had told Hesk, set against the things he had not.

A year ago, in his first months, the column of things not told had been five long, and it had sat in him like a stone in a boot all winter, until, in the spring, he had put all five in one letter and sent it over the hills. He had promised then to tell the next thing as it came. He went through the letter now in his head, line by line, as he went through the exchanges of a bout, and looked for anything he had left out because it was easier left out. The trials: told. The rule: told. The notice changing its story: told, and told plainly, with the part he did not understand left in as not understood. The fan and its empty quarter: told. The farmer: told.

There was only one thing he had not put in, and he stopped on the dyers' steps with his hand on the cold rail and made himself say what it was. He had not told Hesk that Lira was in the alcove until the middle of the night, working at something she would not name. But that was not his to tell. It was hers. He turned it over twice to be sure that he was not using that as an excuse, and decided that he was not, and went on up the steps with the column of things not told standing at nothing, for the first time he could remember. It was a strange, light feeling, like setting down a bucket you had forgotten you were carrying.

---

On the next morning he and Lira went to find out whether *sustained* meant anything.

They did it properly, because there was no other way he could bear to do it. She chalked the cross. She fetched her measuring stick, the one she had cut from a broom handle in the spring and notched in fingers' widths, and laid it on the stone beside the cross. He stood at the post.

"Same as every morning," he said. "Measure it. Count the lock. Tell me if anything's different."

"And if nothing is?"

"Then nothing is, and I'll write that."

He burst. The floor went short, and he went left, of course, half his body's width, and landed on the cross, and locked, and Lira said "*one-and-two-and*," and he was free. She knelt and laid the stick along the line between where he had started and where he had stopped, and looked at the notch.

"Half a body," she said. "Same as yesterday. Same as last week."

He did it again. Half a body. Two *ands*. He did it a third time, from a run, and it was the same, and his hip gave him the same small fee for it, crest to knee, no more and no less. He stood on the cross and felt inside himself, carefully, for anything that was not as it had been: a longer burst, or a lighter one, a change in the way the floor went short, something he could point to and say *there, that is what sustained means*. There was nothing. The burst was the burst. It felt exactly as it had felt for months, and if somebody had told him the notice had never come, he could not have argued.

"Nothing," said Lira, sitting back on her heels. "Not that I can see."

"Not that I can feel." He came off the cross and sat down on the bench and took out the Power Log. "The word changed. The thing I can measure didn't."

"So what's it for, then? The word?"

"I don't know." He wrote it down: *Three bursts, measured. Half a body, two* ands*, the same hip. Nothing measurable changed. The field changed; the thing didn't, that I can find. Yet.* He underlined *yet*, and then, because he did not entirely trust himself, he put a small *?* beside it, so that later he would remember that it had been a hope and not a finding. "Maybe it's for something I haven't done. Maybe it means it'll keep going, if I ever ask it to keep going. Maybe it means it's settled in me and won't fade if I stop using it, the way a thing you only half know fades." He shook his head. "I can make up ten meanings. I can't test any of them this morning, and the ones I can't test don't go in the book."

"You could test one," said Lira.

He looked up.

"*Keeps going*." She was still sitting on her heels by the measuring stick, turning it in her fingers. "You could burst and not land. Try to. See if it'll carry you further if you ask it to keep on."

He thought about it, and found that the thought made the back of his neck cold, and made himself look at why.

"Not today," he said. "Not on purpose, with you three paces off with a staff, the morning after it changed. I don't know what keeping on would do. I don't know where it would put me, or what the lock would be like at the end of it, or whether there'd be one." He turned the pencil over. "That's a thing I'd want to have priced before I paid. And I can't price it. I've got one word and nothing under it."

Lira looked at him a moment, and then nodded, slowly, and laid the stick down along the chalk line, square, the way he laid the straightedge on a page.

"That's your rule," she said. "The one from the trials."

"It's the same rule. It just keeps turning up in new coats." He wrote, under the rest: *Asked whether to try to make it keep going. Decided not, yet. Can't price it.* "When I know what the lock does at the end of a longer one, I'll try a longer one. Not before."

"You'll want to, though."

"I want to now," said Cael honestly. "That's why I wrote down that I didn't."

Lira watched him write.

"You're not frightened of it," she said. "Are you. I thought you might be."

He thought about that honestly.

"A bit," he said. "Not of the word. Of the thing doing something when I'm not looking." He closed the book. "But I'd rather it did it where I could write it down than somewhere I couldn't. A notice is a witness. Even if it's a witness that changes its story." He looked at the book in his hands, the thin board cover, the pencilled name inside. "At least now the book can say so when it does."

---

Dace caught him at the slate wall that afternoon with a stick of chalk in each hand and his head on one side.

"I've got a Blade for you," he said, without any good afternoon. "Copper formal, river academy, two years out. He's been on my benches three times this month, in the same seat, and only ever on your nights."

Cael looked at the wall. The name was already there, in white, on next week's card, in the third slot, with a small blank space beside it where Cael's name would go.

"Only mine," he said.

"Only yours." Dace rubbed out a dot beside another name and wrote it in somewhere else, as though the two things had nothing to do with each other. "He's been watching you, and he hasn't been watching anybody else, and he sat very still in the fifth exchange of your Ulric bout, and didn't cheer, and didn't groan, and went home." Dace turned his head and looked at Cael properly. "I don't have to put you on with him. There's a Shield from Fenrow who'd take the slot and fall over for the money. But I thought you'd rather know who's sitting in that seat than not."

Cael stood in front of the wall for a while.

He thought about the half-breath at the end of every burst, and Lira's sentence, and the people on the benches who were not there for the fun of it. Somebody was going to wait for the landing. He had known it since the spring. He had spent ten trials and two days of a hollow chest finding out what the landing was worth, and he had a rule now, and a half-breath that was no longer only a wound. He had not yet once spent it on purpose against anybody but Lira. He did not know what it was worth with a stranger's strike in it.

"Put me on," he said.

"Third slot. Next week." Dace wrote *Hesk-ward* in the space, in white, and drew a small mark beside it that Cael had never seen on the wall before, a short line with a dot under it. "That's for me," said Dace, before Cael could ask. "It means *watch this one*. Not you. Him." He put the chalk in his pocket. "Vell's got something for you, too. She was asking where you were."

Vell was at her table with the day's book shut and a letter open on top of it. She did not hand it to him. She turned it a little toward him, the way she turned the ledger, so that he could see it was a letter and see the hand, a slanting brown hand he did not know, and the place it came from, which was a river town two days downstream.

"A keeper," said Vell. "Down at the salt end. I've never met her. She keeps a small book at a bad yard." She tapped the letter. "She's had three fighters through in a month who'd been up here, and all three of them told her about a boy in my book with *atypical* written beside his name more often than anybody's she's ever heard of. She wants to know what I mean by it." Vell folded the letter in three. "She's not the first. She's the first who's written. Keepers talk, as I told that guild boy, and they don't only talk about complaints."

"What will you tell her?"

"What I always tell anybody who asks me what a word in my book means." Vell put the letter in the pocket of her coat. "Come and see."

She sat back, and he thought she had finished, and then she reached for the day's book and opened it, not at the day's page but at the very back, at the inside of the back cover, where the board was bare except for a few lines in pencil. She turned it so that he could see one of them and kept her hand over the rest.

It was a single word, *Atypical*, with a row of small upright strokes beside it, the kind you make when you are counting something you do not want to lose count of. There were a great many strokes.

"I've kept that for thirty years," said Vell. "Every time I write that word against a name, I put a stroke there. Eleven, in all the years before you walked down the hill." She took her hand off the page and let him count, and did not wait for him to finish. "You can see what's happened since."

He could. The strokes went on past the eleven in a steady row, and then in a second row under the first, in five-bar gates, the way Hesk counted bearings.

"I didn't write it against you, the day after Darrow," she said. "It looked too small. But I've written it every time since, because it's the only word I've got that's true and doesn't pretend to know more than I do." She closed the book. "If a keeper at the salt end wants to know what I mean by it, the only honest answer is that I don't know either, and I'd like somebody to. So, come and see." She looked at him with her flat thorough look. "I don't suppose you'll tell her."

"I don't know either."

"No," said Vell. "I didn't think you did. I'm only keeping count."

---

He came back from the east bench late that night, past the end of the card, past the time the sweepers finished, when the Ironyard should have been dark.

It was not dark. As he came down the lane toward the boarding-house row, he saw a light in one of the high windows along the north wall, the one above the alcoves at that end. It was a small light, not a lamp from the main floor. Somebody had lit a single practice lamp on a hook and was working by it.

He stopped in the lane.

The side door at the north end was off the latch; it always was, if you knew how to lift it. He did not go to it. He stood in the dark of the lane, across from the window, and listened, and after a moment he heard the sound he had heard every morning for a year: the dry scuff of feet on stone, and then nothing, and then the scuff again, further along. The step. The floor going short under somebody, over and over. And then, after the step, something he did not know. A second sound, close behind the first, as though whoever was making it had landed and not stopped, and gone on, and landed again.

He knew who it was. He knew the rhythm of her feet better than he knew his own handwriting.

She had said *an hour* when she went out, as she said most evenings, with her staff over her shoulder. It was past the middle of the night.

He stood there a long time. He thought about Vell's book, where her line said *Copper-equivalent, high range*, and about the girls from the wall, whom she taught to fall for a copper each, and about her dock partner, who went home when his hour was done. He thought about a girl in a barn copying a left-handed man for a year before anybody at an academy ever looked at her, and about an academy that had looked at her for as long as it cared to and then sent her home. He thought about her face on the bench in the alcove when she had said *I'll tell you when it works*, and the thing that had gone across it too fast for him to read.

Then he thought about two hours on a wall by a pump, and a girl at a kitchen door with flour on her sleeve saying *you can bring me a thing in pieces*. She had asked him, that evening, not to sit alone with a thing until he had made it tidy. She had never once asked him not to let her do the same.

He did not go in. Whatever she was building on the straw at this hour, by one lamp, she was building it because she had decided to, and she would bring it to him when it was ready to be looked at, or before, if she chose. It was hers to bring. He would not take it off her by walking in on it.

But he would not pretend, either, that he had not seen.

He went home, and up the narrow stairs, and into his room, and did not light the lamp. He sat on the end of his bed in the dark with his coat still on, and listened to the house: the heavyset man's slow tread on the floor below, locking up; somebody's kettle; the street going quiet. The door between the two rooms stood open on her empty room.

After a long while he heard the street door, and the heavyset man say something low, and her feet on the stairs, two at a time, quiet. She came along the passage. He heard her stop outside her own door, and stand there, perhaps seeing his door open and his room dark, perhaps not. Then she went in and sat down on her bed, and he heard her let out a long breath, the kind you let out at the end of a thing that has cost you more than you meant to pay.

"Cael?" she said softly, into the dark. "You awake?"

"Yes."

A pause.

"I was a long time," said Lira.

"You were," he said. "Is it working?"

There was a longer pause then, long enough that he thought she might not answer it at all.

"Not yet," said Lira. "Nearly. I'll show you when it's nearly enough." And then, more quietly, so that he only just heard it: "Thank you for not coming in."

He sat with that in the dark for a while after she had gone quiet. Then he lit the lamp after all, very low, and opened the grey book, not the Power Log, because this was not about him, and turned to the back, where he kept the page of things that lived underneath technique, in the plain body.

He did not write her name. He wrote only the date, and under it: *A step that doesn't stop.* And beside that, after a moment, a small *h*, because he had heard it and not seen it, and because he would not write down what he had not seen.

Then he turned the lamp out and lay down, and listened to her breathing on the other side of the open door until it slowed, and his own slowed with it.

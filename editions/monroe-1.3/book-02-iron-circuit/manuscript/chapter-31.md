# Chapter 31 — Two Entries, Dated

Three days after the Shield, he went down early to try the new thing in a room with people in it, because he had run out of ways to put it off.

He had known for a week what he would find. A sense that one big man shifting his weight on a bench could knock flat was not going to do well among a crowd. But knowing a thing and having it in the book were different, and he had promised the book a crowd. So he chose the quietest crowd he could find, which was the Ironyard on a season morning before the first card, when the benches held thirty or so people who had come to sit rather than to shout. Fighters were wrapping their hands, two of Keth's newcomers were arguing about a parry in low voices, somebody's grandfather was asleep with his hat over his face, and Vell was at her table with her tea, going through yesterday's lines.

He took the end of the east bench, put the grey book on his knee, and did not open it. He set his feet flat and let his hands go loose, and looked at nothing in particular, at the middle of the floor where the light from the high windows lay on the stone in long pale bars. Then he did the thing he had learned in the alcove, which was not a thing at all: he stopped asking.

It took a long time to come, and when it came, it came all at once.

It was not thirty people. That was what he had hoped for, somewhere under his sense, though he had told himself not to: thirty small warm weights laid out round him on the benches like coins on a table, each in its place. What came instead was one weight, enormous and shapeless, pressing on the whole front of him at once and the sides and the back. It was the warmth of a full room felt with the skin instead of the face, and there was nobody in it. There was only *people*, all together, the way a river is only *water*. The old man with the hat was nearest, and there was no old man in it to be found; nothing in the weight was shaped like him at all. Cael did not go looking for him. There was only more of the same.

He did not know what else to do with it, so he held it, and counted his own breath to know how long. Ten. Fifteen. The weight did not change and did not tell him anything. Twenty.

At twenty-two a man's voice went up at Vell's table, sharp and loud, and the whole room turned toward it, and so did Cael, and the door slammed.

The band came round his head before he had even found the man with his eyes. It came harder than it had in the alcove, as if twenty-two breaths of a crowd had cost what an hour of Brom cost. He sat with his thumb and finger pressed into the corners of his eyes and let it settle. The voice at the table went on, louder.

He wrote it down with the band still on, small, in the corner of a page, so that he would not lose it.

*Thirty in a room: one weight, no one in it. Held twenty-two breaths. Got nothing. Loud voice: shut. Band, bad.* And under that, because it was the true size of what he had: *The fragment cannot yet count past one.*

Then he looked up to see what the voice was.

It was the man in the academy coat.

He stood in front of Vell's table at the distance a man stands who wishes to be overheard, which Cael had seen him choose once before. He had a paper in his hand again, and this time it was not folded in a pocket but out, flat, held where people could see it.

"That result was bought," he said. "Tuesday afternoon. The north side floor. The unranked boy and Orvet's Shield."

Vell did not look up for a moment, and when she did, Cael saw her know the man, and saw something go across her face that was not quite weariness, but more like the face of a woman who opens her door to a hawker she sent away last spring and finds him back with a new tray.

"Bought by whom?"

"I don't say anybody paid anybody." The man had practised this too; Cael could hear it. "I say the odds were false. Everybody in this building knows he does something nobody can see. The Arbiter can't follow it. The registry can't put a name to it. He's got no Path on his card." He lifted the paper a little. "How is a man meant to lay an honest coin against a fighter whose whole ability isn't written down anywhere?"

"You laid one."

"I laid three marks on the Shield. Like anybody would."

"Then you lost three marks." Vell put her pen down. "That's a complaint about betting. Betting's always been a gamble against what you don't know. Every fighter on that wall has things he's never shown, and every coin on that crate is laid against them. Tuesday's bout was fought under the terms both men agreed before *begin*, and neither man has complained of it." She looked at the paper. "You weren't one of them."

"This isn't about the bout. It's about the circuit." The man did not lower his voice; he raised it, very slightly, so that it went past Vell to the benches. "I've written it down this time. All of it. I've a house on the river that's guild-registered, and I've people who know people, and there's those who'd want very much to hear that a fighter with no Path is being put on cards in an unsanctioned circuit with nobody over him at all."

The room changed.

It did not go quiet; it went still, the way it had gone still the last time, as people go still at a table when somebody has picked up a knife. Two of Keth's newcomers stopped arguing, and the old man under the hat lifted the brim with one finger. And Cael felt something he did not like, which was every face in the near benches turning, one after another, from the man at the table to him.

Vell said nothing for a moment, and when she spoke it was without heat.

"You stood at this table a few months back with a paper in your pocket," she said, "and I told you then how fast a keeper's word goes down the coast. You've brought the paper out this time. That's braver. It's not wiser." She folded her hands on the book. "If a Compact man walks into my ledger because of you, every table from here to the sea will have heard your name by spring. You'd be the man who did it twice. I'd think hard about whether that's what you want written beside you, when half your purses come from tables like mine."

The man's jaw worked, and he looked round the benches for help, and did not find any, and Cael saw him decide to go on anyway, because he had come too far down the room to go back up it.

"Then show me," he said. "Show me it was straight. Show me what he did."

That was the moment, and Cael heard himself speak before he had properly decided to.

"I can show you."

Every face that had turned to him turned again. He did not stand up; his knees did not want it, and standing would have made it a speech, so he held up the grey book from where he sat.

"I keep the bouts I fight. Every one, the same night. I keep the bouts I watch, too, before I fight them." He kept his voice even; he had heard Vell keep hers even for a year and thought he knew how it was done. "Tuesday's in here. So's the Shield, from before. Argue with the page, if you like. It was written the same night, before anybody had lost anything on it."

---

Vell looked at him for a long moment over her spectacles, and he could not read what was in it.

Then she held out her hand.

He got up, then, because there was no other way to give it to her, and went across the floor with the book. He opened it to the Shield's page before he put it down, and turned it round so that the lines faced her, and stepped back.

She did not read it at once, but opened her own book first, the day book, and turned back through it to Tuesday, and found the north side floor's afternoon card, where the junior keeper had written and she had written under him. Then she laid the two books side by side on the table, square to its edge, his small hand beside her large square one. The man in the academy coat came a step closer to see, and so did half the near bench.

"Read it out," said Vell. "Your page. Slowly."

So he read it, as he had read the Log to Brom's face in the alcove, a line at a time.

"First exchange. Survey. His rim's fast on his left. I took it on the forearm." He had the mark still; he did not show it. "Second. He walked me back to the rope. I waited for the drop. It came. One burst, front-left. His rush went into the rope. One strike, his ribs, under the shield arm. He went to one knee."

Vell's finger went down her own line. *Second exchange. North rope. Shield to one knee.* It stopped, and waited.

"Third. He held the drop up, both times. The rush came without it. Two steps off the line, no burst. Same ribs. Hand up."

*Third exchange. Hand up.* Vell's finger stopped again.

"What's the drop?" she said.

Cael turned back a page, to the two entries above Tuesday's, and put his own finger under them. "His lead shoulder. Before the rush. It goes down a finger's width and the rim goes with it." He moved the finger to the first line. "I saw it the first time three weeks before. That's the date. I wasn't sure." He moved it to the second. "Eleven days after, against the tannery boy. That's the date. Then I was."

Vell looked at the two lines for a long time, not at Cael, or at the man, or at the room. She looked at the dates, and at the ink, and turned the book a little toward the window to see the second date better, and then turned it back.

"This was on your page," she said, to the book, but loud enough that the benches heard, "before Dace ever put the two of you on his wall. Two entries. Dated."

"Yes."

"And if he hadn't done it a third time?"

"Then I'd have been wrong," said Cael. "There's a line through half the pages in it. Most of it's guesses with dates on, until something comes along and tells me whether to keep them."

Somebody at the back made a small sound, almost a laugh, and stopped it.

Vell closed the grey book and held it out to him without looking, and he took it. Then she looked up at the man in the academy coat, and spoke to him and not to the room.

"Two records," she said. "Kept by two people, in two hands, on the same afternoon, neither knowing what the other wrote. One of them three weeks old before the bout began." She laid her palm flat on her own book. "They say the same thing in the same order. The boy read your Shield's shoulder because the Shield showed it to him twice, in front of you, on my floor. There's nothing in that the registry needs to be told about. It's called watching." She picked up her pen. "The bout stands. The claim's answered. You can take your paper wherever you like."

The man stood there with the paper in his hand and looked down at it, as if it had changed while he was not watching, and Cael saw that it had, though not a word on it was different. In front of forty people it had become a page of complaint about three marks.

He folded it, and put it in his pocket, and went out at the side door without looking at anybody, and the cold came in where he had been.

The benches let out their breath, somebody said *well*, and the old man let his hat fall back down over his face.

Vell wrote a line in the day book, small, at the foot of Tuesday. Then she looked at Cael, standing in front of her table with his grey book in both hands, and her face did a thing it did very rarely, which was to look tired in front of people.

"Next time," she said, quietly, so that only he heard, "let me ask for it."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. You were right." She uncapped her ink. "But it's my table. If a book's going to be opened at it, I want to be the one who opened it. Then nobody can say the fighter brought his own witness."

He understood that, and was ashamed for a moment that he had not thought of it, and then decided it was a thing worth having learned. He nodded, and went back to the east bench, and the band round his head, which he had forgotten, came back and reminded him.

---

Dace found him at the end of the morning, at the back of the floor by the door to the locked room where Vell kept her shelves.

Cael was not doing anything there; he had gone because it was the quietest place in the building, and because the band still had one hand round his head, and because there was a stretch of plain wall beside the door that nobody ever leaned on but him. Dace came along the wall from the slate, chalk on his cuffs, and leaned on it beside him, the way he arrived at most things, from the side, so that you saw him before he said anything.

"I've had fighters through that door since before you were born," said Dace, "and not one of them could tell me what happened in his own last bout without a quarrel. You read yours out like a carter reading a bill of lading."

"Vell settled it."

"Vell settles them every day. She settles them out of her own book and her own head, and her head's good enough for most." Dace looked out at the floor, at the cards being chalked for the afternoon. "Today she had two books. That's a different thing. That's harder to argue with than anything she's got on her own."

Cael did not answer, because he could feel that Dace had not come to tell him that.

Dace was quiet for a while, and when he spoke again his voice was the same, flat and easy, but he had dropped it a little.

"You know what the Compact would give for a book like yours?"

"A book about one boy's bouts?"

"A book with dates in it," said Dace. "And names. And what each man does with his left shoulder before he goes in, and how long it took the boy who wrote it to find out. Kept the same way every night. Cross-checked against the keeper's." He did not look at the door. Cael saw him not look at it. "Good thing we don't share."

He said it lightly, like a joke between men leaning on a wall, and it was not a joke.

"You've thought about it," said Cael.

"I think about it every time Vell takes her book home under her arm." Dace rubbed his chalky thumb on his trousers. "Every fighter on that wall is on it because the registry's ladder either threw him off or never had a rung for him. This floor is the one place in the river towns where what a man can do is written down by people who don't answer upstairs. If a book like yours went up the hill, it wouldn't be a record any more. It'd be a list."

He was quiet again, and Cael waited.

"There was a lad here," said Dace, "before you came over the hills. Five, six years back. A Stone, from out past the north gate. I've never seen better feet on a Stone. Guild scout sat on my bench three nights running and then offered him a place. A real one, a house on the river, a station in the spring, his name on a letter." He shrugged. "He went. I'd have gone, at his age. Anybody would."

"And?"

"And the house had his whole history inside a season. Every bout he'd fought on my floor, who he'd beaten and how, what he'd been paid. Not from my book. I never gave it. From people in the crowd, and the betting slates, and a keeper up the river who didn't know any better." Dace looked at his own hands. "The first time he asked them for anything, a better slot, a bigger share, they read him his own past back to him. Every unsanctioned bout, by date. Just so he'd know how it would sound if anybody upstairs ever wanted to hear it."

Cael felt the cold of the wall through his coat.

"He came back," said Dace. "Two years on. Fights for me still, sometimes. Good as ever." He paused. "Careful. He was never careful before. Now he looks at a room before he walks into it, as if he's counting the doors. He won't sit on the end of a bench where anybody can get behind him." He pushed himself off the wall. "So I don't share. Not with the guilds, not with the Compact, not with another keeper unless Vell asks. Not my wall, not my head, not the good ones."

"I won't let it go anywhere."

"I know you won't. I've watched you a year." Dace was already turning back toward the slate. "Only keep taking it down the way you do. And remember whose hands it's for."

"Mine."

"Yours," said Dace, "and the people in it."

He went off down the wall, rubbing chalk off his fingers. Cael stood where he was a while longer with the grey book against his chest.

He had thought of the book, for a year, as the most private thing he owned, after the Log, and he had never once thought of it as dangerous. He thought now of the Shield's page, and the river-academy man's, and Keth's, with all its small notes about a finger off a grip. He thought of the two entries, dated, that had just ended a man's complaint in front of forty people. The same two lines in the wrong hands would tell a stranger exactly how to beat a Shield from Orvet's gym, and exactly when Cael had learned it, and from where. A record did not care whose hands it was in; it said the same thing to everybody.

That evening he went through the grey book from the front, and wherever he had written a fighter's name, he took it out. He did not stop at the strangers. Keth's name went, and the Shield's, and Dessa's, and Ulric's, and the river-academy man's; a name in Vell's ledger was the easiest name in the district to find, and a page beside it on how to beat him was the very thing Dace had described. In its place he gave each of them a mark drawn from something only he had seen. Keth became *the finger*. The Shield became *the drop*. The key to the marks went nowhere but into his own head.

He did not cross out a single fact. The facts were true, and some of them belonged as much to the people in them as to him; he had no right to burn a thing Keth might one day want to know about himself. He only made it so that the book would make sense to one reader in the world, and to nobody who picked it up off a table.

---

The letter from the salt end came to Vell that same week, and Cael was at the table when she opened it.

He was there because she had asked him to carry the main-floor book in from the back room before the evening card; she had come to trust his hands with it the way she trusted them with her lamp. He set the red-taped book down beside the day book and stood back. She was slitting a letter with the end of her pen, a keeper's letter, folded in three and sealed with a thumbprint of candle wax, in a hand he had seen once before on the back-room table.

She read it once, quickly, and then again slowly, and then she put it down flat and laid her hand on it and sat very still.

"Fetch me the third shelf," she said. "The second book from the left. The one with the green tape."

He fetched it. She opened it to a page near the back without looking for it, as if her fingers had known the page all along, and ran down it to a line, and stopped.

He read the line upside down. It was a bout of two years ago, on the main floor, on a season night: a Shield, an Iron formal from a house up the river, against a man Cael knew. Everybody in the district knew him: a Shield too, broad and quiet, from the brickworks end, about thirty, who taught Shield work to newcomers in a hired yard for a copper a lesson and was on Dace's wall most weeks in good slots. The line said he had beaten the Iron formal in the second exchange. Under the result, in Vell's hand: *Iron formal guarded left throughout.*

"I wrote that," said Vell. "I wrote *guarded left* and I never asked why."

She turned the letter round and pushed it toward him. It was from the salt-end keeper, the one who had written to ask what Vell meant by *pressed*. It was short. The Iron Shield from up the river had been fighting at the salt end this season, and losing, and after a bad loss he had sat late in the keeper's kitchen and told her a thing he had carried for two years. On the night he fought at Ardenmere he had had two fingers of his left hand cracked under the wrap, from a fall on a dock the week before. He had told nobody, because he needed the purse, and he had held the shield all night with three fingers and kept his left side shut so nobody would find out.

*He asked me to tell you,* the keeper had written. *I think he wanted it off him. I've checked my own lines for his name and found nothing hanging. You'll want to check yours.*

Vell took her hand off the letter.

"He wasn't beaten," she said. "He was beaten one-handed. And I didn't see it." She got up and went into the back room, and he followed her with the lamp. The pine board hung where it always hung, beside the door, with its column of white-painted names. She found the Iron formal's name and touched it, and then went back to the green-taped book and put her finger on the line under it. "The brickworks Shield went in at Iron-equivalent on that win. One link, from a nail. A good link, I thought." She turned pages, and turned them, and her finger went down the years. "And four lines hang off him since."

Cael counted them with her. Four fighters had each been rated Iron-equivalent in the last two years, wholly or partly, by beating the brickworks Shield on this floor. A Blade. A Stone woman from the north gate. A young Wind not long out of Keth's ring. A Force man who had moved up to the evening cards on it.

"What happens to them?"

"The same as happens to him." Vell was already uncapping the red ink. "He goes back to where the book would have put him if I'd seen his opponent's hand. That's Copper-equivalent, high range. And everyone who went up by beating him goes back to what they'd have been without it."

At the north end of the floor somebody laughed, and the sound came down the room very bright and far away.

Lira came in at the side door when they were back at the table, with her staff, and saw the green book open, and saw Vell's face, and came across at once. She looked at the letter and then at the red ink. Cael watched her go a little pale.

"Is Wendel—"

"Wendel hangs off a different nail," said Vell, without looking up. "So do you. So does the boy." She ruled her first red line under the brickworks Shield's name, very straight. "Go and warm up."

Lira let out her breath, and was ashamed of it, Cael could see, and went.

---

Vell told them all on the same night. She would not let it go a second.

She did not do it at her table, and she did not do it from her book. She closed both books at the end of the evening card and put them under her arm. Then she went out across the floor to find each of them where they were, as she had told Cael in his first week she always did: standing in front of them.

He watched, because she had not told him not to, from the east bench, with the grey book shut on his knee.

She went to the brickworks Shield first. He was at the water barrel with two of his newcomers, a towel round his neck, laughing at something. When he saw her coming with the books under her arm he stopped laughing, though he could not have known why. She stood in front of him and said it, and Cael could not hear the words from the bench, but he could see the man's face, which went still, and then red, and then still again. He saw him look at the floor for a long time. Then he said something short, and Vell said something shorter, and the man nodded twice and turned back to the barrel. His newcomers looked at each other.

The Force man took it worst. He had a slot on Thursday's evening card on the strength of the line, and he knew before she had finished that he would not have it now, and he said so, loudly, so that half the room heard. *Two years.* *Two years and you've only now seen it.* Vell stood in front of him while he said it all and did not move or answer until he had stopped. Then she said one thing, and he turned away from her, and kicked the leg of the bench, and sat down on it with his head in his hands.

The young Wind cried, though she did not make a sound about it. She stood with her arms folded and her chin up while the tears ran, and nodded at everything Vell said, and when Vell had gone she went to the far wall and stood with her face to it until it stopped. Two of the girls from the wall came and stood near her without speaking.

The Stone woman from the north gate was not there. Vell asked Dace where she lodged, and wrote it on a slip, and put it in the book for the morning.

The brickworks Shield came to the east bench afterward, while Vell was writing at her table, and sat down at the other end of it from Cael with the towel still round his neck. He did not seem to want anything. He only sat.

"She said it was her fault," he said, after a while. Cael did not think he was talking to him particularly. "She said *I wrote it down and didn't ask, and that's a keeper's fault and not yours.* Stood there and said it in front of my lads." He wiped his face with the towel. "I've been charging them a copper a lesson on that line. *Iron-equivalent.* I've had it painted on the board at the yard."

"Will you take it down?"

"Tonight." The man looked at his own big hands. "And then I'll have to fight my way back up to it, if I can. Which I might not." He got up. "Two years I thought I'd done a thing I hadn't. I don't know which is worse. Being told, or not having been."

He went out. Cael watched Vell at her table. She had the red ink out and the main-floor book open, and she was ruling the lines one after another down the page, and beside every one, in her square hand, two small letters: *t.f.*

He had seen nine of those lines in an old book in his first week, and thought them the cleverest thing he had ever seen in a ledger. He had done the sum then, admiring it: a dozen corrections in thirty years, each one said aloud to the face it cost. He had not watched one happen, or seen a young woman crying at a wall, or a man kick a bench, or the keeper standing still in front of him while he said it. He had not known a record's honesty would have a sound, and that the sound would be somebody's voice going up.

When the last line was ruled he went across.

"Was it worth it?" he said. He did not quite know why he asked. "For a link two years old."

Vell blotted the page and did not look up.

"The four of them climbed two years on a step that wasn't there," she said. "Every bout they fought since, somebody was paid wrong, and somebody laid coin on a line that was a lie, and somebody else got told they were behind a person who was really level with them. That's two years of small wrongs, and I made all of them, with one word I didn't ask about." She closed the book. "Tonight four people hate me. Tomorrow the book's true again." She put the pen down, square to the edge. "The Registry's a lie about who people are. Mine has to be the other thing. It can't be the other thing if I'll only fix it when it's cheap."

She stood, and took both books under her arm, and stopped beside him.

"Go home, Cael," she said. "And next time you think a record of yours is only your own business, remember the girl at the wall."

# Chapter 51 — The Night Before

The tired stage began on the eleventh day, the Saturday, and Lira made sure he was tired for it.

She did not cut the morning short. She gave him the whole hour and then another half on top of it, at full speed, from the right and the left and the front with nothing before any of it. She sent him to the stone with the drop and up again with the roll until his left knee was shaking at the bottom of every drop and his breath was coming in pieces. At the end she stood back and looked at him the way she looked at a horse, and said, "That'll do. Go and give him what's left," and went up the steps without another word.

What was left was not much. He sat on the bench in the alcove at noon with the bread Brom had brought going down in lumps, and felt it: the particular emptiness of a body that has been spent to the bottom and not yet filled again, where every movement has to be asked for twice. His eyes were slow and his feet were stupid. When Brom stood up and set himself in the middle of the floor, Cael got up after him the way an old man gets out of a chair.

"This is the one," said Brom. "This is how you'll be in the third exchange. May I?"

"You may. May I?"

"You may."

It was worse than defending. Defending had pulled his attention to the blows; this pulled his attention to nothing, because there was nothing in him to pull. The knock went out thin and came back thinner. Twice he planted and arrived and simply forgot to ask, and stood there with the gap going by while Brom gathered and sent. Once he knocked and got *inward* clean and could not make his legs do anything about it, and the push took him in the chest and sat him down on the stone, and he sat there a moment looking at the beams before he got up.

Twelve in twenty.

"Three in five," said Brom. "Tired." He did not sound disappointed. "That's where you are. Now we know."

Then, because it was the eleventh day and he had kept the last three for it, they did the redirect. He had thought, after the tired stage, that he would not be able to; but the redirect did not want quick feet or a clear head. It wanted a plant and a turned shoulder and the nerve to let a push run through him instead of stopping it, and he had that much left. Ten went through at the right angle. Eleven went through a little high, and he felt it in the shoulder's groove like a wheel in a rut. Twelve went through clean. Then the bill came, all of it, along the whole groove at once, and he sat on the bench and let it come.

"Twelve," said Brom.

"Twelve. That's the lot." He did not open his eyes. "The shoulder's done till Tuesday. I'll not ask it for anything till I'm on the floor, and on the floor it'll give me three."

"Three's what you said a week ago."

"Three's what it was a week ago. I've only found out it's true."

---

On the twelfth day, which was the chapel day, Lira cut the morning in half and kept the speed. She said she had seen a farrier do it with a horse before a long road: keep the pace, shorten the work, let the legs come back without letting them forget. He did not argue. His legs had begun, that morning, to feel like his again, a little lighter at the bottom of the drop, a little quicker coming off the right foot.

In the afternoon, tired from the half-morning but not wrecked by it, he knocked Brom twenty times with the gathering and the strikes and the circling, all of it at once, the way the floor would be. The knock gave sixteen.

He held the back of his hand to the light from the arch and looked at the rings for a long time, and then he did something he had not done all fortnight. He turned to the page in the Log where he had written every session, and read it down from the top, and added it up.

*At rest: 12, 14, 16.*

*Moving: 9, 13, 16.*

*Defending: 10, 15. Ninth session, ten of the first twelve before the thirteenth.*

*Tired: 12, 16.*

He looked at it for a while. Every column ended in the same place, or near enough, sixteen in twenty, where against Keth it had been nine knocked and five answered, a coin he had never dared lean on. And the band after each knock came later now and left sooner; twenty knocks that used to stack him to the top of his head left a stripe behind the eyes that was gone by supper.

*About one in five fails,* he wrote, under the columns. *At worst. At a forearm good; at a pace still fair; at two paces still nothing. It answers use. It deepens where it's worked. Brom built the steps; I only climbed them.*

And then, because the Log did not get to keep only the comfortable things:

*It gives me when. It doesn't give me where. Brom's push only ever comes at me. His won't have to.*

Brom read it over his shoulder, slowly, from the front.

"You'll not have twenty on the floor," he said.

"No."

"You'll have however many you can get before your head's full. And one in five of those will lie to you." He sat down. "Spend every one on the pulling in. Don't waste one finding out how he's feeling, or whether he's slowing, or anything you only want to know. The pulling in, and that's all."

"I know."

"I know you know." Brom looked at the arch. "I'm saying it because tomorrow I'll not be in here to say it."

---

On the thirteenth day, the Monday, the Ironyard was full before the afternoon card, and it was not even the night.

Dace had a ledger now, a little dog-eared book of his own that he kept in his coat and took out every half hour and wrote in and crossed out again. It was not Vell's book, and he would not let anybody call it that. It was a list of the people he had promised a place to stand, and it had been growing for a week. Cael passed him at the wall at midday and found him with the little book open against the chalk, running his pencil down a column and moving his lips.

"Here's my trouble," said Dace, without looking round, as if Cael had asked. "The stone holds what it holds. Fill it to the rope and you've the floor full and the benches full and every man able to see over the one in front. Past that, they stand on each other's feet and somebody's child gets lost." He tapped the book. "I've given my word to more than that already. And then there's the street, which never asked anybody's leave in its life." He crossed a name out, and wrote it again lower down, and crossed out another. "So tomorrow, at that door, I'll be telling grown men to their faces that they're not coming in, one by one, while two men from the eel market sell stew in my corner." He shut the book. "Go away. You're one more body, and I'm counting you."

The stew had come in that morning on a handcart, in two great black pots, and the eel-market men had set up in the corner by the slate exactly where Dace had said they could, and were sweeping their own patch of stone already with a kind of pride. Somebody had hung three of the mending-stall pennants over the side door. Dace had seen them and said nothing, which Cael understood to mean he had decided not to see them.

The betting man had given up on his slab altogether and set up a board on two trestles by the pump. It had two columns on it now, side by side, and the columns did not agree. Down the left, under *practitioners*, the man from up the river was a long way in front, the way a man with a guild card for Iron rank eight would be in front in any room where people understood what the card meant. Down the right, under *the row*, it was nearly even, and every time the betting man shortened the left, somebody from the stalls came and laid a copper on the right and he had to shorten that as well.

"Never seen it," the betting man said to Cael, without being asked, in the tone of a man reporting a disease. "Never once in twenty years. The ones who know what he is say he wins. The ones who know what you are say you do. And nobody on this hill knows what both of you are." He wiped a figure out with his sleeve. "That's not a line. That's an argument."

Vell was at her table, and had been at it for two evenings, not with the main-floor book but with the old ones.

Cael had seen her the night before, late, with the shelf in the back room open and four of the tied volumes on the table in front of her. She had been tracing something with one finger from book to book, slowly, writing a figure on a slip of paper at each step. He had known what she was doing without asking. She was going back down her chains. Every rating in the main-floor book hung from another, and that one from another, down through the years to the fixed points at the bottom, the handful of fighters with guild cards whose formal tier anybody in the world would accept. The whole ladder was only as straight as those few nails.

And tomorrow a man with a guild card for Iron, rank eight, would stand on her floor and fight under her witness. Whatever happened, she would write it down, and for the first time in years her ledger would have a new nail at the bottom, driven in by a man the guilds themselves had measured, so that every line hanging above it would hang a little straighter. But only if it ran clean. Cael had understood that too, watching her finger go down the page. A man hurt past the rule, a call that half the room shouted at, a crowd that spilled over the rope while Dace was counting at the door: any one of them, and the people going home up the river would not talk about the fight. They would talk about the floor. They would say the keeper's book in Ardenmere was a thing that could be shouted down, and Vell had spent her whole life making sure it could not.

She had not said any of that to him. She did not need to. He had seen it in the way she tied the tapes on the old books, slowly, each one twice.

She looked up as he passed this afternoon, over her spectacles, and lifted her pen a finger's width off the page.

"Are you ready?" she said.

"I think so."

"Good." The pen went back down. "I'll be writing standing. Don't make me sit down."

---

The lamps went up at dusk, all of them.

Men on ladders had been at it since the middle of the afternoon, putting lamps on every beam and every hook and every nail that would bear one. When they were lit the low ceiling of the old foundry was a single field of small flames from wall to wall, and the black stone underneath shone up at it like the bottom of a pan. Cael had seen it lit like that twice before: for Keth, and for the Brom bout before that. It was brighter tonight. Dace had found more lamps somewhere. There was no corner of the building where a man could have stood in shadow.

It was not the night of the bout. That was tomorrow. Tonight was only the Monday card, four ordinary bouts on the main floor that Dace had moved there because the main floor was lit anyway, and the room was fuller for it than it had ever been for any Monday in Cael's life. People had come to see the place where it would happen. They stood along the rope three deep and filled the benches and sat on the steps of the side door, eating the eel-market stew out of the pots' wooden bowls, talking about tomorrow in voices that went up and down like a tide.

Cael had come early, as he came early to everything that mattered, and found a place at the east post where he could see the floor and most of the room at once.

The first bout was two Copper Blades he had watched a dozen times, and he let it go by at the edge of his eye while the rest of him went across the floor.

The man was sitting on the end of the west bench, alone, with nobody within an arm of him on either side, though the bench was full everywhere else. Cael had never seen him, and knew him at once.

He was not tall, but he was broad through the shoulders and neat in the way a man is neat who has been dressed by other people for a long time and has carried it on by himself, in a plain coat that was better than anybody's in the room. He sat with his forearms resting loosely on his knees and his hands hanging, and he was not watching the bout. He was watching the room the bout was in: the faces along the rope, the stew pots in the corner, Dace at the wall with the ledger, Vell at her table. His attention went from one to another without hurry, the way a man reads a page he expects to be tested on, and every so often it went to the floor for an exchange and came away again.

Cael put the gaze on him.

He did it lightly, as he would have on any man on any bench he meant to learn, because not doing it would have been a lie about who he was. He went in at the first layer, the outward one, *what does he do*, and began to look for the things a body gave off when it sat still. A shifting of weight from one hip to the other. The small endless fidgets of a hand that had been trained to hold something. The way a Path sat in a man when he was not using it, which nearly always showed somewhere, in a set of the jaw or a tightness across the back or a breath held a fraction too long. A year of benches had taught him that stillness always leaked. A man sitting doing nothing told you more about himself in ten minutes than he would in a bout, because in a bout he was thinking about you, and on a bench he had forgotten anybody was looking.

The man on the west bench leaked nothing.

Cael watched him through the first bout and most of the second, and the gaze came back empty. There was a man there, sitting, breathing, turning his head now and then toward the stew pots or the wall. But every movement was the one it needed to be and nothing more. The hands hung and did not fidget. The weight sat even across both hips and did not shift. The breath went in and out at the same easy count, as steady as a clock in another room.

At first he thought it must be training, a stillness worn so deep by twelve years of masters that the man's body had forgotten it ever had idle habits at all. Then, in the middle of the second bout, the man's eyes came round the room and passed over the east post, and went on without stopping. Cael felt something go cold at the back of his neck. A man who had simply forgotten how to fidget would have let his eyes rest on whatever they fell on. This one had moved his gaze on at an even pace, exactly as it had moved over everything else, giving the boy at the post precisely as much as he had given the stew pots and not a hair more.

*He knows I'm looking,* Cael thought. *He knows what a bench can take off a resting body, and he's taking care of it himself. He's not leaking because he's decided not to.* He had never sat across a room from anybody who could do that. He had only ever thought of it as a thing he hoped one day to be able to do himself.

He did not reach with the other thing. It was not his to reach with. What the man gave off to the room was the room's, and Cael could have had it; but across a lit floor of four hundred people the read would only have given him the one great weight a crowd always gave, with nobody in it. Going into one man on purpose, to learn what was in him, was the man's to allow, and nobody had asked him. Cael left it shut.

Lira came along the bench in the gap after the second bout and sat down beside him without asking, with her staff across her knees.

"You haven't blinked since I came in," she said.

"He hasn't either."

"I noticed. It's like watching two cats on a wall." She looked across the floor with her own eye, which was a good one, and was quiet for a while. "He's calm. Not the way Keth's calm, as if he's enjoying himself. Calm as if it's all been settled somewhere else."

"I think it has." Cael watched the man watch the stew pots. "I think he sat down a long time ago and worked out what losing would cost him, and found it was a price he could pay. So he doesn't have to think about it any more."

"Is that good or bad, for you?"

"I don't know yet." He looked at her sideways. "Ask me tomorrow."

She leaned against his shoulder for a moment, the way she sometimes did on a bench, the weight of her there and then gone. Then she sat up and stayed beside him, the two of them watching the room, and neither said anything more until the third bout was called.

---

Reydan had come for the room, and he had it. Then, in the middle of the second bout, he found the boy.

The boy was at the east post, half hidden by the crowd at the rope, and Reydan found him by the quality of his stillness before he found his face. In a room built for noise there were always one or two who sat like that. One had been the river-academy coat on the east bench, the first night; this was another, younger, with a grey book shut on his knee. He was watching Reydan. Reydan felt it the way you feel a draught: a steady attention, patient and not at all hostile, going over him a piece at a time.

He let it.

It was a good look. Reydan knew good looks; he had been looked at by masters since he was eleven, and he knew the difference between a man who stares and a man who reads. The boy read. He began where a good master would begin, with the outside, with what the body did when it was left alone, and he did not hurry. Reydan could almost feel him working down from the shoulders.

So Reydan gave him nothing. He had learned to do it at fourteen, at the academy, sitting through examinations with a master behind him writing. He knew that a resting body told everything to anybody who knew how to look, and he had learned to rest without telling. It was a habit by now, like closing a door behind him. He kept his breath on its count and his hands where they were and let the boy look for as long as he liked.

The boy looked for an hour, and did not get frustrated, and did not look away, and did not go and do something foolish to see what Reydan would do about it. Then a girl with a staff sat down beside him and nudged him with her shoulder, and he said something to her without taking his eyes off Reydan, and Reydan was sure, from the set of the boy's mouth, that he had understood exactly what he was not being given.

Reydan found he was pleased by that, more than he could account for.

He looked for the rest of the boy, while the boy looked for him. He was fifteen, perhaps, or sixteen; the story had not said. He was thin in the way of somebody who had been hungry within the last few years and remembered it. There were bruises on both forearms, old and new laid over each other, and he sat favouring his left shoulder by a hair, which meant somebody had been hitting him there all fortnight on purpose. He watched the room the way Reydan watched it, not the bouts but everything round them, and Reydan could not remember the last time he had seen anybody else do that who was not also being paid to.

And that was all Reydan could say. He could judge what he saw, and he had judged it: a boy who watched like a master and had been worked hard by people who cared whether he lived. What the boy was, under that, he did not know. The keeper's line said *no Path designation*. The boy's body, sitting, said nothing about a Path at all, which might mean anything. Reydan sat on the end of the west bench and found, for the second time on that hill, that he did not want to guess.

Toward the end of the third bout he noticed the woman.

She was near the back by the side door, well dressed for the room, with a small book open on her knee and a pencil she used rarely. Reydan knew the kind of book. He had been written in books like it since he was fourteen, by people who stood at the backs of halls and wrote only when something happened worth a line. She was not writing about the bouts. Every time she lifted the pencil, Reydan noticed, her eyes had just been on the boy at the east post.

He wondered whether the boy knew. He decided that the boy probably did, because the boy seemed to know most things that could be seen from where he sat.

He did not look at her again. Whatever she was, she was not his business. His business was sitting at the east post with a grey book on its knee, and it would be standing at the south mark tomorrow under all those lamps, and then he would find out the rest.

---

Cael had seen her early, at the back by the side door, in a better coat than the room and with the small book on her knee, and had known her from Keth's night and Bede's. He asked Dace about her in the gap before the last bout, at the wall.

"She came in with a pass tonight," said Dace. He did not look round at the side door. He did not need to. "A credential. The kind an academy gives the people it sends to look at things. First time she's shown one. The other nights she paid at the door like a carter." He turned the pencil over. "I honoured it."

"Whose academy?"

"It doesn't say. That's what a pass like that is for: it gets you in without anybody needing to ask the next question." Dace's mouth went thin. "I've let in maybe one a year, these last few years. More this season than I'd like. I turned a man away in the spring who had one with a seal on it I didn't care for." He looked at Cael then. "She's been here before. You know that. I've watched her the way I watch anybody who comes in my door more than twice, and I can't find anything in her I need to keep out. She pays her way and sits where she's put and writes only when you do something worth writing." He went back to the wall. "If that changes, you'll hear it from me first."

Cael went back to the east post and gave her a little of the last bout, in glances, when the room roared and every head in it turned, so that his own turning was only one among four hundred. She wrote twice. Both times, he was nearly sure, she wrote just after he had looked at her, and both times she did not raise her eyes from the page. It was a thing he had seen Keth do, on the bench in the autumn: let a man look, and write down the looking, and give him nothing back but the top of your head.

He wrote it in the grey book on his knee, under no mark at all, because he did not have a mark for her and did not want one yet. *The woman with the book. Academy pass tonight; Dace honoured it; whose, it doesn't say. Writes when I do something new. Won't be looked at back.* He looked at it. Then, smaller: *Tomorrow is a bout. It may turn into something else before it's finished. I don't know what.*

When the last bout ended and the crowd began to move toward the doors, slowly, talking, with the stew pots scraped and the eel-market men sweeping, the man on the west bench stood up and went out by the main door without hurrying. He did not look at the east post. He did not need to; he had looked enough. The woman by the side door was already gone. Cael had not seen her go.

---

Brom found him at the arch of Lira's alcove, as he was going in to be alone with it.

He did not come in. He stood the way Dace stood at arches, with the light of every lamp in the building behind him and his face in shadow, and his big hands at his sides.

"You can win this," said Brom.

"I know."

"I don't mean get through it." Brom's voice was flat and careful, as if he had been carrying the words a long way and did not want to drop any. "I don't mean lose it well and write down what it taught you, the way you've done with everything since I sat down on your bench. You've got good at that. You're the best I've ever seen at it. I don't mean that." He let a moment go. "I mean win."

Cael looked at him for a long time.

He thought of the thirteen days since Dace's shadow came across the alcove floor: Lira's staff from the right with nothing before it, and the drop that had gone into his legs. Twelve redirects and a groove worn through his shoulder. Sixteen in twenty, tired. Ansel buttoning his coat, and the clerk's fourth page, and the half-second in the ninth session that he could not call. A man on the west bench who managed what he leaked. He had always gone into a fight leaving room for a loss that would teach him something, a way out at the back of every plan. He looked for it now, out of habit, and found that it was not there. He had not taken it away. It had simply stopped being there at some point in the last few days, while he was busy.

"I know," he said.

It came out different. He heard it come out different, and so did Brom.

Brom stood in the arch a moment longer. Something in his big shoulders let go, very slightly, the way they let go after the hold when the breath went out through his nose.

"That's what I came down to hear," he said. "I'll be at the north rope. She'll be at the south. If you look for either of us you'll find us, and if you don't look you'll know we're there." He stepped back out of the arch. "Be on your own with it now. I'll walk her up the hill."

He went, the way he always went, without any ceremony, and Cael heard his feet go away along the main room and Lira's voice meet him at the far end. Then he was alone in the alcove with the lamp, and the straw post, and the chalk cross on the floor where Lira had made him drop four hundred times, and the noise of the building going quiet round him.

He sat on the bench against the back wall with the Log open on his knee and did not write anything for a long time.

The lamp burned down a little as he sat. Out in the main room the sweepers went over the stone, and the eel-market men's handcart went out at the side door with its pots knocking, and voices went past the arch saying *tomorrow*, and went on. He turned the pages back, past the fortnight's tallies and the clerk's account and Ansel's four words, and past the two columns, with *Going right* crossed out of *Shape* and written into *Habit* with its cost. He went past the three entries with their notices at the head, Wind and Pressure and Iron, and further, to the eight unasked instances carried from the old book, and the farmer, and *copies of real people, taken on real days*. A year and more of it. He had come down the hill with a bag and a direction, and this was what he had to show for it, and it was not nothing.

He began with his own body, because his body was the purse he would be paying out of all tomorrow night, and he wanted to know what was in it.

*Forearms: yellow mostly, the left still blue where Ansel hooked it. Ribs: quiet unless pressed. Left knee: stiff in the morning, sound by noon; it took all the dropping. Right foot: mine. Shoulder: spent twelve, rested two days, will give three. Lira halved the last two mornings and kept the speed. Legs lighter than they've ever been. Tired underneath, the good kind.*

Then the things he had, short, so that he could carry the list onto the floor in his head and never need to open the book.

*Wind: three an exchange at most, and the lock is two *ands* he'll count. The hip only when I know; when I don't, the feet, which have no doorway.*

*The knock: on the pulling in and nothing else.*

*Pressure: three redirects, each paid for in the side and the breastbone I'll need for the next exchange, so buy only a place I mean to stand in. The giving face, three on the beat, if he has a beat to ride.*

*The gaze: no ration tomorrow.*

He looked at the list for a long time. Then he turned to the front of the book, where the fragments were, and counted them the way he had counted them on the first page a year ago, one finger on each.

Three integrated fragments, one anomaly.

That was the whole of it. Three things he had, with prices he knew, and one thing that had happened once in an alcove and charged him nothing and would not come when called.

He turned to the back again, to the page headed *Anomalies*, and read the entry through. Under it there was a space he had left without knowing why. He knew why now. He wrote one line in it, in the field he had always kept at the foot of every entry for whatever did not fit anywhere else.

*Note: Session nine. Could not reproduce. Still don't know what that was.*

He did not add anything to it. There was nothing to add.

Then he turned to a clean page and wrote the man's name at the top of it, and the plan under it, in a hand that was steadier than he had expected it to be.

*Reydan. Iron-tier, rank eight. Pressure, burst. What I can feel: the pulling in, at a forearm, four times in five. What I can't see: anything else. What I know of him from the clerk: he builds a read of a man, uses it, and the moment it fails he drops it and builds a new one. He's never kept a broken read in his life. Every new one has to be built from what's in front of him at that moment.*

*If I can force him to abandon three consecutive reads, his fourth response will be pattern-broken. That's when I move.*

He read it back once; it was true, as far as he could see, and it was more than he had had on the first night, which had been four lines and a number.

He closed the book.

He did not go up the hill straight away. He sat on in the alcove with the lamp low and the Log shut on his knee, and did not think about the plan, or the knock, or the man. He thought about nothing in particular for a long time: the chalk cross on the floor, the straw post on its rope, the cold coming in under the arch. Fear came and sat with him, and he let it, the way he would have let a dog lie down at his feet, and after a while it was only another thing in the room.

When he went out at last the main room was dark except for the lamps the sweepers had left at the doors. The stone gave back his footsteps. Above it, on every beam and hook, the hundreds of lamps hung cold and black and waiting to be lit again.

Somewhere up the hill, above a saddler's, a man who had been paid for since he was ten was lying in a narrow bed. He had walked out of the building tonight without looking back, at an even pace, the pace of somebody who had never in his life needed to hurry.

Cael went up the steps into the cold, and home.

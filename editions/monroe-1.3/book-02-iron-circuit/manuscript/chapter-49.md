# Chapter 49 — Ansel's Round

Ansel came to the arch on the morning of the eighth day, before Lira, with the frost still white on the steps behind him and his coat buttoned to the throat, and stood there with his big quick hands in his pockets as if he had not yet decided whether he had arrived.

Cael was alone, working the drop against the straw post with his eyes half shut. He felt the light change at the arch and turned, and saw who it was, and stood still.

"The dock man says you can't get anybody fast," said Ansel. He did not come in. "He says she's quick, your girl, but she's a Wind, and she knows you too well, and the big one's slow on purpose. He says you've had nobody in front of you all week who moves like a man who means to finish you." He looked at the straw post, not at Cael. "I've been lying awake since the stew. Six nights. I'd like to stop."

"You said not to ask you again."

"You didn't. I'm asking you." Ansel took his hands out of his pockets and looked at them, front and back, the knuckles that had been broken and set. "One round. Three exchanges. On my terms, or not at all."

"Tell me the terms."

"My speed. Not what I am now, after two years of the cooper's back room and a bag of sand. What I was that autumn. As near as I can get it." He said it flatly, a man reading out a list he had written in the dark. "I'll not hold back to be kind and I'll not hold back because you're a boy. You'll use whatever you've got. Anything. I want to see it." He paused. "And not in here."

"Where?"

"Out there." He tipped his head at the main room behind him, at the long side floor that ran under the east benches, where the newcomers worked in the mornings and the Tuesday cards were fought. "On the stone, in front of whoever's on the benches. Not in a hole in the wall where nobody can see." His mouth moved, very slightly. "I've not stood on a floor where anybody could watch me since I came down the hill. I sweep the cooper's and hit sand in the dark. If I'm going to do this, I'll do it where it counts."

Cael studied him for a while, the buttoned coat and the flat coast face that would not let anything show, and understood that the terms were not for him.

"There's one more thing," he said. "On my side."

Ansel waited.

"There's a thing I do, close in, that some people mind." He chose the words with care, because they would have to be true without being the whole of it. "At about a forearm, if I ask for it, I can tell where a man's weight is going, and whether he means a thing or only looks as if he does. Only that much. It doesn't tell me what he is, or what he's thinking. Only the weight." He met Ansel's eyes. "I'd like your leave to use it. If you'd rather I didn't, I won't."

Ansel looked at him with something in his face that Cael could not read.

"You're asking," he said.

"Yes."

"He didn't ask." Ansel's voice did not change, but something in his shoulders did. "He read every part of me in a minute and a half and he never asked me for a thing. Not that it was his to ask. A floor's a floor." He put his hands back in his pockets. "Ask it all you like. You've my leave. I'd like to see if it does you any good."

---

They fought at the end of the side floor, under the east benches, an hour after dawn, in front of perhaps a dozen people.

It was not a bout. Vell's table was empty and her books were locked away in the back room, and nobody would write a line about it anywhere. But word had gone round the alcoves the way word went round everything in the Ironyard. By the time Ansel took his coat off and folded it on the end of the bench, there were six of Keth's newcomers on the second bench, and the tall lad and the girl who kept her eyes open, and two stall-keepers who had come in for warmth. The dock partner stood at the west post with his arms folded on his chest. Lira had come down under the arch to find the alcove empty and followed the noise. She stood at the rope beside Brom with her staff upright against her shoulder, and did not say anything, and her face was very still.

Ansel stood at the north mark in his shirt with his sleeves rolled, rolling his shoulders, slowly, one and then the other. He looked smaller with his coat off, and like somebody Cael had not met before.

"Begin when you like," said Brom, from the rope. Nobody else had any right to say it.

Ansel began.

He came in from the mark without any warning at all. It was not the stillness Lira had been teaching, the stance with nothing before it; it was simply fast, faster than anything Cael had stood in front of in a fortnight, a short-stepping rush that ate the floor between the marks in three strides, and then his hands were working. He threw them from very close, short and hard, left and right and left, a hook into the ribs and a straight at the face and another hook, all inside the space of one breath, the quick close work a coast house had sent up the river to do it credit.

Cael did not try to work him out.

It was the hardest thing he had ever made himself not do. Every part of him that had been built on a bench wanted to watch the hands, and find the order, and look for the shoulder that went before the hook. He let it all go by and moved instead. He went round on short steps the way Lira had made him go round all week, never settling, never giving Ansel a place to aim at for longer than a heartbeat. When the first hook came from his right he dropped into his left knee and rolled across onto his right foot, low and over, and the fist went over his head through the space where his head had been.

Somebody on the second bench said *oh*.

He had dropped like that four hundred times in the alcove against Lira, and it had never once felt like this. In the alcove the drop had been a thing he did on purpose, slowly at first and then less slowly, while she watched his knee. Here it had happened the way a hand comes up to a face when something is thrown at it, with no part of him deciding, and he came up out of the roll on his right foot with the stone cold under him and the sound of the benches arriving late, as if they had seen it a moment after he had done it.

The second combination caught him. It was the straight, at the face, which he slipped, and then a short hook under it into the left ribs that he never saw at all, and his breath went out of him in one piece. He went back three steps and Ansel let him go, and stood at the middle of the floor breathing easily, watching.

The third combination he felt coming before it came.

He had planted his left foot at the end of a step, and arrived, and let the knock go out in the space before the next step began, the way he had been learning to do for six days with a man circling him. Ansel was a pace and a little more away, a distance at which the knock had always been poor. But Ansel was coming forward, closing the pace as Cael knocked, and the answer came back clean through the closing: *there, coming, heavy on the right*. Cael went left before the right hand had started, and the hook went by in the air, and he was out on Ansel's side with the open ribs in front of him.

He did not hit them; he had thought about it all night, and decided, and he stood off.

Ansel turned and looked at him, and something went across the flat face for the first time, a kind of surprise.

"End of the exchange," said Brom.

In the second Ansel did what Cael had been waiting for him to do.

He did not rush, but came out from his mark slowly, on his toes, and stood at a pace with his hands up and loose, and let Cael come to him. When Cael came, with a short straight at the shoulder to see what it would bring back, Ansel moved enough to let it miss and no more, and watched what Cael did. Cael tried a second thing, a feint at the ribs and a step to the left. Ansel moved again, exactly enough, and watched.

Cael felt it then, in his stomach, and knew what it was: Ansel was doing what had been done to him. He was standing in front of Cael and reading him, the way he had been read in a hall up the river three years ago, letting Cael bring his things one by one so that he could look at each and put it away. He had been lying awake six nights, and he had spent them on this: on what it had been like to be on the wrong side of it, and on what it would be like to be on the right side, once, with somebody else.

And Cael understood that this was the thing he had to learn, the thing in the clerk's fourth page, and that Ansel had brought it to him on purpose.

So he stopped bringing things.

He stood off at a pace and a half and moved. He went round on his short steps, left and right and back, with his hands up and his weight never settling, and he did not throw anything. He did not feint, or try the hip, or step in, or do any of the dozen things a year of watching had taught him to do to find out what a man was. He only stayed alive, and stayed out of reach, and gave Ansel nothing new to look at.

It went on for a long time, the longest he had ever stood on a floor without trying to win.

It was terrible. Every heartbeat of it his body was telling him to go and find out. Twice he caught his own weight beginning to go forward of its own accord, toward a gap he had seen in Ansel's guard, and stopped it, and felt the stopping in his whole frame like a cart dragged up short. The benches did not understand it. He heard somebody on the second bench say *what's he doing*, and somebody else say *nothing*, and the first say *well that's no good*.

Ansel understood it.

Cael watched him understand it, not in the flat face, which did not move, but in the feet. For a while they were patient; Ansel stood at his pace and watched and waited for Cael to bring him something, the way a man waits at a stall for the stall-keeper to come back. Then they were less patient. Ansel's weight began to come forward a little, onto the balls of his feet, a little further at each step. Then he tried a thing of his own, a quick straight at the shoulder to draw something out. Cael moved enough to let it miss and no more, and brought nothing back. Ansel tried another, a hook to the ribs. Cael dropped and rolled right and was gone, and brought nothing back.

And Ansel, who had been sent up the river three years ago because he was the quickest thing on the coast, and who had not been allowed to be quick on any floor since, ran out of waiting.

Cael felt it go before it went. He knocked from a planted foot at a pace and the knock came back with a kind of urgency in it that it had never given him before: *there, coming, all of it, now*. Ansel came in with everything, the whole of the quick close work at once, left and right and left. It was too much and too far, because he had waited too long and wanted it too badly, and his weight went a hand past where it ought to have stopped.

Cael was not in front of it. He had gone left at the knock, on the hip, a read burst, front-left round the outside of the first hook, half a body. In the lock, the two *ands* of bright stillness at the end of it, he saw what he had known he would see. Ansel's whole frame had gone past him with nothing behind it, and the back of his ribs was open, and his feet were wrong.

Cael put a short straight strike into the air a finger's width from Ansel's ribs, and held it there.

Ansel stopped. He stood with his weight gone over his front foot and Cael's fist beside his ribs, and looked down at it.

"End of the exchange," said Brom, quietly.

Ansel walked back to his mark and stood there with his hands on his hips and his head down, breathing, for a long time. Then he looked up, at Cael, across the floor.

"That's what he found," said Ansel. "In the hall. That's what it was."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I wanted to know." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "I've been telling myself for three years that he was too fast, and that's true, he was. But it's not what beat me. I beat me. I couldn't stand there and be read without trying to read him back, and while I was trying I gave him everything." He let out a breath. "One more. My speed. And don't stand off this time. I want to see what you do when it's real."

The third exchange was fast and short and ugly, and Cael lost the first half of it.

Ansel came at full speed, as he had in the first, with nothing held back, and Cael met it with everything Lira had put into him in eight days. He dropped and rolled right under the first hook. He knocked from a planted foot at a pace and got *there, heavy left* and went right on his feet, a beat slow still but not a whole beat. He took a short hook on the forearm that numbed it to the elbow, and another on the shoulder he had spent nine redirects on, and felt the shoulder tell him so. Once he knocked and got smear, a confused rush of weight that did not know which way it was going. Because he had decided before the round that smear meant *don't go*, he did not go. He stood off and took a straight on the forearm instead of walking into the hook behind it, and was glad, after, of the bruise.

At the end, Ansel came in once more with the whole of his quick work, and Cael knocked and got it clean, *right, coming, all of it*. He dropped and rolled right, under it and over, and came up off the right foot into the open side with a short strike that he stopped against the cloth of Ansel's shirt over the ribs.

Ansel's last hook had stopped too. It was resting, very lightly, against the side of Cael's head above the ear, where it would have landed a moment later if either of them had meant it.

They stood like that for the length of a breath, each with a fist against the other, in the middle of the side floor under the east benches, while the twelve people watching made no sound at all.

Then Ansel laughed. It was a short, rough sound, as if it had not been used for some time.

"Even," he said.

"Even," said Cael.

---

Ansel did not go to his coat straight away.

He stood in the middle of the side floor in his shirt, with the sweat going cold on him and the newcomers on the second bench looking at him with the frank interest of people who had just seen something and did not yet know what. The tall lad was staring at him openly. The girl who kept her eyes open had her hand half lifted, as if she wanted to ask a question and had not decided what it was.

"Who are you?" said the tall lad, finally, because nobody else would.

Ansel looked at him for a moment.

"Ansel," he said. "I was Bronze, on the coast. Three years ago they sent me up the river to an invitational and I drew a nineteen-year-old called Reydan." He said the name plainly, as he might have said the name of a street, and Cael saw the dock partner at the west post lift his head. "He put me on the floor of a hall in front of three hundred people inside a minute and a half. My house didn't keep me. I came down here and I've been hitting sand in the back of a cooper's ever since." He picked his coat up off the end of the bench. "He's the one who's coming. Tuesday. That's who this boy's fighting. I came to show him what fast looks like, because I'm the only one on the hill who's stood in front of it."

The tall lad's mouth was open.

"Was he that fast?" said the girl. "Faster than you were just now?"

"Faster," said Ansel. "And it wasn't the fast that did it." He put his coat on and buttoned it, one button at a time, to the throat. "It was that he'd let me show him everything first. I'll tell you about it sometime, if you'd like. Not today."

He walked off the floor.

Cael watched him walk toward the arch past the end of the benches and past the empty table where Vell would sit at noon, at an even pace, neither quick nor slow, with his coat buttoned and his hands loose at his sides. At the arch Brom was waiting for him, and held something out. Ansel looked at it, and Cael saw that it was the two coppers from the cookshop board.

Ansel took them. He weighed them in his palm a moment and put them in his pocket, and said something to Brom that Cael did not hear, and went up the steps into the morning.

"What did he say?" said Cael, when Brom came back along the rope.

"He said he'd buy the next one." Brom watched the arch where Ansel had gone. "He said it was the first time in three years he'd said that name on a floor and not wanted the floor to open under him." He was quiet for a moment. "He walked out like a man who'd paid a debt. Did you see?"

"I saw."

Lira had not said anything all through it. She came along the rope now and stopped in front of Cael and looked at his forearm, where Ansel's hook had gone in, and at the shoulder, and then at his face.

"You stood off," she said. "In the second. For as long as I've ever seen anybody stand off in this building. And you didn't go."

"I wanted to."

"I know you wanted to. I watched you want to. Twice your weight went." She looked at him with something in her face he had not seen since the night of Maud. "You didn't go. That's the thing I couldn't do against her. I was too hungry. You weren't." She turned away. "Ice that arm. You've got me at dusk."

He wrote it before he iced the arm, sitting on the bench in the alcove with the Log on his knee and the forearm going stiff. *Ansel, with his leave. At his speed, a pace and closing. Six knocked, four answered, one smear (didn't go; right), one late. Stood off the whole of the second and gave him nothing. He ran out of waiting before I did. I didn't know I still could.*

---

The defending stage began that afternoon, and the knock fell to pieces again, exactly as Brom had said it would.

It was not Brom's slow burst that did it but the other things. Brom circled, and gathered, and sent the slow push the way he had all week, but now between the pushes he struck as well. They were plain blows from his big forearm, slow and heavy, at Cael's head and ribs and shoulder, and Cael had to meet them or get out of their way while he knocked. Every blow that came at him pulled the whole of his attention to it, for the length of the blow, and the knock in that moment went out thin or not at all. Twice he knocked at the very moment he was blocking, and the answer that came back was only his own arm.

Ten in twenty.

He had expected it, because Brom had told him to expect it, and it still went into him like cold water. Four days ago standing still had been a place the knock could live, and then moving had become one, and now he had taken those away and put a man in front of him who wanted to hit him, and the whole thing had fallen most of the way back to where it had started. He understood, sitting there with the band stacking, that this was what every stage would do. Each time Brom took something away, the knock would fall, and each time they would have to build it up again from wherever it landed, and there was no way to skip the falling.

"Half," said Brom. "From four in five, moving. That's the price of having something in front of you that wants to hurt you." He sat down on the bench. "It's the same price every man with a read pays. Mine fell to nothing the first month I fought in the boat shed. I could read a man standing in front of me plain as a sign over a shop door, till he hit me. Then it was gone till I'd stopped being hit." He rubbed his jaw. "You'll get it back. Not as fast as moving. It's harder."

Cael sat with the band stacking behind his eyes and the forearm Ansel had hooked throbbing in time with it.

"Six days," he said.

"Six days. Plenty." Brom stood. "Tomorrow."

---

Lira came under the arch on the morning of the ninth day and did not begin at once.

She stood just inside the alcove with the staff in her hand and looked at him, and he waited, because he had learned that look and it meant she was going to say something she had decided on the way down the hill.

"The knock," she said. "Brom's taught it all week. Standing, moving, defending. All on him."

"Yes."

"I watched you yesterday with Ansel. It caught him coming at a pace, and he's quicker than Brom will ever be." She turned the staff in her hands. "And I'm coming at you every morning with nothing before it, which is the whole of what the man on Tuesday does. And you've been holding that thing shut every morning, all week, because I told you not to go into me, the night of Maud."

"You did."

"I know I did. I meant it then." She looked at him levelly. "You have my leave. In here, in the mornings, while I'm drilling you, till the floor. Not on the stairs, and not at the table, and not after Tuesday unless I say so again. And you tell me every time it answers. Every time, out loud, so I know what you've had."

He stood very still.

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to. You keep saying that to me as if I didn't know." She came to her mark. "It's no use to me having you train against a man with no beat while you're keeping the one thing shut that might catch it. That's not fairness. That's waste." Her face went short. "And I'm not made of glass. Ready?"

He was not ready, and she came anyway, from the right with nothing before it, and he dropped and rolled and was gone under the staff before he had decided to.

He knocked on the next one. He planted his left foot at the end of a short step and let the knock go out toward her as she began. It came back cleaner than anything Brom had ever given him, *there, coming, all of her, left*, quick and small and alive, like a bird going off a sill.

"Left," he said, and was already gone right.

"Yes," said Lira. She did not stop. "Again."

He knocked on thirteen of the twenty she sent him that morning, and eleven of the thirteen answered, and he called every one of those aloud. The other seven he did not knock at all, because his feet had already gone, and a knock would have shut him in place to ask. He found that the drop and the knock would not share any better than the hip and the knock had, and wrote it in the Log at noon. *Knock, shut, then go: still true. The drop is a go. Don't knock while I'm already dropping. Choose.*

In the afternoon, defending, with Brom striking slow and heavy between the pushes and Cael meeting the blows and knocking in the gaps between them, the knock gave fifteen in twenty.

Brom looked at the rings on the back of Cael's hand for a long time.

"From ten," he said. "In one day."

"Lira gave me leave this morning."

"I heard." Brom looked at the arch, where Lira's staff could be heard faintly from the alcove beyond, working the post alone. "That'll be it. You've had a morning of somebody who doesn't give you a beat, and you've had it with the thing open." He stood up. "That's three stages. Tomorrow we finish the defending. Then tired." He was quiet a moment. "Tomorrow's the ninth session. I'll come at you harder than I have. I'll come as near his speed as Iron Skin lets me."

"Yes."

"You're not frightened of it."

"I'm frightened of everything," said Cael. "It's only that I've stopped minding."

Brom looked at him for a while, and then laughed, which he did not often do, a big slow sound that filled the alcove.

"Good," he said. "That's about right."

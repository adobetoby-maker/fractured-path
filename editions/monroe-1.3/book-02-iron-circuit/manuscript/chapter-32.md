# Chapter 32 — A Trade

Lira cooked most nights, plainly, in her one iron pan, for whoever was at the table. But perhaps four times a year she cooked properly, for company, and every time she told the whole house about it two days in advance, as a town crier tells a street about a hanging.

"Thursday," she said, at breakfast on the Tuesday, to the table at large. "I'm cooking Thursday. Upstairs. For three."

The heavyset man's wife looked at her over the porridge pot with the expression of a woman who has seen Lira cook before.

"Which three?"

"Me. Him." Lira pointed her spoon at Cael. "And Brom."

There was a small silence, and the sister put down her cup, and the heavyset man, who had been reading a carter's bill held at arm's length, lowered it an inch.

"The big one," said the sister. "The one who did the legs."

"The one who did the legs," Lira agreed. She went on eating. "He's coming to supper. I've asked him. I asked him yesterday at the corner and he said yes as if I'd asked him the time, and I'm cooking, and that's all."

Cael, who had not known any of this, looked at her across the table, and she did not look back; she had the face she wore on a mark before *begin*, the one that had decided a thing and did not wish to discuss it.

He asked her about it that evening on the landing, quietly, so that the sister would not hear through the floor.

"You asked him."

"I asked him." Lira was mending a strap and did not look up. "He's eaten yesterday's bread on that wall every day for a month, and he's paid a week ahead at the carters' inn, and he's never once in your hearing said anything about anybody feeding him." She bit off the thread. "And you sit with him every morning and come home with your arms blue and you've stopped telling me what he says unless I ask. So I'm going to sit across a table from him and find out for myself."

"You said you'd find out what he was on your own time."

"Thursday's my time." She held the strap up to the lamp and looked along the stitches. "And I'm cooking. If he's rude about it, that'll tell me something too."

On Thursday the smoke reached the attic before Cael was halfway up the hill.

He could smell it from the corner of the row: onions, and something under the onions that had gone past brown and was arguing about black. When he came in at the front door the hall was blue with it, and the sister was standing at the foot of the stairs with a dish-cloth over her nose and mouth, flapping the street door to and fro to let it out.

"She's burning the house down," said the sister, through the cloth. "Your girl. She's burning it down a pan at a time."

"She's not my—"

"She's burning it down," said the sister, and flapped the door.

In the kitchen the heavyset man's wife was standing back from the range with her arms folded, as a farmer watches a neighbour plough a field crooked and has decided not to say anything until the furrow is finished. Lira was at the range with her single iron pan, the one she would not let anybody else in the house touch, and her sleeves pushed to the elbow, and her face red from the heat and from something else. The pan held barley and mutton and a great many onions, some of them very dark.

"It's meant to be like that," said Lira, before he could say anything.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to." She stirred. "The dark ones are for taste. Everybody at the barn did them like that. Go and lay the table."

"What table?"

"The one on the landing." She pointed upward with the spoon. "I've carried it into my room. Don't let the sister see you."

So he laid the little table from the landing, in Lira's room, with the two plates and the two bowls the house would lend and a third plate of his own that did not match. He put the three cups where they would go and moved the lamp twice, and found he was more nervous about it than he had been about the Shield, and could not have said why.

Brom came at the hour exactly, with frost on his coat and a jar under his arm.

He stood in the doorway of Lira's room and looked at it: the narrow bed, the little table with its three odd plates, the staff in the corner and the spare one behind it, the window with the alcove lamp a small far light below. He looked at all of it the way he had looked at Vell's book on his first day, from the front, as if he meant to read it to the end.

Then he held out the jar.

---

It was a big stone jar with a wooden lid tied down with string, and something moved in it, slowly, when he set it on the table.

"What is it?" said Lira.

"Pickled," said Brom.

"Pickled what?"

Brom considered the jar as though he had not, himself, entirely settled the question. "A trade," he said.

"A trade for what?"

"For a thing I did for a carter." He untied the string. "He said they were the best he'd ever had. He's from the north. I've found they mean something different by *best* up there."

Lira took the lid off and looked into the jar for a long time.

"Some of those are eggs," she said.

"Some of them," Brom agreed.

"What are the rest?"

"I was hoping you'd know."

She did not know, but she took one out with a fork and turned it in the lamplight, and then another, and set them on the edge of a plate in two small rows according to some private judgement of her own. Then she went down for the pan.

It was a good supper; Cael would think so afterward, and he thought so at the time, which was rarer. The barley was soft and the mutton was not, quite, and the dark onions tasted, as Lira had promised, of something, though it was not clear what. Brom ate two bowls of it without a word and then a third with several, all of them in praise. He said it was the first hot meal he had eaten sitting at a table since he left the river city, and he said it plainly, as a fact, without asking anybody to be sorry. Lira went pink and said that it was mostly onions. They balanced the bowls on their knees because the little table would not take the bowls and the pan and the jar at once, and the jar would not leave.

After the second bowl Brom sat back on the trunk Lira had given him to sit on, with his cup in his hands, and looked round the room again.

"You've made a home of it," he said. "The two of you. Here and through there. An actual one."

"It's a room and a half and a table off the landing," said Lira. "Don't go soft on it."

"I'm not going soft. I'm saying what's here." He lifted his cup a little toward the staff in the corner, the spare one behind it, the measuring stick that lived by the stairs, the grey books on Cael's shelf through the open door. "Two years on the road. Three cities. Inns, mostly, and a boat shed for most of one winter, and one bad month on a loft floor over a stable. I've never had a room that looked like somebody lived in it." He drank. "I don't know what that says about me. I haven't worked it out yet."

"It says you keep moving," said Cael.

"Maybe." Brom looked into his cup. "Or nowhere ever made me want to leave a coat on a chair."

He said it the way he said everything, flat, as if it were the price of bread. But it sat in the room for a moment afterward, and Cael saw Lira see it. She did not say anything, but got up and took the jug off the floor and filled Brom's cup, which was not empty, and sat down again. Brom looked at the cup and then at her, and nodded once, and that was all.

They talked about nothing for a while after that, which was the best part. Brom told them about the carter and the thing he had done for him, which turned out to be lifting the back of a loaded cart out of a ditch on the north road on his own while the carter held the horse. Lira told them about the sister's hen, which had laid an egg on the coal-box lid that morning in full view of the kitchen, as if to make a point. Cael told them about Dace's tick and the betting man's prices, eleven to four, and Brom laughed his short laugh through his nose, and Lira wanted to know why Cael had never told her, and he said he had only just finished counting. Through the floor they could hear the sister telling somebody about the smoke.

It was in the lull after that, while Lira was sorting the jar a second time, that Cael tried it.

---

He had not planned to, and he would think about that afterward, and be honest with himself about it, and not like what he found.

But the room was very quiet. He was sitting still, with a full stomach and his cup in his hands and his back against the wall, and there was nothing on earth he wanted to aim at. Across the little table, an arm and a half away, sat the one strong signature he had spent a fortnight learning. It was the exact opposite of the morning in the Ironyard: one man instead of thirty, a quiet room instead of a crowd, sitting instead of standing, and nothing riding on it. He thought, *this is the other end of the scale*, and that was the last thing he thought before the door came open.

It came open easily, which surprised him. He had sat for half an hour on the alcove floor some mornings to get it, and here it simply opened, the way a door opens that was not quite latched, and Brom was there, a great warm weight on the front of him across the table.

And Brom was not resting.

Cael had expected him to be. Everything his eyes told him said Brom was resting: the loose shoulders, the cup held in both hands, his legs out under the table, a big man at his ease after three bowls of barley. On the alcove floor, with Brom standing still to be found, the weight had been loud and plain, a man holding his read open on purpose. This was not that; this was quieter. But under the ease, under the cup and the loose shoulders, Brom's read was still going.

It was low, so low that Cael could not have said how he knew it was there, except that he had sat a bench's length from it for twelve days and felt it on his own skin like a hand. It was turned down the way a fire is turned down at night, raked over with ash so that it will last till morning, giving out almost nothing, never once quite out. It lay on the room. It lay on Lira, sorting the jar. It lay, very lightly, on him.

It never stopped, Cael understood. Not at supper, not with a cup in his hands, not in a room with two people he had come to eat with. Brom had told him once, on the floor, that he did not know where to be hard without it. He had not understood until now that it had been like this every hour of every day for two years. Brom could not put it down any more than a man can put down his own hearing.

He held it. Six breaths. Seven. It was the longest he had ever held anything, and it cost him nothing yet, and he knew the bill was coming.

At eight Brom lifted his eyes from his cup and looked straight at him across the table.

"You're doing it," he said. "Now."

Cael let it go. The door shut. "Yes."

"I can feel it." Brom did not sound angry. He sounded the way he had sounded about the bruise, interested, and a little pleased, and something else. "Not much. Like somebody standing too close behind me in a queue." He drank. "Your range is terrible."

"I know. An arm and a half. Sitting. In a quiet room." The band was arriving; he felt it gather behind his eyes and closed them. "That's all of it there is."

Lira had stopped sorting the jar. She looked from one of them to the other, the fork with something pickled on the end of it held up in the air.

"What," she said, "are the two of you doing at my dinner table that I can't see?"

"Yes," said Brom and Cael together.

She looked at them both a moment longer, and then she took the thing off the end of her fork and threw it at Brom, and took another out of the jar and threw that at Cael, and went back to sorting.

The band was bad, and he sat through the worst of it with his eyes shut and his palms over them. When he opened them again Brom was watching him, and had been for some time.

"It doesn't go out," said Cael. "Yours. Even now. Even here."

Brom turned his cup on his knee.

"No," he said, after a while. "Not since the boat shed. Before that I could shut it, if I went somewhere quiet and tried. Somewhere that winter it stopped needing me to do anything, and then I couldn't stop it either." He seemed to look at the thing he was describing, somewhere a little in front of his face. "I don't notice it. It's like breathing. I only notice it when it finds something." He looked at Cael. "Nobody's ever known it was there. Two years of rooms full of people, and not one of them could tell. You're the first."

"I should have asked."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry. I didn't mean to. That's not an answer, I know." Cael made himself say the rest. "It's a thing I'll be able to do to people. Without them knowing. I hadn't thought about that until just now."

Brom was quiet for a while.

"Mine lies on the room," he said at last. "I can't help that, any more than you can help hearing a cart go by in the street. Who's here, where, whether they're still. That's the room's." He turned the cup. "Going into somebody's another thing. Finding out what's under them, what they are. I don't do that unless they're across a rope from me, or they've said I can."

"And I went into you."

"You went into me." Brom did not make it heavier than it was. "Ask, then. Next time. I'll mostly say yes." The corner of his mouth went in. "It turns out I don't mind. I thought I would." He drank. "Being noticed. It's company, a bit."

Lira had finished with the jar, and had two rows on the plate now and a third, very small, set apart from the others on the very edge, which she did not explain. She sat back on the bed with her cup, and Cael saw her look at Brom for a long moment in the lamplight, with no expression, as she had looked at him across the main floor on the night of the bout. Then something in her face settled.

"You talk like a farmer," she said to Brom. "Not like an estate."

"Velmere talks like Velmere. I stopped doing it somewhere on the road." The question seemed to please him. "We had more forks than people at Velmere. Silver ones, for every kind of food. There was a fork for pears." He thought about it. "I was eleven before I found out other people ate pears with their hands. I thought it was the most dangerous thing I'd ever heard of."

Lira laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, the real one, short and surprised, with her head going back, and Cael had not heard it in that room in weeks.

Brom set down his cup on the little table, carefully, among the plates, and looked at her properly.

"Your Wind," he said. "The one you fight with. It's not the Wind in the manuals."

Lira went still. Cael saw her shoulders come up a finger's width, and saw her not let them go further, and knew what she was hearing. It was every instructor at Fenmark who had ever told her that a step was wrong because it was not the step in the book.

"I changed it," she said. Her voice was flat. "It's better."

"It's better," said Brom. "I've watched three of your bouts, and your feet against the dock partner most mornings. Good. You should be competing at Iron-equivalent."

"I am. Provisionally. Two more bouts and Vell writes it."

"Not provisionally." Brom shrugged. "I read people for my living. You can tell me I'm wrong if you like. I won't be any less right."

Lira stared at him. She looked, Cael thought, the way she had looked on the night of the clock, when Vell wrote *Matched pace* under her name: a person checking a gift for the catch in it. There was no catch; Brom had already gone back to his cup.

She turned her head and looked at Cael. There was something in her face he had not seen there since the winter on the straw, the morning a step had first come right for him and she had been gladder of it than he was.

"I like him," she said.

"I thought you might."

"You could have said."

"You'd have argued," said Cael. "You had to see him take three bowls of your onions first."

She threw the last thing in the jar at him. It was, as far as anybody could tell, an egg.

---

The lamp burned down twice before anybody thought about the hour, and each time Lira trimmed it without getting up from the bed. Brom told them about the river city, about the boat shed and the men in it, and a Force practitioner there who could crack a walnut with his elbow and did so at every meal to prove it. Lira told them about the girls from the wall, and the hen, and the cobbler who had agreed with her about the boot. Cael listened, mostly, and at some point past the chapel bell he noticed that he had not had the grey book out all evening, and had not wanted it, and had not been adding anybody up. He let himself notice it, and then let it go.

When Brom stood to leave he had to stoop under the lintel, and he stood a moment in the doorway with his coat over his arm, looking back at the room, the plates and the jar and the three cups.

"Thank you," he said to Lira. "For the supper."

"It was mostly onions."

"It was the best supper I've had in two years." He said it as a fact. "I'll bring another jar."

"Don't you dare," said Lira.

He was going. She stopped him with a word, on the landing, when he was already two stairs down and the stairwell was dark.

"Brom."

He turned.

"Your feet. On your left side." She stood in the doorway with the lamp behind her. "When somebody quick comes round you that way, you give them ground square, flat-footed, all at once. I've watched you do it to the newcomers. A quick Wind would be round the back of you before you'd turned." She folded her arms. "Come to the alcove at first light. Before the dock partner. I'll show you how not to."

Brom stood on the stair a long moment.

"You'll teach me footwork," he said slowly.

"Somebody has to. It might as well be somebody who's good at it."

Cael saw, in the light from the door, the back of Brom's neck go red from the collar upward, as it had in the alcove over the Log.

"First light," said Brom, and went down.

They heard the street door, and his steps going away down the row on the frost. Lira stood in the doorway a while longer, listening to them go, and then she came back in, and looked at the room, and began to stack the plates.

"Leave them," said Cael. "I'll take them down."

"You won't. You'll drop them." She stacked them anyway. "Go to bed. Your head's still aching. I can see it."

He went through to his own room, but he did not go to bed; he sat at the crate desk with the lamp turned low and opened the Log, not to the Iron-adjacent entry but to Tuesday's page, the bout night's, where the narrow column ran down the edge. There was one line in it, and he wrote a second under it.

*B., across the table. Did. Didn't ask. Will.*

Then he turned to the Iron-adjacent entry and wrote what was new, and only that.

*Eight breaths, seated, an arm and a half, a quiet room, nothing wanted from it. Opened easily. B. at rest is not at rest: his read never shuts. It runs low, under everything, like a banked fire. He felt me at eight. Band after, an hour.*

He looked at that a while. Then, outside the fields, in the smaller hand:

*Two things, not one. What a person gives off to a room belongs to the room: that they're there, where, still or moving, how they press. I can't help that, any more than I can help hearing feet on the stair. Going into one person on purpose, to find out what's in them (kindled or not, working or resting, what's under the ease), belongs to them. That I ask before I do. Tonight I went into B. without asking. That was the wrong one, and it was mine.*

He closed the Log. On a loose page at the back of Hesk's notebook, where he kept the things that belonged in no book of his own, he wrote one more line, because the day had held it and he did not want sleep to take it away.

*Last winter I came over the hills with a bag and a direction. Tonight three of us ate at a borrowed table and nobody in the room was afraid of anything. Somewhere up the hill there's a file with my name on it. Tonight I forgot it was there.*

Through the wall he could hear Lira still moving about, putting things away, humming something with no tune to it at all.

---

He went down to the Ironyard at first light the next morning for his own hour with Brom, and found that his hour had been taken.

The side door was unbarred already, and the night man was asleep by his brazier with his blanket up to his ears. Across the dark floor, in the second alcove, the lamp was lit on its hook, and in its light two people were doing something to each other's feet.

He stopped at the arch and did not go in.

Lira had chalked a line on the alcove floor, a short straight stroke from the back wall toward the arch, and she was standing on one side of it with her staff laid across her shoulders and her hands hooked over the ends. Brom stood on the other side, in his shirt, with his coat on its nail and his hands unwound, looking down at the line as if it had been written in a language he could almost read.

"Again," said Lira. "I'm coming round your left. Slowly. Don't give me the ground. Give me the line."

She came, at a walk, round his left side, and Brom did what Cael had watched him do to a dozen quick newcomers. He gave her ground, all of it at once, flat-footed and square, a big man stepping back from a door that has opened toward him.

"No." Lira stopped. "You've just shown me your whole back. If I were quick I'd be in it." She tapped his left heel with the end of her staff. "This one. You pick it up and put it down behind you. Don't. Turn on it. Let the hip go round and take your shoulders with it, and I'm in front of you again instead of beside you. You haven't moved a step. You've moved the angle." She went back to her mark. "Again."

He did it again, and it was wrong again, in a different way: he turned on the heel, as told, but he turned too far and too heavily, so that he finished facing the wall.

"Better," said Lira. "Now you're guarding the wall. Nobody's going to hit you from the wall."

Brom laughed his short laugh through his nose, and set his feet, and said, "Again," before she could.

Cael watched them for most of a quarter-hour from the dark of the arch, and neither of them saw him; or, he thought, Lira had not, and Brom must have felt him come up on the read and had said nothing, because it was not his morning to be watched. Lira was hard on him, harder than she had ever been on Cael in the first winter, and she explained less. She would show it once at the speed she meant and once slowly and then not again, and when he got it wrong she told him exactly how, and when he got it right she said nothing at all and came round him faster.

And Brom took it. That was what Cael kept watching. The man who had put him on the stone four times on the main floor stood on a chalk line in the lamplight and was told he had shown his whole back to a girl with a staff, and took it the way he took a light hit on the shoulder, without minding. Every time she said *again* he set his feet. Twice he asked a question, short and exact, the kind Cael would have asked. Once he got it so nearly right that Lira stopped halfway round him and stood still and looked at his feet for a long moment, and then said, "There. That. Do that a thousand times."

"A thousand."

"It's not many. I did it ten thousand before it was mine." She went back to her mark. "And stop reading me. I can see you doing it. Look at my feet, not at whatever you look at."

"I can't stop reading you."

"Then read my feet," said Lira, and came round him again.

Cael went away from the arch, quietly, across the dark floor, and sat on the end of the east bench in the cold until the sweepers came. He did not mind; he found, a little to his surprise, that he was glad, and that the gladness was not about him. A few weeks ago Lira had sat at a kitchen table and said she would find out what Brom was on her own time. This was what her own time looked like. It did not need him in it.

When the dock partner came in at the side door, broad and broken-nosed, with his breath going up, Brom came out of the alcove with his coat over his arm and saw Cael on the bench and sat down beside him, heavily, like a man at the end of a long day instead of the start of one.

"She's very good," he said.

"She's the best there is."

"I know that now." Brom looked at his own boots. "My feet are terrible."

"They're terrible," Cael agreed, "and so were mine," and Brom looked at him sideways, and the corner of his mouth went in, and they sat a while longer in the cold and watched Lira begin with the dock partner as if the last hour had been nothing at all.

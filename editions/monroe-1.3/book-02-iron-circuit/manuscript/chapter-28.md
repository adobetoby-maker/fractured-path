# Chapter 28 — The Whole Log

Brom stopped being careful with him at the first touch, and Cael felt the difference in his teeth.

It was a week to the day since the main floor, a Tuesday again, grey and very cold, with a skin of ice on the water barrel by the side door that the night man had broken with his heel. They had the alcove to themselves for two hours before the sweepers. Brom had hung the lamp, and taken his coat off, and stood on his cross and said nothing at all, not even *what are you testing*, because Cael had told him on the walk down from the corner. *All of it. What you are when you're not being kind.*

So he found out.

The first answer threw him off his cross and two strides across the alcove, into the wall, shoulder first. The second turned him so hard over his bad knee that he heard it, and stood very still on the stone for a moment, waiting for the knee to tell him what it thought. It thought it would hold, and he said nothing. Brom watched him say nothing, and set his feet again, and the third answer came up through Cael's arm like the shock of a dropped anvil, and the fourth sat him down on the floor.

He got up. The first hour was mostly that: the stone, and then his hands under him, and then his feet.

But he learned more in that hour than in the two mornings before it together. Brom at full did not only send him further; he sent him truer. The careful answers had been rounded off at the edges, softened in the turn, and now the edges were back. Cael could feel every one of them. He could feel the lean in the first touch as plainly as a word spoken aloud, and the well, and the angle the turn came out at; and he found that if he kept the whole of himself in the skin of his knuckles, he knew before the turn came which wall he was going to hit.

He did not stop to write anything. His hands would not have let him. He went back to his cross, and set his feet, and hit, and was answered, and went where he was sent, and came back. After a while it stopped being a test of anything. It was only a thing his body did, over and over, in the grey light, like a man splitting wood: set, swing, the shock up the arms, set again. He stopped counting the exchanges somewhere after thirty. He stopped thinking about the lean. He was not watching Brom any more in the way he had watched him from the bench; he was simply there, close, a forearm off, with nothing between them but the next blow and the next answer.

He was coming back to his cross after the last of a run, with his weight still settling, one foot down and the other not yet, when the quiet came.

He knew it at once; he had felt it four times in his life and every time something had been written afterward in the dark behind his breastbone: at Torvin's by a lamp, and twice more in his first year, and in Lira's alcove in the middle of a broom drill. There was no mistaking it. A hush began somewhere in the middle of him and moved out to the edges, and the morning went on without him, as if a heavy door had been swung shut on it from his side. The alcove went on. The lamp went on burning. Brom was a forearm away on his cross. But all of it was suddenly on the far side of something, very clear and very distant, like a room seen through a window at night.

And then, differently from every time before, something settled.

It did not settle in his hip, where the Wind lived. It did not settle low under his ribs, where the giving face sat waiting. It went outward. It spread over him from somewhere in the middle and lay down on the outside of him, thin, all over at once, the way the first frost lies on a field: on his arms and his back and his face and the palms of his hands, a fine new layer of something laid on his skin that he had not put there. And through it, for one long breath, he could feel the alcove.

Not see it, but feel it: the cold dense weight of the stone wall at his back, a breath behind him, pressing very faintly on the back of his neck as a wall presses on you in the dark. The post on its rope to his left, a small hard shape hanging. The lamp's little warmth above. And in front of him, close, a forearm off, a great warm blurred weight in the air, big as a door, steady as a post. It pressed against the front of him everywhere at once, so that he could have told, with his eyes shut, exactly where it stood and how far.

Then it went. The layer did not leave him; he could feel it was still there, lying on his skin. But it folded away, quiet, like a sheet folded and put in a drawer, and the door between him and the morning swung open again, and he was a boy standing in an alcove with one foot not yet down, in grey light, on a cross of chalk.

---

He stopped moving altogether.

Brom had begun to set his feet for the next one. He stopped too, half set, and looked at him, and Cael saw him feel it: not see it, feel it, on the read, close. The big man went very still.

"Something," said Cael.

It was the term. He said it out loud because he had promised to, and because it was the only word he had.

Then he sat down where he was, on the cold stone of the alcove, with his legs crossed under him the way they had been when the shoulder rang, and he reached for his coat on the floor by the wall and got the Power Log out of the inside pocket. He did not look at Brom, and he did not say anything else. He opened the book on his knee to a clean page in the middle, and uncapped the ink, because this was a thing he would want in ink, and waited.

The letters came in the dark behind the bone, as they had always come, plain as print held up to a lamp. He copied them as they came, slowly, letter for letter, in a hand that shook a little and then stopped shaking.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Iron-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
Note: surface-awareness component. Pressure read, limited range.
```

He read it.

He read it three times through, with his finger under each line as he had read Vell's book on his first morning in the district, before he would let himself believe any of it. It was a notice, the shape of the others; it had their fields, their flat voice, their *unnamed* and their *partial*, and the same bare word in the place where a tier should be. He knew how to read it.

Except for the last line, because none of the others had had a last line like it. The Wind's notice, at Torvin's, had told him a name and a duration and nothing about what the thing did; he had spent months finding that out with Lira and a stick. The Pressure's had been the same; they had always left him to work the rest out on the floor. This one had a *Note*. It said what the thing was for, or what it was made of, in six words and a comma, as if whatever kept the form had decided that this time it would tell him something to begin with.

*Surface-awareness. Pressure read, limited range.*

He sat with the pen still uncapped in his fingers and read the six words again, one at a time.

*Surface* he understood, because he could feel it: the new thin layer lying on him, on the outside, where the Wind had never been and the giving face had never been. *Awareness* he thought he understood, because of the breath when he had felt the wall at his back and the lamp's warmth and the great blurred weight in front of him. *Pressure read* frightened him a little, in a way none of the other words did, because he knew what a pressure read was. He had sat a bench's length from one for twelve days while it laid itself on him like a hand on a beam. He had heard it described, in the alcove, by the only man in the district who had one, as a sense of who was walking about on the floor upstairs.

And *limited range*: that one almost made him laugh. He had worked out the range of the original himself, from a bench, by counting where a big man chose to sit. Now the form was telling him that his copy of it was short too, and he did not yet know how short. Shorter than a bench, he thought, and very likely a good deal shorter.

He looked up from the page at last, at the big man standing on the cross in front of him with his hands still half set, and understood exactly whose *pressure read* it was, and exactly what *limited range* meant. The length of a bench. Close, whatever you do.

He had not asked for it; he had been splitting wood. He had not reached for anything all morning; there had been nothing in him reaching. It had come the way the Wind had come and the way the Pressure had come, after close days of somebody's whole body against his own: not taken, not called, simply arrived and found in him afterward, like a word you discover you have picked up from someone you have spent too long beside.

---

Brom had not moved. He was standing on his cross with his weight half set and his face doing nothing at all, and Cael saw that he had not moved because he did not know whether he was allowed to.

He reversed the book on his knee, so the ink faced the other way, and lifted it.

Brom came off the cross then, and crouched, so that their heads were level, and took the book in both hands as though it might spill. He read the notice with his lips moving very slightly, the way he had read the keeper's book from the front, each word on its own, making sure of each before he trusted the next. He read it again. Then he sat back on his heels and looked at Cael, and then at the page, and then at Cael.

"You absorbed part of my Path."

He did not say it as an accusation. He did not say it as a wonder, either. He said it the way he had said *something I've never seen before* in the alcove a fortnight ago: a man reading a figure off a page and wanting to get every digit of it right.

"Part of it," said Cael. "The edge. The read, it says, and not much of that. Limited." He looked at his own hands, turned up on his knees, blue at the wrists. "Not the hardness. I don't think I'll get that by standing here. I don't think it works like asking."

"When?"

"Just now. Coming back to the cross. I'd stopped thinking about anything." He touched his own forearm, the outside of it, where the new thing lay. "It went over me. On the skin, all over. And for a breath I could feel the room. The wall. You." He looked up. "You're big."

"I know I'm big."

"On it, I mean. You're very big on it." He found he was almost laughing, and did not know why. "Like standing next to a stove."

Brom was quiet. Then he said, carefully, because it was the term and he had promised: "I had you. On the read. When it came." He seemed to look for the words, as he had looked for them for the shelves. "The weight in you that moves about. For a breath it had a thing in it that I knew. Like walking into a strange house and seeing one of your own chairs." He stopped, and decided that would do. "Then it went back to being you."

"Can I use it?" said Cael, mostly to himself. "That's what you want to ask."

"It's what you want to know."

"Not yet. I've got it. That's a different thing." He thought of the Wind, months of mornings on the straw before it would come when he called it. "It'll be a long while. If it's like the others."

They sat on the floor of the alcove facing each other in the grey light, the boy with his legs crossed and the big man on his heels, and neither of them said anything for a while.

Cael was thinking about the wall at the top of the market square, and the bread, and *Show me the log sometime*. He had said *maybe*, and he had meant it: not *no*, and not *yes*, but *not until I know*. He had thought it would take a long time to know, and that he would have to watch the man for months, the way he watched everybody, and add him up, and come to a ruling. But he had watched him already. He had watched him for a month from a bench, and on the main floor through four exchanges, and on a wall telling the end of his own story before its beginning, and for three mornings a forearm off with his whole weight coming back up Cael's arms on every line it was sent. There was nothing left in the man to add up. There had not been, he thought, since the wall.

And there was the other thing, which he did not like to look at directly. The man in front of him had just had a piece of himself turn up in somebody else, without being asked. If it had been Cael, he thought, he would have wanted to see the whole of the place it had gone. Not the one page. Not a careful summary read out by the person who had it. Everything, so that he could judge for himself where his piece had landed and in what company.

It was not a debt, exactly, because Brom had not asked for anything. He was crouched on his heels on the cold stone with his face doing nothing and his hands open on his knees, waiting, as he waited for everything. But a man who had told the end of his own story first, at a wall, so that the listener would know where it was going and could stop him, was owed at least as much as that.

The maybe had run its course.

He held out the book, not open at the page but shut, the whole of it, the cover with *The Power Log.* written inside, and every page after.

Brom looked at it, and did not take it.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

---

Brom read it from the front.

Of course he did. He sat down properly on the alcove floor with his back against the wall and his knees up and the Log open on them, and he started at the inside of the cover, at *The Power Log.* in Cael's best hand, and went forward one page at a time, slowly, as his grandmother's builder had taught him to walk a floor.

Cael sat across from him and watched him do it, and found it was the hardest thing he had ever sat through. Harder than the main floor. On the main floor he had at least been able to move.

Brom read the six fields with their *Claim* and *Evidence* and *Ruling*. He read the Wind, the whole of it: the half-breath, the lock, the hip, the fan and the empty quarter, *It does not shorten. It is not a fault. It is a price.* He stopped on that for a long time. He read the REVISIONS, and the update word for word, and the chain of two, *the fill shows*. Once he lifted his eyes from the page to Cael's chest, the way Lira had, and went back down.

He read the Pressure more slowly still. He read the taking face and the giving face, and the wrist, and the ceiling, *egg, fist, hole*. He read *It isn't the thing that fails past three. It's me*. And then he came to the bottom of the entry, and Cael watched his eyes go along a line, and stop, and go back to the start of it, and he knew which line it was.

*Ceiling for B.: nought. See the sheet.*

Brom laughed.

It was the second time in a week, and it was larger than the first. It came out of him all at once, a short helpless sound, and he put his hand over his face with the Log still open on his knees and his shoulders going, and when he took the hand away his eyes were wet at the corners.

"*See the sheet*," he said.

"It was a real sheet."

"I know it was a real sheet." Brom wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand. "You spent an hour finding out how much of a thing you could throw, and the answer was three, and then you wrote down that against me it was none." He looked at the page again. "And sent yourself to a sheet."

"The ink one's further down."

Brom read the ink one. *Against B.: no giving face. Not once.* He stopped laughing. He looked at it for a while, and then, without saying anything, he looked across at Cael's right shoulder, the one the quarter had rung like a bell, and back to the page.

He went on.

At the head of the Wind entry, under *Source*, there was a name, and Brom's finger stopped under it for a moment. He looked up at Cael.

"She knows."

"Since the start. Since before I'd written any of it down properly." Cael found he wanted Brom to understand this, more than he had expected. "She's read every page. She measured half of them. That's her stick in the margin."

Brom looked at the page, at the notches drawn in pencil where Lira had marked the half-body and the hand's width, and at the small *L.* beside a line, and the *Lira* written over it in ink.

"Good," he said, and went on reading.

He read the back of the book, the things that were not fields. *The fragments are not static.* The farmer. *Whatever I ever borrow will come with fingerprints.* Then the eight instances carried from the old Log. He read Tuesday's page, the whole of it, the plan and the second door and the third exchange and the accounting. He read the two ledgers, and the narrow column at the edge of the page headed *Carrying*, with nothing in it.

Then he came to the bottom of the page, and the small line under the column, and stopped.

He did not laugh at that one, or say anything. He sat with the book open on his knees and looked at the line for a long while, and Cael, across the alcove, watched the back of the big man's neck go slowly red, from the collar up, all the way to his ears. Brom did not look up. He turned the page, quite carefully, onto the morning's notice in its fresh ink, and read that again, and then he closed the book and held it out across the stone in both hands.

Cael took it.

"You're going to be something no one has a word for," said Brom.

"I know."

"Good." The corner of his mouth went in, and stayed in. "That means you get to choose the word."

---

"Does it change anything?" said Cael. "Now you've read it."

Brom thought about that with the whole of his attention.

"It makes it bigger," he said at last. "What I don't know. I thought I didn't know one thing about you. Now I don't know a whole book." He looked at his own open hands. "That's better. I'd rather have a book."

"But."

"But." Brom looked at him. "Three fragments in a year is fast."

Cael waited.

"At home, when a thing came fast, my grandmother used to say the bill came after. A good harvest, a quick promotion, a marriage settled in a month. Fast meant there was something nobody had read yet in the small print." He moved one thumb on his knee. "I don't think she was right about most things. But I've watched a lot of people in three cities, and when something comes to somebody fast, it's very good or it's very bad, and they can't tell which from inside. And neither can anybody who's only watched them a month." He paused. "I don't know which this is. I'd like to watch long enough to find out. I'd like to be there if the bill comes."

"You think it's bad."

"I think it's early," said Brom. "I think you should go on keeping that book the way you keep it. It's the most honest thing I've ever read about anybody, and you wrote it about yourself, which is harder." He got up off the floor, slowly, a big man unfolding, and stood looking down at the cross of chalk. "Who else has read it?"

"Lira. From the start."

"Good."

"Nobody else."

Brom nodded, as if a number had come out where he expected.

He stood a while with his hand on the coat on its nail, not putting it on.

"The column," he said. "At the edge of Tuesday's page. *Carrying*. There's nothing in it."

"I don't know what goes in it yet."

"I do." Brom said it to the wall. "Two years I carried a word my family had for me. Nobody said it. They didn't need to. I carried it into the boat shed and into the second city and four hundred miles down the road, and it cost me every day, and I never once used it for anything." He took the coat off the nail at last. "That goes in it. Things like that. You'll find you've got some."

Cael looked at the book in his hands.

"I think I've got one," he said. "It's a word, too."

"I know you have," said Brom. "I read it in her book on the first morning."

"Not tomorrow," he said. "The sparring. I'll come at the same hour, but we're not going to hit each other." He reached for his coat on its nail. "I've had the read two years. Since my station. I'm the only person in this city who knows what it's meant to feel like from inside, and you've just been handed a piece of it with no instructions but six words and a comma." He put the coat on. "Tomorrow you're going to try to open it. And I'm going to stand there and tell you why you can't."

---

Lira was at the kitchen table when he came in, with the lamp, and her own book of something she never showed him shut under her hand. She looked at his face, and then at his forearms, and then at his face again, and put the book in her pocket.

"Something happened."

"Yes."

He sat down across from her and opened the Log to the morning's page and turned it round, as he had turned it round for her a hundred times in a year, and she read the notice. She read it with her lips not quite moving. When she got to the *Note* she read it twice, and then she looked up at him with an expression he had not seen on her since the day, a year ago, when he first turned the old Log round on a table and let her read it.

"Three," she said.

"Three."

"His." It was not a question. She put her finger on *Pressure read*. "That's his. The thing he does with you on the bench."

"The edge of it. Not the rest."

Lira sat back with her hands flat on the table either side of the book. Then she said, carefully, "Did you show him?"

"Yes."

"The notice?"

"The whole Log," said Cael. "In the alcove. He read it from the front."

She did not say anything for a while. She turned the lamp down a little, which it did not need, and turned it up again.

"That was ours," she said at last.

He had known she would say it, or something like it, and he had thought on the way up the hill about what he would answer, and none of it had seemed any good. So he did not answer; he waited.

"No," said Lira. "That's not right." She was looking at the lamp. "It was yours. It was always yours. I read it because you let me, and I liked being the only one you let." She folded her hands on the table. "I minded, just now. When you said. I'm telling you I minded so you'll know I'm not pretending I didn't. And now I've said it, and you can stop looking at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like a man who's counted his own teeth." She almost smiled. "What did he say? When he'd read it."

Cael told her. *Something no one has a word for. You get to choose the word.*

Lira listened to it with her head on one side. Then she said, quietly, "He's right," as if it had cost her something to say.

"He laughed," said Cael. "At the Pressure. At *See the sheet*."

"He laughed?"

"Twice this week. Once when I sat down on the floor, and once at that."

Lira looked at him for a long moment, and then down at the notice, at the six words of its *Note*.

"Mine didn't come with a note," she said. "The Wind. When you got it off me."

"No."

"Just *Wind-adjacent* and nothing, and you had to find out the rest with me and a broom handle for a whole winter, on cold stone, while I hit you." She turned the page back to look at it again. "And his comes with a *note*." She shook her head slowly. "I'd have liked a note."

"I'd have liked one too."

"You'd have argued with it," said Lira. "Then you'd have tested it to find out whether it was lying." And then, as if she had only just heard it: "He read it from the front."

"From the inside of the cover."

Lira laughed then, short and surprised, the way Brom had, and put her hand over her eyes.

"Of course he did," she said. "Of course he did."

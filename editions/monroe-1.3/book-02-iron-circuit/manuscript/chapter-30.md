# Chapter 30 — Main Season

Dace put the main season up on the Monday, as he had said he would, and the first thing Cael saw on it was his own name, three times, in white chalk, where people had asked for it.

He had not expected that to feel the way it did. Dace had warned him, the week before, standing on the step with the bill rolled under his arm: *there's men on it who'll want you on their card now that didn't on Tuesday morning*. Cael had not quite believed it. Now he sat on the end of the east bench with the grey book shut on his knee and looked at his name in the second slot of an evening card, against a Copper-formal Shield who had asked for him by name, and he found that being wanted was less comfortable than being overlooked. A man who asked for you had been watching you. That was the whole of what it meant.

Dace did it standing on an upturned crate with a fresh stick of chalk and the whole floor watching him. He began at the top left corner of the long wall by the slate and worked right, and he did not stop until he ran out of wall. Two cards on most nights now instead of one, and three on the busy ones; an afternoon card every day but the chapel day; the main floor lit twice a week. The rings and dots and stitches of his private grammar went in beside the names as fast as the names did, so that by the time he climbed down the wall looked less like a schedule than like a field somebody had sown in a hurry.

"That's the season," said Dace to nobody, and dusted his hands on his trousers. "Anybody who wants to be on it and isn't, the list's in my head. Anybody who's on it and wants to be off it, the list's shorter."

The floor laughed, and then it went to the wall, all at once, the way pigeons go to a dropped crust.

Cael did not go. His knees would have carried him, though they still had opinions about stairs; but there were forty people between him and the wall, and he could read the wall from where he was, which meant he could read the forty people as well. He watched who went to the top of it first.

At the top, in the corner where Dace always put the line, a name stood by itself on the first main-floor night of the season with no opponent chalked against it. *Keth.* Under it Dace had drawn a small square and left it empty: the box for whoever Keth would fight. The whole floor seemed to know without being told that the box would stay empty until Keth himself had said who he wanted in it. Cael looked at the empty square for a while. Then he looked down the floor to the newcomers' ring, where Keth sat on the edge of a bench with his practice blade across his knees, not looking at the wall at all, watching a girl's feet.

He had a page on that man, though he had never meant to have one; it had grown anyway, a line or two at a time, through a whole season of dawns, and he did not open it now.

The district followed the board, a half-step behind it. The chestnut man moved his brazier to the main door on the nights Dace had marked for lamps and was always there an hour before the doors opened. The pie stall baked a second batch on main-floor nights and sold it cold the next morning at a third off. The purses followed it too: twice the Assessed bouts at the same twelve marks, and a main-floor season night that could pay out more than a side floor paid in a month.

And Dace was better at the board than the betting man was. Cael had not meant to find that out. He had only noticed that Dace marked some bouts with a small hooked tick, the *watch this one* mark, and that the betting man's chalked prices did not always agree with it. So for a fortnight he wrote them both down, and then who had won. Where the two disagreed, Dace was right eleven times and the betting man four. He showed it to nobody, because it was not his business, but he underlined it, and he thought of a man who could keep a district's faces in his head like a room full of benches and seemed to know which of them would get up.

The other thing that doubled was Vell's queue. The more people fought, the more people lost, and the more people lost, the more of them came to the keeper's table in the mornings with a reason, having slept on it. Vell heard every one with her pen down and her spectacles on, and Cael began, without meaning to, to keep a tally in the bottom corner of a page. Money, mostly: somebody had laid coin and lost it and wanted the result to be the reason. Then grudges between gyms, dressed as complaints. And once in a while a real question about the rules, which Vell answered at length and with what looked very like pleasure.

By the end of the first week the corner said *money 9, gyms 3, rules 1*.

---

He showed Lira the *Carrying* column on the Tuesday, because he had promised to show her first, and he had already waited four days longer than he liked.

He did it in the kitchen before the household came down, with the lamp between them and the frost still on the window over the basin. She had come in from the dock partner's hour with her hair damp at the temples and her staff on her shoulder. She stood it in the corner and sat down with her bread before she saw that the Log was open on the table at her place, turned round to face her.

She looked at him. Then she looked at the page.

He had turned it to Tuesday's page, the bout night's page, where the narrow column ran down the edge under its heading, with one line in it, very small, in pencil.

*L., through the wall. Didn't.*

Lira read it, and read it again, and did not touch it.

"When?"

"The teaching day. That night." He made himself say all of it, in order, as he would have said it to the book. "I wasn't trying. I was sitting at the desk with my head aching and nothing to aim at, and it opened on its own, a crack. You were on the other side of the wall. About two paces. I could feel that somebody was there." He stopped. "I shut it. As fast as I've ever shut anything."

"What did I feel like?"

He had not expected that question, and he gave her the true answer because he had no other ready.

"Light. Quick. Never still for long." He looked at the table. "Like a bird on a sill that keeps shifting its feet."

Lira was quiet, and then she breathed out through her nose, not quite a laugh.

"That's me, is it."

"That's what it said."

"It's not wrong." She turned the bread over in her hand and did not eat it. "You could have not told me. I'd never have known."

"I'd have known."

"Yes." She looked at the column again, at the one word on the end of the line. "*Didn't.*" She said it as she said the name of a step she was teaching, as if trying it for weight. "That's the right word. You didn't. It's not *couldn't*, and it's not *won't*. It's the thing you did instead." She pushed the book back across the table to him, gently, with two fingers. "Thank you for showing me."

"It might happen again. I don't know how to stop it opening. Only how to shut it."

"Then when it does," said Lira, "tell me the same day. Not four days after." She began on the bread at last. "I'd rather know I've been heard than be kept from it. Being kept from things is what Fenmark did." She chewed, and swallowed, and pointed the end of the loaf at him. "And if you ever find out what I sound like when I'm cross, don't tell me that. I don't want to know."

He laughed, and she did too, and the heavyset man's wife came down the stairs into the middle of it and looked at them both with deep suspicion and put the porridge on.

Afterward, alone at the crate desk with the Log open, he looked at the column for a while. He had ruled it for himself, for the cost of carrying a thing shut. It had not occurred to him until that morning that the things he carried shut might be about other people as well, and that some of them might want to know.

---

The first thing the new sense taught him, once he began asking it anything at all, was that it did not care how hard he tried.

They worked in the alcove before the sweepers, as they had for the crosses, with the lamp on its hook and the cold coming up out of the stone. Brom stood three paces off with his arms loose, and Cael sat on the floor with his legs crossed and his hands open on his knees, as he had sat on the teaching day, and waited for the door to come open. It came, on the seventh try that first morning, and he felt the big warm weight in front of him. Then Brom took one slow step backward, to four paces, and it went.

"I had you at three," said Cael.

"I know. You leaned." Brom came back to three. "When I stepped away, you went after me. Not your body. The other thing. I felt you reach."

He tried again, and again, for most of that week, and at first it went the same way every time. The door opened at three paces, and Brom stepped back, and something in Cael that had been trained for a year to follow a thing that moved went after him. The moment it went, it was aiming, and the door shut.

He began to understand on the fourth morning, and it was from his knees that he learned it. They had been bad on the stairs, and he had come down to the alcove tired and sore and in no mood to try for anything, and had sat down on the stone as a man sits who does not mean to get up for a while. And the door opened at three paces and stayed open. Brom stepped back to four, and it stayed; to five, and Cael did not go after him, because he was too tired to go after anybody. He only sat there, and let the big man move away across the alcove, and the weight on the front of him went with Brom as the warmth of a fire goes when you walk away from it. It thinned, but it did not stop.

At six paces Brom stopped, with his back nearly to the arch.

"Still?"

"Still," said Cael, and as he said it the door shut. Talking shut it. Everything shut it.

The band came round his head a minute later, hard, and he sat with his palms over his eyes while it settled.

"Six," said Brom, from the arch. He sounded pleased in his flat way, the way he had sounded about the bruise. "Twice what you had on the first day."

"I didn't do anything."

"I know. That's what did it." Brom came and sat on the bench. "You went still. Properly. Not the way you go still on a bench with your book, with your eyes going like a pair of ferrets. Still like a stone in a field."

Cael thought about it with the band round his head, and found it was true, and found that he did not like it. Everything else he owned had grown when he pushed it. The Wind had gone from a hand's width to half a body because he had pushed it every morning for a winter. The giving face had found its ceiling because he had pushed it until it broke. This would not be pushed: when he reached, it got shorter, and when he stopped reaching, it got longer. The more he wanted from it, the less it gave, and the only way to buy distance was with stillness.

He wrote it in the Log that evening, under the day-one entry, and he wrote only what was new.

*Range: bought with stillness, not effort. Reaching shortens it. Six paces once, sitting, tired, not trying. Costs the same band, a little worse.*

Then, because it was the thing he was worst at, he added a line under it. *Every other thing I have, I learned by pushing. This one I'll have to learn by stopping.*

---

Cael had been watching the Shield back for most of a month.

The man fought out of Orvet's gym in the tannery lanes. He was broad through the chest, a little older than most on the Assessed cards, with a round iron-rimmed shield he carried on the left arm as if it had grown there. He was good, patient behind the rim and hard to get round, and when he had a man where he wanted him, near the rope with nowhere to go, he came off his back foot in a shield-rush that put his whole weight behind the rim and his short club behind that. It had put three men on the stone in the bouts Cael had watched.

And before it, every time, his lead shoulder dropped.

It was very small: a finger's width, the rim of the shield dipping a little with it, as a man's shoulder dips when he sets it against a door he means to shove. Cael had seen it first on a side floor three weeks ago and written it down, with the date, and a question mark after it. He had seen it again eleven days later and written it down again, with that date, and taken the question mark off. Both entries sat on the Shield's page in the grey book, one under the other. Under them was a third line, in ink: *Drop, then rush. Two of two. Wait for three.*

The bout was in the middle of the afternoon, on the side floor by the north wall, and the room was thin, the way the Ironyard was thin at that hour even in the season. There were fifty or sixty on the benches, carters between loads, the newcomers from Keth's ring, a few of Orvet's people at the west post in a knot round Orvet himself. Orvet was already shouting, with a red neckcloth on and a grey beard that wagged when he shouted, and Cael had long since stopped hearing him except as weather.

At the betting man's crate by the side door, a man in a good coat with academy stitching across the shoulders was counting coins into the betting man's palm. Cael knew him; he had last seen him at Vell's table a few months back, with a folded paper in his pocket, being told what the keepers would say about him from here to the sea. The man looked across at Cael once while he paid, and then away.

Cael noticed it, and put it with the faces, and went to his mark.

His knees were good, not new but good; two weeks of the stairs and the alcove crosses had brought them back to something he could trust for a short floor and four exchanges if he did not ask them to do anything clever. He had decided, walking down, what he would ask of them, and it was not much. The Wind on a read, or not at all. The Pressure left where it was; the Shield did not hit hard enough to need it answered, and he did not want it on anybody's page. And the new thing on his skin, he would not open at all. On a floor, against a man who was moving, it would be worth exactly nothing, and it would take the other two down with it when it went.

"Begin," said the junior keeper at the side table, a young man of Vell's who wrote the afternoon cards in a hand very like hers.

The first exchange was a survey, and Cael paid for it. He let the Shield come to him, as the man liked to, and went round the rim to the left and the right to see how fast it followed. It followed fast, and once he was slow getting out, on the left, where the knee was weaker, and the iron edge of the rim came across his forearm with a crack he felt in his teeth. It was nothing that would last, but it was a fact about the man's left side that he had not had from the bench, and he took it and filed it while his arm was still ringing.

The Shield did not rush in the first exchange; he was watching too.

In the second he came forward, and kept coming, and began to walk Cael back toward the rope.

It was well done, and the man did not hurry it. He came forward a half-step at a time with the rim up and his club low behind it, and each half-step took a little more of the floor away, so that Cael had to choose, each time, between giving ground and going round. Going round meant the rim, which was fast. Giving ground meant the rope.

He gave ground. He gave it slowly, on short steps the knees could sign for, and he watched the shoulder.

Two more half-steps, and his back heel touched the chalk of the boundary. Behind it, a hand's width away, was the rope, and the benches behind that, and faces. Somebody on the near bench drew a breath. At the west post Orvet's voice went up a tone, telling his man something about his feet that his man did not need telling.

The Shield's weight settled on his back foot.

Cael did not look at the club, or the rim, or the man's face. He looked at the lead shoulder, at the place where the shirt met the strap of the shield, and he waited, and he did not breathe more than he needed to.

The shoulder dropped. A finger's width, as it had in both entries; the rim dipped with it.

The hip went on the read. It went as it went now when he had paid for it with watching first, cleanly, cheaply, with the half-breath held. The floor went short, front-left, half a body along the chalk and out of the line, and he was in the lock, two *ands* of bright stillness with the world going by in front of him.

The world was the Shield, coming off his back foot with his whole weight behind the rim, into the place where Cael had been.

There was nothing there, and the rim went on into the rope, which took it, and bowed, and two people on the near bench went backward off their seats with a yelp. And the Shield's whole left side was open to Cael where he stood in the lock: the arm out straight behind the rim, the ribs under it, everything a man shows when he has put all of himself into one direction and there was nothing at the end of it.

The lock let go.

Cael put his short straight strike into the ribs under the shield arm. He threw it with nothing from his middle in it, only the hip and the shoulder and the step, and it was enough. The Shield grunted and came off the rope sideways, and went down on one knee on the chalk with his arm wrapped round himself.

"End of the exchange."

The room made its noise. At the west post Orvet was shouting something long and aggrieved about the rope.

Cael went back to his mark and went over himself. The hip line was there, thin, the read price, a pencil line and not a brushstroke. The forearm rang where the rim had caught it, his knees were fine, and his breath came back into its count in four. He had spent one burst and nothing else, and he still had his whole afternoon in him.

Across the floor the Shield got up, slowly, and stood with his hand flat on his side for a while. He looked at Cael, and he was not angry; he looked like a man going back over a sum he had got wrong, trying to find the line where it went bad. Then he looked down at his own left shoulder, as if it belonged to somebody else.

He knew, Cael thought. Not what it was. But he knew that something he had done had been read, and that it was his.

The third exchange was short.

The Shield came forward again, more carefully than before, and he walked Cael back again, but not so far. Twice he set his weight on the back foot for the rush. Both times Cael saw the shoulder begin to go down, and both times the man caught it and held it up by main force. So the rush that came after was a rush with no shoulder in it. It was slow and stiff and short, a man pushing a door with his arms alone because he no longer trusted his back. Cael did not need the hip for that; he stepped off the line on his own feet, two short steps the knees agreed to. The rim went by at the length of an arm, and he put the same strike into the same ribs, a little harder.

The Shield went down on both knees this time, and stayed there a breath, and then lifted his club hand off the floor, open.

"Called," said the junior keeper. "Hand up. Third exchange."

---

Vell came over from her table while the young man was still blotting, which she did not usually do for an afternoon card, and read the line over his shoulder.

"Write *atypical*," she said.

"It says *atypical*."

"Then write it again under mine." She wrote it herself in the day book she carried on her arm. "*Cael. Win. Third exchange.*" The pen moved. "*Atypical movement pattern.*" She looked at Cael over her spectacles. "Clean," she said, which was all she said, and went back to her table.

The Shield came across the floor to shake hands, with his arm still held in against his side. He had a broad plain face with a white scar through one eyebrow, and he did not let go of Cael's hand straight away.

"What did you see?"

Cael thought about it, because the man had asked a fair question, and he had a right to an answer of some size. But the answer was on a page in a grey book, with two dates on it, and the page belonged to the book.

"Something you'll find yourself," he said, "if you go and look at how you stand before you go in."

The Shield considered that, and then nodded slowly, as Ulric had nodded in the spring.

"I'll look," he said, and went off toward the west post, where Orvet met him with both arms going.

The academy-coat man was no longer at the betting man's crate. Cael looked for him along the benches and did not find him, and noticed that he had looked, and wrote it down that evening in the corner of the Shield's page, under the result: *The man from Vell's table, the Orvet complaint. Laid coin on the Shield. Watched me while he paid.* It was nothing, and he wrote it down only because he wrote everything down.

The purse was twelve marks, eight his. He paid his rent out of it on the way up the hill, a week early, to the heavyset man on the step, who took it without counting and said, "Win?" and nodded at the answer as though it had been a weather report.

At the crate desk he wrote the bout plainly, a line an exchange, as he always did. *First: survey. Rim fast on his left; my left slow; took the rim on the forearm. Second: walked me to the rope. Waited. The drop came. One burst on the read, front-left; lock; ribs. Third: he held the drop up by force, and the rush died without it. Two steps, no burst. Ribs. Hand up.* Under the second exchange he wrote the cost: *one read burst, thin line.* Under the third he wrote the thing he liked best about the afternoon, which was that he had not needed the hip at all.

*He corrected his tell in one exchange,* he wrote. *It cost him his rush. Fixing a tell can cost more than the tell.*

Then, under that, the date, and a small tick beside the Shield's page: three of three.

There was a letter on the desk when he came in. He had seen the hand on it from the door, square and slow, with the tail of the *k* in *Hesk-ward* drawn the long way as it was in every letter he had had since he came over the hills. He opened it last, when the bout was written, as a man saves the best thing on the plate.

*Your letter came on the Thursday, after,* Hesk had written. *I read it at the table and knew from the first line you'd be writing me another, because a lad who expects to win writes about the bread. So I've had two days to make up my mind you lost, and I'm sorry if you didn't, and gladder than I can say about the boy. You found him. I could tell from how you wrote it. My father used to say a man who knows what he's afraid of before and tells you after is worth two who don't. The race has ice at the edges now and the wheel runs slow. Mind the knees. Whatever they are, mind them. H.*

Cael read it twice. He had not told Hesk about his knees; he had told him nothing at all yet about the bout. He sat a while wondering how a man at a kitchen table four days off by post had known to mind them. Then he understood that Hesk had not known anything; he had only known Cael, and guessed what a boy who lost to a man like that would have paid for it.

He put the letter in Hesk's notebook, at the back, with the others, and turned down the lamp.

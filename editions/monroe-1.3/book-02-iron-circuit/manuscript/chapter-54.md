# Chapter 54 — On the Stone

Reydan lay on the stone and took stock of himself, because that was what you did first, before anything else, before you even thought about the man who had put you there.

He had been taught it at eleven by a master with a bad hip who had lost more bouts than any man in the academy and taught more winners than all the rest together. *When you go down,* the old man had said, *you are a clerk before you are a fighter. Count what you have. Then decide what to do with it.* Reydan had gone down perhaps twenty times in eleven years of floors, and every one of those times he had lain still and counted. He counted now.

The ribs on the right side, low, under the arm. That was the first strike, the one that had gone in through the hard of him as if the hard were not there; and then the last one, whatever the last one had been, through the same place. Something in there clicked when he breathed past halfway. Not broken, he thought. Cracked, perhaps. A month of sleeping on his left side and not laughing.

The front of the right shoulder, where the joint was. That was the hook, the left hand, which had come up from below on the beat of his own release when he had been certain the boy's left arm was finished. It had not been finished. It had only been put away, which was a different thing, and he made a note of that, a note to himself in the place he kept such notes.

The right thigh above the knee. That had been the low one, into the gathering. It had not hurt much at the time, and now the whole muscle had gone dull and heavy and would not do what he told it.

And under all of it, a deep plain shaking that he knew very well and did not like: his body reporting that it had been sent to fetch something heavier than itself, and had fetched it anyway.

His mind was perfectly clear. That was the strange thing. He had given his body three orders since he landed, *elbow, knee, up*, and it had heard all three and done none of them. So he had stopped giving them and laid his head down on the stone, which was cold, colder than he would have thought a floor could be in a room this hot, and he lay there and listened.

There was no sound at all. Then there was a woman's voice saying his name and the boy's name and a rating and a method. Then there was a noise like a roof falling in.

*Well,* thought Reydan. *That's that.*

He found that it did not feel like any loss he remembered. He had lost once in the river halls, years ago, to a man who would not come out and play, and that had felt like being cheated, though he had known even at the time that it was nothing of the sort. This did not feel like that. This felt more like reaching the bottom of a staircase in the dark and finding one more stair than you had counted. He was not angry. He was surprised, and under the surprise there was something he had not felt on a floor for a long time, and it took him a moment to know it for what it was. It was interest.

He began to get up.

He did it the way he had been taught to get up after a bad fall, one part at a time, asking each part before he trusted it. The left elbow first, which answered. The left knee, which answered. The right leg, which grumbled and then answered. He came up to sitting and stayed there with one hand flat against his right side, taking a few breaths that stopped short of the click, and then he got his feet under him and stood. He stood for a while longer than he needed to, letting the room settle and his legs decide that they were legs.

The boy was standing in front of him.

He was a pace and a half away, no more, and he had not moved since the end. His right arm hung at his side, and the way it hung told Reydan that it was no use to him at all just now. His left was held across his ribs. He was very pale under the lamps, and he was breathing in short careful pulls, the way a man breathes when every breath has a price. And he was looking at Reydan with exactly the look he had worn on the east bench the night before: patient, steady, unhurried, reading.

Reydan had spent eleven years on floors, and he knew most of the things that could be done on one. He had been stopped by an Iron Skin who hardened at the exact instant his burst arrived; he had been turned aside by a Pressure man who saw it early enough to step off the line and take it on the slant. He had never once had his own burst come back to him. Not turned aside, not stopped, not spread into the floor. Sent back, along its own line, into the place it had come out of, with all its weight still in it. He knew the weight of his own burst better than he knew the weight of anything in the world. He had been hit by his own work.

"What Path is that?" he said.

"I don't have one."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know."

The boy did not look away when he said it, and he did not look pleased with himself either. He said it the way a man tells you the road is out ahead, plainly, because it is true and you will need to know it.

Reydan found that he believed him. He did not know what he believed; only that the boy was not lying, and that whatever the true answer was, the boy did not have it either. He looked at him a moment longer, running the fourth exchange through again behind his eyes, and it still came out at the same place: a turned shoulder, and then nothing he had a name for.

He let it go, for now. There was something else he wanted to say, and he wanted to say it before the room came back.

"There's a woman at the back by the side door," he said. "Grey coat. A book." He did not turn his head to look; he did not need to. "She's been writing about you since before I sat down last night. I know the kind of book. I've been in a few. People who write in books like that do it for somebody, and they don't stand at the back of a hall like this one for the pleasure of it." He paused. "I thought you'd want to know. I think you know already."

"I know."

"Good." Reydan breathed, short of the click. "Find me later. I want the answer when you have it."

He turned away. A man in the front row of the crowd was holding out a coat to him, a good plain coat, with both hands, as if it were something that might break. It took Reydan a moment to remember that it was his own.

---

He walked home by the river road because it was the longer way, and because he did not want to be in a street full of people telling each other what they had seen.

The night was very cold and very clear. The lamps of the district went down the hill behind him and the river went along beside him, black and quick and talking to itself, and he walked slowly with his coat buttoned to the throat and one hand inside it, against his ribs. Two or three people passed him going the other way and looked at him, and one of them looked twice and opened his mouth, and Reydan looked back at him pleasantly until he shut it again.

He had been beaten once before, in the river halls in his second season, by a man who would not come out and play. There had been perhaps two hundred in that hall. He remembered the faces turning toward the other man as the fifth exchange began, the way a field of barley turns when the wind changes, and the cold feeling of being, all at once, not the thing everyone had come to watch. He had felt that cold again tonight, on the stone, and it was not as bad as he remembered. It was, if anything, rather clean.

The saddler's was dark. He let himself in at the side door with the key the saddler's wife had given him, and climbed the narrow stairs one at a time, and lit the lamp in his room and sat down on the edge of the bed to take off his boots. It took a long time. The right leg did not want to bend, and the right side did not want him to lean.

When he had them off, he did not lie down. He sat with his hands on his knees and ran the bout back from the beginning, the way his masters had taught him to do with every bout he had ever fought, won or lost, before he slept.

The first exchange. He had come straight, to see how the boy moved, and the boy had moved four ways in four bursts and thrown nothing. There had been two openings, and the boy had seen both and let both go. Reydan had seen him see them. At the time he had taken it for caution. Now, sitting on the bed, he did not think it had been caution at all. He thought it had been a boy buying something.

The second. He had fired into the landing, because the landing was the one thing about the boy that never changed: two counts, every time, a shape you could hang a burst on like a coat on a peg. It had been the best idea of his night. It had cost the boy an arm and put him on the floor. And then the boy had got up, in pieces, carefully, and looked at him across the stone with no expression at all, as if he had been sent a bill he had been expecting.

He had walked him to the rope, and the boy had gone out of it sideways, crooked and ugly and through a burst that ought to have stopped him. *He paid for that,* Reydan thought. *He paid for every step of that. He chose to pay.*

The third. The boy had come.

Reydan sat for a long time with that. The whole of his evening had been built on a boy who stood off. Every burst he had fired had been fired at that boy, and every price he had paid had been reckoned against him. And then the boy had come across the floor on the same strange sideways step he had used to get away, so that Reydan had fired at a place a retreating boy would have been and found nobody in it. Then he had been inside, closer than anyone stood to Reydan by choice, close enough to step into the one place a burst cannot go. And he had hit him three times, with a weight that did not belong in a boy that size, on the beat every time.

*He made me build three pictures of him,* Reydan thought, *and he made me spend all three.* He had heard of fighters who did that. He had never met one. He had always been the one doing it.

And so he had stopped making pictures. He had gone to the post. Twelve years of his life had been spent at one post or another, in his father's yard and the academy's, pouring out bursts on a count until he could do it asleep, and when the pictures ran out he had gone back there without even deciding to. And the boy had heard the count. Reydan was sure of it. He had seen the boy's lips move, once, in the middle of the fourth exchange, as if he were counting along.

And then the turned shoulder. And then the thing he had no name for.

He went back to it three times, sitting on the edge of the bed with his boots on the floor beside him, and three times he came to the same place and could go no further. A Pressure man could turn a burst aside. An Iron Skin could stop one dead. Nobody he had ever heard of could do what had been done to him, and the boy who had done it had stood there afterward and told him, honestly, that he had no Path at all.

There were two ways to carry a thing like that home.

He had seen the first. One autumn the other house had withdrawn its boy before the final of the invitational, over a wrist that was perfectly well, and the boy had never forgiven Reydan for a bout that never happened. He had let it go bad in him. He had come back the next year with a grievance instead of a plan, and then not come back at all. Reydan had seen men in the river halls do the same with a single bout, keep it like a stone in a shoe for years. They walked a little crooked all their lives and blamed the road.

The second way was harder, and he had only ever seen it done once or twice, by masters. It was to be more interested than you were hurt.

He sat in the lamplight in the room above the saddler's and found, a little to his own surprise, that he had already chosen. He had chosen it on the stone, with his cheek on the cold floor, at the moment he had thought *that's that* and found that what he felt was interest. He had come three days down the river on a wool wagon because a keeper in his own city had heard a story about a boy with no Path. He had wanted to know whether the story was true. It was truer than the story. And he still did not know what it was.

*Then I'll ask,* he thought. *Not tonight. Not this year, perhaps. But I'll ask, and I'll keep asking until he can tell me.*

He lay down carefully on his left side, and put out the lamp, and lay a long time in the dark listening to the river and to the small click in his ribs, before he slept.

---

Lira got to him first.

She came under the south rope without asking anybody's leave and across the stone at a walk that was very nearly a run, and she did not say anything at all. She put her hands on him. She went down his left forearm from the wrist to the elbow, pressing, watching his face. She went to the ribs on both sides, and then to the left shoulder and the right. At the right shoulder she stopped, because he could not quite keep his face still. She looked at him, and took her hand away, and put it back again more gently, flat, the way you put your hand on a horse that has been frightened.

"You did it," she said.

"Yes."

She did not say anything else for a moment. She stood with her hand on his shoulder and looked at him, and he saw that her face had been somewhere he had not been, the whole of the second exchange and the whole of the fourth, at a rope she could not cross with a mouth she could not open. He saw it come back, slowly, from wherever it had been.

"Your arm," she said.

"It's not broken."

"I know it's not broken. I felt it." She took her hand away. "It's going to be the colour of a plum by midnight, and you're not going to be able to hold a cup with that hand tomorrow, and I'm going to have to hold it for you, and I'm going to enjoy it."

Brom had come round by the north rope. He stood a little way off, not crowding, with his arms folded, and looked at Cael the way he had looked at him from three benches back on the first night they ever sat in the same room, as if Cael were a page that had turned out to have writing on the back of it.

"What did it feel like?" said Brom.

Cael thought about it. He thought about it honestly, because Brom had asked honestly, and because it was the first question anybody had asked him since the end that he could answer. He went back through the fourth exchange: the mill, and the count, and the shoulder full, and the last one turning; the moment at the fork, and his knees, and the half second afterward when he had not known whether they would hold.

"Like I had enough."

It was true. He knew it the moment it was out of his mouth. It was not that he had won. It was that for once, at the very bottom of everything he had, there had been enough.

Brom nodded slowly, as if Cael had given him a figure and the figure was right.

"Sit down," said Lira. "Before you fall down. Sit down here, on the stone, and don't argue with me."

He sat down on the stone.

---

The floor did not clear quickly. It took the better part of an hour, and for most of that hour Cael sat at the edge of the north mark with his back against the rail post and his left arm across his knees, and let it go past him.

People came up to the rope and looked at him and went away again. Some of them said things. The dock partner came and put one enormous hand on the top of the rail post above Cael's head, and left it there a moment, and went away without a word. Ansel came, with his coat buttoned to the throat, and stood at the rope for some time looking at the middle of the floor where Reydan had lain. Then he looked at Cael and nodded, once, a whole sentence in it, and went out by the side door into the cold. Vell did not come. She stood at her table with the red-taped book open and wrote, and wrote, and turned a page, and wrote on. Dace went round the walls with the doorkeeper, gently moving people toward the doors like a man herding hens.

Lira sat beside him on the stone with her shoulder almost against his and did not talk. Brom stood. Brom watched the doors.

"You looked at me," said Brom, after a while, to the doors. "In the third. Before you went."

"I did."

"I nodded."

"You didn't nod. You did the thing with your chin you do when I say a number aloud and it's right."

Brom thought about that. "I didn't know I did that."

"You do it every day."

"Then I'll go on doing it," said Brom, "now that I know what it's for." His eyes came round to Cael at last. "Put it in the book, with the rest. A man ought to know what his own chin's saying."

Cael was going to answer him, and then he saw the woman in the grey coat.

She was standing three strides off, at the edge of the stone, with her small book shut in one hand, and he had not seen her come. He had been watching the doors with Brom, and the sweepers, and the stew pots going out, and somehow in all that watching she had crossed half the floor without once being a thing he looked at. He knew how much practice that took. He had been trying to learn it himself for a year. She was waiting, without any sign of impatience, for him to finish seeing her before she said a word.

He finished. She was perhaps forty. Her coat was a good coat but a travelling one, soft at the cuffs and the hem from long use, and her boots had walked a great deal of road and very little floor. Her hands had no marks on them. She stood like a woman who had stood at the backs of a great many rooms and been noticed in none of them, because she had arranged it so.

Lira's shoulder went still beside his. Brom unfolded his arms.

"My name is Quenna," said the woman. "I'm a senior teaching-practitioner at Greyvane Academy. Silver-tier." She said it without any weight, as if she were telling him the time. "And what I do, mostly, is this: I specialize in irregular Path development assessment."

---

He made himself sit up straighter against the post, though it cost him, because he wanted to see her face while she talked.

"You've been here before," he said.

"Six nights, counting tonight." She did not look at her book. "On the first two I watched every bout on the card. Something had come up the river to us, about this hill, and I came to see whether it was a fighter or a story. By the end of the second night I had stopped watching the card. The other four nights were for you." She tipped her head a little toward the side door. "Your Circuit Master knew my pass came from an academy. Tonight, at the door, I showed him which one. I thought he'd earned that."

"Four nights," said Cael.

"Watching what you do with a thing the first time you do it." She seemed to find nothing odd in saying so. "That's my work, all of it. I keep a drawer at Greyvane with eleven years of notes in it, about fighters on floors like this. Most of them showed me everything they had on the first night, and every night after that they showed me the same thing a little better. You've shown me something new every time I've come. Tonight you showed me more than I've written down in a year." She turned the closed book over once in her hand. "I'm not here because I was curious. Curiosity I can feed at home. I came down to make you an offer."

She laid it out the way a woman lays a table, one thing at a time, each in its place.

Greyvane's charter held a provision, very old, which nobody there had ever had much use for. It had been written for children whose ability showed itself before any Arbiter had looked at them, so that the academy could take a child on what she could do and not wait on a paper that did not exist yet. It was called the demonstration-provision track. For as long as anybody remembered, it had been used for those children and for nobody else.

"But it doesn't say children," said Quenna. "It doesn't say *unkindled*. I've been through it word by word more often than I'd like to admit, and the words are these: *any practitioner without a standard Arbiter-issued classification*." She let the words stand by themselves on the stone between them. "That is you. I don't need to know why it's you. There are people at Greyvane who would like to know whether that sentence means what it says, and I am the one who has been saying for years that it does. I'd like you to be the proof."

There was something under the last words that was not quite pride. Cael heard it and put it away for later. She was not reading him a rule. She had an argument, she had been carrying it for years, and she meant to win it.

"The Compact keeps a file on me," he said, at once, plainly. He did not want her to find it out later and wonder why he had hidden it.

"We know that." Her voice did not change. "There are people in the building whose whole work is that sort of trouble. They have handled it before, and they will handle it again after you."

"My classification—"

"No." She lifted a hand, very slightly, and he stopped. "Nobody at Greyvane will ask you what you are. That isn't what the provision is for. Once a month you will stand in front of two of the assessment staff and show them what you can do, and they will write down whether it reaches the standard. That is all. Month after month, in front of people who know what they are looking at." Her eyes went once to the middle of the floor, where the chalk of the south mark was scuffed nearly away. "Tonight would reach it with a good deal to spare."

"And the month it doesn't?"

"Then we sit down, you and I, and find out what's changed, before anyone decides anything." Something sharpened in her voice for the first time, very slightly, the way a knife catches the light when it is turned. "The ordinary road through an academy is a gate with one question at it, asked once, and if you can't answer the question you never get as far as showing anybody anything. Most of the names in my drawer are carters now. A few are dead. I think that's a waste, and I've said so to the masters for eleven years, often enough that some of them have stopped inviting me to supper."

"How many?" said Lira. "That you've brought in."

Quenna looked at her properly for the first time. It was long and unhurried, such as a woman gives when she has already read a page about somebody and wants to see how close the page came.

"Three, in eleven years. One stayed a season and went home to keep bees. There was nothing wrong with his fighting; he simply couldn't bear a bell telling him when to eat, and I ought to have seen that in him before I asked. Two stayed. One of those two teaches now." She looked back at Cael. "Two masters and an afternoon, every month, for every one of you. That's what you cost us. I've never yet thought it too much."

Cael turned it over, looking for the catch, the way he would have looked for the real fist behind a feint. He could not find one. That told him more than anything she had said. A woman selling a dream would have rounded the numbers. She would have left out the man with the bees. She would not have said *every month* as if it were a weight she wanted him to feel before he picked it up.

"What about expelled practitioners?" said Lira.

She said it flat and careful, in the voice of somebody who has taught herself not to let a thing matter until she has heard the answer. Quenna did not need to open her book.

"Wind Path, Copper-tier, expelled from Fenmark Academy."

"Yes."

"There's a second provision. Re-certification. It's a different door, and it opens into the same rooms. It asks you to be assessed every month as well, and every month to show that you've gone further than the month before." Quenna paused. "It is not a kind road."

"I can do that."

"I thought you could," said Quenna.

There was something nearly warm in it, under the plainness, and it was gone almost before it arrived. Lira did not move. But Cael, beside her, felt something in her shoulder let go by a hair, like a rope eased a single turn on its cleat.

Brom shifted his weight from one foot to the other. Quenna's eyes went to him and rested there, for about as long as it takes to read a name off a list. Then they moved on. She said nothing to him at all. Brom's face did not change.

"I'm at the inn at the top of the market row," said Quenna. "Take three days. Come and tell me what you've decided, whichever way it goes." She looked at each of the three of them in turn and inclined her head to each. Then she turned and went back across the stone. Within a dozen steps she was one more coat going out at the side door, and then she was not even that.

Nobody said anything for a while. At the far end of the floor the sweepers had begun. Behind them, the eel-market men were scraping the last of the stew out of their pots.

"She wasn't surprised by a single one of your questions," said Brom.

"No," said Cael.

"She's done that before."

"Or she spent four nights getting ready for us."

"Both," said Brom, and went back to watching the doors.

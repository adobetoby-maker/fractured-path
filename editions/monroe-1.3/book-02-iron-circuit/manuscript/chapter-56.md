# Chapter 56 — I'm Going

The lamp was lit in the north window of the Ironyard at dusk, for the first time in a fortnight.

Cael saw it from the bottom of the market steps on his way back from the tannery lane. It was a small yellow square high up in the dark wall of the old foundry, and he stood in the cold and looked at it for some time before he went on down. All fortnight there had been no lamp there, because she had put it out on the third day so that every hour she had would be his. He had not known, until he saw it lit again, how much he had missed it. It had been there through the whole of the autumn. It had been the last thing he saw from his window at night and, often, the first thing in the morning. On the nights when the Log would not come right, or a bout would not leave his head, he had lain and watched that square of yellow across the roofs and known that somebody else on the hill was awake and working at a thing that would not come right either. It had not made the thing easier. It had made it less lonely, which was a different help, and in some ways a better one.

He did not go up. It was her hour. He went round instead to the alcove by the side passage, which was dark and cold and smelled of chalk and straw, and sat on the bench against the back wall with his arm in the scarf. Through the arch, across the empty main floor and up the north stair, he could hear her.

He could not see her. He did not need to. He had heard her at that lamp a hundred nights from his bed, through two streets and a wall, the sound of a staff end on stone and feet on boards, and he knew the shapes of the sounds the way he knew the shapes of the words in Hesk's hand. Tonight they were slow. There was a step, and a long pause, and a step. A turn. The staff end touching down once, lightly, like somebody tapping a table to see whether it is sound. Then the same again, from the other side.

She was not training. She was asking her feet questions, one at a time, and waiting for them to answer.

After a while the pauses got shorter. Then there were no pauses at all, only a long unbroken run of sound from the north stair: step and turn and step, the staff end, the heel, the step that did not stop. He sat on the bench and listened to her feet remember where they lived. Cael found that he was smiling in the dark like an idiot, alone in an alcove with his arm in a scarf.

Brom came in at the side passage when the hour was nearly over. He had a covered pot in one hand, held out in front of him as if it might go off, and a loaf under the other arm.

"The cookshop," he said. "I told him who it was for. He gave me twice as much and wouldn't take the second copper." He sat down on the other end of the bench, carefully, with the pot on his knees. He listened for a moment to the sound from the north stair. "She's found it."

"She never lost it."

"No," Brom agreed. "But she was afraid she had." He was quiet a moment. "That's not the same, and it's not nothing either."

The sound on the stair stopped. They heard the lamp go out, and her feet come down the north stair and across the main floor in the dark, quick and sure. Then she was in the arch with her staff on her shoulder and her face pink from the cold and the work, and she looked at the two of them sitting on the bench like a pair of owls, and at the pot on Brom's knees.

"Good," said Lira. "Upstairs. My table. Now. Before I lose my nerve."

---

Her table was the landing table, in her room at the boarding house, the one with a short leg that she had wedged with a folded bill from the paper stall. They sat round it with the pot in the middle and the lamp beside it and the door to the landing propped open, because three people and a lamp in Lira's room was too warm for anybody with their coat on. The heavyset man's wife came up the stair while they were spooning out the stew and put a fourth bowl on the landing without a word, as if a fourth bowl were simply what happened now. She went down again.

It was a small room and it held a great deal of Lira. Her staff leaned in the corner by the window with its grip half mended. Her single iron pan hung from a nail by the door, scoured bright, because she cooked for four on the house's fire and would not let anybody else touch the pan. Her measuring stick lay along the top of the press, notched in pencil from the winter, when she had measured every one of his bursts against it and written the numbers in his book in her own hand. Over the bed, on a string, hung the wrist cloths she had worn through, washed and dried and kept, for no reason she had ever been able to give him.

Cael could not hold a spoon in either hand. Lira held it for him, as she had the night before, without any comment at all this time, between her own mouthfuls.

When the pot was half empty Cael began. He had planned what he would say, walking back from the tannery lane: the offer as Quenna had laid it out, one piece at a time, the two tracks and the monthly assessments, the file, the three in eleven years. He had meant to set it all out on the table between them like a page of the Log, so that each of them could look at all of it before anybody said a word.

He got as far as the first piece.

"I'm going," said Lira.

He stopped.

She had put the spoon down. She was not looking at him, and she was not looking at Brom. She was looking at the lamp.

"I'm going," she said again, as if she wanted to hear how it sounded the second time. It sounded the same. "I knew it on the stone last night, before she'd finished saying *Fenmark*. It was there this morning when I woke, sitting on the end of my bed like a cat. I lit the lamp tonight to make sure I wasn't only saying it because I was frightened, or because you'd go anyway and I didn't want to be left on this hill on my own." She turned her head and looked at him at last. "I'm not. I went up there and asked my feet. They said yes."

"I know," said Cael.

"I know you know. You knew before I said it."

"I knew before you lit the lamp."

That made her laugh, a short surprised sound, and then she stopped laughing and was serious again. It was the face she wore at the south mark, before a bout.

"There's a thing I want to say about why," she said, "and I want to say it properly, so let me get to the end of it before either of you does anything with it." She put her hands flat on the table on either side of her bowl. "It's not the ceiling. Or it is, but not the way it was. You both know about the ceiling. A Wind on a Copper card with *expelled* on the bottom of her paper can't get a guild to sponsor her, and without a guild she can't get to an Arbiter, and without an Arbiter she can't move. I've walked to their gate four times this year and been turned round at it four times, and I've stopped walking. That's all true and it's all still there. But that isn't why."

Brom had stopped eating. Cael had not been eating anyway.

"It's Maud," said Lira.

She said the name plainly, the way Vell would have said it.

"I lost to her. Fifth exchange. You both saw it. And Vell wrote *provisional* and kept it there, because I hadn't fixed the middle yet, and she was right." She turned one hand over on the table and looked at the back of it, where the bars Maud's staff had left had faded to a faint yellow. "And afterward, for weeks, I kept thinking: nobody here is going to make me prove it again. Vell will book me when she thinks I'm ready. Dace will put me on a card. Half the hill will come and shout for me because they like me, and the four girls will cry whichever way it goes, and if I win, the line in the book will move up a rung, and that'll be that. Proved once. Kept forever." She looked up. "This hill is the kindest place I've ever been. It's kinder than my own mother's kitchen. And it's never once asked me the same question twice."

"Quenna will," said Cael.

"Quenna will. Every month. Somebody who doesn't know me and doesn't love me and doesn't care which girls are crying will stand at a chalk line and ask me whether I'm as good as I was last month, and better." She took a breath. "I want that. I didn't know I wanted it until she said *monthly* as if it were a stone she was handing me to carry. I thought: *yes, give it here.* I've been proving it to people who already believed me for a year. I want a stranger with a slate making me earn it again, every month, from nothing."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The lamp hissed. Down in the house, the heavyset man's chair creaked by the front window.

"That's the end of it," said Lira. "You can do things with it now."

"I'm not going to do anything with it," said Cael. "It's right."

"I know it's right. I wanted you to hear it before you started telling me about the re-certification track as if I hadn't been sitting on the stone when she said it." She picked up the spoon again, and filled it, and held it out to him. "Eat. You've gone white again."

---

Brom took a great deal longer.

He did not say *yes* and he did not say *no*. He ate the rest of his stew, slowly, all of it, and wiped the bowl with a piece of the loaf, and put the bowl down, and then he began to ask questions.

He asked them one at a time, in his flat careful voice, and he waited for each answer before he asked the next. What did Greyvane teach about Iron Skin, if anything? Was there anybody on the staff who read, or who had ever stood in front of somebody who read? How long did a standard student stay? Who paid, and how much, and was there work in the town for a man who had to earn it? Could a Copper with a clean line and no complaints against him simply walk to the gate and ask to be taken, the same as anybody else? What did such a man have to show, and to whom?

Cael answered the ones he could, which were few. Lira answered one or two more, from Fenmark. At Fenmark, she said, a standard student came in at fourteen or fifteen and stayed four years, if he was not sent home; the town round an academy always had work for anybody with a strong back and no pride, carrying and kitchens and the stables; and every academy she had ever heard of kept somebody on its staff whose whole business was reading. "They'd not have a reader like you," she said to Brom. "Nobody has a reader like you. But they'll have somebody who'll know what you are when you stand in front of him."

Brom nodded at that, and filed it, and went on to the next.

Then Brom asked the last one. Cael understood, from the way he asked it, that it was the only one he had really wanted to ask all along.

"Does the form ask for your family?"

Cael looked at him.

"Not the name," said Brom. "Anybody can have a name. Whether they want the house. Whether they want a letter from your father, or your father's friend, or the station that did your reading, saying who you are and what you're for." He had his big hands flat on the table, one on each side of the empty bowl, the way Lira had held hers. "Because if they do, I'll not go."

"You've got a real classification," said Cael slowly. "Iron Skin. Copper. From an Arbiter. You don't need any provision."

"I know I don't. That's what frightens me." Brom said it without any heat at all. "The provision's for people the paper can't hold. The paper holds me fine. It holds me in exactly the place my family's letters put me, with a man's note in the margin saying I was taught wrong from the start and ought to be read again at a better station." He turned his head toward the window, where the cold was coming in round the frame. "I got up from that table at fourteen because I wasn't going to be measured by what was written down about me in a room I'd been sent out of. I'll not walk into a new room and hand them the same paper and let them measure me by it all over again. Not even if the paper says something kind."

"So what do you want?"

"I want to go in on what my hands can do," said Brom. "On my line in Vell's book, and on my hands, and on whatever they put in front of me at their gate. The same as anybody. If they want Velmere, they can have it without me."

Lira was watching him with an expression Cael had seen on her face only once or twice before, at the south rope, when somebody she had bet on in her own head did something she had not seen coming.

"Ask her," she said to Cael. "Tomorrow. Ask her every one of those questions, the way he asked them."

"I'll write them down," said Cael. Then he looked at his hands and said, "You'll write them down."

---

Quenna's inn stood at the top of the market row, where the row turned into the road to the north gate: a tall narrow house with a bow window, which the carters used when they had money and the carters' inn when they did not. She was in the front room at a small table by the window, with a pot of tea and her small book open beside it. She did not seem surprised to see him come in with his arm in a scarf and a folded page held out in his left fingers.

"Sit down," she said. "Before you fall down. Everybody in this town seems to say that to you."

He sat. The front room of the inn was the kind of room a carter with money would think of as fine: a carpet worn through to the threads in a path from the door to the hearth, a clock on the mantel that ran a quarter of an hour fast and that everybody in the house allowed for, and a bow window full of the market row going about its morning, barrows and baskets and breath going up in the cold. Quenna had her back to the room and her face to the window, so that she could see the whole row without being seen from it, and Cael noticed that, and filed it, and then gave her the page. Lira had written Brom's questions out in her own fast slanting hand, every one, in the order he had asked them, and at the bottom, underlined, *DOES THE FORM ASK FOR HIS FAMILY*.

Quenna read the page through once, and then again, more slowly. Then she laid it flat on the table and put her cup on the corner of it, as if to keep it from blowing away.

"Brom," she said. "Iron Skin. Velmere."

"You know."

"I know his line. It's on the wall of your Circuit Master's building and in your keeper's book, for anybody who stands there long enough, and I have stood there six nights." She turned the cup a quarter-turn on the page. "Eight bouts on the main floor, seven won. The one he lost, his first. His win over you. A season of side floors before that, up the river, which I wrote to ask about. And his name in a stable book at Velmere, which I did not write to ask about, because I didn't need to."

"Then why didn't you speak to him? Last night?"

Quenna looked at him over the cup for a long moment. It was the first time he had seen her decide whether to say something, and he filed that.

"Because the doors I hold are for people who can't get through the ordinary one," she said at last. "He can. He is a Copper on a clean card with a better line than half the second-years I teach. If I had turned round on that floor and offered him a door, I would have been carrying him through it. I watched him for two nights before I ever watched you. I did not think he was a man who would thank me for being carried anywhere."

Cael said nothing. He was thinking that she was right, and that she had seen it in two nights from the back of a hall, and that it had taken him most of a season.

"Tell him this," said Quenna. "Every one of these questions has an answer, and I'll write them out for him if he wants them. But the last one is the only one that matters, so here it is. The standard enrollment asks for what a man can show. It asks for his record, attested by somebody who saw it, and it asks him to stand at the gate in front of two of the staff and show them what he is. That's all. It does not ask for his house. It does not ask for letters from his father. It does not ask for the station that read him or the man who wrote in his margin." She tapped the page with one finger, under the underlined line. "If any letter comes to Greyvane from Velmere, with or without him, it will go in a drawer. I will see that it does."

"He'll want to come on his own."

"Then he'll come on his own." She took her cup off the page and pushed the page back across the table to him. "Tell him to bring his keeper's word for his line. That's all he needs. The rest he can show us with his hands."

---

He found Brom in the alcove, on the bench, with his elbows on his knees, not doing anything. Cael had never once seen Brom not doing anything. Even sitting still, Brom was always doing something: reading a room, or a man, or the grain of a loaf of bread. Now he sat with his big hands hanging between his knees and looked at the stone between his boots like a man in a waiting room who has been told the doctor will be some time. Lira was sitting on the floor against the straw post with her staff across her lap, mending a split in the grip with a strip of leather and pretending not to wait.

Cael stood in the arch and told him. He told him plainly, every word of it, in the order Quenna had said it, the way Brom liked things told: the record and the keeper's word and the two of the staff at the gate, and no house, and no letters, and a drawer for anything that came from Velmere. He told it bare.

Brom listened to all of it without moving.

When it was finished he sat for a while longer, looking at the chalk cross on the floor where Lira had dropped Cael four hundred times. Cael could see him going back through it, slowly, from the front.

"Then I'll walk in at their gate like anybody else," said Brom. "On my hands."

He said it to the chalk cross. Then he lifted his head and looked at Cael, and Cael saw that whatever had been loose in his face all evening had been fastened down, like a hatch before weather.

"You're not done being interesting, and I haven't solved the problem yet."

"The problem being me."

"The problem being you."

Lira snorted into her mending.

Brom did not smile. He looked down at his own hands, and turned them over, and looked at the backs of them. When he spoke again it was more quietly, in the voice he had used at the market wall the first time he ever told Cael anything true.

"And there's the other thing," he said. "I'll say it once, and then I'll not say it again, because it's not the kind of thing I say." He turned his hands back. "I had a home once, with a long table in it and good people round it, and it was the one I was supposed to have, and I got up from it. And then I came down a river to a town that didn't know my name, and sat on a bench beside a boy everybody said wasn't possible, and a girl with a staff hit me in the legs at dawn for my own good. And somewhere in that I found I had another one. One I picked." He looked at the arch, where the light from the main floor came in. "It's not this building. It's not this hill. I'll not let it go because it's moving three days east."

Nobody said anything.

Lira put down her mending and got up off the floor. She went to the bench and stood in front of Brom, and then, with great seriousness, she reached out and pulled one of his ears, hard, the way the sister pulled the ear of the dog that slept under the stairs.

Brom went red from the collar to the hair.

"That's for making me cry in an alcove," said Lira, who was not crying, quite. She sat down beside him on the bench and leaned against his arm. "Twice in two days. I'll not have it."

Cael stood in the arch and looked at the two of them on the bench, the big one red to the ears and the small one leaning on him with her staff across her knees, and found that he did not need to write anything down at all.

# Chapter 44 — Standing Room

On the Tuesday nearly three weeks after Lira's bout with Maud, Cael did not go down to the Ironyard at all, because there was nobody on the evening card he needed to see and because Lira had asked him to hold a lamp.

She was at the kitchen table with her sleeves pushed up and her hands flat on the boards, under the light, and she wanted to look at them properly. The bars Maud had laid across the backs of them, waiting each time in the middle of Lira's catch, had gone from black to plum, and then to a green like pond water, and were now a yellow that only showed when the lamp came close. She turned them over and back, and made two fists and opened them, slowly, and watched the knuckles as if they belonged to somebody she was thinking of hiring.

"Closer," she said.

He brought the lamp closer.

"There." She touched the second knuckle of her left hand with one finger of her right. "That one still talks when I close it hard. The rest have stopped." She closed it hard, to be sure, and her mouth went thin, and she opened it again. "A week. Then it's quiet."

"You've been using them every night."

"I've been using them every night because that's where the middle lives, the half-beat where my catch turns into a throw. Maud said a year. I'd like it to be less than a year. So I'm not resting them, I'm teaching them, and they can complain while they learn like everybody else." She took her hands off the table and put them in her lap, out of the light. "Put that down before you burn the cloth. What are you doing in that book?"

He had the Log open at the back, at the two columns he had ruled on the night Bede beat him. The *Habit* column, for things he did and could train out at a price, had five lines in it now, and a cost against each. The *Shape* column, for the way the borrowed things had been built in him, had five lines and no costs, because shapes could not be bought out, and under each he had been trying for a fortnight to write how he would stand so that nobody was ever in the place it left open. Four of the five had something under them; the fifth did not.

*The right is only ever my feet,* it said, and under it there was nothing at all.

He turned the book round and showed her.

Lira read it with her chin on her fist. The heavyset man's wife came through with the kettle and looked over her shoulder at the page, as she did at anything written in the house, and went out again, satisfied that it was not a bill.

"Your feet are a beat slow going right," said Lira.

"I know they are."

"And you've put it under *Shape*." She tapped the column. "Your hip only goes left. That's how the Wind was made in you; it came out of my left hip and my barn master's before me, and you can't argue with it. Fine. But your feet aren't the Wind. Your feet are only feet." She looked up at him. "Nobody built them slow going right. You did, by never asking them. That's a habit."

He opened his mouth and found that he had nothing ready to put in it.

"I'm not saying I can fix it," Lira said. "I'm saying you've filed it in the wrong column, and you of all people ought to mind that." She stood, and took the lamp out of his hand, and set it on the table where it belonged. "I'm going down to the alcove. Don't wait up. And don't move it till you've had a better look at it."

He did not move it. He sat in the kitchen after she had gone with the book open in front of him, while the heavyset man read his carter's bill by the range and the sister's hen made its small complaints from the coal box. He looked at the line, and in the end he did a thing he had never done to a page in the Log before. He drew a small question mark in the margin beside it, in pencil, so that it could come out again.

Two streets away, on an ordinary Tuesday card, the Ironyard's door was taking money.

---

The man at the door wanted four coppers for standing room, and Reydan paid it.

He paid it in coppers, because he had learned on other trips that silver at a door like this one made the door remember you, and he had not come to be remembered yet. The doorkeeper was an old fighter with one ear thicker than the other, sitting on a stool with a tin box on his knee, and he looked at the coat first and the face second, as doorkeepers did everywhere. The coat was plain and good. The face, Reydan had been told by people who meant it kindly, gave very little away even when he wanted it to. The doorkeeper looked at both, dropped the coppers in the box and jerked his head at the room, and turned to the carter behind him.

Reydan went in and found a place to stand.

He had come down the river road that afternoon on the back of a wool wagon, three days from the city where he had lived since he was eleven, with one bag and the price of a room. He had found the room above a saddler's and left the bag in it. He had eaten something at a stall without noticing what it was, because his attention had already gone ahead of him to the building at the bottom of the hill, as it always did on these trips. The trips were the only time it ever did that any more.

The place he chose was at the end of the south rope, where the lamplight was good and the floor could be seen whole, and far enough back that nobody would have to step round him to see past. He folded his arms and settled his weight, and looked at the room before he looked at anything in it.

It was better than he had expected.

He had seen a good many rooms like it, in more cities than he liked to count. Most were yards or sheds or the backs of taverns, and they were run by whoever had the loudest voice, and they kept their records on a slate that got wiped when it rained. This was an old foundry, by the stone of the floor, and the stone was dense and black and swept. The ceiling was low and the lamps had been hung by somebody who had thought about where shadows fell. There was a long wall by the slate covered in chalk names in a careful hand, with small private marks beside them: rings, dots, short stitches. A narrow man with chalk on his cuffs stood in front of the wall with a pencil he never used, reading it.

And at a table by the rope, with a great book open in front of her and a second book under her elbow, sat a woman in her fifties with spectacles and grey hair pinned flat. When the first bout was called, she stood up to write it.

Reydan noticed that. He noticed that nobody else in the room seemed to find it strange. He noticed that when she said the words she said before every bout, the two fighters both answered her aloud, and that she waited for the answer, and that the room went quiet while she waited. He did not catch every word. He caught *the rule in this room*, and he caught the quiet, and he put both away to look at later.

On the east bench, among the carters and the stall-keepers and the girls who had come to shout, sat a young man in a coat Reydan knew. It was a river academy's coat, two or three years out of the house by the wear at the cuffs, and the young man sat in it with his hands flat on his knees and watched the empty stone between bouts as if something were still happening on it, and nobody sat near him. Reydan looked at him for a moment with something like fellow feeling. There was one in every room, he had found, who sat like that and watched the floor when nothing was on it. Usually it was him.

The first bout was two Coppers. One was a Stone, a solid man of thirty or so with a grinder's patience, who set himself in the middle and let the other come. The other was a Blade, a woman, quicker and younger, with a losing look about her that Reydan recognized. It was the look of a fighter who had lost three in a row and come back to stand on the same stone anyway, because the stone was where you found out.

He did not watch the strikes. He had stopped watching strikes at about fourteen, when a master at the academy had stood behind him at a sparring floor and put two fingers on the back of his neck and turned his head, gently, from the blades to the feet. *The blade tells you what happened,* she had said. *The feet tell you what's about to. And between the exchanges, when nobody's looking, the feet tell you what the fighter's afraid of.*

So he watched the Blade's feet between the exchanges.

In the first rest she stood square, flat, and shook out her sword hand. In the second she stood with her weight on her back foot and her front heel just off the stone. She had been hit on the forward step in the second exchange, a solid knock on the hip from the Stone's forearm, and her body had decided, without asking her, never to be caught going forward again. It would cost her the bout if she let it. The Stone would see it in a while, if he was any good.

The Stone did not see it. In the fourth exchange the Blade found a gap that came of the Stone's own slowness, not of anything Reydan had seen in her feet, and put the flat of her sword against his ribs. The narrow man at the wall made a mark. The woman at the table wrote standing. The room made the noise rooms made for a tidy finish, and Reydan did not make it with them. He noted the gap, and noted that the woman had not known about her own back foot, and that she had won anyway. That was worth knowing too, about a room: a fighter could win here while still carrying a fault she had not found.

---

The second bout went wrong, and Reydan watched it go wrong with the same attention he had given the first.

It was a Force against a quick lad of nineteen or twenty whose Path Reydan could not name from the rope, something light and fast that wanted distance. The lad was good. He kept the Force at the end of his reach for two exchanges, darting in and away. The room liked him; there were girls on the second bench who shouted his name. In the third he came in once too often on the same line, half a step deeper than he had come before. The Force had been building all that time, slow and deep, and had been waiting for that half-step. He put the whole of what he had built into the lad's chest.

The lad went off the floor backward and landed on the stone on his shoulder, hard, with a sound Reydan felt in his own teeth.

Several people near the front looked away. One of the girls on the second bench put her hands over her mouth. Reydan did not look away. He watched the lad lie there and try to breathe. He watched the Force stand back at once with his hands open, as the woman at the table's rule presumably asked, and he watched the narrow man come off the wall at a walk, not a run, and crouch by the lad and say something, and wait. After a long moment the lad got up, holding his arm against his body, and walked to the rope on his own feet, and the room let its breath out.

The fall had told Reydan more than the clean finish.

It had told him that the lad's half-step deeper on the third entry had not been a decision. His body had grown confident, and his body had gone a little further than his head had agreed to. The lad would not do it again for a month. And then, Reydan thought, he would do it again, the first night he felt safe, and somebody would be waiting for it. People on the bench thought a loss like that was a thing that happened to you. He had never thought so. A loss like that was a fact about you that somebody else had found first, and a fall was only the price of being told.

He had learned that young, and expensively, in a room much grander than this one.

His father's house had a training yard, because his father was a guild officer of the middling sort who believed in such things, and at ten Reydan had been put in it with a hired man who was paid to make him better. At eleven, on the strength of what the yard had made of him, the academy took him; at fourteen he Kindled with a Pressure Path that the Arbiter wrote down as burst-compression and the examiners called promising, and nobody at the academy was surprised. He had been there, in one way or another, ever since. He had risen through every examination his tier asked of him, cleanly and on time. He had sparred every morning of his life against people who were paid to find what was wrong with him and say so, and he had been told what was wrong with him so many times, and so precisely, that at twenty-two there was very little left that anybody could find.

That was the trouble. He did not think of it as a boast. He thought of it the way a man might think of a well that has gone dry: nobody's fault, and no less dry for that.

The guild bouts had stopped meaning anything around his nineteenth year. It had not happened all at once. Opponents had begun to be ill on the days they were drawn against him. Trainers had begun to speak to their prospects quietly before a draw went up. Then, three autumns running, the two academies in his city had redrawn their invitational so that he and the other house's best boy sat in brackets that could not meet before the final. On the third autumn the other house withdrew its boy before the final, with a letter about an injured wrist. Everybody knew the wrist was well. Nobody said so.

Reydan had not been angry about it. He had tried to be, because it seemed the right thing to feel, and found that what he felt was mostly hunger.

So he had started taking these trips. He would hear of somebody, as you heard of anybody: a name passed in a guild hall, a story told at a supper, a champion in some small town who had beaten everyone for a year. He would go, and pay at the door, and stand at the back, and watch. Mostly the stories had been smaller than their telling. There had been a salt-town Copper with heavy hands and a good heart, who had beaten everyone within fifty miles because there was nobody within fifty miles to show him where his ceiling was. There had been a Blade in a river port who was quick and clever, and had never once in her life been made to wait. He had watched each of them for one bout and known by the second exchange, and gone home the next morning without speaking to anybody. He did not resent the trips. A trip that came to nothing was cheaper than a story ignored that turned out true. But he had begun to think, lately, that he would stop taking them, and that thought had frightened him more than any opponent had for years.

---

The story about the boy had reached him in a guild hall in the spring, and it had been absurd.

A boy with no Path, it said, a boy the Compact's own registry had looked at and written down as nothing, had put a Bronze Rank 1 on the ground in a yard in a district at the bottom of a hill somewhere down the river. The boy had done it, the story said, with his bare hands and nothing else. It had been told by a man who had heard it from a man who had been on the river, and by the time it reached Reydan it had grown a crowd of a thousand, and a sword, and a broken arm that nobody could afterward account for.

He had distrusted every word of it on principle. Stories about circuit fighters grew in the telling, like fish, and an impossible one grew fastest of all. But he had learned on the trips that the principle was not enough. Every so often an impossible story was only a true story told badly. The only way to know which was to go and look, and he had been looking for three years, and he was very tired of the salt-town Coppers.

So this time he had done a thing he had never done before a trip. He had gone to the keeper of the unsanctioned book in his own city, an old man who sold lamp oil by day and kept the circuit's records by night, and asked him to write down the river for one line. The old man had looked at him strangely. He had written all the same, to a keeper he had never written to in his life, and had sent it with the courier. A short letter had come back eleven days later in a narrow, careful hand, with the whole of the line copied out at the foot of it.

*Iron-equivalent. Cael. No Path designation.*

Reydan had read it standing in the lamp-oil shop with the smell of the oil all round him. The story had said *no Path*, and the story had been told by fools. But a keeper's line was not a story. A keeper did not write *Iron-equivalent* in a book that other keepers read because a crowd had shouted. And *No Path designation* in a keeper's careful hand was not a boast or a legend. It was simply what was there, set down by somebody whose whole life was being right about such things. He had read it twice, and then gone home and packed one bag.

Now he stood at the end of the south rope in the room where the line had been written, and watched the woman at the table close her book on the second bout and stand the pen in its pot. He had come here to find out whether the story was only told badly, and he had seen two bouts and not the boy. But he had seen the room, and the room was the first half of the answer. It had a keeper who stood to write, and a rule that both fighters said aloud, and a narrow man who walked rather than ran to a fallen boy because running would have frightened the crowd. It was not a small pond. Whatever the boy was, he had been measured here by people who knew how to measure.

The crowd began to move between bouts, toward the slate and the stalls by the side door. Reydan unfolded his arms and went the other way, along the rope, to the wall.

The narrow man saw him coming. Reydan watched him do it: the eyes going to his feet first, and then his shoulders, and then his hands, and only then his face, reading him in the order the academy masters had read their pupils on the first morning. The man did not stop reading when Reydan stopped in front of him. He held the unused pencil against his chest and waited to be told which kind of problem this was.

"You keep the card," said Reydan.

"I'm Dace. I keep the card." The man looked at the coat. "You're not from the hill."

"No. From up the river." Reydan let a moment pass, because he had found it was kinder to say a thing once and plainly than to work up to it.

"I want to fight the unranked one."

Dace looked at him.

"He's Iron-equivalent now," said Dace. "He's not unranked any more. He's got a line in the main-floor book, in the keeper's hand. He earned it on this stone."

"I know that. I want to fight him anyway." Reydan nodded at the table, where the keeper was talking to a man with a pie in his hand. "Somebody in this room sent that line up the river to the keeper in my city. I read it in a lamp-oil shop the week before last. That's why I'm here and not somewhere else."

Something in Dace's face moved a very little. Reydan saw him put the courier and his own letter together, and find that he did not entirely like the sum.

"You've not seen him fight," said Dace.

"No. I've seen your room." Reydan looked round it once, at the lamps and the stone and the girls on the second bench, who were still talking about the lad's shoulder. "I watched two bouts. Your Stone doesn't know the Blade had stopped going forward. Your quick lad will go a half-step too deep again in about a month. And your keeper stands up to write." He looked back at Dace. "That's enough to tell me the line is worth something. It isn't enough to tell me about him. That's what I came for."

"What's your Path? And your tier?"

"Pressure. Burst, not the long kind. Iron-tier, rank eight." Reydan said it as he would have said his street. "Twenty-two, if your keeper wants it for her book. I trained at an academy. I'm not here for a purse. I'll take whatever the floor pays and put it back in your box if you'd rather."

Dace did not answer that. He looked at the wall instead, at the chalk names and the rings and the stitches, and Reydan watched him read it as he must have read it a thousand times, weighing what a thing would cost before he wrote it down.

"Two weeks," said Dace. "Main floor."

"Thank you."

"I'll not book him anything before it. He'll have a fortnight with nothing on his card but you." Dace turned the pencil over in his fingers. "Most men in your place would ask me next where he trains in the mornings."

Reydan understood that he was being weighed, and did not mind it. He had come three days down the river to look at the boy, and the man who could tell him where to look was standing in front of him with a pencil.

"I'm not going to ask," he said. "He's never seen me. I've never seen him. Let it stay that way until the floor." He thought about how to say the rest of it, and said it. "Tell him I'm not here to embarrass him, or anyone. I didn't come to prove a story wrong. I came because it was the first one in three years that was worth the road."

He let that stand a moment, and then gave Dace the rest.

"I'd like it to stay that kind of fight."

---

He went out past the doorkeeper into the cold, and up the hill through the district, by streets he did not know, toward the saddler's and the room.

It was a poor district; that was plain from the doors and the windows, and from the way the people still out at that hour walked in the middle of the street. But it was not a frightened one. A pie stall was shutting at the corner of a square with a pump in it. The woman packing it up said goodnight to two carters by name. A boy went past with a can of milk and a dog and did not look at Reydan's coat at all. When he looked back from the top of the hill, the foundry roof was dark below him except for one high window at the north end, where a lamp was burning, small and steady, though it was late for anybody to be up.

He thought about the room as he walked, and the keeper's careful hand, and the line.

He had no idea what the boy was, and he knew it. The story said no Path; the line said no Path designation; and those were two different things, and he did not know which one was true, or whether either was. A fighter with no Path would be a curiosity, a boy who was very good with his hands and his eyes. He would be beaten in two exchanges by any trained Iron with a Path to spend, however good his eyes were. A fighter with a Path the registry could not name would be something else again. Reydan had no words for what. He found that he did not want to guess, and that the not-wanting surprised him. He had spent his whole life being told what things were before he met them. It was, he thought, a long time since he had walked toward a fight knowing nothing at all.

He could judge the room, and he had. He could judge the line, and he had judged it worth three days on a wool wagon. The boy he could not judge yet, and would not, until the boy stood at the other mark under those lamps and did whatever it was he did.

At the saddler's he climbed the stair, and lay on the narrow bed in his coat, and looked at the ceiling. He realized after a while that he was smiling, and that he had not done so on any of the trips before, not once, not even on the way there.

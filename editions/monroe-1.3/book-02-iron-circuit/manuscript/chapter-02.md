# Chapter 2 — The Ladder

By the middle of the next morning the thigh had gone from an ache to a colour, and the colour was purple going on green. He limped down the market steps on it with the particular dignity of someone who has decided not to limp and is limping anyway.

The fruit woman saw him from the bottom step. She sat where she always sat, behind her baskets at the foot of the stairs, with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes on the far end of the row. She did not look at him as he came down, which was how he knew she had seen. In a year he had never once heard her name. She gave short weight to anyone who called her *auntie* and full weight to everyone else. She had told him once, in a cold week, that a person who wanted her name could earn it by buying apricots for forty years.

"Pears," said Cael, and pointed, because pointing was what she liked.

She weighed them. She put her thumb on nothing, which was her way of being friendly.

"Which one is it this week?"

"Left leg."

"Last week it was the right arm." She tipped the pears into his hand. "One of these mornings you'll come down those steps on two good legs and I won't know who you are."

"I'll try."

"Don't try too hard." She had already looked away, back up the row, where a woman was haggling with the bearded fish cousin over something that had clearly been a good fish yesterday. "A boy that never limps isn't learning anything."

He went up the row eating a pear and thinking about that. It was the kind of thing people in the district said to him more and more, as if word had gone round that the Hesk-ward boy collected sayings and would write them down, which was true. Hesk had said much the same in his own way in the workshop at home, with a cracked housing in his hands: *a thing that's never failed has never been loaded*. Lira said it every morning in the alcove with her staff, in one word, which was *again*. Here was the fruit woman saying it over a basket of pears. He had begun to suspect the whole of Valdris was saying it, in a thousand different accents, and that most people simply did not stop to hear it.

At the top of the row Red Cap was selling chalk.

He was selling it out of a cloth bag at a copper a stick. He sold it to the fighters who drew their own plans on the Ironyard's floor before a bout, and to the bookmakers, and to anybody else who looked as if they might want to write on something. Where the chalk came from was a question Cael had decided not to ask. Red Cap saw him and held up a stick and said "For you, two," which was a joke, and Cael bought one for one, which was the answer to it, and Red Cap grinned with the gap in his teeth and said that the whole of the east lanes had the fifth exchange off by heart already, and half of them had it wrong.

"Which half?"

"The half that weren't there." Red Cap tucked the copper into his cheek, a habit Lira had been trying to break him of for months. "They're saying you went sideways a whole yard. I told them half a yard. Was it half a yard?"

"Less."

"I'll tell them less," said Red Cap, satisfied, and went off up the lane shouting *chalk*.

Cael turned the stick over in his fingers as he walked, and turned something else over with it. It was a question he had carried for months without spending, the way he carried most of his questions now. He had learned that a question asked before he had done his own looking bought a worse answer than the same question asked after. The person answering could tell how much work you had done and pitched the answer to match.

He had done the looking. He could have drawn the ladder from memory on a slate. A newcomer went into Vell's book as *unrated*, with nothing after it, though they came with a cart full of honours. After two bouts under her eye they became *Assessed*, and she put a metal beside the word, and never after one bout, in all the year he had watched. Above that came the rungs proper, and the rungs wore the Compact's own names with a tail tied on, *Copper-equivalent* and *Iron-equivalent* and *Bronze-equivalent*, and stopped there, because anyone who could climb past Bronze-equivalent was being paid better by somebody with a roof that did not leak.

The joint he could not see was the one between the two systems. Her rungs said *Copper* and *Iron* as though those words were hers to use, and the whole building treated them as though they were. Somewhere there had to be a place where her word was fastened to the Compact's, or it was all only a woman with a book, saying things.

---

He asked her in the slack hour after noon. The main floor was empty but for the sweeper, the lamps were out, and the light came down grey and level from the high windows along the north wall.

"Where does your *Copper* get fastened to theirs?"

Vell was at her table with the day's book open and nothing in it yet, drinking tea from the chipped cup she had carried down from the Cinder House. She considered him over its rim for long enough that he began to wonder whether he had asked it wrong.

"Most people ask me why they're not higher," she said. "That one's been in your pocket a good while. I can tell by the shine on it." She closed the book, and stood, and took the key on its string from round her neck. "Come and see."

The back room was behind a plain door in the wall behind her table. He had seen it shut a hundred times and open never. Inside, it was narrow and high, with one barred window up near the ceiling, a desk under it, and a wheeled ladder on a rail that ran the length of the shelves. The shelves went all the way up. The books on them were tied, every one, with a length of cotton tape in a bow, and the bows had been tied by the same hands so many times that they all sat at the same angle, like birds on a wire.

But it was not the books she went to. It was a board on the wall beside the door, a plain pine board with a column of names painted on it in white, and a date beside each, and some of the dates struck through and painted again.

"My fixed points," said Vell. "Everybody in this district, and some outside it, who holds a formal tier from a real Arbiter at a real station, and has fought under my eye, so that I know what that tier looks like on them. Copper formal. Iron formal. Two Bronze. They're the nails in the wall. Every rung in my book hangs off one of those nails by a string, and I can show you the string."

She took down this year's book, the one she had written his line in the night before, and found it without searching, and put her finger on Ulric's name.

"Him. Copper, from a guild station, sealed and entered. He's on the board." She moved her finger to the line above it. "You beat him. That's one link. Last night you hung one string's length from a Copper seal the Compact put on him itself." She looked up. "That's how my word gets fastened to theirs. I don't ask their Arbiter what you are. I ask it what *he* is, and then I watch you fight him."

"And if I'd beaten someone who'd beaten him?"

"Two links. Further from the nail. More give in it." She turned back three pages and showed him a line where somebody's rating had a small number written in the margin beside it, a *3*, in pencil. "That's how far out she hangs. Three strings. I'll give a rung on three, if the fights are clean, but I watch it, and when she fights somebody nearer a nail, I see whether she holds."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then the string was bad somewhere. A link I thought was sound wasn't." Vell turned further back, through a year and then another, until she came to a page with a short red line ruled under a name, and a date beside it, and two letters, *t.f.*, in her square hand. Below it, every line that hung from that name had the same: the red rule, the date, the two letters. There were nine of them. "When a link rots, I go back to it and come down the chain, and every line that hangs off it gets corrected. Every one. And then I find each of them and tell them, standing in front of them, what I've done and why." She tapped the letters. "*Told, face.* I don't change a figure in a book they'll never read and hope they won't notice."

He looked down the column of red rules and small letters. He tried to imagine nine people, one after another, being told by a grey-haired woman with a cup of tea that they had been Iron-equivalent for a year by mistake.

"How often?"

"About a dozen times. Thirty years." She closed the volume and tied its tape, and the bow sat at the same angle as all the others. "It's the worst part of the work. It's also the reason anybody believes the rest of it. A book that won't say it was wrong is a book you can buy."

He did the sum standing there, because he could not stop himself. Thirty years of books; a page an evening in season; somewhere in the tens of thousands of results, and a dozen corrections, every one of them said aloud to the face it cost. Hesk would have signed his name to an instrument that kept that kind of error. And the *t.f.* was the clever part, the part that made it hold. A record that admitted its mistakes to the people they hurt left nobody anything to whisper about.

---

He did not have to ask about the rule. She told him, while she put the book back, as though it had been next on a list.

"You know what we say before a bout. Nobody dies in the circle." She slid the volume into its gap with her palm flat on the spine. "People think that's me being kind. It isn't. Or it isn't only. The day somebody dies on Dace's floor, the Compact gets something it's never had from us, which is a reason it can write down. A body is a reason. And once they've a reason, they'll come in with seals and take the ledger for evidence, and that's this room gone. The circuit will start again somewhere, under a new name, with a new keeper and a new book that knows nobody." She turned. "So I stop bouts. Three, in thirty years. Three times I've walked into the circle and put my hand up and said *done*, and three times the person I did it for walked out of the building on their own feet."

"How did you know when?"

She took a while over that, and he waited for her.

"You watch where they mean to fall," she said at last. "A fighter who's losing still has a plan for the floor. You can see it. When the blow comes they're already choosing which shoulder, which hand, how to come up. They're bargaining. And then there's the other one, who's stopped bargaining. Their body isn't arranging anything any more. It's only standing there, quite still, waiting for something it already knows about, the way a dog waits at a door." She shook her head. "It's quiet. That's the thing nobody tells you. The one about to be hurt is the quiet one."

He thought of the yard in the spring, and of her standing over a man in the raked groove, counting aloud at the pace she had counted at all her life, and three hundred people going still to hear her.

"Darrow was quiet," he said. "On the dirt. You counted him out."

"Darrow was empty." She did not seem surprised that he had asked; she seemed, if anything, to have been waiting for it. "That's a third kind, and you don't see it often. He wasn't bargaining and he wasn't waiting. He'd asked his body for the thing it had never once refused him, and it hadn't got it to give, and he knew that before I did. He lay there looking at the sky like a man who's found his well dry and is working out which way to walk for water." She turned her cup on the desk. "I counted because the rule says I count. If I'd thought he was hurt, I'd have stopped it on three and nobody in that yard would have argued. I knew by two that he wasn't. I've never seen a man so tidy about being beaten." She looked up at him. "It was you I was watching, after. You were the one with the face like a bridge had gone."

He did not know what to say to that, and she did not seem to need him to say anything. She drank the last of her tea, which must have been cold for a quarter of an hour, and made a face at it.

He kept that word for word. That evening he wrote it on a page near the back of the observation book that had no heading yet, a page for things that lived underneath technique and underneath Path, in the plain animal of the body. There were three things on it already: the hum of a planted foot in the ground, a held breath before a feint, and the way his own neck nodded before he struck. Now there was a fourth, and it had come out of thirty years of watching he could never have done himself, handed to him across a desk as if it were nothing.

There was one more thing he wanted, and he was not sure he had the right to it, and he asked it anyway, because she had brought him in here and shut the door.

"Ulric's guild has him on paper as six years trained, Copper formal, a credit to his station. Your book has him on its back on your floor with his hand up. If somebody from his guild came in here and said your book was wrong about him, that their paper was the real thing and yours was a story, what would you say?"

Vell looked at him for a long time without any weighing in it.

"The Compact's Registry is a lie about who people are," said Vell. "My ledger is the truth. These fights happened. These results are real. Nobody gets to say otherwise."

She said it the way she said *begin*, flatly, setting it down where it went.

Then, because he was still looking at her, she went on in an ordinary voice. "Not a lie about what a sigil said to them at fourteen. That's true enough, as far as it goes. A lie about *who they are*. It says a word and stops, as if the word were the person. My book doesn't stop. It goes on writing down what they did, every month, for as long as they come through that door." She opened the back-room door and held it for him. "You'd know a bit about a word that stops."

He did. He went out past her into the grey light of the main floor without saying so, because there was nothing to add that was not already in her face.

---

Dace caught him by the elbow before he had gone ten steps and steered him toward the big slate by the door.

"She's had you an hour," said Dace. "Now you can see the part that actually keeps the roof up."

Dace was a narrow man of fifty or so with chalk dust in the creases of both cuffs and a pencil behind his ear that Cael had never once seen him write with. He did everything quickly and said what he meant. If you wanted him in the Ironyard, you looked in front of the slate wall by the big door. He could usually be found there with his head on one side, frowning at it as if it owed him money.

The wall was black slate from the floor to above a tall man's reach. It was covered from end to end in chalk: names in white, cards in columns, and over and among the names a scatter of small marks that Cael had seen every day for months without ever knowing what they meant. There were little rings. There were dots. There were short crosses between pairs of names, like stitches.

"Tell me what you think they are," said Dace, "and I'll tell you when you're cold."

Cael looked. He had a year of the room in his head to look with.

"The rings are people who are hurt," he said slowly. "There's one beside the Stone woman from the tannery lanes, and she's been wearing a wrap under her sleeve all week."

"Warm."

"Hurt and not telling anybody. Healing. You put the ring there so you won't pair them with anybody who'd find it." He went along the wall. "The crosses are pairs you won't make. Those two are brothers. That one owes that one money." He frowned at a cross between two names he did not know. "That one I don't know."

"Their mother," said Dace. "She comes to every bout either of them fights, and if they ever fight each other she'll have to pick which of her sons to stand behind, and I won't do that to a woman who brings me apples." He tapped a dot. "And these?"

The dots were the hardest. They sat beside names in no order he could find, sometimes one and sometimes two. Cael watched Dace's hand hover near them as he talked, and saw that the hand never rubbed one out without writing a name somewhere better.

"Debts," he said. "Somebody took a bad slot when you needed it filled. You owe them a good one."

"Hot." Dace looked pleased, in the narrow way he looked pleased about anything, by moving one eyebrow. "That's the whole book. Not who can beat whom. Vell does that. Mine is who can stand to be in a room with whom, and who's hurt, and who's owed. None of it's written anywhere else."

"It's written there."

"It's written in chalk." Dace drew his thumb through a dot, lightly, and it smeared. "Chalk wipes. That's what I like about it."

A woman came to the wall while they stood there, a solid Copper-equivalent with a good record and a better opinion of it, and asked for a pairing on the next big card against one of the brothers. Cael looked at the board before Dace could speak. There was no cross between her name and the brother's, and no ring beside either. But there were two dots beside the other brother, and none beside her.

*He'll give the slot to the brother he owes,* Cael thought, *and give her the next card, and she'll be angry.*

Dace gave the slot to the brother he owed, and promised her the next card, and she was angry, and went away to tell somebody about it.

"You had that before I opened my mouth," said Dace.

"I had a guess."

"Get two more and call it a habit." He took the pencil from behind his ear and turned it over and put it back. "Vell's told me about your rule of three. I'll give you one of mine for nothing, since you're here. This board answers to the people on those benches, and to nobody else. Not a guild, not a district office, not a man in a grey coat with a pin. If the Compact wants to know who fights in Ardenmere, it can pay at the door and sit on a bench and find out the slow way, like everybody else has to."

He said it lightly, but Cael had seen the corner of Dace's desk through the door of the little room where the door money was counted. On it, under a lump of slag from the old foundry that served as a paperweight, there was a letter on thick cream paper with a printed head. It was the third such letter Cael had seen there in a year. He had never seen one answered. He had seen one used, on a wet evening in the spring, to light the lamp over the north benches when the spills ran out.

"They write to you," he said.

"Every season. *A registered venue. Licensed bouts. A modest annual fee, in return for the protection of the Guild's good name.*" Dace said it in a dry high voice, like a clerk reading out a will to people who were not in it. "I've got the Guild's good name. It's under a lump of slag on my desk. It's very useful when the spills run out." He turned back to the slate and squinted at a dot. "The day I take their licence is the day they get a copy of this wall. Not once. Every week, by law, with the rings on. And then a woman who's six weeks out of a cracked wrist is a line in a clerk's book in the Ranked core, and the clerk doesn't care whether she gets put in front of a hard fighter or not." The dot came off under his thumb and went back on, smaller. "So they don't get the wall."

---

Stedd lost that afternoon, in the second bout of a thin card, and Cael learned more from the way he did it than from anything Vell had said all day.

He was a Copper washout of forty or so, soft in the middle and slow on his feet, with a bald patch he made no effort to cover and a laugh like a barrow on cobbles. He had not won in Vell's book since before Cael first walked down the hill. He fought a young Force practitioner who was too quick for him from the first exchange. He went down in the third with his breath knocked out of him, and lay on the stone a moment looking at the beams. Then he got up, slowly, the old way, a hand and a knee and the back last. He shook the young one's hand. And then, without dusting himself off, he walked straight to Vell's table and stood in front of it.

"Fair," said Stedd.

That was all. Vell nodded and wrote, and Stedd went to the water barrel.

And on his way the room nodded to him. Cael watched it happen and could hardly believe it. Fighters on the near benches lifted their chins as he passed. One of Dace's sweepers leaned on her broom to say *well fought*. A Fenrow bookmaker who was famous for acknowledging nobody took one finger off his slate. A man who had just lost in the second bout of a Thursday card crossed the Ironyard like a man coming home with a good harvest.

"He's not won since before I came," said Cael to Vell, when he could.

"He's never once told me I was wrong." Vell blotted Stedd's line. "Eleven years of bouts. Win or lose, he walks to this table first, before he's even got his breath, and tells me it was fair. Some of those weren't, as it happens. I've called a few against him I'd call different now." She put the blotter down. "That's what's worth anything in here. You can't win it in a night, and nobody can give it you. You turn up when you said, you fight clean, and you take what I write without making it anybody else's trouble. Keep doing that for eleven years and people will step aside for you at the water barrel. It's the only kind of rich this building recognises."

He watched for it afterwards, and it was everywhere. The benches nearest Vell's table belonged to the long clean records, whatever their rung, and nobody had ever made it a rule or ever broken it. The bookmakers had the northwest corner and kept to it, and their noise was allowed right up to *begin* and not one breath past it.

And then, near the end of the afternoon, two strangers came in through the big door together, and he saw the thing he would write down first.

They were both fighters; he could tell by the way they stood. One wore the stitched shoulders of an academy training coat and boots with thin soles cut for sprung wooden floors. The other wore a patched jacket and the kind of boots you could walk to Fenrow in. They stopped inside the door side by side, and each of them looked, first, at one thing.

The guild boy looked at the slate, at the names, at how big the chalk was and whose it was.

The circuit girl looked at Vell's table, to see whether Vell was at it, and what she was writing.

It took less than a breath. Neither of them knew they had done it. And there it was, the whole difference between the two worlds he lived between, in one turn of two heads: one of them walking in to see who could be beaten, and the other walking in to see who was keeping the account.

He wrote it all down that evening at the crate desk, in the grey book and not the slim one, because none of it was about him. The fixed points on the pine board, and the strings that hung from them, and the small pencilled number for how far out a rung hung. The red rules and *t.f.* The rule, and the three stopped bouts, and the quiet body. Dace's rings and dots and stitches, kept in chalk because chalk wipes. Stedd's *fair*. The two heads turning at the door.

Then, under it all, he wrote something that was not a fact, and boxed it, so that he would know later that it was only what he thought.

*Nobody made them build it honest. People the registry wouldn't let in built this out of nothing but a book and a slate, and they could have built it to sell. Every day somebody in that building chooses not to, and that's all that holds it up. The record is only as straight as the hand that keeps it.*

He looked at it for a while, and then put a small star beside the box, the first star in the new book, and sat with the lamp until the oil began to smell.

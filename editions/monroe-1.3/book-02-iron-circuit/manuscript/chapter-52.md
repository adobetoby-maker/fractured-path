# Chapter 52 — Six Hundred

Dace was at the main door with his little book open in one hand and the other hand flat on a man's chest.

"No," he was saying. "You were told Thursday. I wrote you down Thursday, and then I drew a line through you, because you told me you were bringing your wife's brothers. Your wife's brothers are four of the biggest men on this hill." He did not raise his voice. "Stand in the street. The doors are open. You'll see the floor from the step if you stand on your toes, and if you can't see it you'll hear it. And if you push past me you'll not come in this building again while I'm alive."

The man looked at the hand on his chest, and then at Dace, and went back out into the street with his wife's brothers behind him, grumbling, to find a place on the step.

Cael came up the last of the stairs from the side passage with his coat over his arm. Dace saw him without turning his head.

"Five hundred and four," said Dace, to the book. "Inside. On the stone, on the benches, on the ladders and the beams, and two on the slate shelf that I'll have down before the first exchange if I have to pull them down by the ankles." He made a mark. "And the street, which I can't count, because it keeps moving. Call it a hundred. Call it more. The doorkeeper says it goes back to the pump." He shut the book on his pencil. "Six hundred people, near enough, and not one of them paid what it's worth, and I'll be sweeping them out of my corners till Thursday." He looked at Cael at last. "Go on. You're the only one I don't have to count."

The building was hot. That was the first thing.

The night before, with four hundred in it, the old foundry had been warm the way a kitchen is warm. Tonight the air under the low ceiling was thick and close, and it smelled of lamp oil and wet wool and too many people breathing the same breath. Dace had found more lamps again, from somewhere. They hung from every beam and every nail, so close together now that the ceiling was one sheet of small yellow flames, and their heat came down on the crowd while the crowd's heat went up to meet it. At Cael's back, through the great open doors, the cold of the street came in long draughts that touched his neck and were gone.

He went along the wall toward the north end, and the room saw him going.

It was not a cheer. Six hundred people cannot cheer for a boy walking along a wall; they did not know yet what they would be cheering for. It was more like a change in the weather of the noise. Heads turned down the room ahead of him in a long ripple, and voices dropped where he passed and rose again behind him with his name in them. He heard *that's him*, and *he's younger than I thought*, and *the one with no Path*, and once, from somebody on the beams overhead, *small, isn't he*, which made the people round the speaker laugh. He did not look up.

The eel-market men had their two pots going in the corner by the slate, with every bowl out and in use. The betting man had given up his trestle board entirely and was standing on it instead, calling the two columns over the crowd in a voice that had gone hoarse an hour ago. The mending-stall pennants hung over the side door, a dozen red triangles with a white rope stitched on each, and somebody had hung another dozen along the north rail. The four girls from the wall had a place on the second bench, close together, with their knees drawn up, and the dock partner sat behind them like a cliff behind a beach.

Vell stood at her table on the west side of the floor.

She was not sitting. Her stool was pushed under the table, out of reach, and she stood with the red-taped main-floor book open along her left forearm and the pen held upright in her right hand, like a woman who has been given a candle to keep alight through a long service and means to keep it alight. She did not look at him. He had not expected her to.

Brom was at the north rope, where he had said he would be, with the crowd giving him a little room on both sides the way a river gives room to a rock. He did not wave. He met Cael's eyes once over the rope, and nodded, a small nod, and went back to watching the south end of the floor.

Lira was at the south rope.

He found her across the whole length of the stone, behind the place where the other man would stand. She had left her staff at home for once, and her hands rested on the rope. She was looking at his feet. She looked up when she felt him find her, and her mouth did something very small at one corner, and then she looked at his feet again.

At the back of the room, by the side door, where the light from the lamps came down thinnest, the woman in the grey coat had her book open.

He laid his coat on the north rail and stood on the north mark.

---

Reydan came out of the crowd at the south end without any sign that he was coming.

One moment there was only the crowd, packed to the rope, and the next there was a man stepping over the rope as easily as a farmer steps over a stile. He walked to the south mark. He took off his coat, the plain one that was better than anybody else's, and held it out to his side at arm's length without looking where it went. Somebody in the front row took it, because there was nothing else to be done with a coat held out like that. Then he stood on the mark in his shirt and looked at Cael.

It was the first time Cael had seen him stand up properly. Sitting on the bench, the man had been a shape; standing, he was a weight. He stood square, with his feet a little wider than his hips and his hands loose at his sides, and nothing about the standing told Cael anything at all. There was no favourite foot. There was no little lift of the heel, no settling of the hips, no restlessness in the fingers waiting to become a fist. Cael had seen a thousand fighters stand on a mark, and every one of them had stood ready for something. Reydan stood ready for everything at once. From twelve strides away it made him look like a man who was not ready for anything at all, a man waiting for a cart.

*Good,* thought Cael. *Then I'll not waste the gaze on his feet.*

He put the gaze on him anyway, at full depth, because there was no ration tonight. The room closed in at the edges of his eyes until the lamps on either side of the floor smeared into two pale walls. Ten degrees left and right, and blind past that; he had measured it with chalk in the autumn. He did not need the edges tonight. Everything he needed was on the south mark.

Vell's voice came across the floor without being raised.

"Reydan," she said. "Pressure Path. Iron, on a guild card. Rank eight." The pen moved. "Cael. Iron-equivalent. No Path designation." It moved again. "The rule in this room is that nobody is killed on my floor. Both of you know it. Both of you say so."

"Yes," said Reydan.

"Yes," said Cael.

The room was very quiet now. He could hear the stew pot ticking on its coals in the corner, and somebody's child asking a question and being hushed, and the street's cold coming in at his back.

"Then it's a bout."

Reydan was already moving.

He came straight, without a feint and without any hurry that showed, and covered the first third of the floor in two strides. Cael had planted his left foot before the second stride came down. He did not reach. Brom had said it a hundred times: *plant, arrive, then ask*. He arrived. He asked.

The knock went out from his planted foot, one hard rap on the door of the man in front of him. It came back with a single word: *inward*.

It was the drawing-tight he had felt in Brom every day for eleven days, a whole man becoming a fist in the making. It came from Reydan's right side, low, and it came faster than Brom's ever had. Brom had timed the gap on the post, counting on his own pulse, from the pulling in to the firing: a little less than half a second, four-tenths at the most. Cael had spent a fortnight learning what to do with four-tenths of a second, and he did it now. He shut the knock. He let the hip go.

The Wind took him left and forward in its fan, half a body over, and the burst went through the place where his ribs had been.

He felt it go past. It was not like a fist going past, or a staff. It was like a door slamming very close to his head, a flat hard shove of air that he felt on his cheek and in his ears at once. At the north rope behind him he heard the front row catch its breath and give ground, a whole line of people rocking back on their heels at once. Then the landing beat had him, the two *ands* of the lock, and he stood in it with his weight caught and his breath held and waited for whatever would come into it.

Nothing came. Reydan had not followed. He had stopped, square, and was looking at the place where Cael had landed.

*One.*

The second came off a short step to the left, low, at his knees. *Inward*, said the knock. This time Cael did not trust the hip, because the burst was low and the fan went forward. He went down instead, into the drop that Lira had put into his legs with four hundred repetitions: the left knee, the roll across onto the right foot, and up a body's width to the right with no doorway at the end of it. The burst hit the stone where his shins had been. Chips of the old foundry floor flew and rattled against the rope, and the crowd made a sound like a pot boiling over.

*Two.*

The third he knocked for and got smear, a confused rush of somebody large that did not know which way it was going. He had decided in the alcove what smear meant. He did not go. He stood with his hands up, and Reydan's right arm came forward with nothing in it at all. It was a feint, made with the whole body, and it would have sent a man who moved on a guess straight into the path of the real one. The real one came a heartbeat later, off the same arm. *Inward*, clean, and the hip, and the fan, left, and gone.

*Three.*

The fourth was quick. It came short and early, while he had only half finished landing, as if Reydan wanted to know whether he moved to a count or to something else. He knocked from a foot that had barely found the stone, and the answer came back clean anyway. He went right on his feet, a short ugly step with nothing of Lira's drop in it, and the burst went past his left shoulder close enough to pull at his shirt.

*Four.*

And twice, in among the four, the floor had opened in front of him.

After the third, Reydan's weight sat back on his left heel for the length of a breath, with the ribs on his right side turned toward Cael and nothing in front of them. After the fourth he stood too square and too near for half a beat. Both times Cael saw it with the gaze, as plainly as a gate left open in a wall, and both times he let it close. His right hand stayed where it was. The giving face waited in the hollow under his breastbone, three strikes deep, and he kept it asleep. *Not yet.* The room was full of eyes, and every pair of them would go home and write down whatever he showed. He did not mean to show anything until he had to.

He threw nothing back. He stood off and moved, and that was all.

Reydan stepped back. It was a small, formal step, the kind a man takes to say a sentence has ended. He stood a little way from his mark, breathing easily, and looked at Cael with an expression Cael knew from the inside: a man adding something up.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

---

He went back to the north mark and stood with his hands on his knees and let his breath come back into its count.

The room let its breath go too, all at once, and the sound of it was enormous. Somebody behind the north rope was saying *did you see him go down and come up the other side*, and somebody else was saying *he's not touched him once*. The betting man on his trestle was calling something new in his ruined voice. Cael let all of it go past.

Five knocks, four answered, one smear. Two bursts of the Wind, both left, both clean, and the hip line already warm from the crest to the knee. One drop. One short step right that he would not want to make again. No hits either way.

Four bursts, he thought, and four different ways of asking. Low, high, the feint, the early one. Reydan had not been trying to hit him. He had been finding out how Cael moved, and Cael had told him: left on the hip, right on the feet, and exactly how long the landing lasted, every time, in the lock. The man had stood and watched him land four times. He was not a man who needed telling twice.

*That's the first read,* Cael thought. *He's built it. Now he'll spend it.*

He looked across the stone. Reydan had walked back to the south mark and was standing on it again, square and empty and ready for everything. Over his shoulder, Lira's hands were on the rope.

Cael straightened up.

"Second exchange," said Vell.

Reydan came in from the right this time, angled. Cael planted and knocked, and the knock said *inward*, clean, low on the man's right side, exactly like the first burst of the night. It had the same weight and the same drawing-tight and the same height, and there was nothing in it that he had not felt before. He shut the knock. The hip went. The fan took him left.

He was in the air, half a body over, when he understood.

The burst was not going through the place he had left. It was going through the place where he was about to be. It came low and flat, a hand's breadth inside the spot where he would land, as if the man had aimed at the floor and trusted the floor to have Cael on it by the time the burst arrived. He had been given the old warning and a new aim. And there was nothing to be done about it, because the landing was two *ands* long, and in those two *ands* his legs belonged to the lock and not to him.

He had one thing he could still move, and he moved it. The left forearm came up across his ribs.

The burst struck it halfway between wrist and elbow. He heard it before he felt it: a short dull crack, like a mallet on a wet post. Then the force went on through the arm, and through the arm into him, and took his feet off the stone as if he weighed nothing at all.

He came down on his right side.

The stone was cold. He noticed that first, oddly, before anything else, the old foundry floor pressing its cold up through his shirt into his ribs as it had come up through his boots every night of the autumn. Above him the ceiling was one sheet of small flames from wall to wall, and he lay and looked at it. Somewhere a long way off, six hundred people had made one sound and were still making it.

*Fingers,* he thought, and told them to curl. They curled, slowly, as if the message had to go round by a longer road than usual. From the elbow down the arm was a long ringing numbness with a hot seam through the middle of it. *Not broken. Badly hurt. It guards now. It doesn't block.*

He put his right palm flat on the stone and got up.

---

Lira had known it a breath before he did.

She had watched his feet the whole of the first exchange, the way she had watched them every morning for eleven days in the alcove and every night for a year before that. She watched them the way a woman watches a thing she made, waiting for it to go wrong in the place she knows is weakest. Four times they had been right. Four times she had seen the left hip drop and the body go left and forward in its fan, or the knee go down and the roll come across, and every time her own feet had moved a little inside her boots at the south rope, as if she were the one on the stone.

And four times she had watched the man watch him land.

Nobody else had seen it; she was sure of that. The room had been watching the bursts, because the bursts were the noise and the danger. It had watched Cael vanish and reappear, and gasped, and laughed. But Lira stood almost behind Reydan's shoulder, close enough to see the side of his face, and she had seen where his eyes went after each burst. They did not go to where Cael had been, and they did not go to Cael. They went to the stone a little way in front of the place where he would come down, and they stayed there, very still, while he stood in the lock.

He was counting. She knew it as surely as if he had said the numbers aloud.

She had seen it once before, in the autumn, when the Shield with the tally book had stood in front of Cael and waited for him to land and counted two. Bede had counted it. She had told Cael so herself, afterward, at the landing table in her room with the lamp between them, and the two of them had sat there half the night with his Log open and worked out together what it meant: the lock was a shape and not a habit, a patient man could read it from a bench, and there was nothing Cael could ever do to make it shorter. They had written it in the Log, in his column for the things he could only guard. Then they had built the feet, so that he would never need the hip when he did not know. *The hip only when I know.*

And he had known, each of those four times. He had known when. That was all the knock had ever promised him: the moment, and the moment was enough to go. And going left was the only way the hip ever went.

When the second exchange began and Reydan came in from the right, Lira saw the gathering come into the man before the room did, a tightening down his right side as if somebody had pulled a cord through him. She saw Cael plant. She saw the hip begin to drop.

She saw Reydan's eyes go to the stone a little to the left of where Cael would land. It was the same place, exactly the same, and his eyes stayed there.

Her hands closed on the rope.

She could not call out. Nobody could. The coaching rule was the oldest rule on this floor after the one about killing. Vell had once stood at her table and told a man to his face what it would cost to break it: *you won't fight in any circuit from here to the coast*. If Lira opened her mouth now, the bout would stop. Vell would stop it, and look at her, and write a line about her in the red-taped book that nobody could ever take back. She did not open her mouth.

She stood at the rope and watched the thing she had made carry him exactly where the man had decided he would go.

The burst went in under his landing, and his arm came up, and she heard it hit. It was not a loud sound; it was short and flat, like a sack of grain dropped on boards, and she felt it in her teeth. Then he was in the air without having jumped, and then he was on the stone on his side. The whole room was on its feet and roaring, and Lira found that she had stopped breathing and could not remember how to start.

He lay there.

One breath. Two. She counted them the way she counted for him in the alcove, without meaning to. He lay on his right side with his left arm across his body and his face turned up to the lamps, and he did not move. The room roared. And she thought, very clearly, *if he doesn't get up I'll not be able to keep my hands on this rope*.

His right hand moved. It came down flat on the stone beside his hip, the way a man puts his hand on a table before he stands.

He got up.

He did not get up quickly. He came up one piece at a time, the hand and then the knee and then the other foot, the way she had taught him to come up out of the drop when he was tired, with nothing wasted and nothing hurried. He stood. He held the left arm across his ribs. Then he looked across the floor at Reydan with the face he wore on the bench when he was watching somebody he had not worked out yet. It was as if the stone and the noise and the arm had all happened to someone else and been reported to him by letter.

Lira breathed.

---

It did not get better after that, and she had not thought it would.

Reydan did not give him time to think. He came on, not hard and not even fast, but steadily, and he fired the bursts in pairs now. The first went at Cael's body; the second went, before the first had finished, at the place the first would send him. She watched Cael knock and go, and knock and go. He did not use the hip again. He went on his feet every time, and on his feet he was slower, and every step went on the bill of his legs. She knew those legs better than anybody alive, better than he did. She could see the extra half step where a single step should have done it. She could see the knee that did not quite come all the way up out of the roll. Each one was a coin out of the purse that would have to last him the night.

Once he knocked and stood still, and she knew from his stillness that the knock had come back smeared, and that he was keeping to what he had decided about smear. The burst after it went past his chest by a hand. The one after that took him across the shoulder blade as he turned away, a glancing shove, and sent him three steps toward the rope.

Toward her rope.

She saw what Reydan was doing then, and wished she had not. He was not trying to hit him any more. He was moving him. Every pair went a little to the same side of Cael's body, so that every time Cael went, he went the same way, and the way led to the south rope where she stood. The rope was the end of the floor. A man who went over it had gone out of the bout, and that was forfeit, as plain as a hand raised. Reydan was walking him off the stone the way a drover on the river road walks a frightened beast into a pen, without ever laying a hand on it, only by standing each time in the one place where it does not want to go.

Cael was a stride from the rope. Then less. She could see the back of his neck, wet, and his shoulders going up and down. She could have reached out and touched him.

She kept her hands on the rope.

*Drop,* she thought at him, with all of herself, as hard as she had ever thought anything. *Drop and roll. Right. Off the line. You've got the legs for one. I put it there.*

He did not drop.

He planted, a stride from the rope, and turned his left shoulder to the next burst. She had watched him do it in the alcove against Brom's slow push, three times a session and never more. It was Brom's idea, and Cael had bled for it, and he had told her he would only spend it on a place he meant to stand in. He spent it now, with his left arm hurt and held against his body. The burst went into the turned shoulder and through him at a slant, and it went crooked. She could see the hip catch on it. It went out past him into the rope post beside her, which jumped. And the push of it, going past, shoved him sideways and in, along the rope and off the line, in three long ugly strides with his feet scrambling under him and nothing of her teaching in them at all.

Then he was out of the corner, in the open floor, with his back to nobody, and the crowd made the noise a crowd makes when a thing it was sure of does not happen.

Reydan stopped. He looked at Cael for a moment from where he stood. Then he took his small formal step back.

"End of the exchange," said Vell. Lira heard her pen go down on the page and stay there, longer than it needed to.

Lira looked at her own hands. They were white across the knuckles where she had held the rope, and when she let go they shook, so she pressed them flat against her coat to make them stop. Across the floor at the north rope, Brom was looking at her, not at Cael. He had seen her face, she thought, all the way through. He did not nod. He only looked, steadily, for as long as it took her to look back.

Then she put her hands on the rope again, lightly this time, and went back to watching the feet.

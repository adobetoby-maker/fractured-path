# Chapter 33 — Section Twelve

The second thing the new sense would do, once he stopped asking it for the first, was walk.

He found it out on the market row, on a grey morning in the second week of the season, with Brom beside him and a loaf under each of their arms. They had come up from the baker's door by the long way, slowly, because Brom had said that if Cael was going to try it on his feet he had better try it somewhere he could not fall down a stair. Cael had been trying it for a week, in the alcove and on the walk home and once, foolishly, on the boarding-house stairs, and every time it had gone the same way: he would open it standing still, and take one step, and it would go out like a candle carried too fast across a room.

On the market row it went out three times in the first twenty paces.

"You're asking it too much," said Brom.

"I'm not asking it anything. I'm walking."

"You're asking it *who*." Brom shifted his loaf to the other arm. "Every time somebody comes near us, you want to know who it is and where they're going and what they've got in their basket. I can feel you want it. And it can't tell you that while you're moving. Mine couldn't for a year." He nodded down the row at a woman coming up it with a basket of turnips. "Ask it something smaller."

"Like what?"

"Like *is there anybody*."

So Cael tried that. He let the door come open, standing still, by the water trough at the corner of the row, and then he walked on, at a slow even pace, the pace he would have used to carry a full bucket, and he asked it nothing at all except that one thing. *Is there anybody.*

The woman with the turnips came past on his left, close, a pace off.

*Yes.*

That was all it gave him: no shape and no weight, nothing he could have told apart from anybody else. There was only a small bright *yes* on the left side of his skin as she went by, as clear as a knock on a door, and then, as she went on behind him, the *yes* went on too, thinning, and was gone.

He kept walking. A man with a barrow: *yes*. Nothing for five paces: *no*. Two boys running, very fast, past them both: *yes, yes*, there and gone so quickly that he only knew they had been there after they had passed. A dog: nothing at all, which surprised him, and which he wrote down later with a question mark.

He got almost to the top of the row before a cart came out of a side lane with its iron tyres banging on the cobbles, and he looked at it, and the door slammed.

"Thirty paces," said Brom. "That's a long way for the second week."

"It only said yes and no."

"It only said yes and no," Brom agreed. "That's what it says when you walk. The rest you have to stand still for." He turned the loaf over and looked at the crust. "It'll say more, later. But you've got to let it say the small thing well first, or it'll never trust you with the big one."

On the way down again he found the other thing it would do, and the first thing he did with it was wrong.

They had stopped at the corner where the row met the square, to let a carter's dray go by. Cael was standing still, and the door was open, and there were three people near them waiting for the dray too: a girl with a basket of eggs, an old man with a stick, and a young man in a blacksmith's apron with his sleeves rolled up.

"Two of those are kindled," said Brom, without looking at any of them. "One isn't. Which?"

Cael stood very still.

The street had given him the three of them already, as three small weights at his side, the way it gave him the cart and the cobbles: there, and there, and there. That much was the street's. Then, because Brom had asked, he went into the nearest. He did it gently, on purpose, the way he would have leaned toward a door to hear what was said behind it. The old man was there, warm, a weight on the air and nothing else; a person, plainly, but only one thing.

He was turning toward the girl with the eggs when he heard what he was doing.

He had written it down a few nights ago, at the crate desk, in the small hand. *Going into one person on purpose… belongs to them.* The old man had not said he could. The old man did not know there was anything in the world to say yes to. He was standing at a corner with his stick, waiting for a dray, and a boy he had never seen had just gone in under his coat to find out what he was.

Cael let the door shut.

"I just went into him," he said. "The old man. I didn't ask."

Brom looked at him, and then at the old man crossing behind the dray, and then back.

"No," he said. "You didn't. And I set it you, and didn't think." He shifted the loaf. "Mine tells me that much without going in, off the street, the way it tells me who's on the stair. Yours won't, yet. I forgot whose I was asking." He was quiet a moment. "That's mine, then, as much as yours."

They did the rest of it that week in the alcove, with people who had said yes.

Lira said yes before he had finished asking, and then, "Do it properly, if you're doing it," and stood on the chalk with her arms folded while he went in. She was there, the bird on the sill, and then, as he attended, there was something more inside the first weight, like a second coin under the first in a closed hand. The dock partner said he didn't mind, for nothing, and was the same: a weight with something in it. The heavyset man, asked at the kitchen table, looked at him for a long moment over his cup and said, "If it's for the book," and came down to the Ironyard for the first time since the Brom bout to stand on a chalk cross with his hat in his hands. He was there, big and slow and warm, and nothing else at all. He was very pleased to be told so, though he did not say why.

The third thing was smaller still. He asked the girl with the sacking in her hair from Keth's ring, who thought it was a joke and said yes. When she began to warm up at the post, the something inside her weight changed. It was not louder, exactly; it was awake, the way a dog lying down is different from a dog that has heard a step, though both are dogs and neither has moved. When she stopped and sat down to rewind her hands, it went down again.

*Kindled or not,* he wrote, that night, *close, standing still, with leave. Working or resting, close, standing still, with leave. Walking: yes or no only, thirty paces once; that's the street's, and needs nobody's. Nothing else. That's the whole of the vocabulary.*

And in the narrow column on Tuesday's page, under the other two: *The old man at the dray. Did. Didn't ask. Stopped.*

Lira's first confirming bout went up on Dace's wall on the Saturday, chalked for the Thursday evening card on the main floor, against an Iron-equivalent Stone from up the river. She told Cael about the other thing on the Wednesday night, on the walk up the hill from the alcove, because she said she would rather he heard it from her.

"I asked Brom," she said. "This morning. After the feet."

"Asked him what?"

She walked a few steps before she answered. The frost had come down hard that evening, and her breath went up in front of her, and she was walking with her staff on her shoulder the way she did when she was thinking, with both wrists hooked over it.

"Whether I'm past Copper," she said. "Really. Not in Vell's book. Not on Dace's wall. In me." She looked at the cobbles. "He reads people. He's the only person I've ever met who can stand a forearm off me and know what's in there without me telling him. And every examiner I ever had looked at me from across a room with a board in his hand, and told me what I was from that." She shrugged under the staff. "I wanted somebody to look properly once. Before Thursday."

Cael said nothing, because he could see what it had cost her to ask it, and that she had not finished.

"He made me stand on the line," said Lira. "And he stood there with his eyes shut for about as long as it takes to boil an egg, and I nearly hit him. Then he said it." She drew in her breath. "He said my feet are past it. He said they were past it before I ever came to this city, and he could feel it the first morning, the way my weight goes before I do. He said in my feet I read like an Iron Wind, and better than some."

"That's—"

"And then he said my hands are Copper still." She said it quickly, all of a piece, as if it had to be got out before it stuck. "When I catch a thing. When I stop. He said I catch like a Copper and send like an Iron, and there's a place in the middle where the one has to turn into the other, and it's slow. Not by much. He said he could feel it when he put his hand on my staff while I caught." She walked on. "He said a good Iron would feel it too, sooner or later, and go in there."

They had reached the corner of the row, and Lira stopped under the lamp there, and Cael stopped with her.

"Are you angry?"

"I was," said Lira. "For about as long as it takes to walk across the alcove. Then I thought about it." She took the staff off her shoulder and stood it on the cobbles and leaned on it with both hands. "Every examiner I've had told me what I wasn't. Nobody ever told me *where*. Not once. They said *not Iron*, and wrote it down, and sent me home." She looked at him. "He told me where. To the place. I can work on a place."

"You've got a night and a day."

"I know." She looked down the hill to the north window, where the practice lamp was dark yet. "I'm not going to fix it by Thursday. I'm going to know it's there on Thursday. That's different."

She picked up the staff and they went on up the row. At the door she stopped again, with her hand on the latch.

"I thanked him," she said. "He went red. All up the back of his neck, the way he does." The corner of her mouth went up. "Somebody ought to tell him he does that."

"Don't," said Cael.

"I'm not going to," said Lira. "I'm going to watch him do it for years."

Assessor Havel drew the Ardenmere file for its quarter's review on a Monday, from the middle of a stack of four, and this time he had been waiting for it.

He had not let himself know that he was waiting, because he was not a man who looked forward to files, but he had kept the date of its turn in the back of his notebook, the one with the cardboard cover, in pencil, small, among other dates that meant nothing. He had watched the rota come round toward it all season, a desk at a time, the way a man watches weather come up a valley. When the supervisor read out the morning's stacks along the long table and put a thin grey folder from the river side on top of a dyer's renewal, Havel felt something in his chest that he did not care to name.

He did the dyer's renewal first, because it was on top, and closed it.

The quarter's review was a newer thing than the visit. It had come down that spring with a printed sheet, half a page, which the registry wanted clipped to the inside of every passive monitoring file at its turn. The sheet asked an assessor seven questions, in boxes, and wanted a tick or a cross in each, and initials at the foot. *Residence confirmed. Contact log current. Guild affiliation unchanged.* He had filled in perhaps two hundred of them, and could have filled them in asleep.

He filled in the first five in a quarter of an hour. Residence: the keeper's book, his own visit, nothing since to say otherwise. Contact log: his own entry, and Coss's few, and nothing new. No guild. No incident. No travel outside the district on any record that had come up to the floor.

The sixth box said *Handling markers confirmed against manual*.

He had ticked that box two hundred times without opening the manual, as everybody did. A handling marker was a line in the classification block, entered by somebody with a right to enter it, signed and dated in the right-hand column. To confirm it against the manual meant to read the line and see that it was the kind of line the manual allowed, and every line on every file he had ever drawn had been the kind of line the manual allowed. Every one had initials beside it.

He looked at the classification block on the first leaf, at its three lines. The registry's, with its stamp. His own, the green slip, with *H.* and a date some weeks gone. And between them the line with nobody's name, in the short printed form the registry used, a designation and a figure, and an empty column at its end.

He could tick the box, and nobody would ever check it. He had stood in this room every Tuesday for four years and been told, in the supervisor's flat voice, that the sheets were for the registry's comfort and not for anybody's reading.

Or he could do what the box said.

He sat there for some time with the pencil over it. It occurred to him, distantly, that this was the first time since the yellow room the thing had been offered to him with a reason attached. Not a curiosity. Not a question he would have to send upstairs with his own name on it, asking why a line existed. Only a box on a printed sheet that told him, in the registry's own words, to go and look.

He put the pencil down and went to the supervisor's shelf.

The supervisor was at his desk by the window with the morning's returns, and did not look up when Havel stood beside him and asked for the back sections of the manual against a quarter's review. He only held out his hand for the review sheet, read the sixth box, and gave it back, and then pushed the loans book across the desk with one finger and went back to his returns.

Havel signed the loans book. *Manual, back sections, volumes two and three. Quarterly review, river side. H.* The date.

The back sections of the manual lived on the top shelf behind the supervisor's chair, in two thick books bound in the same grey board as the files, with their corners worn white. He had held them once, in his training year, when an instructor had passed them round the table so that each new assessor could feel the weight of everything he would never need to read. Nobody had opened them then. He carried them back to his desk now as carefully as a full tray. He was aware of the man by the door who hummed, and of the two assessors he shared the rota with, and of the fact that none of them looked round. A man carrying the manual to his desk on a review day was a man doing his job.

He opened the second volume at its index of designations.

It took him a long time to find. The short form on the file was a run of four letters and a gap and a figure, and the index ran to forty pages of short forms in close print, by letter, with their long forms beside them and a section number after each. He went down them with his finger as he went down a contact log, a line at a time, and did not let himself skip. Two-thirds of the way through the fourth letter he found it.

He read the long form, and read it again, and copied it on the back of the review sheet in his own hand, so that he could not later tell himself he had misread it.

*Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4.*

The section number after it was twelve.

Section Twelve was in the third volume, and it was short, three pages, which surprised him; most of the manual ran to great length about small things, and this ran short about something that was plainly not small. He read the whole of it twice before he let himself understand any of it, and then he read the first paragraph a third time.

A Priority Level 4 designation, said the manual, was reserved for practitioners of Gold tier or above under active security review. It authorized monitoring at a weight and frequency the manual set out in a table, which Havel read and did not quite believe. It was to be entered only on the written authority of an officer of a grade the manual named, who would sign and date the entry. Every such entry would carry a date for its own review, not more than a year from its making, and would lapse if not renewed in the same hand.

He read the table after that, because he could not stop himself. A practitioner under Level 4 was to be visited by an assessor not less than once a month and reported on in full each time, on a longer form than any Havel had ever filled in. A named officer two grades above the assessor was to read every report and initial it. Any travel was to be known beforehand, any change of lodging within a day, any new associate within a week. There was a column for the costs, which were large, and a line that said to whom they were to be charged.

He turned back to the file.

*Cael Hesk-ward.* The classification, in the registry's square print: *[SHATTERED]*. No tier. That class carried none; it was the whole of the trouble with it. No guild. No incident anywhere in the record, not so much as a fine. A residence in a boarding house two streets from a warehouse, confirmed by a keeper's book. A boy on a pump step in a market square who had answered every question on the form correctly and then, unasked, had held out a pear.

And under the class, in the place where a handling marker went, a designation the manual reserved for Gold-tier practitioners under active security review. It had no officer's name beside it, and no date, and no review date. It had nothing in the column at all.

And nobody had ever treated the boy as if it were there. That was the other thing, and he saw it only now, with the table open beside him. One visit in more than a year, his own. Coss's few short lines, and then nothing. No monthly report, no named officer reading anything, no associates listed, though the boy shared a landing with another lodger and went every day, by the keeper's own account, to a warehouse full of practitioners. The line said the registry should be watching this boy as it watched the most dangerous people it had. The file said nobody was watching him at all. Havel did not know which of them to believe, and found that he could not believe both.

Havel sat back.

He had known since the evening in the yellow room that the line was strange. He had thought, if he had let himself think anything, that he would find it was some small thing. A clerk's carry from another file, perhaps, or an old marker for a class that had since been folded into another, or a mistake in the short form that meant something dull. He had been ready for dull, and had very nearly wanted it.

This was not dull. This was two things on one leaf that could not both be true by the manual's own words. A Level 4 needed a Gold practitioner and a signature, and the file had neither. And it sat there in its proper place, in its proper form, as quietly as the registry's own stamp.

He thought of the four old files in the archive, the only others of that class there had ever been, which his training year had read. Every one of them had carried markers, and every marker had been signed, and dated, and explained, in a hand anybody could find in the duty records. Not one of them had carried this.

He closed the third volume very gently, as though it might wake.

---

He wrote the query twice.

The first draft came out of him fast, in a quarter of an hour, and he read it back and was frightened by it.

*Contact of record, Ardenmere file. On quarterly review I find a Priority Level 4 designation entered beneath the subject's classification without the officer's signature, date or review date required by Section 12. The subject holds no tier and is under no security review on any record in the file. The designation cannot be correct under the manual as written. Please advise who entered it.*

Every word in it was true. He could have defended each sentence to the supervisor line by line. And it read, from the first line to the last, like a junior assessor telling a senior officer that something in the senior officer's file was wrong, and asking for a name. He imagined it arriving on a desk two floors up, and the man at that desk reading *cannot be correct*, and *who entered it*, and then reading the initials at the foot. He had stood outside the duty board for four years learning which words made a man known, and *who* was the first of them.

He folded the first draft into four and put it in his inside pocket to burn at home in the grate; it would not go into any office basket, where a cleaner might take it out again.

The second took him most of the afternoon, though it was shorter.

*Contact of record, Ardenmere file (Hesk-ward, C.), river side. Quarterly review. A Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4 designation appears in the classification block. I am unable to confirm it against Section 12 of the manual for the review record. Requesting guidance on its origin and continued application. H.*

He read it back four times. It asked for nothing a contact of record could not give with one line. It did not say *wrong*, and it did not ask *who*. It said only that a box on a printed sheet could not be ticked, which was true, and was the registry's own problem and not his. If anybody two floors up ever wanted to know why a junior assessor had asked about a marker above his grade, the answer was clipped to the inside of the file. The sheet had told him to.

He knew, sitting there with it, that it was still a question with his initials on it, going up. He had decided against this once, in the yellow room, on a careful man's reasons. He was not sure the reasons had changed. What had changed was that he had now read Section Twelve, and he could not unread it. A correct man, he had thought then, would raise a line with no name on it, and a careful man would wait until he knew what he was raising. He knew now.

He put the query in the inner-post tray at the end of the room for the afternoon round, with the review sheet unticked in its sixth box and clipped inside the closed file beside him, and went back to a carter's licence.

---

The answer came before the lamps were lit.

It came down in the last round of the afternoon, in an inner-post envelope with his name on the front in a careful upright hand, every stroke of it the same weight. He knew the hand from the contact log, from a year of very short entries. He opened the envelope at his desk while the man by the door hummed, and unfolded the slip inside, and read it.

*Follow standard procedure. Do not attempt to reclassify the monitoring level. File regular reports.*

That was all: no greeting, and no signature but an initial. There was no word in it about origin, or Section Twelve, or the empty column, or anything he had asked. He had not asked to reclassify anything; he did not have the grade to try. But the slip told him not to, in the flat closed voice of a door shut quietly and firmly by somebody who did not mean to open it again.

He read it twice, and noticed, as a careful man notices such things, that it had come back the same afternoon. Queries to a contact of record waited a week at least, and a fortnight in the season. This one had been answered within three hours of leaving his desk. He did not know whether that meant the question mattered more than he had thought, or that somebody wanted it finished before it could start to matter. He did not know which of those he would rather it meant.

He did what the slip said. He put it inside the file, behind the review sheet, as procedure required for any instruction on a monitoring file. In the sixth box, instead of a tick or a cross, he wrote in his smallest hand, *Queried; instructed per contact of record*, and his initials. That was true, and it was all the box would hold. He closed the file and set it on the pile for the morning's return.

He sat in the emptying room a long time after that, until the hummer had gone and the lamp on his desk was the only one lit.

He had learned, in four years, how to tell a question from a challenge. He knew the exact place where a junior officer's curiosity stopped being diligence and became the other thing. He knew that the slip in the file was that place, written down. He would not send another query, or take the volumes down again. He would do as the slip said: standard procedure, regular reports, the level left exactly where somebody had put it. He knew, too, that he would do it well, because he did everything well, and nobody reading his returns for the next ten years would ever find a line in them that the slip had not allowed.

But the slip had told him what to do with the file. It had said nothing at all about what he was to know.

He took the notebook with the cardboard cover out of his inside pocket and opened it to the page where the earlier slip was still folded, the one that said *file thin for class*. Under his note from the yellow room, *Not raised. Kept*, he wrote the date, and then, slowly, the way a form would have wanted it if there had ever been a form for this:

*Quarterly review. The line is* Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4. *Manual, Section 12: Gold tier and above, under active security review. Officer's signature, date and review date required. None here. Subject: no tier, no incident. Queried contact of record in the plainest words I had. Answered the same afternoon:* Follow standard procedure. Do not attempt to reclassify the monitoring level. File regular reports. *Complied.*

He read it back. It was true and it said no more than he knew.

Then, under it, smaller, because it was not a fact and the rest of the page was:

*Who enters a Level 4 and doesn't sign it? And who answers a question about it in three hours without answering it?*

He looked at the two questions for a while. They were the only thing in the whole matter that belonged to nobody but him. They were not in the file, and the slip could not reach them, because they had never been asked anywhere a slip could follow. They would stay in a cardboard cover in his inside pocket, where they would ask nobody anything, and do no harm, and not go away.

He thought, for no reason, of the boy on the pump step holding out a pear in the cold.

He put the notebook away. He took the first draft out of his other pocket, the frightening one, and looked at it once more, and put it back to burn. Then he blew out the lamp, and went home through the Ranked streets, which were wide and named and very quiet, and did not look back at the river.

In his room he knelt at the grate before he lit the lamp, and put the first draft on the cold coals, and set a match to the corner of it.

It burned quickly, the way thin office paper does. He watched the edge go brown and then bright along the lines of his own level hand. He read them going, without meaning to: *cannot be correct*, and *under the manual as written*, and, last, curling up black at the very end of the sheet, *who*. Then that was ash too, and the grate was dark again, and there was nothing in the room but a smell of burnt paper and a young man on his knees in his coat.

He had done the right thing; he was nearly sure of it. The plain query had asked what a review sheet allowed, and been answered, and he had obeyed the answer, and the file was correct. A careful man and a correct one had both been satisfied, and it had taken him only a season and a burnt page to find out how.

He got up, and lit the lamp, and made his supper, and did not take the notebook out of his coat again that night. He could feel it there all evening against his ribs, small and flat, with its two questions in it.

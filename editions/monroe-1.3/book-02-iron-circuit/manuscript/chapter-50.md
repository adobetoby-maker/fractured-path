# Chapter 50 — Session Nine

On the morning of the tenth day, the Friday, the mending stall had pennants.

Cael saw them on his way up for bread after Lira's hour, with his forearms singing and his left knee stiff on the market steps. They hung from a string across the front of the stall like washing: small triangles of cheap red cloth, a dozen of them, with a short bar of white stitched across each one. It took him a moment to see that the white bar was meant to be a rope, and the red was the Ironyard's lamps, or the stone, or simply red, because red sold. The mending-stall woman sat under them with her iron spectacles on her nose and a needle going, stitching a thirteenth.

"Two coppers," she said, without looking up. "Three for five. Had a carter buy six before breakfast for his nieces." She bit the thread. "Don't look at me like that, lad. I've mended the Ironyard's shirts for thirty years and never once made a copper off a bout. A woman's allowed one week."

He bought one, without knowing why, except that it seemed rude not to, and put it in his coat beside the Log, where it stayed.

The row had changed again in the four days since the betting man's split line. Two of the food stalls by the pump had begun to bake in larger batches, and the pie woman had a boy helping her who had not been there before. Outside the Ironyard's side door, when Cael passed it, two men he knew by sight from the eel market were standing with Dace in the cold, talking with their hands, and Dace was listening with his pencil against his chest and the face he wore when he was being asked for something he had never given anybody. Cael heard *inside the doors*, and *three hours on their feet*, and *a copper a bowl*, and slowed without meaning to.

Dace saw him over the men's shoulders and lifted the pencil an inch, which meant *wait*. The two men went off toward the eel market looking pleased with themselves, and Dace came over to him with the expression of a man who has just sold something he had sworn never to sell.

"Stew," he said. "Inside my doors. On the night." He looked at the side door as if it had betrayed him. "Twenty years nobody's eaten inside that building but the rats. I've thrown men out for bringing in a pie." He turned the pencil over. "But I've got five hundred people who think they're coming on Tuesday, and room for four hundred if they all breathe in. They'll stand three hours. If they go out to eat they'll not get back in, and then they'll stand in the street and shout, and the street will be full of people shouting the night the whole hill's watching to see if we can run a thing properly." He put the pencil back against his chest. "So the eel-market men can have the corner by the slate. A copper a bowl, and they sweep up after, and if I find one bowl on Vell's table I'll throw the pot in the river."

"You've never let anybody."

"I've never had to." Dace looked at him. "You'll learn this, if you stay in any building long enough. You keep your rules for twenty years so that when you have to break one you know exactly what you're breaking, and how much, and you do it once." He went back in at the side door.

The paper-stall man asked him again whether he was feeling well, with less care this time, and Cael said *very*, and the man nodded and let the slate line stand where it was.

All of it was a long way off. He noticed it the way he noticed weather, as a thing that was happening round him, and carried the bread back down the hill, and ate it on the bench in the alcove with Lira's staff leaning on the wall beside him. He had knocked her thirteen times that morning, with her leave, and twelve had answered, and he had called every one aloud. She had said *yes* to each without stopping, and at the end she had said *twelve*, and nothing else, and gone up to sleep for an hour before her own day. That was what mattered this morning, and the pennant in his coat did not.

It had been the strangest of all the mornings, because with her leave given the alcove had become a different room. Every time she came at him he could feel her coming for an instant before she came, small and quick and alive at the end of his knock, and every time he called it aloud she said *yes* without breaking stride, so that the morning had gone by in a kind of conversation, his voice and her staff and the one word back. He had not been hit nearly as often as he had been all week, but hard enough, the times he was, to remember that a knock was not a wall.

What mattered this afternoon was the ninth session, and Brom had said he would come as near the man's speed as his Path allowed.

---

Brom came down at noon with his sleeves already turned back, and did not sit.

"Defending again," he said. "Same as yesterday. I'll come at you with the push and I'll strike between, and you'll deal with the strikes and knock for the push. But I'm going to come faster. Not fast; I can't be fast. But faster." He set his feet in the middle of the alcove. "I've been working it on the post. If I don't breathe in for the hardening, if I take the breath before and carry it, I can gather in about a third of what it took on the first day. It's ugly and it costs me. I'll not do it long." He looked at Cael. "May I hit you harder than I have?"

"You may. May I?"

"You may." Brom almost smiled. "We're very polite."

They began.

It was faster, and Cael felt that from the first push. Brom's gathering had always been a slow tightening that came up through him and could be watched from the bench by anybody who knew where to look. It was smaller now, and quicker, a drawing-in that was over almost before the knock could reach it. Between the pushes the strikes came heavier: the big forearm swinging in at his shoulder, his ribs, the side of his head, slow enough to meet but heavy enough that meeting it wrong would put him on the stone. He met them and moved, and planted and arrived and knocked in the gaps between them, and called what came back.

*Inward*, and he was already turning, letting the push go past him at the slant.

*Inward*, and the drop, right, under it.

A strike, met on the forearm Ansel had hooked, which complained.

*Nothing*, and he stood, and the strike came instead of the push, and he met it.

*Inward.*

It was the longest he had ever stood in front of Brom without either of them stopping, and somewhere about the fifth push he stopped noticing the band behind his eyes, or the forearm, or the cold coming in under the arch. There was only the next gap. He had felt this before, on floors, in the middle of a bout that was going well, a narrowing of everything down to the width of one man and one moment. He had never felt it in the alcove, in a drill, with nothing riding on it but a number on the back of his hand.

By the eighth he had found a rhythm of a kind, not comfortable and not clean, but a rhythm: strike, met; gap, knock; push, gone. By the twelfth his shirt was stuck to his back and the band behind his eyes had stacked twelve deep and was beginning to fill the room, and the forearm had stopped complaining and gone numb, which was worse. He had called ten of the twelve right, and knew it without looking at his hand. Two had smeared, and he had stood off from both of them, and taken the strike that followed instead of guessing at the push.

Ten in twelve, better than he had ever done, defending. He felt it as a kind of quietness in his chest, and did not let himself feel anything else about it, because there were eight more to go and Brom was already gathering.

Then Brom fired the thirteenth.

It came after a strike. That was all Cael would be sure of afterward, and he was sure of it because he wrote it down within the hour, before he could make it into a better story. Brom's forearm came in at his right shoulder, slow and heavy, and he met it on his own forearm, and the meeting jarred him to the elbow. And in the same instant, under the strike, so close behind it that there was no gap at all for a knock to go into, Brom gathered.

There was no time, as Cael knew afterward, no space between the strike and the gathering for his foot to plant and arrive and ask. He did not plant, and he did not knock.

And he did not need to.

It was as if the gathering and his answer to it were one movement, the way a foot finds the last stair in the dark before you know you have reached for it. There was no reading first and then deciding, and no deciding first and then doing. There was Brom drawing in, small and fast, and there was Cael, in the same instant, already meeting it. He was not meeting the push, which had not happened yet, but the drawing-in itself, the gathered thing, as it gathered. It was not the knock, or the turned shoulder of the redirect, or the hip, or any of the three things he had. He could not afterward have said what part of him it was.

And the push was not there.

Brom's hands came forward, the way they had a hundred times that week, but what should have been in them was not in them. It had come apart somewhere between being gathered and being sent, the way a wave comes apart on a flat beach, and Brom's two hands arrived at Cael's chest with nothing behind them but the weight of two hands, and stopped there, resting on his shirt.

They stood like that, and neither of them moved, Brom's hands flat on Cael's chest. Cael's own hands were somewhere in the air between them, open, where they had been when it happened, and he did not remember putting them there.

Then Cael noticed that nothing hurt.

It took him a moment to understand why that was strange. He had spent the whole fortnight being hurt in small, exact ways, each of them a price for something, so that by now his body was a kind of ledger that he could read without thinking: the forearm for Ansel, the ribs for Lira's first morning, the groove through the shoulder for nine redirects. He had expected, without knowing he expected it, that whatever had just happened would add a line to that ledger, the way everything else had.

He waited for it, because he knew how to wait for a price. Every one of the things he had came with one, and he could name every one: the hip's thin line from crest to knee, and the lock after it; the knock's stripe behind the eyes; the giving face's hollow under the ribs; the redirect's groove through shoulder and side. He stood in the middle of the alcove and waited for this one to send its bill, the way he would have waited for a bruise to come up after a fall.

Nothing came.

Not in the shoulder, or the ribs, or behind the breastbone. Not behind the eyes, where the twelve knocks were still stacked exactly as high as they had been before the thirteenth, and no higher. Not anywhere. Whatever he had just done, it had cost him nothing that he could find, and he looked for it in every part of himself, and it was not there.

That frightened him more than anything had all fortnight.

He knew why, too. He had known why since he was nine years old, sitting on an upturned crate in Hesk's workshop with a cup of milk, while Hesk fitted a bearing into a housing on the bench. A man had come round that morning selling something on credit, nothing to pay until spring, and Hesk had sent him away. Cael had asked why, because it had seemed like a good thing to be given something and pay nothing. Hesk had gone on easing the bearing in for a while without answering. *Lad,* he had said at last, *anything that charges you nothing at the counter means to send the bill later. And it'll send it in a coin you never said you'd pay in.*

"What was that?" said Brom.

His hands were still on Cael's chest, and he took them away slowly, and looked at them.

"I don't know," said Cael. His voice came out strange. "Again. Do it again."

Brom looked at him for a moment, and then went back to his place, and set his feet, and struck. It was the same strike, at the same shoulder, slow and heavy, and Cael met it on the same numb forearm; and under it, close behind, Brom gathered.

Cael went after it, whatever it had been.

There was nothing there to reach for. He felt for it the way he would have felt for the hip, or the knock, or the turning shoulder, and his hands closed on air. Brom's push came, ordinary and whole, and took him in the chest and put him back three steps against the wall.

"Again," said Cael, from the wall.

They did it again, and there was nothing; only the push, whole, and the wall.

He tried it standing closer, and then farther off. He tried it with Brom not striking at all, only gathering. He tried it with his eyes half shut, the way he had been standing when it came. Then, last, he tried knocking first, from a planted foot, to see whether the knock would lead him to it. The knock came back *inward*, clean and ordinary, and he redirected the push at the slant out of pure habit before he could stop himself. The shoulder sent its bill at once, all along the groove, as if to show him what a real price felt like.

It went the way a name goes when you turn to say it to somebody, known one moment and simply absent the next, with no sense of where it has gone or how to call it back. Whatever had opened had shut again behind itself, cleanly, and left no handle on the outside.

At the seventh try he stopped, and stood in the middle of the alcove with his hands at his sides and his breath going hard, and looked at Brom, and Brom looked back at him.

"That's enough," said Brom.

"Once more."

"No. That's enough." Brom's voice was very quiet. "You're reaching. I can feel you reaching from here. You'll put the thing you're reaching for into the stone and break your hand on it." He sat down where he was, on the alcove floor, with his back against the post and his knees up. It was not a thing he did. "Sit down. This is going to take longer than standing."

---

Cael sat on the floor facing him, while the straw post swung a little above them on its rope from where Brom's back had touched it, and slowed, and stopped.

"Tell me what you read," said Cael. "Exactly. Not what you think it was."

Brom took a long time. He sat with his big hands on his knees and his eyes on the floor between them, and Cael could see him go back through it, slowly, the way he read a page, from the front.

"I had the strike," he said at last. "I felt your forearm meet it. Then I gathered, under it, the way I've been working on the post. Fast for me. And I felt you." He stopped. "I've felt you all week, every push. When the knock comes, it's like somebody rapping once on a door I'm leaning against. And then your answer, after, when you turn or drop or go. There's always a gap between them. The knock, and then you. However quick you've got this week, there's a gap. There's a gap for everybody. There's a gap for me. I read a man, and then I answer him. I've never once in my life got to skip it, against anybody."

"And this time?"

"This time there wasn't one." Brom looked up. "There was no knock. There was no gap. There was you, already answering, while I was still gathering. Not before. Not after. In the same place in time as the gathering itself, as if you'd read it and answered it with one hand." He turned his own hand over on his knee. "And the thing I'd gathered came apart. I didn't let it go; I felt it go. It was in me and then it wasn't. I've never felt that."

Cael said nothing.

"You haven't got a Tide," said Brom.

"No." Cael was quite sure. He had the Log by heart; he could have recited every field of every entry. *Wind-adjacent. Pressure-adjacent. Iron-adjacent.* Three, and their notices, and their costs. "There's nothing in the Log like it. Nothing I've had has ever done that, or anything near it."

"I know. I've read it." Brom was quiet for a moment. Then he said it carefully, like a man setting down a thing that might break.

"What I read in that one exchange was Tide-adjacent."

Cael sat very still.

"Listen to all of it before you do anything with that," said Brom. "I've never read a Tide. Not once. I've never stood in front of one close enough to put my read on him; there's not one on this hill, and there wasn't one in the boat shed. I know what a Tide reads like the way you'd know what the sea looks like from a man who'd been to it." He held up a finger. "There was a man in the boat shed who'd fought one, on the coast, years before. He talked about it once, over a fire, because I asked him. He said it was like fighting water. He said there was no gap in it. You'd push, and it would already be giving, and answering, both at once, as if it had known the push before you did. That's all I've got. One man, once, by a fire." He let the finger fall. "And what I felt today matched what he said. That's the whole of it. That's all I'm telling you."

"Could you be wrong?"

"Easily. I could be wrong in every way there is to be wrong." Brom said it without any heat. "It's a secondhand picture of a Path I've never read, laid over a thing I've only ever felt from you, out of a shelf I've told you twice I haven't got. I can't tell you what it was. I can only tell you how it felt from my side, with the little I've got to feel it with. Those are two different things, and I'll not pass you one dressed as the other."

Cael found that he was grateful for that, more grateful than he could have said. Brom had always kept the two things apart: what he had felt, and what he would claim. That was the same line the Log had been ruled down from its first page, *claim* on one side and *evidence* on the other.

"In the autumn," said Cael slowly. "In the bout. The third exchange."

"I know what you're asking." Brom shook his head. "It wasn't that. In the third I read nobody. I put my read on you and there was nobody there, like a room you've just walked out of. Today I read somebody. I read you, and I read something in you doing a thing I've no shelf for, except a man's story by a fire." He looked at Cael. "Those aren't the same either. Don't put them in one box because they're both strange."

They sat on the cold floor for a long time, not talking, while the light at the arch went from yellow to grey. Somewhere out in the main room the afternoon card was being called, and the benches made their noise, and it came to the alcove faint and far off, like something happening in another town.

"It didn't cost anything," said Cael at last.

Brom looked at him.

"Everything I've got costs something. I know the price of all of it. I've written every price down." He looked at his hands. "That cost nothing. I've looked everywhere. There's nothing."

"And that frightens you."

"Yes."

Brom nodded slowly, as if Cael had said something sensible.

"It'd frighten me," he said. "A thing that's free when you've done nothing to buy it." He got up off the floor, stiffly, and held his hand down to Cael. "We'll not try it again today. Or tomorrow. You'll write it down, and we'll leave it where it fell, and if it comes back it comes back. And we'll do the tired stage tomorrow as if it never happened, because on Tuesday it won't be there to help you, and you'd better not walk out there hoping it will."

Cael took the hand and let himself be pulled up.

"You'll not tell anybody," he said. It was not quite a question.

"It's in the alcove." Brom let go of his hand. "Like my method. Like everything of yours. That was the fifth term, and it holds both ways." He looked at the place on the floor where they had been sitting. "I'll tell you what I'll do. I'll think about it, every way I can think, and I'll not come to anything, and I'll not tell you what I didn't come to unless you ask." He picked his coat off its nail. "That's the most honest thing I can give you about it. It's not much."

"It's more than anybody else could."

"That's true," said Brom, without any vanity at all, "but it's still not much." 

"Thirteen," he said. "It was the thirteenth."

"Of the ninth session," said Brom. "I'll remember."

---

He told Lira that evening, before the dusk hour, on the landing, with her door open and her staff across her knees on the top stair. He told her the same day, because he had always told her the same day, and because if he had waited until morning he would have begun to tidy it.

She listened to all of it without a word. When he came to Brom's hands resting on his chest, with nothing behind them, she put her own hand flat on the staff. When he came to *Tide-adjacent* she looked up, and did not look away again until he had finished.

"You've had nothing from a Tide," she said.

"No."

"And it cost nothing."

"Nothing I could find."

She was quiet a long time.

"I'm not going to say anything clever," she said at last. "I haven't got anything clever. I've got a cold feeling in my back, that's all, the same one I get when the floor goes quiet before something." She stood up. "Write it down. All of it. Exactly the way it was, before you make it into anything." She went down a stair, and stopped, and looked back up at him. "And then come and let me drop you on your head for an hour. Some things you can only do about by doing something else."

He wrote it in the Log before he went down, at the crate desk, in ink.

He did not put it under any of the three entries, because it did not belong to any of them. He turned instead to the back of the book, past the two columns and the *Carrying* page, to a clean leaf, and ruled a line across the top of it and wrote a heading he had never needed before.

*Anomalies.*

Then, under it, as carefully as he had ever written anything:

*Session nine, the thirteenth simulated burst: Brom gathering under a strike, faster than all week, with no gap for a knock. Read and answered in one motion, with no knock, no decision, and no gap between them. The answer met the gathering itself, not the push, and the push came apart before it was sent; his hands arrived with nothing behind them. Not Wind, not Pressure, not Iron, not anything in this book. Cost: none found, and I looked everywhere. Seven tries after, close and far, with and without the strike, with and without the knock, and nothing. Could not make it come again.*

*Brom's read: Tide-adjacent. His own words for his basis: never read a Tide; one man by a fire in the boat shed who had fought one; a secondhand picture laid over a thing he's only felt from me. "I can't tell you what it was. Only how it felt from my side." Description, not diagnosis. Not the third exchange: that was nobody; this was somebody.*

He stopped there for a long time, with the pen lifted, and then he wrote the rest, slowly, because the Log did not get to keep only the comfortable things.

*First possibility: something new arriving, not finished enough yet to be called on, showing itself once before it's whole. If so, it's the first that has ever come this way. Every other one came with a notice, named, after; this came with nothing, before.*

*Second possibility: something already in me doing a thing I've never found it doing. One of the three, or the three together, under a pressure and a closeness that hasn't happened again. If so, nothing's new, and what I have to look at is how much I've missed in things I thought I'd measured to the last line.*

*Third possibility, the one I least want, and so it goes in: that it isn't any one piece at all. That it was the architecture itself showing a glimpse of its own shape. Whatever holds the pieces, for half a second, instead of the pieces.*

*Can't choose. Not enough to choose with. Evidence insufficient.*

*Filed as anomaly. Monitor.*

He read it back twice, and then he capped the ink and sat with the book open under the lamp. The fear had not gone; it had only been written down, which was all he had ever been able to do with fear, and which had always been enough to carry it.

The hip went left. The knock gave him when and not where. The redirect cost three a session and he had three left in his shoulder for the eleventh day. Those were things he had, with prices he knew. This was a thing that had happened, once, for half a second, and had charged him nothing, and might be the most important thing in the whole book or might never come again in his life.

It would not be there on Tuesday; Brom was right, and he would not walk out there hoping for it.

He went down the stairs to let Lira drop him on his head for an hour, and found, at the bottom, that his hands had stopped shaking somewhere on the way down.

She did not mention it once in the whole hour. She came at him from the right and the left and the front with nothing before it, and he knocked when he could and dropped when he could not, and called what the knock gave him, and she said *yes* or *no* to each. At the end of it she stood in the middle of the alcove with her breath going white and looked at him for a while.

"You were here," she said. "The whole hour. I thought you'd be somewhere else."

"So did I."

"Good." She blew out the lamp. "That's the right place to be."

---

He lay awake afterward for a long time in the dark, with the window a grey square over the crate desk and no lamp in the north window of the Ironyard, because she had said there would not be one.

He thought about Brom's hands on his chest with nothing behind them, and about the clerk's nine pages, and Ansel buttoning his coat to the throat, and the drop that had gone into his legs. Four days. Somewhere up the hill above the saddler's, a man who had been paid for since he was ten was lying in a narrow bed, or not lying in it, and thinking about whatever such a man thought about four days out. Cael tried to picture him and could not, because he had never seen him. He had a name and a tier and nine pages, and the half-second that had come apart in the alcove that afternoon was more real to him than the man was.

And then, somewhere toward morning, he found that he was thinking something he had not let himself think all fortnight. He was not thinking about how to survive the floor, or how to learn the most from losing on it, the way he had learned from Brom and from Bede. He was thinking about winning it. He was thinking about it plainly, as a thing that might happen, the way he would have thought about the weather tomorrow.

He could not have said where it came from. He lay still and looked at it for a long time in the dark, the way he looked at a new line in a column, and did not cross it out.

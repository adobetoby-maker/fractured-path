# Chapter 7 — Corridor

It was Bracken who knew the phrase first, and he knew it from the wrong side.

Karis had the ninth paragraph under her finger, the longest in the instrument, and had read it out once, and the four of them sat with it. It said that an unclassifiable practitioner inside a chartered house's evaluations raised a question of *evaluation integrity*. It said that the demonstration provision had been drafted for practitioners whose classification was only pending, and not for one who had none to wait for, and so could neither foresee that question nor settle it. And it said that the question therefore fell to the registry itself, under its general charge over the soundness of its own evaluations.

"Evaluation integrity," said Karis. "I've never once met that phrase in a hearing. Where does it live?"

"In the second volume," said Bracken, "in a part nobody opens unless something has gone wrong with a frame."

He did not reach for the volume. He sat with his hands folded on the table, looking into the lamp in front of him, and when he spoke again it was in the voice of a man remembering a particular day.

"I filed one once. My fourth year in the office. The district station in Ostrand had a frame that took to reading every Stone who stood in it a rank higher than he was. Not by much. Enough. A month of Stones went out of that station with ranks on their papers they hadn't earned, and nobody noticed until a quarryman's boy came up this hill with a certificate that said Rank Four and couldn't lift what a Rank Two lifts." He turned his folded hands over. "I wrote it up under the integrity provisions, because that's what they're for. They took the frame off its floor and tested it for a fortnight, and found a seam in the casing that let the damp in, and mended it, and read every Stone of that month over again. Then the frame went back on its floor and read true." He looked down the table at Cael. "That's an integrity question, in that volume. A frame that has drifted. You take it off the floor and test it until it reads true."

Nobody said anything.

By then the second volume lay open in front of the counsel. She had reached across Bracken for it while he talked, and found the place, and now she went down the sections with her rule.

"Twelve sections," she said. "Frames. Stations. Assessors suspected of being bought. Panels whose records argue with themselves. Instruments, or the people who keep instruments." She lifted the rule. "I've looked tonight for the case where somebody turned these sections round to point at the man standing in the frame instead of the frame. There isn't one. This is new."

"So they've stopped asking whether a house may teach me," said Cael.

"They've stopped asking that entirely. They gave it away in the sixth paragraph." The counsel closed the volume on her rule to keep the place. "Now they ask whether the registry can read you. Everybody at this table knows the answer. It has been in your file since your Kindling."

"So I'm not wrong," said Cael. "I'm the frame."

"You are the frame." The counsel's voice did not change at all. "And a frame that will not read true comes off the floor."

Cael sat with that. All his life frames had been the things that failed to read him, and he had stood in front of them and been the question they could not answer. Now the paper had turned it round. He was the instrument on the bench, and they meant to open his casing and look for the seam that let the damp in, as Bracken's men had done with the station in Ostrand. He found he was not afraid of their looking. He was afraid of what they would write when they found no seam at all, because a frame with nothing wrong in it that still would not read was not a thing the second volume had a section for. It was a thing chapter fourteen had.

Karis had turned to the back while the counsel spoke. She had marked the thirty-fourth paragraph with a slip when the counsel first read the paper upward, and she found it without looking and read it in a voice with nothing added.

*34. That the enrollee be compelled to re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol, and that the enrollment stand or fall upon that evaluation's result.*

She said nothing about it. It had been written so that nothing needed saying.

The counsel turned in her chair then and looked down the table at Cael as if, for the first time that night, he were the subject of it and not the paper.

"What have you got there?"

He turned the observation notebook round and slid it up to her. She looked down the column: nineteen leaves, thirty-seven paragraphs and three schedules; nine discretions to three evidences and one fraud, in the negative; eight provisions cited, two minutes, no rulings and no witnesses. She read it twice, which he had not seen her do with anything else.

"Do you know the difference, in front of a seat," she said, "between a description and an evaluation?"

"A description says what I did. An evaluation says what I am."

"Good. Their whole case will be that your floor is a description: a panel, a figure, a day, witnesses, nothing more. Mine will be that it is an evaluation, and the oldest kind, older than any frame, and I'll make it as well as anybody living could make it." She slid the notebook back. "Then the seat will choose. You've counted how many times they wrote the word it'll choose with." She tapped the column at *nine*. "Go on counting. Bring me a column like this every week until it's over. A count is evidence. Indignation never was."

---

The third volume lay shut at Bracken's elbow, and for a while after the counsel had finished he left it there.

Then he drew it to him, opened it at a place he did not need to look for, laid a strip of plain paper under the first line, and put on his spectacles. He read it flat. Cael had heard him read a hundred pages aloud and never one like this; there was nothing in the voice at all, because Bracken would not let there be.

"Registry code. Evaluation procedures. Chapter fourteen." He did not look up. "*Where an evaluation conducted under standard Arbiter protocol returns no valid classification, the following shall apply in sequence.* One. *Nullification of any enrollment, sponsorship, or standing predicated upon the evaluand's practitioner status.* Two. *Suspension of practitioner status pending review, such review to be initiated by the registry at its discretion.* Three."

He stopped. The next word was one he had read a thousand times and never once had cause to say, and Cael watched him find it heavier to lift than to look at.

"*Asset-restriction.* Section fourteen, part nine. *The registry may restrict, freeze, or hold in abeyance any standing, record, sponsorship, endorsement, travel credential, or institutional relationship attaching to an unclassified person, pending resolution of practitioner status.*"

He took the spectacles off and held them by one arm.

"Clerks have a joke in their first year," he said, "that chapter fourteen is the only page in the code nobody has ever had to stamp. I've stamped every other page in that book. Fees. Transfers. The frame that read Stones high." He looked at the three numbered steps. "Never this one. It wants a frame that reads nothing at all, and frames don't."

He looked up the table at Cael.

"And now one's sitting at my table," he said. "Somebody, a long time ago, sat in a room like this one and imagined you, and wrote down what we should do with you, in three steps, and filed it, and went home to his supper." He put the spectacles back on. "I've always thought of the code as a thing written by careful men. I'd never thought before tonight about what they were careful of."

Withrow had come back down while he read. Cael had not heard her on the stair. She was simply there, behind Bracken's chair, with the brown book under her arm and one hand on the chair's back.

"That's what the code calls it," she said. "Let me tell you what it looks like from the morning after."

She came round the end of the table into the light and spoke to Cael, and to nobody else.

"The morning after it's entered, a practitioner goes down to the ferry as he goes every week, and holds out his pass, and the ferryman has had a letter. A short one, very polite, in the plainest form. The pass is held in abeyance. The ferryman is sorry. The ferryman has a licence of his own and a family to feed." Her voice did not rise at all. "So he goes up to his guild hall, where they've drunk with him for twenty years, and they've had a letter too. It tells them their association with him is *under restriction*, and the master reads those two words and understands that his own name goes on the next letter if he so much as nods to the man in the street. So he goes to the records office to see his own file and argue with it, and the clerk tells him it's sealed. Kept, every page, perfectly safe, and nobody may open it. Not he. Not anyone he pays. Not anyone who might stand up somewhere and say what's in it on his behalf." She paused. "And every house that ever put its name beside his gets a letter. This house would get one. After that, every question anybody asked about him would reach us already answered."

She looked at him steadily.

"It's expulsion that follows you," said Withrow. "You can walk out of a town. You can't walk out of the post. It's waiting at the next town, and the one after, and nobody is obliged ever to begin the review that would stop it." She took the brown book from under her arm and held it in both hands. "Twice in my registry years I put my name to one. The first I'd sign again tomorrow; he had done worse than the registry ever learned. The second had a daughter." She looked at the book and not at them. "She came to my counter every week for a year to ask whether the review had begun. Every week I told her the truth, which was that nobody had begun it and nothing in the code obliged anybody to. And every week she thanked me, and went home." A pause. "I have never forgotten her name. Put that down."

"Noted," said the counsel, and wrote it, and did not write the name, because none had been given.

Withrow stood a moment longer. Then she laid her hand on Bracken's shoulder, briefly, which Cael had never in two years seen her do to anybody, and went back up the stair without another word.

---

Bracken took a card out of his coat.

It was the registry's pocket calendar for the year, printed small on stiff card, every day in its own square. Cael had watched him take it out a hundred times across his counter to settle a question about a fee, and put it back without looking at anybody. He laid it under the lamp now and took up his pen. When the weight on the table had grown too great to look at, Bracken had reached for the one thing on any paper that weighed exactly what it weighed, which was its dates.

He made three marks without a word and turned the card round to Karis.

"The first," she read. "The eighth. The twenty-second."

"Dated the first day of the year," said Bracken. "The morning of convocation. Somebody a week's road from here pressed that wax while the Chancellor stood on her platform telling this house to be what its papers say it is. I don't suppose they knew. I don't suppose it matters." He touched the second mark. "Served this morning. Seven days on a road the post does in three, at a walk, with three horses and a man in the middle who'd been told the pace. It wasn't sent to arrive. It was sent to be watched arriving. I watched it for a full minute from my window, and so did the gate, and so did the coats at the foot of the hill."

"And the twenty-second," said Karis.

"Fourteen from service. The code's plain about that much, for once." He laid the pen across the card. "By close of business on the twenty-second our answer lies on the seat's table in Ostrand. Not the twenty-third. On the twenty-third the seat's clerk writes three words in his docket, *respondent made default*, and in thirteen years I have never seen a seat take those three words back for anybody's lame horse." He looked round the table. "Counsel."

"Minuted," said the counsel.

Bracken put the card away in his coat beside the brass glass.

The hall's clock struck somewhere in the dark at the far end of the long room. Cael had stopped counting bells before midnight and did not know which this was. The lamps had burned down to their last third and gone orange, and the four of them sat in a small close island of light with the tall windows blind on every side. Bracken got up and went down the table with his trimmer, turning each wick a little higher as he passed it, and came back, and sat.

Karis had been writing on small slips of paper.

Cael had watched her do it without knowing what they were for. While Bracken marked his squares she had torn a sheet into six strips and written a single word on each in her finding hand. Now she laid them out on the table in a row, from her own end toward Bracken's, like stepping stones across a stream.

*Executed.* *Valid.* *Integrity.* *Re-evaluate.* *Nothing.* *Fourteen.*

She did not explain them. She touched each in turn and said only its word, and the table, which had spent the whole night on every one of them, needed no more. At the fifth her finger stayed.

"Nobody will argue that one," she said. "Not them, not us. A frame at Denvash had nothing to say about him, and every office since has copied the same nothing into its books." She moved on to the sixth, and lifted her hand.

"Bracken would carry a paper like this," she said. "So would I, if it came across my desk in order. So would every honest clerk between here and the seat, and every one of them would be right to, because there isn't a lie in it anywhere, and no villain either. Look for the trick all night and you'll come up empty, and the end is still the end. It was the end the morning they sealed it."

She looked down at the six slips.

"It's not a question. It's a corridor. Every door in it is real law, and every door opens one way."

Nobody spoke.

The counsel wrote it down, slowly, every word. Bracken's spectacles stayed in his hand. Above them, through the ceiling, a board creaked once, the sound of somebody who has stood still a long time shifting her weight at last.

From the foot of the stair, where none of them had heard her come down again, Withrow said, "The house will answer."

"The house will answer," said Karis. "Because the other choice has its own word in the docket, and the word is *fled*, and this house is named on that paper, and I won't have *fled* written anywhere near it while I live here." She swept the six slips into one hand and tucked them into the back of her notebook. "I'll have bones on it in four days. And when the seat reads it, the seat will go through it with a pen and find nothing to mark. If the end's already fixed, I still want our tracks in the record, every step of them exactly where the code says a step goes."

She drew a clean sheet toward her and wrote a heading and ruled a line under it, and when she spoke again it came out sideways, to the paper, with the pen still moving, as if the words had got ahead of her and she was only following them down.

"There's one more thing in it. I can't place it."

They waited, and the pen stopped.

"That clause is why he's never stood in a station since Denvash," she said, to the heading and not to him. "It's never once asked a frame anything. It keeps him on a floor in front of people, where no Arbiter ever comes." She underlined the heading. "Every paper they've sent up this hill has broken itself on that clause. This one hands it back to us whole and asks for one thing only. A station. The provision doesn't interest them at all. They're trying to move him back inside the instrument."

Nobody answered. She did not seem to want anybody to.

"I don't know why that distinction feels important. Noting it."

She turned to the inside of her notebook's front cover. Over two years Cael had watched a few lines gather there that belonged to no case: the fielding clause, a line about the squad rule, a few words copied from the founding articles of a charter she had promised herself not to open until the cold term. She wrote the new one under them, small, and closed the cover, and was back at her heading before the ink had dried.

Cael copied her sentence into the observation notebook and gave it a question mark in the margin. She would answer it in her own time. She always did.

---

*Go up,* Karis said a little after, without lifting her pen. *There's nothing left tonight that you can count.*

So he went out into the court with his copy under his arm. The fountain was the only sound on the hill. On his way across he looked, as he always looked, at the assessment wing, and at the far end of its long front, on the ground floor, behind the counter's window, one lamp was burning. At that hour, in a wing where nobody had business before the third bell. He stood in the court and looked at it for a moment, and did not let himself guess what the man at the copying table was making. Whatever it was, it would be made properly, and it would arrive without a note.

The residence front was dark except for one window, and he went up the stair toward it.

It was the common room, and it was not empty.

Brom was at the table. There was a pot of tea on the hob of the banked hearth, and from the look of the hearth he had been keeping it hot for hours, feeding the embers a little whenever they sank. There were cups on the table. Four, set out in a row with their handles all turned the same way, and the fourth stood in front of the empty chair at the table's end, where Karis always sat because the lamp fell best there.

Lira was in the window seat with her knees drawn up and the red drill-book open against them, and she had not turned a page in a long while.

They looked at him when he came in. Neither of them asked anything. He understood, standing in the doorway with the copy under his arm, that they had been sitting up for hours, and that it was not for him.

Karis came up a little after. He heard her step on the stair and moved out of the doorway, and she stopped on the threshold with ink to the second knuckle of both hands and her notebook against her chest.

He watched her count the room. She did it all at once, as she counted anything: a lamp lit at an hour no lamp should be, a fire kept in that should have been let die, two people awake with no work to do, a drill-book open and unread, four cups with their handles turned. He watched her reach the fourth cup, in front of her own chair, and stop.

She said nothing about it. She went to the table and held the cup while Brom filled it, and drank half of it standing.

"Four days," she told the room, and finished the cup, and set it back in front of her chair.

She went to the stair. On the first step she stopped with one hand on the rail, and did not turn round.

"Thank you for the tea," she said to the stairwell, and went up, and they heard her door.

Brom looked at the empty cup in front of her chair.

"She counted the cups," he said.

"She counted us," said Lira, and put the drill-book face down on the window seat.

"Well," she said. "Tell us."

He sat in Karis's chair because it was nearest, and Brom pushed a cup across to him, and he told them.

All of it had taken nine hours, and he did not give them all of it. He gave them what had moved the table. The frame at the Ostrand station that read every Stone too high, and what the integrity sections were for. The honest man's three sentences, quoted whole and made a warrant. The ferryman's daughter whose initials he had written down. Chapter fourteen in Bracken's voice. The ferry pass, and the guild hall, and the daughter at Withrow's counter every week for a year. Three marks on a calendar card. Six slips of paper laid out like stepping stones.

When he came to the corridor he said it in Karis's words, every one of them.

Brom had been looking into his cup. He went on looking into it for a while after Cael stopped, the way he looked at a weight before deciding where to take hold. Then he said one word to the cup, quietly.

"Corridor."

Cael told them the rest: the answer anyway, the four days, and last the thing Karis had said sideways with her pen still moving, that the paper did not want the clause at all. It wanted him back in front of a frame.

The embers ticked on the hearth. Nobody said anything for a while.

"Then what's the answer for?" said Brom at last. "If the doors only open one way."

"Because the text has held every time." Cael turned the cup in his hands. "Every time somebody on that side failed, a warden who wanted me gone, a provost who was frightened, a tournament that didn't want me on its roster, the paper caught me when they dropped me. Karis is going to read this paper the way she read those. Somewhere in it there's a place where they read their own text wrong."

Lira had not moved from the window seat. She was looking at the dark glass, not at him, and when she spoke she said it carefully, the way she set one foot on a floor she did not trust before she put her weight on it.

"And this time?"

He waited.

"Every time you've told me that, it's been people dropping you and the paper catching you." She turned her head. "What if the people didn't fail this time?"

He opened his mouth and found the answer already there, whole and certain.

"Then there's a place where they read it wrong," he said. "There always is. Karis will find it."

Lira looked at him for a long moment and did not argue. Then she swung her feet down off the window seat, took her drill-book, and went up the stair without a word.

Brom gathered the four cups onto the tray with their handles turned the same way, and carried the tray to the hob, and banked the fire properly at last. At the door he stopped with his hand on the frame, not looking at Cael.

"Corridor," he said, once more, to nobody, and went up.

---

Cael did not light his lamp. The records hall's long east front was still lit from end to end across the court, and that was light enough.

He sat on the sill with Hesk's book on his knee and Hesk's steel pen in his hand, and laid his copy of the instrument open beside him at its first page. The entry was finished before the pen touched paper. He wrote it down the way you copy a thing out fair.

*I can work within the system. I have the receipts: a hearing, a provision, a stamp renewed four times, a tournament their own clause put me in. The system has text, and text has held me every time the people failed. This is more text. We'll read it better than they do. We always have.*

He did not change a word. Every clause had been earned on a floor or in a hearing room or across a counter, with people he loved watching. He sanded it and gave it a full minute by Hesk's clock. It looked like the truth, because, as far as he could see from that window, it was.

Across the court, the records hall began to go dark.

He knew its lamps. Six on the long table, two on the counter, one over the door of the press. The counter's pair went first, together. Then, after a pause, the table's, two at a time, from the far end toward the head, somebody walking back up the room with a trimmer and in no hurry about it. Cael knew the pace. The last pair at the table's head went, and for a moment there was only the one lamp over the press, alone at the end of the long front.

Then that one went too.

The court was dark, and the fountain went on in its basin below. Beside him on the sill the copy lay where he had put it, first page face up to the night, *Given under the Compact's hand* at its foot in type no larger than the rest. He did not close it. He left it there, open, waiting for better light.

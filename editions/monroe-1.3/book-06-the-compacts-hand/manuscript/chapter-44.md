# Chapter 44 — Conceded

The coach went down the bluff road on the Fifth-day behind Withrow's carriage, in a fine cold rain, and the town watched it go by.

Cael sat up straight. He had sat up straight in that coach five times now, with his back to the horses and his good coat buttoned, and it had become a thing his spine did by itself when the wheels came off the last switchback onto the cobbles. Karis sat opposite with the document wallet on her knees and a board across the wallet. Beside her Bracken held the house's seal in its box on his lap, with both hands, as another man might have held a hat.

At the foot of the road the two coats stood under the chandler's eaves. They looked at the carriage, and at the coach, and at the wallet on Karis's knees through the wet glass. One of them took out a pencil.

"Sixth sitting," said Bracken, to nobody. "I said eleven. I should like it noted that I now say fourteen, and that I said so in the second month, and was thought gloomy."

"Nobody thought you gloomy," said Karis, to the board.

"The senior clerk did. He has since apologised."

The bridge was open, the river running brown and quick a long way under its second ring, and they went up the market street at a walk. There were perhaps a dozen people on the four steps of the wool exchange this morning, where once there had been thirty, and only two of them had folding boards. A merits sitting, the house's counsel had said, was a thing correspondents came to on the first day and the last. In between, it was paper, and paper did not sell.

The coachman took them round by the stable arch, as he always did, and set the brake under the wall.

The young officer at the side door had got over his cold at last, and he had the log open on its board, and the first name already written, in the hand of a man who had written it so often that he no longer saw it. Cael read it upside down as he went past, as he had read it at every sitting since the fortieth day. *Caelen Hesk-ward.* And in the second column, under *standing*: *the enrollee of record, unclassified.*

The officer wrote Karis's line under it without being told how. *Advocate of record for the respondent, under the hearing provision.* She had been in his book under that title since the fourth sitting, and he had stopped looking up to check the spelling.

The house's counsel was waiting inside, small and grey and straight-shouldered, with her bag in one hand and her steel rule in the other.

"He's going to give things away this morning," she said, by way of greeting. "I had it from his clerk's face at the inn. Don't thank him for any of it." She looked at Cael, from his boots up. "And you. Whatever he gives, you look as if you'd expected it since breakfast."

"I have," said Cael.

She considered that, and something that was not quite a smile came and went at the corner of her mouth.

"Yes," she said. "I suppose you have." And she led them into the long room.

---

Nothing in it had changed. He had half thought something would, after the recess, after Ostrand and the borrowed office four streets back, as he had half thought the hill would look different on the morning after the case. But the wool exchange smelled of lanolin and damp coats, as it always had, and the forty benches ran back to the doors, a quarter full, with lot seventeen cut into the floor under his left boot. On the platform the hood sat in its place behind the long table, grey and still, and below it the five chairs of the evaluation seat stood in their row with their covers off and five people in them. Four were district evaluators whose names he had heard read once and had written down; and in the middle, with a file open on her knee at its usual angle, sat Ilsev, reading.

At the records table the senior chair had the minute, and Havel had the times and the receipts. Havel did not look up; Cael had learned by now that the man would not, and no longer waited for it.

He sat, and put his hands on the house's table, one on each side of the place where his notebook would have gone if he had brought it into this room, which he never had.

He knew what was in the case on Karis's shelf, and that this room ran on it. The frame they meant to stand him in was the thing on the leaf, appended to the reading and not the reading, and every person on that platform would go home tonight not knowing it, and so would Jent, perhaps, and so would the clerk. He was the only one in the long room, besides Karis at his elbow, who knew what the argument was about.

His face did nothing. He had practised that at a counter, in a queue, behind a girl with a new badge.

"In the matter of Caelen Hesk-ward," said the seat's clerk. "Upon the merits. The sixth sitting."

Jent stood.

He stood as he always did, without hurry, with his spectacles hanging on their black cord and his empty hands at his sides, a man of middle height in a brown coat whose cuffs had been mended in a slightly different brown. His travelling inkwell sat on the petitioner's table with its lid screwed down. There was a single folder beside it, and he did not open the folder.

"If the seat pleases," he said, in his pleasant carrying voice, "the petitioner would save it some time."

The hood did not move.

"The respondent filed, in the week before the recess, a supplemental table of the renewals of this enrollment, with the day of each sitting and the day of each seal set side by side. I have read it. I read it twice, on a boat." He turned his head a little toward the house's table, toward Karis, and inclined it. "It is correct. The petitioner accepts it as filed, in every line, and adopts it."

At Cael's left the counsel's pen moved. She had her privileged minute open in front of her, and across the top of the page, in her small capitals, written before any of them sat down, stood three headings. *RENEWALS. NINE DAYS. EVALUATOR.* She drew a line through the first.

"It follows," said Jent, "that the second schedule to the petition is wrong in one particular. It suggests, at its ninth line, that the first renewal was sealed out of its order. It was not. It was sealed nine days after the panel sat, because an officer of an inspecting delegation held the registry's copy for his countersignature, as he was entitled to do, and the respondent's advocate has shown the seat the very leaf." He took one sheet from the folder after all, and held it out to the clerk. "The petitioner withdraws that line, and tenders the schedule again without it. I should not like the seat to rule upon a schedule with a mistake in it. Least of all one of mine."

There was a small sound along the front benches. The counsel drew her second line.

The clerk carried the sheet to the records table, and Havel took it, and laid it beside another leaf from his own pile, and bent over the two of them.

"And lastly," said Jent. "A great deal was said at the preliminary about the evaluator of record at Halcenvane. I said some of it. I should like it in this minute, in plain words, that the petitioner accepts that officer as competent in every particular, accepts his panels as properly held, and accepts every figure he has signed as honestly come by." He let his hands fall. "The petition relies upon none of these matters. It never has. I mention them only so that nobody is put to the trouble of proving what I do not dispute."

The third line. The counsel turned her steel rule a quarter turn on the table, and it ticked.

Three points, in something under ten minutes. Cael counted them, because he could not help it, and waited to feel what he had felt at the preliminary when the first three headings went down, the hard small lift under the ribs of a thing won.

It did not come.

They had spent four days on that table of renewals, in the annex, at the start of the year. He had found the nine days himself, in his own old Log, and Karis had let him find them. And a man in a mended coat had just stood up before a hood and said that every line of it was right, and thrown away a piece of his own petition rather than leave an error standing.

It was as clean a win as anything in the whole proceeding, and it weighed nothing, because Jent had not needed the ground he was giving. He was a man clearing a table before a meal. Every concession left the petition smaller and harder, and nearer the single thing it had always been: a question for the seat's discretion, about a frame.

*None, Advocate.* He had said so in the fourth sitting, in so many words, and Cael had written it down and had gone on bringing receipts.

"The supplemental submission of the respondent upon the renewals," said the clerk. "Eleven leaves. Adopted by the petitioner. Received and entered."

Then the hood spoke.

It did not speak often, and when it did the long room went still to hear it, even now, even on a day of paper. It had the same unhurried voice it had used at the preliminary, a voice with no grade in it.

"The seat notes the petitioner's acceptance of the evaluator of record. It will nonetheless have his notes before it. The respondent's house is ordered to produce that officer's notes upon the enrollee for the current academic year, entire, at the eighth sitting. So ordered."

"So ordered," said the clerk, and wrote.

Karis had it down before he had finished. Cael saw her write *Gault. Notes, entire. Eighth.* on the board in her wallet, and under it, after a second, *ordinary*.

It was. A production order was the most ordinary paper a seat could make. Bracken would carry it up the hill tonight in his coat, and Gault would read it in his office behind wing three with his instrument case very straight on the bench beside him, and would say that he had written it down. There was nothing in Gault's notes that could hurt anybody. There was a year of figures in them that breathed and never settled and never went past a red line.

The seat rose.

---

Jent overtook them on the steps.

The rain had thinned to a mist, and the market street ran shining down toward the river with the carts standing in it. Withrow had gone ahead to her carriage and bowed to nobody, and Bracken was at the coach door with the seal. Jent came down the four steps behind the house's party with his one folder under his arm, and at the bottom he stopped beside Karis and waited until she had to look at him.

"Advocate," he said. "Your table was better than ours. I said so in there, but a minute is a dry place to say a thing. I wanted to say it on a step."

Karis had the wallet against her chest.

"You adopted it," she said. "You didn't have to."

"No. I could have let you prove it, line by line, across two sittings. You would have. It would have been a pleasure to watch." He put his spectacles on to look at the sky, and took them off again. "But it would still have been right at the end of it, and I'd have known that from the start. I dislike sitting through an argument whose answer I already hold. It seems a discourtesy to the person making it."

He nodded to her, and to Cael, with exactly the same nod, and went away down the market street with his folder under his arm. He did not look like a man who had given up three points.

Cael did not send the read after him; he had sent it five times and found nobody in the coat.

In the coach, going up, Karis sat with the wallet shut on her knees and looked out at the rain for the length of the bridge.

"We won three," she said.

"I know."

"Say it as if it mattered." She did not turn her head. "You said you'd come to every one. I said I'd write it as if it were ground. I can't do my half if you sit there looking the way you looked when the hood rose." Her pale fingers tightened on the wallet. "I saw you. You looked as if somebody had handed you a receipt for something you never bought."

He thought about that. It was fair; it was, in fact, exactly what it had felt like.

"We won three," he said, and made himself hear it as he said it. The nine days. Four days in the annex, and his own old Log, and her pencil stopping when he found it. "The table's in the minute now as the petitioner's own. Nobody can ever go behind it. Whatever they rule, they rule knowing every renewal was good."

"Yes," said Karis.

"That's not nothing."

"No." She let out a breath. "It isn't. Thank you." And she opened the wallet again, on her knees, and began to draft the covering leaf for Gault's notes while the coach climbed.

Bracken, in the corner, had shut his eyes. He opened one of them.

"Fourteen," he said.

---

Havel had the two leaves under his hands for a little less than four minutes.

It was not long, but it was longer than he had held any single thing at that table since the year began, and he was aware of every one of the minutes, because he was the man who wrote down the times.

The work was simple: a party had withdrawn a line from a schedule and tendered the schedule again without it. The junior chair's part was to lay the new leaf beside the old and see that nothing had changed between them but the line withdrawn; to receipt the new one, and mark the old one *superseded* in the corner, with the day and the sitting; and to pass both to the senior chair for the minute.

He laid them side by side on the blotter and went down them with the point of a dry pen, a line at a time, as the manual said.

It was the petition's second schedule. He had receipted it once already, on the fortieth day, in a stack of nineteen leaves, and had not read it then, because a junior chair receipted leaves and did not read them. Today he had to. The schedule was drawn in the old grammar that declarations used, a list of what the registry held to be so about the person the petition named, one thing to a line.

The ninth line was gone from the new leaf, as tendered. He checked the eighth against the eighth and the tenth against what was now the ninth.

The fourteenth line, on both leaves, was a designation with its title written out in full.

He knew the title. He had first seen its mark on a pump step, in a town a long way west of here, beside a form of four questions, when the young man at the far table had still been a boy. He had asked about it then, in the proper way, and had been told once, in a room with the door shut, that it was not his clearance. He had written that down, on a sheet of his own, and it lay in his kit tonight between the second manual and the third with five others.

The point of the dry pen rested under the fourteenth line on the old leaf, and then under the same line on the new. The words were the same. The spelling was the same. A capital that the copyist had made a little too tall on the first was a little too tall on the second, because the second had been copied from it by a careful hand.

That was what he was asked. Whether the two agreed. They agreed.

He moved the pen down to the fifteenth.

He did not look across the room. He had looked at that boy before, on the pump step and at Greyvane and on the sprung oak at the bluff, and he remembered every one of the looks, and he would not give himself another. He had decided that long ago, and the deciding had held through eleven weeks of Fifth-days in the same long room, forty feet from the house's table. It held this morning. He went down both leaves to the foot and found them alike in everything but the one line. Then he wrote *superseded* in the corner of the first, in the plain hand he kept for corners, with *sixth sitting* under it, and entered the second in the receipt book against the time.

His right hand ached at the web of the thumb, and he opened and closed it once, under the table.

"Junior," said the senior chair, without turning his head, and Havel passed the leaves across.

The petitioner was speaking again by then, about an evaluator. Havel wrote the time.

---

He logged a great deal that fortnight.

The merits were heavier than the first sittings had been, though there was not more talk; there was less. But every sitting put paper on his table, and all of it had to be receipted and numbered and laid against the lists. At the sixth sitting, the respondent's supplemental upon the renewals: eleven leaves, a table, a certified copy of a minute. At the seventh, a week later, the petitioner's acceptance of the tournament office's record, in one leaf; the respondent's second supplemental, upon the manner in which that record had been kept, in nineteen; and a correction slip, signed by the registrar of the house, altering the date on one exhibit by a single day, with the reason given.

He liked the correction slip, and was a little ashamed of how much. It was a small square of the house's paper, and it said that the registrar had found, on reading his own exhibit list a fourth time, that he had written the fifth of a month for the sixth, and begged leave to amend. Nobody would ever have caught it, and it could not have mattered to anything. The man had found it himself, and walked it down a hill.

Havel numbered it and pasted it in beside the line it corrected.

The house's exhibits stood at a hundred and seventy-nine by the end of the seventh sitting. He had receipted every one, and in eleven weeks he had not found among them a leaf out of order, a seal smudged past reading, or a count that did not come to what its contents leaf said it came to. He had sat junior to a good many proceedings, and had never sat junior to a record like it.

He thought the petitioner knew that. The petitioner said so, at every sitting, to the hood, in a voice that seemed to mean it, and then conceded another piece of ground; and the proceeding went on at the same pace as before, toward the same question. Havel wrote the times, for it was not his place to notice where a proceeding was going. It was his place to see that when it got there, every leaf it had walked over could be found again.

At the inn by the bridge, on the night of the seventh sitting, he ate his supper in the long room downstairs among the carters, because the fire was better there, and then went up.

The river was quiet, for it had dropped since the flood weeks, and from his window the middle pier stood dry to its second ring, and the bridge-ward's lantern showed a plain black water going by under it with hardly a sound. He lit his own lamp and set the kit on the table and unbuckled it.

He took out the six sheets.

He had not had them out since the night he wrote the last. He laid them down in their order, left to right, and read them through twice, slowly, as the manual told a records officer to read anything he meant to rely on.

A mark in a boy's file with no office behind it. That was the first, and the oldest, and the hand it was written in was younger than his hand was now. Three more lay between, each in its year. The fifth was a sentence on a senior desk at Norhold, read upside down, shutting a door the first had found open. The sixth was a seal that stood in no registry of seals.

Six things the registers could not answer. He had written each of them down on the day he found it, as it had happened, and nothing of what he made of it.

He sat and looked at them.

He had thought, this morning a week ago, with the dry pen resting under a fourteenth line, that there might be a seventh. He could admit that here. What he had hoped, for the space of perhaps a breath, was a simple thing: that a designation written out under seal before a seat would have to name its office.

The schedule did not give one. It gave the title, in a clerk's fair copy, and no office after it, on the old leaf or the new. The petition had only set down that the mark was there. He had known that since the pump step, and had written it on the first sheet, and a thing written once did not want writing again.

Everything else about the line, the registers answered perfectly. Second schedule, fourteenth line, received on the fortieth day, re-tendered on the hundred and seventeenth, both leaves alike. There was nothing there to write.

And a sheet for what he felt, sitting forty feet from that table every Fifth-day with a pen in his hand, was a thing he had never kept and did not know how to begin.

He did not take a clean sheet from the kit. He looked at the place in it where the clean sheets were, and he left them there.

He thought instead of the words on his rotation order. *The seat's Fifth-days, until relieved.* He had read them in the first week as he read any order, for what they required of him. He read them differently tonight, in his head, with the river going by. A man could be relieved because the work was done. He could also ask.

He had never asked to be moved off anything in his life. He did not ask now. He only noticed, as he might have noticed a door in a wall he had walked past for years, that the order had the word in it.

He put the six back between the second manual and the third, the same way round, and buckled the kit.

---

Withrow sent for him on the Second-day evening, by the porter's boy, and when he came to her door she was writing.

She did not stop, but pointed with her left hand at the chair across the desk, and he sat in it, and she went on to the end of her line and the end of her leaf. She signed it with her whole name, and then she blotted it and laid it face down on a pile at her right hand.

There was a pile at her left hand too. It was taller.

"Thirty-four," said Withrow. "You like counting, so I'll give you the number before you start on it yourself. Thirty-four letters from the heads of other chartered houses, since the preliminary. They have come in ones and twos. Bracken puts them on my desk in the morning with the seals uppermost, so that I can see whose they are before I decide whether to have breakfast."

She was a tall woman, and she sat very upright, and tonight for the first time since he had known her she looked as if she had been sitting upright for a long while. The lamp was turned low. The charter hung in its frame on the wall behind her with its locked case beneath.

"Eleven of them are kind," she said. "I answer those last, because they're easy and I save them. Nineteen want to know what the house will do if the seat goes against it. That is a fair question from people whose own charters are written in the same words as mine." She touched the taller pile. "And four have advised me, very civilly, to let you go. Not expel. Nobody has written that word. *Release.* *Come to an accommodation.* One of them wants me to *spare the house a ruling*." Her voice was quite even. "Their reasoning is not foolish. If this seat rules that those sections can be turned upon a person for what he is, that ruling stands in the registry's books for every house on the continent. If I let you go before it rules, there is no ruling. One boy leaves one hill, and thirty-four charters sleep sound. The man who put it most plainly has been my friend for twenty years. He wrote it in his own hand, and it cost him something to write."

"What did you answer?"

"That the thing he's afraid of is done the day a house hands over an enrollee to avoid it. After that, nobody needs a ruling. They need only file, and wait for us to count the cost. I put it more gently than that. He's a good man, and frightened, and I have been both."

The fire ticked. He looked at the two piles, and at her right hand, which had ink on the second finger to the knuckle.

"You're answering them yourself," he said. "All of them."

"Every one."

"Bracken has clerks."

"He has. Excellent ones, and they've offered." She capped the inkwell. "A clerk's answer is the house's answer. These people did not write to the house. They wrote to a woman they've sat beside at convenings since before you were enrolled anywhere, to ask whether she has lost her judgment. That is not a question I can have answered for me in a good round hand. They would know it for what it was."

She looked at him then, directly, as she had looked at him across this desk with a charter folder open between them.

"I didn't send for you to be thanked, and I'll be annoyed if you try. I sent for you because you keep a column. I've seen it. Four names at the head." She did not say how she had seen it, and he did not ask. "I would rather you had the right figure under mine than a guess. Thirty-four letters, and thirty-four answers by the end of the week, and not one of them says I'm sorry I did it."

For a moment he could not speak.

He knew a thing she did not. He knew that there was a door, one day wide, that would let him walk out of her house after a ruling and leave nothing in her folder but an entry in an old enrollment book. He knew that he was sitting through every Fifth-day in a room he no longer believed in, in part so that the door would still be there when it was wanted, and her charter clean on the other side of it. He could not tell her any of that, or why he had stopped believing in the room.

She was answering thirty-four letters by hand for a boy who was keeping the largest thing he knew from her. It was not a lie. It sat in the room exactly like one.

"I'll write it down right," he said.

"I know you will. That's why I told you." She drew the next letter from the taller pile and turned it to the lamp, and he saw the seal on it, blue-grey, the lake house's. "Go to bed. You've a floor the day after tomorrow, and I'm told you're to look ordinary on it."

At the door he turned.

"Chancellor. The four. Did any of them say what they'd have done? In your chair, on the eighth day?"

Withrow did not look up from the lake house's letter.

"No," she said. "It's the one thing none of the four thought to put in."

# Chapter 40 — The Instrument

Cael said it on the Third-day night, in the reading room, before he could talk himself out of it.

"There's one man outside this room who's owed the case."

They were all there. They had come up by their three ways after supper as they did every evening now, without needing a floor schedule to tell them when. Karis had put the enrollment book back on Bracken's counter on the First-day before the second bell, as promised, and Bracken had weighed it on his palm and written it in. The case stood on its shelf between the grey boxes, and the lamp burned in the window.

They all knew who.

"He warned us," said Cael. "At Norhold, in a room that belonged to neither office, with forty clocks in it and nothing written down by either side. He told us to stop competing in public, and why, and he didn't have to. It cost him something to say it, and he said it anyway, and then he went back and was set aside for saying the same thing at the convening." He made himself go slowly. "And he's still the one evaluator in the Compact they need a sentence from. When the seat comes to re-evaluate me, somebody has to certify the protocol, and it'll be him they ask. He's going to be asked to sign the delivery, and he doesn't know what's being delivered."

"Seln paid twelve years for that case," Cael said, "and we've had it six nights. We can't file it, and we can't publish it, and we can't use it. But we can let one honest man inside the thing see what the thing is, before they hand him the pen." He looked at the cloth. "I think we owe him that, and I think it's the same debt as the stair. I don't know how to tell the difference."

"Then say the worst of it," said Lira, "all of us, in turn, before anybody puts a hand up." She looked round the table. "That's how we did the reach on the Second-day before the road. Everybody says the worst thing they can think of, out loud, so nobody's carrying one they didn't say."

Brom went first, because he was nearest the kettle and had been looking at it.

"He takes it up the stair the morning after," he said, "because that's what an honest man does with a paper he's been shown. He doesn't keep it in a drawer. He sends it to the office it concerns, with a covering note in his best hand, the way that clerk in the river town did." He did not look at the case. "And the stair eats it. And somebody at the top of the stair reads the covering note and asks where an Archmarshal saw forty copied orders in a surveyor's case, and the answer's a wing at Halcenvane. And Seln's gone by the end of the month, and none of us ever finds out where."

He sat back.

Karis went second.

"He verifies it," she said. "He's an evaluator; it's the whole of what he is. He doesn't believe a thing because it's on a table; he measures it. So he goes home and pulls a frame reading, or a station sheet, or a fresh reading on one of the forty, to see whether the case is true." She turned her pale fingers over on the cloth. "And the frame's theirs, and the station's theirs, and the reading runs through the function. Every instrument he could check it with comes back and says the case is wrong. And he believes his instruments, because he's believed them for forty years. And somewhere it gets minuted that a forgery was offered to the Archmarshal by persons connected with the respondent at Halcenvane."

She folded her hands.

Lira went third.

"He believes it," she said, "all of it, at once, the way you'd want him to, and it breaks him." She was looking at the case. "He's been measuring true for forty years because he thought true was the point. We show him the point was never true. And he goes home, and sits at his desk, and does nothing, because there's nothing he can do. He signs what he's given, or he doesn't, and either way he's a broken man signing things." She lifted her chin. "And we'd have done that to him. We'd have spent the one good man in the whole building for a bad night's sleep."

Seln went fourth. He did not take long.

"He is already theirs," he said. "Norhold was a knock, dressed as a kindness. A lent room, no paper, a warning that made you trust him. That is the third grade done well. And the case goes to the people it was taken from, by the hand of the one man you would have given it to."

He put his hands back flat on the table.

Cael went last, because he had proposed it.

"He reads it," he said, "and he asks about me. He's an evaluator, and I'm the one subject in the Compact he's never been able to read, and he's sitting across a table from the case and from me. He asks the natural question: why is the boy's file marked, what is he, how did the function fail on him." He made himself say the rest. "And somebody in the room answers, not because they mean to, but because it's the natural thing, and he asks well, and he's kind about it, and it's late."

Karis looked at him for a long moment.

"Then that's the rule," she said. "That's the one that matters. The rest are risks, and we can weigh them, but that one's a rule." She put both hands on the table. "He gets the case. Nothing about Cael. Say it, everybody, out loud. I want to hear it in every voice in this room, so that whoever's tired at the end of an afternoon in a strange room hears their own voice saying it."

"He gets the case," said Brom. "Nothing about Cael."

"He gets the case," said Lira. "Nothing about Cael."

Karis said it again, the third time, the same words.

"He gets the case," said Cael. "Nothing about me."

Seln was last. He sat with his hands flat on the cloth and looked at the shelf, at the dark leather between the grey boxes, for so long that Brom's kettle began to tick.

"He gets the case," said Seln. "Nothing about the boy."

"Hands," said Lira.

Brom's went up first, the right, because the left still did not like going over his shoulder. Then Karis's, with the pale fingers, and Lira's, and Cael's. Four hands stood up in the circle of the shaded lamp.

Seln's stayed flat on the table.

Nobody hurried him. They held their hands up and waited. It was his case and his twelve years and his name on a roll somewhere, and if his hand did not go up, then theirs would come down, and none of them would ever mention it again. Cael did not know which way it would go. He could have borne either, and that was new.

Seln lifted his hand off the cloth. He held it there a moment, a hand's breadth above the table, as if weighing it. Then he raised it.

"Five," said Lira.

---

Seln found the way in two days, and nobody asked him how until he had found it.

The wing posted station closures every week on a board behind the counter, because an enrollee could not be sent to a station for a frame reading on a day the station was shut. Nobody ever read the closures but the desk clerk, and the desk clerk only to answer a question. On the Fourth-day Seln read them, and there it was, in a district clerk's printed form: the river stations shut in turn for the Archmarshal's yearly inspection, which he walked himself, as he had walked it every year for a decade. The Ostrand station was down for the coming Seventh-day.

"He'll lie in Ostrand the night before," said Seln. "The inspection starts at the first bell. Officers of the Compact who are not of the seat lie at the inn on the wharf, the long low one where the seat's clerks eat in sitting weeks. They have done for as long as I have been in the trade."

"And then?" said Karis.

"And then a note." Seln took a small square of plain paper from his waistcoat pocket, the kind the wing used for marking places in ledgers, and laid it on the cloth, already written. "No name, no seal, no hand anybody would know. An address and an hour, and one line."

Cael leaned over and read it.

*Four streets back from the seat, on the wharf side. The tall house with the brass plate. The third floor. The second bell after noon. Neither office's. Nothing written.*

"He'll know it," said Seln. "It's his own note to you at Norhold, cut down to four words. Nobody saw that note but the people at the long table that night, and he knows that, and he'll know the line couldn't have come from anyone else."

"And the room?" said Brom.

"Is borrowed." Seln very nearly smiled. "A records administrator's, on the third floor of a leasehold building four streets back from the registry seat. He is on circuit in the hill stations for six weeks. His landlord's keeper lends the room by the morning or the afternoon to anybody with a reason and a deposit, and writes the reason in a book, and does not read it twice. My reason is a reading." He looked at the note. "I have used that kind of room before, but never that one."

"And if he doesn't come?"

"Then we go home on a Seventh-day," said Seln, "and nothing in the world is different, and we keep the case."

He took the note down the bluff road himself on the Fifth-day, in the house's ordinary post, with a dozen of the wing's letters, and left it at the inn on the wharf with the inn's boy in an envelope addressed in a copying hand to *the officer of the river inspection*. The coats at the foot of the road saw a clerk with the post. They had seen him with the post every week for two years.

---

They went down on the Seventh-day by five ways, because it was a Seventh-day and the whole hill went down to Ostrand on a Seventh-day by any way it liked.

Lira went with the program's carriers on the market cart at the first bell, because Rooke's program bought its tape and chalk at the chandler's by the bridge and somebody had to carry it, and she was not allowed to carry anything heavier. Brom walked, by the long road round the bluff, which took an hour and was the only exercise his shoulder would let him take. Karis took the house's errand to the fee office with a letter of Bracken's in her wallet. Seln went with the post. Cael went last, alone, by the ferry, because it was the way he always went when he went at all. He sat on the bench by the rail and let the coats at the landing look at him as they had looked at him for two years, and nobody wrote down anything but the hour.

The lane was narrow and steep, with a gutter down the middle and the backs of the warehouses on one side and a row of tall old houses on the other, let out floor by floor to clerks and factors and men who did a trade in paper. The building was the fourth along, with a door with a brass plate beside it listing its tenants, and a keeper's window, and a stair. Seln was at the keeper's window when Cael came in, writing his reason in the book, and did not look round.

The stair went up round a narrow well. On the second landing there was a clock on the wall, the building's own, an old round-faced thing in a box. Cael passed it on the way up and it said a quarter to the second bell.

It was a records administrator's room on the third floor of a leasehold building four streets back from the registry seat, with nothing of anyone's in it.

Cael stood in the doorway and looked at it in Seln's order before he knew he was doing it.

What the room had most of was absence. Along one wall the shelves still kept the outlines of the man's ledgers in the dust, clean oblongs of bare wood where his work had stood until a week or two ago, and above them on the plaster three paler squares showed where he had hung whatever he liked to look at. He had taken all of it on circuit with him, or locked it away, and left the shapes. What remained belonged to the building: a long scrubbed table, six chairs carried up from some store below and no two of them alike, an iron stove gone cold in the corner, and a brown door with a row of empty hooks on its back. The one tall window had small panes. Through them, across a lightwell and the wet slates of the lane, a green cupola stood four streets off, over the wool exchange where the seat sat.

"It's a good room," said Lira, from the chair by the stove. She had been there half an hour. "I've been counting, and there's nothing in it to count."

Brom had the chair with its back to the wall. Karis had the chair at the table's end, with the case on the floor at her feet, its lock still shut; Seln had carried it down inside the post satchel, between the wing's letters, and given it to her in the keeper's hall without a word. Seln came up last and locked the door behind him and gave the key to nobody. He did not sit. He went to the window and stood a little to one side of it, where the frame hid him from the lane, and looked down.

They waited.

Nobody talked. There was nothing to say that had not been said in the reading room, and nobody wanted to say anything in a room that was not theirs. The building made its sounds round them. Somebody on the floor below dropped a weight on boards. On the floor above, somebody was singing at his work. A cart went down the lane with its brake on, scraping. Through the open door of the room, across the landing, Cael could hear the clock in its box on the second landing below, ticking, slow and heavy, a building's clock that had nothing to do with any of them.

"Front stair," said Seln.

He said it quietly, to the glass. Then he stepped back from the window.

"He came in by the front, not the yard. He walked up the lane from the wharf end, on the gutter side, in grey, with nothing in his hands, and he stopped and read the brass plate by the door, and then he went in past the keeper's window, where the keeper could see him." Seln looked at the door. "By the clock on the landing, eleven minutes early."

They listened.

The steps came up the stair, single steps, not hurried, the whole first flight and then the second without a pause on the landing. They went past the clock and came up the third flight, and did not go on to the fourth, where the singing was. They stopped outside the door.

He knocked once.

Seln unlocked the door and opened it and stood aside.

---

Vastin stood on the landing in his plain grey coat with his hands empty at his sides, and did not come in at once. He looked at the room the way Seln had looked at the reading room on the birthday, the door and the window and the bare table and the shapes on the shelves, and then the five of them, one at a time. He did not let his eyes stay on any one of them longer than on the others, not on Cael, and not on the man who had opened the door to him.

Then he came in, and Seln shut the door and locked it.

"I was told nothing would be written," said Vastin.

"Nothing will be," said Karis.

He nodded. He bowed, not to any of them, but to the room, a short stiff bow from the waist of the kind evaluators gave a sitting before they took a chair. Then he took the chair across the table from Karis, the one with its back to the window, and sat down with his hands on his knees.

Karis lifted the case onto the table and turned it to face him.

Seln came to the table's other end and took the brass key from his waistcoat and opened the lock with its dry sound, and lifted the lid, and stepped back. He did not lay anything out this time. Karis did, in the order it had come out of the case on the first night, with the leaf in its sleeve last and a hand's width apart. When she had finished she sat down again in her chair and folded her hands.

Nobody said anything about anything.

Vastin read.

His left hand was the only part of him that moved for a long while. It lay on the wood beside the rows, and now and then, without his seeming to know it, it opened and closed twice, slowly, as a man tries a hand that has begun to dislike mornings. The rest of him read. A pile grew at his right elbow, face down, a pair at a time, each one turned over only when he had been through the station's record, the order clipped to it and the narrow slip pinned behind, in that order and never another.

Cael counted the pairs as they went face down. He could not help it.

At the twenty-third, Vastin found the letter. He unfolded the father's sheet, and read it, and unfolded the clerk's four lines inside it, and read those. Then he read the father's again from the top. He folded them both along their old folds, exactly as Lira had folded them, and slid them back under the clip, and laid the pair face down on the others. He sat with his hand on top of the face-down stack. Then he took the twenty-fourth.

He read all forty. He untied the pink tape, and read the twenty-three reports, and at the first one that carried *Continue observation* under its stamp he stopped. He stopped there for longer than he had stopped for the letter. He held the sheet a little nearer the window's light, and read the evaluator's line under the clerk's stamp twice, and put it down very carefully on the others. He went on.

He read the memoranda from the bottom, as Karis had, without being told. He read the seals. At the third issue he took out a small lens on a cord from inside his coat and looked at the seal through it, and at the seventh, and at the nineteenth, and put the lens away.

He came to the leaf last.

Karis took the cotton gloves out of her coat and held them out across the table without a word, and he looked at them, and at her, and took them, and put them on finger by finger as she had. He drew the leaf out of its sleeve by its edges and laid it on the table in the light from the window and read it.

He read it four times. Cael watched his lips not move.

The clock on the landing below struck the half after the second bell, and then the third bell, and then the half again. Nobody in the room had moved. Brom sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the floor. Lira had her chin on her fist. Seln stood by the window, a little to the side, where the frame hid him.

Vastin put the leaf back into its sleeve, carefully, by its edges, and laid the sleeve on the table a hand's width from everything else, where Karis had put it. He took off the gloves and folded them and laid them beside it.

Then he sat back in the chair with his hands on his knees and looked at nothing.

The silence went on.

It went on past the point where silences end in rooms, past the point where somebody clears his throat or shifts in a chair or says a thing about the weather to let the room breathe. Nobody did any of those things. They had agreed, on the Third-day, without saying it, that whatever he did in the room would be his and nobody would help him with it.

The clock on the landing ticked. Through the floor came the sound of the weight on the boards again below, and somebody laughing, and a door. The singer on the floor above had stopped, and Cael did not know when. Out of the window, across the slate roofs, the green cupola of the wool exchange stood in the grey afternoon, four streets off, with a thin smoke going up behind it from somebody's chimney in a straight line, because there was no wind. Brom breathed, slow and even, the way he breathed in a long hold at the frames. The cold stove ticked once, the way iron does when the day changes round it. Cael's ribs, which the healer had called mended a week ago, reminded him where they had been.

Vastin did not move. His left hand lay on his knee, and once it opened and closed. His eyes were on the window, on the cupola, or past it, on nothing in particular, the way a man looks at the sky when he is listening for something a long way off. His face was quite still.

Cael could not look at it for long. He looked at the shapes of the ledgers on the shelf instead, the clean oblongs in the dust, and counted them, eleven, and then counted them again, and there were still eleven.

Then Vastin spoke. He said it to the window.

"I knew the system was imperfect. I didn't know it was designed to be."

---

When at last he turned from the window, his face had been tidied. Whatever had been in it was put away somewhere behind his eyes, as a careful man folds a letter he cannot yet answer and shuts it in a drawer, and he looked round the table at the five of them in turn, the first time he had let himself look at them since he began.

"Nobody here has asked me for anything," said Vastin. "I noticed that on the landing, and I have been noticing it for two hours." He laid his left hand on the scrubbed wood. "So I will save you the asking. There are three things I will not do, and you should go home knowing which."

He put one finger down on the table.

"The first is verification." His eyes did not go to the case. "I want to. I have wanted to since the twenty-third pair. I could ride home and send for a frame and a station's own sheet and a fresh reading on any one of those forty children, and watch the case come true under my own hand, and sleep." The finger stayed where it was. "But there is no instrument in the Compact I could test it with that does not run on the thing it accuses. The frames are theirs. The sheets come up the same stair. The reading passes through the very function on that leaf before anybody writes it down. Whatever I measured would be a play put on for my benefit by the defendant." He was quiet a moment. "And a play of that kind has a cast. A girl's name on a request, or a junior's on an order. I will not put a child in a ledger so that an old man can feel sure."

A second finger went down beside the first.

"The second is a report. I have sworn to every report I ever made, and the oath is made to the office that receives it." He looked at the memoranda, at the nineteen seals. "That office is the one that wrote these. I should be swearing, in my best hand, to the man who forged them, and he would read it with great courtesy and enter it and thank me."

The third finger.

"The third is certification." For the first time he looked at Karis, only at Karis, and Cael understood that the not looking at himself was being chosen, and chosen again, every moment. "Before this year is out the seat will send to my desk for my certificate on the protocol for a re-evaluation at Halcenvane. I have known it was coming since the convening. I meant to sign it, because the protocol is sound, and in forty years I have never refused my name to a sound protocol." He lifted his hand off the table. "When it comes, I shall decline. It is the only sentence I have left that they still need."

Nobody spoke. Down in the lane a dog barked twice at something and gave it up.

"I have made something over nine thousand evaluations," said Vastin, to the case, very quietly. "I counted them once, as a young man, and stopped counting when I stopped being one. Each of them I wrote down exactly as I found it, and sent up." His left hand opened on his knee and closed. "How many went into the registry as I wrote them, I don't know. I never shall. That is what I am taking home from this room. Not the paper. That."

Lira had been watching him all the while from her chair by the cold stove, her hands loose in her lap, her chin up off her fist now.

"Are you with us?" she said.

He gave her the whole of his attention before he answered. It was the look he had given the room from the doorway, slow and level and entire, the look of a man who has been sent to evaluate something and means to do it fairly even when the something is a girl with a turned ankle asking him a straight question.

"No. I am not with anyone."

There was no unkindness in it anywhere. Lira took it the way she took a panel's figure she had already worked out for herself, with a small nod, and something in her face that looked like respect for the man who had not rounded it up.

Vastin got to his feet.

For a moment he stood behind the chair with both hands on its rail, and his eyes went once round the borrowed room: the oblongs in the dust, the pale squares on the plaster, the stove gone cold, the cupola in the tall window with its straight thread of smoke. Then he made his bow again, the short stiff one from the waist, and made it to the room, and to nobody in it.

"Thank you," he said.

Seln turned the key for him. He went out onto the landing, and they heard him take the stair the way he had come up it, one even step after another, down past the old clock in its box and the keeper's window and out by the front door into the lane, where anybody at all could see him go.

Seln stood a little aside from the window and followed him down the lane with his eyes until the lane was empty.

Then he said, to the glass, "He was not managing us."

Karis had begun putting the case back in its order and did not raise her head. Brom was standing with his good hand on the rail of the chair Vastin had left, as though it might still hold some of the warmth.

Cael stayed where he sat, looking at the leaf in its sleeve, a hand's width apart from everything else on the table.

"He didn't join us. I'd have trusted him less if he had."

---

The Archmarshal sat at his desk high in the Compact's house, on the fifth morning after, with the window on his left and the porter feeding the pigeons in the paved court below from his paper bag, as he did every morning at the same hour, and did not look at the trays.

He had come back the night before from the river stations by the post road. He had walked all six in four days. Every frame was sound, though one had been shimmed and would read the damp no longer, and he had written that it would read the damp no longer, and signed it. His left hand had disliked the mornings on the river very much. His clerk had had a fire lit in the room before he came in, without being asked, which the clerk did perhaps twice a year, and had said nothing about it.

At the fourth station, a day upriver from Ostrand, the keeper had walked him through the frame room as keepers always did, proud of his brass, and at the end of it had opened a long drawer under the counter to show him the station's own sheets for the year. A keeper kept his sheets as a miller kept his tallies. They were the station's private record of what its frame had read on each child, before any clerk of entry had touched it, and this keeper wrote a good hand and wanted it seen.

"Every one since I came, sir," the keeper had said. "Margins and all. My predecessor never wrote the margins. I always have."

The Archmarshal had stood at the open drawer for what must have seemed to the keeper a very ordinary moment. The sheets lay in their order, a year of them, the top one in the keeper's careful hand. He could see from where he stood that there was something written at its margin.

He had not put his hand in the drawer. He had said that it was a very well-kept drawer, which it was, and that the keeper did his station credit, which he did, and he had watched the man close it with his own hands and turn the little key in it. Then he had signed the inspection book and gone out into the rain to the next station, and he had not been able to say afterward what the next station's frame looked like.

The three trays stood at his right hand in the order he had kept since before he had a desk worth the name. *In*, *held*, *done*.

He took the copy out of *held*.

It had lain there, under three other things, since the third evening of the junior's visit. A station's Kindling entry, four years old, for a girl in the northern district, her Path and her first rank in a station clerk's ordinary writing. The junior's notes beside it, in a hand still deciding what shape it meant to be: the frame, the clock, the bouts, the tables. The two recommendations they had settled between them. *Second frame reading: within tolerance.* And under the station's line, *Continue observation.*

He read it again, the whole of it, from the beginning.

It was exactly as it had been. Nothing on the page had changed, and he had known nothing would. She did everything her Path said she should, the junior had said, only as if the station had written down somebody a year younger. Inside tolerance every way he measured it. *I still don't like it.*

The Archmarshal read the station's line, and then he read the margin beside it. There was nothing in the margin; it was clean. The station clerk had written her Path and her first rank in a straight line and left the margin as clean as the oblongs of wood on a borrowed shelf.

He had read forty margins on a scrubbed table in a borrowed room in Ostrand. Every one of them had had something in it before it was cleaned.

He thought of the room in Ostrand, though he had told himself on the post road that he would not. Four young people and a man at a window. The man had stood a little to one side of the glass, where the frame hid him from the lane, the way the field service teaches its people to stand, and had copied forty orders in a round level hand that never once let itself be hurried. The Archmarshal had known what he was from the doorway. He had known, reading, roughly what the copying must have cost and how many years it had sat somewhere before it came to that table. He did not know the man's name, and he had been careful all the way home not to wonder it.

He would not write the man down. He would not write any of them down: not the girl with the turned ankle who had asked him a straight question, not the big one with the bad shoulder, not the researcher who had held out her gloves, not the boy he had not let himself look at. A description is a file with the name left off, and he had spent forty years learning how easily the name comes back.

Then, because it was what he was, he went back through it as he would have made a witness go back.

He could pull a third frame reading. The junior would do it on his word in a day. But he had stood some weeks ago in the house's frame shop and watched an old man in an apron put a shim into a river station's housing so that it would stop telling anybody about the damp. A frame could be made to stop telling. The frame shop would have no trouble telling him that a girl in the northern district was within tolerance, as it had told him twice.

He could ask for a fresh reading at her station. The reading would go up through the thing on the leaf and come back advised.

He could send to the station for its own sheet. A keeper writes at the margin of his own sheet, whatever the clerk of entry is told to enter, and the district clerk in the river town had said so in four lines. The sheet would be there, in a drawer in the north, with the keeper's hand on it. But a request from the Archmarshal's office for a station sheet goes in the dispatch ledger downstairs in his clerk's tidy upright hand, with a number and a date and a name, which would be her name. Then she would be a file, and somebody somewhere would read the ledger. There would be an order, clipped to a copy, and nine weeks of somebody watching her, and a slip on the back of it with nothing on it.

He could call the junior up the too-steep stair and tell him. He thought of the young man's face, nervous and good. He would make him a witness to a thing he could never afterward unknow, and send him back to a career of writing *within tolerance* under margins he would know had been cleaned.

Every road went the same way. Each of them was theater, and some of them had a victim.

---

In the middle of the afternoon there were steps on the too-steep stair, quick, the whole flight without a pause, and the junior put his head round the door.

He had a file under his arm, a different one, and a question about a guild hall's frame that took a minute to ask and a minute to answer. When it was answered he did not go. He stood in the doorway with the file against his chest, nervous and good, and looked past the Archmarshal at the tray.

"The northern girl, sir," he said. "I've been wondering. Should I ask for a third reading? I could have the frame shop send a man up. Or write to her station for its sheet." He coloured a little. "I still don't like it."

The Archmarshal looked at him, at the hand still deciding what shape it meant to be, at the young face that had not yet been told anything it could not unknow.

"No," he said. "Continue observation."

The junior waited, as if there might be a reason.

"Write what the instrument says," said the Archmarshal. "What you feel about it is information about you." He heard himself say it, the sentence every evaluator is told once, the sentence he had handed down to every junior he had ever had, and he heard for the first time what it would sound like to a man who knew what was on a leaf in a sleeve in a borrowed room in Ostrand. He did not take it back. It was the only safe thing he had to give the boy. "Keep her file as it is. Don't send for anything. If she does well, let her do well."

"Yes, sir," said the junior, and went down, and the Archmarshal listened to his steps go all the way to the bottom.

He had told a frightened young man to do the very thing the twenty-three evaluators in the pink tape had done, in the very words two of them had used, and he had done it to keep him safe. He sat with that a long while. It was, he thought, very probably how most of them had come to write it.

He did not take a slip of paper. He did not open the letter-book of fair copies in which everything that left the room had been entered for nineteen years. He did not open the lowest drawer, where the folder with its tab lay under his own hand, *Pending a proceeding worth sending it to*. He knew now that there was no proceeding, and never would be, and that the tab would stay as it was.

He did not write.

At the sixth bell his clerk stood in the doorway with the day's wallet against his chest, looking not at the desk but past it, at the window, where the evening had come in grey.

"Will you be getting home tonight, sir?"

"Yes," said the Archmarshal.

The clerk waited a moment, as if there might be more, and there was not, and he went down.

The Archmarshal looked at the copy a little longer. Then he put it back in *held*, under the three other things, exactly where it had been, and pushed the tray back to its place. He turned down the lamp. He took his coat from its hook and went down the too-steep stair at the ordinary hour. He crossed the paved court where the pigeons had gone to roost and went home.

It was the hardest thing he did that week, and nobody saw him do it, and nothing anywhere recorded that it had been done.

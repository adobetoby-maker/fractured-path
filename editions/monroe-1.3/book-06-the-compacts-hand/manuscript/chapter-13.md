# Chapter 13 — The Hand

Karis spent the house's money for the first time on the thirty-fifth day, and she made Cael come down to the counter to watch her do it.

"Not because I need a witness," she said on the library stair. "Because I'm going to want to remember what it felt like, and you remember things better than I do when they're not on paper."

The budget line was small. Cael had seen the figure in the house's accounts, on the leaf the council had sat twice to head: a sum for copying and couriers and archive fees, the kind of money a senior instructor spent in a year without noticing where it had gone. Karis had not touched it in five weeks. She had built an eleven-pound answer to the Compact without spending a mark of it, because the answer was the house's, and the house paid for the house's paper. This was the first thing she had wanted for herself.

She laid the request on Bracken's counter, filled in, in her finding hand. It was addressed to the archive at Greyvane, to the keeper of the founding collection, and it asked for certified copies of the signature leaves of the oldest compilation the archive held: the founders' own hands, at the foot of the founding text, as they had set them down before anybody had written a line of commentary over them.

Bracken read it from the address to the signature. Then he took the house's budget book from under the counter and opened it at her line, and entered the sum against it in his small upright hand, and wrote the request's number beside the sum, and blotted both.

"Greyvane," he said. "Prynn, still?"

"Still."

"Then it'll be right when it comes, and it won't come quickly." He sealed the request and put it in the pigeonhole for the Greyvane post. "She has never filled a request of mine in under a month, and she has never once sent me a wrong leaf. Before the midyear recess, I'd say. Not long before."

For a while Karis stayed at the counter with her hands on the wood, looking at the pigeonhole where the request had gone. Cael had seen her ask for things at counters before. At Greyvane she had asked on her own account, for a student's fee, and been made to wait. At Norhold she had asked on the tournament's, and been answered by a clerk who did not look up. She had never asked as anybody's. Now there was a line in a book with her name above it, and a house's seal on her request, and a registrar who had entered it without a word, as he would have entered a request from the Magister.

She took out her notebook and turned it over, and opened it from the back, where the six slips from the long table lived in their pocket behind the last leaf with Brom's two dates beside them. On the inside of the back board she wrote the request's number and *Greyvane: the founders' hands*, and under it she ruled three short lines and left them empty.

"For what they tell me," she said, before he could ask. "If they tell me anything."

"And if they don't?"

"Then I'll have three empty lines, and I'll know exactly what they cost." She shut the book. "It feels like being trusted with a key by somebody who'll notice if you lose it. I've never been so careful in my life."

Her assistant made the fourth mistake of the month that afternoon, at the outer table, and Cael was at the reading-room window to see what Karis did about it.

The girl had been asked to set out, on a single leaf, which of the integrity provisions' sections a seat could act on of its own motion and which needed a party to ask. She had done it in an hour, neatly, in two columns. Karis read it standing at the outer table, all the way down, and then put her finger beside one line in the left-hand column and took the finger away.

She did not say what was wrong with it.

She went to the shelf, took down the second volume of the code, which Brom had carried up that morning on his shoulder, and laid it on the girl's table, closed.

"Find it," said Karis, and went back into the reading room.

Cael watched the girl through the open door. She looked at the line Karis had touched. She looked at the closed volume. Her face went red from the collar up, and then it set, the jaw first and then the mouth, the way Lira's set on the painted floor when the Stone stood on the cross she should have been on. She opened the volume and began to read. She did not ask anybody anything. She turned pages, went back, turned forward again, went to the index and found the index wanting, went to the digests on the lower shelf without being told they existed.

It took her twenty minutes. Then she crossed the landing with the leaf in one hand and the open volume in the other, and laid both on the long table in front of Karis, and put her finger on a sub-section near the foot of the page.

"*Upon a party's motion only*," the girl said. "Not of its own motion. I had it in the wrong column. There's a proviso at the end of the section I didn't read."

"Yes," said Karis.

That was all she said. The girl stood there a moment longer, as if waiting for the rest, and there was no rest. Then she went back across the landing with her leaf and her volume and sat down at her own table, and Cael saw her face go slowly pink again, not from shame this time. She had been told nothing at all but *yes*, and it had been the best thing anyone had said to her since the intake.

---

Somebody was using the second floor after the eighth bell. Cael saw the lantern from the court on the first evening, one low light behind the middle roof's windows at an hour when the cohort had gone to supper, and went up to see whose it was.

It was two men's. The old striking frames stood in a row along the east wall, padded posts on tired springs, nothing like the program's new frames in the east hall. Brom had the one at the north end. Ephram had the one beside it. Cael stood at the rail by the door, and neither of them looked round, and he stayed.

They did not spar. He waited for them to, that first evening, and they did not. Brom ran his defensive work on his frame from the top: the forearm going hard, the turn, the reset, the drive. It was the long sequence he ran before every road, slow at first and then at fighting pace. Ephram ran Blade work on the next frame at the cohort's speed. The entry line, the cut along it, the recovery. Each of them had his own post. Neither touched the other's.

But they were a hair apart, and they kept the same time.

On the second evening Cael saw the shape of it. Ephram finished a sequence and stopped, and stood with his sword arm down, and watched Brom reset. Watched the turn, and the half-breath after it, and the drive. Then he went back to his own frame. A little later Brom stopped, in the middle of his work, and stood, and watched Ephram's entry line go in: where the feet went, where the weight sat, which way the shoulder came round. Then he went back to his.

On the third evening Lira came up the stair and stood at the rail beside Cael. She did not ask what they were doing. She watched them for a long time, leaning on the rail, and Cael saw her lips move once, counting something, and then stop.

On the fourth evening the lantern burned low, and neither of them trimmed it. Ephram stopped at his frame and watched Brom's reset one more time, the whole of it, top to bottom, with his head a little on one side. Brom finished it. Then Brom turned round from his frame and stood and looked at Ephram, straight, the way he looked at a floor before he stood on it. Ephram looked back.

Neither of them nodded. Neither of them did anything at all. After a while Ephram picked up his kit and went down the stair, and Brom turned back to his frame and finished his work alone, and trimmed the lantern, and put it out.

Cael went down by the other stair with Lira beside him. In the court under the covered walk's north arch she stopped, and looked back up at the second floor's dark windows, and then at him. She did not say anything. She did not need to. He had seen it too: two men learning a floor they expected one day to stand on across from each other, each taking the other's measure as thoroughly as either knew how, and neither in any hurry to say what for.

Karis called them to the annex the night before the sitting.

Only the four of them: Lira cross-legged on the window seat, Brom in the doorway because the reading room had three chairs and he would not take one, Cael at the window. One lamp. The girl had been sent to bed and had gone, which she did not always do. On the long table Karis had laid out a sheet on which she had drawn the selling floor of the wool exchange from Bracken's description, with the seat on its platform at the far end and the tables below and the benches behind, and every chair marked in small square letters.

"Tomorrow is a form," she said. "Filled in, by six or seven people, in a room."

Her finger went to the petitioner's table. "He enters the petition." To the house's. "Our counsel enters the answer." To the platform. "The seat receives both. It rules on neither. It confirms the schedule, and it rises." She took her finger away. "Nothing that happens in that room tomorrow decides anything. Look at it now, here, on paper, so that tomorrow you'll know the room for what it is: the place where the paper gets read."

"Then why are we going?" said Lira.

"Because we said we'd go to every sitting, and the town watches the coach." Karis looked round at the three of them. "And because there's one thing in the room worth watching. Tell me what."

"Jent," said Lira.

"No. You've seen him. He'll be the same man."

"The gallery," said Brom, from the doorway. "Who comes."

"Closer. No." Karis put her finger on the platform, on the square she had marked with a small circle. "The seal. Our counsel is going to stand up and lay eleven pounds of true things on the table under it. Watch what it does when she sits down." She turned down the lamp. "If it does anything at all, I'll take my six slips out of the back of this notebook in front of you and burn them."

---

At the half-bell on the fortieth day the coach drew up below the market street's last rise, behind Withrow's carriage, in a morning already hot.

Cael counted the steps of the wool exchange through the coach window before the door was opened, because counting was cheaper than deciding what to feel. Four steps, the width of the building. Thirty-one people on them. Nine of them correspondents, with folding boards under their arms and pencils behind their ears and the air of men who had been waiting since first light for somebody to step down from a coach. Two in the dark coats and badges of the river guild. The rest were Ostrand's own: clerks, factors, a woman with a market basket who had plainly stopped on her way somewhere else and forgotten where. They had come early to the top of the town to see a chair with no name on it decide about a boy with none either.

The side door was finished. Its hinges had been blacked, and the dust swept off its sill, and a registry officer stood beside it with a ruled board.

He was young, younger than any registry officer Cael had seen in a doorway, and he had a heavy cold. He held a folded handkerchief in the hand that was not holding the board, and between names he turned his head politely away and used it, and turned back, and found his place on the board with one finger. He did not apologize for the cold or for the door or for the people on the steps. He read the list.

"The Chancellor of Halcenvane Academy."

"Present," said Withrow.

He read the house's counsel next, by her name, which Cael again did not quite catch. Then Karis, as *documentary support*. Then Brom and Lira, together, as *the respondent institution's attendance*, and Lira went past him with her chin up as if attendance were a rank. Then Seln, as *records support, Halcenvane*, and Seln went past him at his even pace without looking at the board at all.

Then the officer came to the last line, and blew his nose, and read it.

"The enrollee of record."

"Present," said Cael.

The officer looked up from the board. "Name for the door log?"

Cael opened his mouth to say his own name, the one people said, and the house's counsel spoke before he could.

"Enter him as the instrument has him," she said. "Exactly."

The officer looked at his list, where the instrument's address had been copied for him by some clerk in a back room, and began to write in the door log's two columns. In the first, for the name, he wrote what the registry had always written: *Caelen Hesk-ward*. In the second, for the standing in which a person came through that door, he wrote what the paper said he was. *The enrollee of record, unclassified.*

Cael counted it. He could not help counting it. From the moment the pen touched the first column to the moment it lifted from the second, eleven seconds.

He filed them where the other eleven lived. A circle of pale wood in an office at Denvash, on the morning of his Kindling, and a brass plate at Pellin's elbow that had stayed dark for eleven seconds before it lit, while a fourteen-year-old boy stood in the circle and did not breathe. He had thought then that eleven seconds was the longest any office would ever take to decide what he was.

The side door let them in at the foot of the long room, close by the records table, so that the first thing Cael saw of the proceedings was its paper.

Two officers sat there with their books squared to the table's edge. The senior was a heavy man he did not know. Behind him, at the junior chair, sat a man Cael did know, though he could not have said from where without thinking: a records officer with a careful face and a board, whom he had seen at a desk at Greyvane, and on a chair along the east wall of the evaluation room on the oak, and at other tables before those. The man was writing the date at the head of a leaf. He did not look up.

He walked up the strip of floor between the two parties' tables to the house's place, and the old trade went under his boots as he went. The wool-factors had carved their lot numbers into the planking a hundred years ago, row after row, so that every bale had its place and every buyer could find it, and the registry had planed and waxed the boards and set its furniture down on the numbers without troubling to hide them. *Nine*, *ten*, *eleven*. Across the strip Jent was already at the petitioner's table with his inkwell unscrewed; he rose when Withrow came in, to his measured height, and sat again. Cael sat down at the house's table with *seventeen* under his left foot.

Only then did he look up the room to its end. A low platform. The registry's sigil over it, very large, in plain black. The presiding seat on the platform, and to one side of it a bench of five chairs under grey cloth covers, waiting for evaluators the district had not yet called. Behind him he could hear the public benches without turning: every one of them taken, all the way back to the great double door, and people standing along the wall where there were no benches left.

Cael counted the room, because that was the first thing he did anywhere. Benches, doors, windows, tables, officers. He counted it a second time, and a third. Each time it came out a face short.

The presiding seat was occupied. A registrar sat in it, in the senior grey of the registry, the hood of the seat drawn forward over the head, so that from the house's table there was nothing to see under it but shadow. The hands lay on the bench in front of the hood. They were an ordinary pair of hands, a little lined, neither young nor old, a man's or a woman's, Cael could not have sworn which. The clerk at the side of the platform stood and read into the minute the seat's designation: the seat of the registry at Ostrand, sitting by its seal in proceedings the registry had designated institutional. He read no name. The hood offered none.

So Cael watched the hands, because the hands were all there was.

They kept time with the procedure, and a beat behind it, like a second clock in a house that has been set by the first. When the clerk passed up the minute, the hands took the pen and initialled where the clerk's finger had been, and set the pen down. When the schedule was read, they turned one leaf. The shoulders under the grey did not settle or lift. The hood did not turn toward the respondent's table, or the petitioner's, or the benches. In forty minutes he saw nothing move under that hood that the procedure had not asked to move, and nothing in the hands that the procedure had not put there.

He had spent his life reading people. It came to him, about halfway through, that every one of them had given him something to read whether they meant to or not, because there had been somebody inside the coat doing the giving. There was a coat here, and a seat, and a seal. If there was anybody inside, the seat had been built so that it did not matter.

---

Havel had his rotation sheet for the Ostrand sittings on a Fourth-day, from a clerk who did not know what was on it, and he read it in his room at the inn by the bridge that evening with the lamp turned up.

*Registry seat, Ostrand. Records support, junior chair. Rotation: the seat's Fifth-days, until relieved.* There was nothing else. There never was. A rotation sheet told you where to sit and for how long. It did not tell you why, and in all his years of rotation sheets Havel had never once expected it to.

He came to the wool exchange at the first bell on the fortieth day, an hour before anybody else, because the rotation manual said the junior chair would be in its place an hour before the sitting, and he had been in his place an hour before every sitting he had ever been posted to. He laid out the junior chair's things on the records table in the manual's order. First the minute-book, opened to a clean leaf. The receipt book for exhibits. The door log's copy, which the officer at the side door would send in with the porter at the half-bell, to be checked against the list. Two pens. Sand. A clean blotter. He squared each one to the table's edge.

His right hand ached at the web of the thumb, as it did every morning now. He opened it and closed it twice under the table, where nobody could see, and then he trusted it with a pen.

The senior chair came in at the half-bell, a district records officer named on Havel's sheet only by his grade, a heavy, competent man who nodded to him and sat and opened his own book and did not speak to him again all morning. That was correct. The senior chair kept the minute. The junior chair kept the times, the receipts and the door log, and checked the one against the others, and spoke when spoken to. Havel had sat in a great many junior chairs.

The door log came in with the porter. He checked it against the list. Every name on the list had a line in the log, and every line in the log had a name on the list, and against the last line the officer at the door had written the enrollee's registry name and his standing, as the instrument had him. Havel initialled the check and set the log aside.

The respondent's table he left alone.

He had known the boy would be at it. The house had said it would attend every sitting, and the house kept what it said. Havel did not look at him, because the rotation manual's page on courtesy said that an officer of a seat would show the parties ordinary courtesy and no more, and because looking at the boy was not ordinary. He had looked before. On a pump step at Ardenmere, where looking had been the work. Twice at Greyvane, from the recorder's desk below the bench. Three times on the oak at the bluff, from a chair on the east wall: once as the boy came in, and twice when the frame broke, the second quicker than the first. He remembered every one. He did not mean to add to them.

So he kept the times instead.

*The seat opened at the second bell.* The clerk read the designation; Havel wrote the minute it ended. *The petitioner's procurator entered the petition.* Jent stood at his table with his leaf in one hand, read the petition's working paragraphs in an even voice that gave no one of them more weight than another, named his nine exhibits and sat; Havel wrote the minute he stood and the minute he sat. Nine minutes. *The respondent's counsel entered the answer.* She rose, small and grey with a steel rule in her hand, and summarized eleven pounds in a voice that did not hurry and did not linger; twenty-three minutes. Havel took the receipts for both lists of exhibits in the receipt book, nine items and a hundred and seventy-three, and wrote the numbers, and initialled them.

Then the senior chair passed the minute up to the platform for the seat's initial, and it came back down, and Havel saw the seal.

The seat's seal was pressed into the foot of the minute's first leaf, in the grey wax the registry used for proceedings. He gave it the attention he gave every seal that crossed a table he sat at, which was close attention, because looking at seals was part of the work. It was the seat's own. The registry at Ostrand, the old device with the river and the scale. He had seen it on ten thousand minutes. There was nothing in it, or about it, that he had not seen before.

He noted that there was nothing.

He thought, sitting at the junior chair with the minute in front of him, about the five sheets.

They were not with him. They never came to a sitting. They were in his kit at the inn, between the second manual and the third, where they had lived since the night in Norhold when he had written the fifth on a narrow table behind the east tower, with the line he had read upside down on the senior seat's desk at its head. Five entries. The first was a pump step at Ardenmere, and a form with four questions on it, and a mark in the administrative layer of the boy's file that had no office behind it. The last was a sentence closing a door that the first had found open. In between were three more, and every one of the five recorded something that had happened, and nothing that he made of it. That was the rule of the sheets.

He had been told, once, a long time ago, in a room with the door shut, that the mark was not his clearance and he was to let it be. He had let it be. He had only written down that it was there.

He looked at the ordinary seal on the ordinary minute and felt, very faintly, the shape of a sixth sheet, the way you feel the shape of a word you cannot quite remember. And then he looked for the fact to put on it, and there was no fact. A seat sitting by its seal was lawful. A hood was lawful. A procurator who conceded everything was lawful and, in Havel's experience, sensible. The seal was the seal it should have been. Nothing in this room had done anything that was not in the code.

A sixth sheet would need something that had happened. Nothing had happened. A form had been filled in by people in a room.

He went back to the times.

*The seat confirmed the schedule. The evaluation seat to be empanelled at a date to be notified. The seat rose.* He wrote the minute it rose, and blotted the leaf, and squared the receipt book on top of the door log, and did not look up at the respondent's table even when the scrape of its chairs went down the long room.

That evening, at the inn, he took the five sheets out from between the manuals and read them through, every line, in order, the first to the fifth. He read them twice. Then he put them back between the manuals, the same way round, and buckled the kit, and sat at the window over the bridge for a while with his hands folded and the right one aching.

He did not write a sixth. There was nothing to put on it.

---

The long room stood when the seat rose. Cael was on his feet behind Withrow's chair, and so he saw her bow from behind: the line of her grey collar going down, and stopping, and the back of her neck held straight. He had watched her bow on the house's steps to visiting officers for two years and could have told its depth with his eyes shut. This was the same. It was the registry's own measure, the one she had been taught at twenty and had given across a platform like that one for twenty years from the officers' side, and she gave it now to a hood with no face under it as she would have given it to a registrar she had dined with.

In the stir of the benches emptying, one coat at the far end of the last bench, nearest the side door, stood a fraction after all the others. It was Seln's. Cael had not seen him all morning. While the others went out to the coach he slipped back in for a moment and stood where Seln had sat, and found that from there a man could see both doors, all three tables and the platform without once turning his head. He went out again before anybody missed him.

Withrow's carriage was waiting at the foot of the four steps, with the correspondents' pencils going on either side of it. She spoke to none of them. At its door she turned, with one hand on the frame, and found Cael with her eyes.

"The same depth," she said. "From this side. I measured."

Then she got in, and the carriage went off down the market street, and the coach went after it.

Brom had his face to the window. Lira was watching Brom.

"You were counting somebody," she said. "All morning. I saw your lips."

"The records officer. The junior." Brom did not turn round. "At Greyvane he sat at the recorder's desk under the bench, and twice he looked up from it at Cael. I was in the gallery. I counted." He was quiet a while. "This morning, none."

"So he's done looking," said Lira, with her boots up on the opposite bench and her back against the coach's door. "Or he's been told to be."

The house's counsel had her bag on her knees. "He tendered nine," she said. "I tendered a hundred and seventy-three. The clerk receipted them on one leaf, one line under the other, and the seat initialled the leaf without turning it over."

"It wouldn't have turned it over for one or for a thousand," said Karis. "A seal can't count."

"And the seal?" said Lira. "You told us to watch it. Did it do anything?"

"Nothing." Karis kept her eyes on the window. "It initialled where the clerk pointed and pressed where the code said. I watched it the whole time our counsel was on her feet. It didn't so much as turn a page faster." She put her hand on the notebook in her lap, over the back board. "The slips stay."

Seln was at the far end of the bench with his eyes shut. He had not said anything since the wool exchange.

"Under the hood, no man's grade at all," he said, without opening them. "The seat's own. It goes with the seal, whoever is wearing it. A monitoring officer on his rounds behind the senior chair, and the senior chair itself a district man." The coach went over a stone and he did not move. "They keep the minute, all three. None of them has ever decided anything. That's done somewhere else, and they write it down."

Nobody answered.

At the residence door, when the others had gone in ahead of them and the coach was rolling off toward the stable yard, Seln stopped on the step, and Cael stopped with him.

"The office has written that kind of minute," said Seln, to the door. "Not that one. Others like it."

Then he went in, at his even pace, and up the stair, and Cael stood a moment on the step by himself before he followed.

*A morning,* he wrote that night at the sill. *A hood, two hands, a seal. A procurator with nothing on him. A junior at the records table who wouldn't look. A room doing exactly what the code says a room does.*

*I keep looking for the person and finding the hand.*

---

He laid the year's dates along the sill two nights later, on the Seventh-day, on slips of his own.

There was one slip for each. Lira's first meet off the hill, three weeks away now, on a road, against Silvers from other houses. Brom's panel, the last Fifth-day of the year, at the district seat in Ostrand. The next sitting at the wool exchange. And a fourth slip with nothing written on it, for the re-evaluation, whose date the seat would fix when it ruled. He set them in a row, as Karis had set out her six on the long table, and sat looking at them with Hesk's book open on his knee, and wrote.

*The month closes, and for once the people I came up this hill with have each been handed something fair. Lira has her ladder, a point short, with the point named. Brom has his number, written on a docket a street from the seat. Karis has a shelf, and a key, and a girl to teach, and a line in a book with her name above it. None of it bought. None of it begged. All of it given across a counter, honestly, for once.*

*Set it down tonight, before it gets complicated. Whatever this challenge costs me, it should not cost them.*

Karis came up the stair while he was sanding it, reading her draft aloud to herself as she climbed, the way she did when a sentence would not sit. He heard her in the doorway. *The respondent submits that the integrity sections, read with their own proviso,* and then nothing.

He did not turn round. He heard the draft's paper stop rustling, and her breath close behind his right ear, and he knew where her eyes had gone. His hand moved to close the book, from a habit older than the bluff, and stopped, and lay flat on the sill beside it instead.

A finger came past his shoulder, ink on its second knuckle, and rested on the sill beside his last line without touching the page.

"That is not how any of us are built," said Karis.

It was her findings voice, the flat one.

He took up the pen again and wrote under the line, in the parenthesis the Log kept for things said aloud at the window, and she watched every letter of it go down.

*(Karis read this entry over my shoulder and said "that is not how any of us are built." Noted. Filed. Afraid of it.)*

When the pen reached the last three words, her finger on the sill tapped once.

"Afraid of it," she said. "Good." She took her finger off the sill. "Then be afraid of it accurately. Not at three in the morning, in general, about everything. Give it dates. Give it a column. Every week, put down what it's cost each of us, in the coin it actually costs, and add it up at the foot." She laid the draft on the sill beside his four slips. "Then on the day it starts costing us more than we can carry, you'll know which day it was. Not some other day. That one." She looked at the blank fourth slip. "That's what the notebook's for. Not for being frightened in. For knowing exactly how much."

She went to the door, and stopped there, with her hand on the frame.

"Read the draft first," she said. "Before anybody else does. That's the arrangement."

"That's the arrangement," said Cael.

She went down the stair. He sat a long while at the window with the four slips and her draft on the sill, and the blank slip at the end of the row, and the fountain going on below in the dark court. Then he turned to a clean page at the back of Hesk's book, past the minute and the costed rule, past the first pole and every entry since, and ruled a column down it, and wrote four names at the head of the column.

He left the rest of it empty, for now. It was the first page he had ever begun that he hoped he would never need to fill.

# Chapter 34 — A Good Room

Rooke struck the three bouts before breakfast, standing at the program's board in the east hall's doorway with a stick of chalk, and Cael watched him do it from the foot of the residence stair because his ribs had made the stair a thing to be taken slowly and he had stopped halfway to rest.

The card was pinned at the board's head in Rooke's square capitals, as every card was. Under *LIRA* there were three lines, each with a date and a floor and a name. Rooke did not rub them out. He drew one stroke through each, level and unhurried, from the first letter to the last, the way a clerk strikes an entry he means to leave legible, and at the end of each line he wrote two words in the margin. Cael could not read them from the stair. He did not need to. He knew Rooke's two words for a bout not fought, because he had read them in that margin on other fighters' cards, and had never once expected to read them under hers.

*Not fought.*

Rooke stood back and looked at the board for a moment with the chalk in his hand. Then he put the chalk in his pocket and went in to the program floor and shut the door behind him, and Cael heard the bolt go home on the inside, which it never did.

He went down the rest of the stair one tread at a time.

Lira was at the long table in the refectory with her strapped foot up on the bench and a bowl in front of her that she was not eating. She did not ask whether Rooke had done it. She looked at his face as he lowered himself onto the bench across from her, and nodded, and pushed the bowl an inch further away.

"Eat," he said.

"In a minute." She looked at him across the table. She had her elbows on the board and her chin on her two fists, the way she sat when she was about to say something she had been carrying round for a while. "You know what day it is."

He did. He had known since he woke, with the grey light on the ceiling and the new thing in him quiet, named, asking about nothing. He had lain there and said it to himself, and then not said it to anybody.

"It's the eleventh of Sowing," said Lira. "Hesk told me. Two years ago. I wrote to him from Greyvane to ask, and he wrote back in three lines, and one of them was the date and one of them was *thank you* and the last one said *he won't have told you*." She took her chin off her fists. "You weren't going to say."

"There didn't seem to be a place to put it."

"There's a place tonight. The reading room, after supper. Karis has said yes and Brom's bringing the kettle and I've asked the kitchen for a honey cake, and they said yes because I didn't tell them who it was for." She picked up her spoon at last. "And I've asked Ephram."

He looked at her.

"He's been on the same table as you for two years," said Lira, "and he's never once been in a room with the door shut where you were. He's going to be tonight. He said he's got grievances. I told him to shut them early, and he looked at me as if I'd asked him to set fire to the captain's file, and then he said he would." She began to eat. "Eighteen. You're eighteen today. Somebody should say it out loud at least once before supper, so I've said it."

He sat across from her with his ribs strapped and his left arm bound to the elbow and his hands round a cup of the refectory's tea, and let it be said.

He had been seventeen for a year. It had been a long one. He had been seventeen at Norhold on the stone with a healer's fingers in his hand, and seventeen on the road home in a cart, and seventeen behind a bell-frame on a gatehouse roof, and seventeen five days ago on his knees in a chalk cut with a cage of points round him. And now he was not. Nothing in him had changed in the night. Nothing ever did, on a birthday. It was only a line in somebody's register moving down a row, but it was his line, and it had moved, and Lira had said it out loud across a refectory table before anybody else could.

"Thank you," he said.

"Eat your bread," said Lira.

The parcel came up in Bracken's pouch at noon, among the house's ordinary post, with Cael's name on it in Hesk's hand.

Bracken brought it up himself. He did not have to; the porter's shoulder was mending and Seln was doing the sort, and Seln would have put it in Cael's hole with the rest. But Bracken came up the residence stair with it under his arm at the noon bell, and knocked, and held it out in the doorway, which was only a doorway, and did not come in.

"From Denvash," he said. "Carrier's mark from the far end of the country, nine days on the road. It's been rained on twice. The inside should be dry; whoever packed it knew what he was about." He weighed it on his palm a moment, by habit. "A little under three pounds."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I only carried it up a stair." Bracken looked at him over his spectacles. "Many happy returns of the day, enrollee. I have it in the book. Your file has had your date of birth on its first leaf since the day you came up the hill, and I have never once had cause to say it aloud." He nodded, the small nod he gave a form that had been filled in properly, and went back down.

Cael did not open it.

He set it on the sill beside Hesk's book and looked at it for a long while. It was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed thread in Hesk's own knot, the one that came undone with a single pull if you knew which end and held like iron if you did not. He knew which end. He did not pull it. Lira had said tonight. He thought he would let it be tonight.

---

The reading room had never had so many people in it.

It had three chairs and a bench and a window seat and the long table, and Karis's shelves along two walls to the ceiling, and the brass lock on the door with its one key on the cord round her neck, and Bracken's card in its brass frame at eye height outside: *Reading room. Assigned: K. Dellenmoor.* When Cael came up the library stair after supper, slowly, with his ribs, the door was standing open and the lamp was lit and the room was warm.

The table had been cleared.

Not cleared. Karis did not clear a table; there was nowhere to put what was on it. But every sheet on it had been turned face down. Every stack had been squared to the table's edge and turned over so that only the backs showed, plain and grey and blank, the code's three volumes shut and laid flat with their spines to the wall, her notebooks closed, the attestation wallet buckled. The whole long table was a field of the backs of things. In the middle of it, on a clean cloth, stood a honey cake on a kitchen plate with a knife beside it.

The assistant was just going. She had carried the cake up the library stair herself, she said, so that nobody would drop it, and she put her head round the door to say so, and wished him a good birthday with ink on two fingers, and went down the stair again before anybody could ask her to stay. Karis had sent her home early. Cael heard her singing to herself all the way down.

Brom's spirit kettle stood on the end of the nearest shelf over its little blue flame, not boiling. Brom himself had the chair by the shelf, with his left arm in its sling across his chest and his long legs out in front of him. Lira had the window seat with her strapped foot up on a stool somebody had carried in from the annex across the landing. Karis stood by the table with her right hand unwrapped.

He looked at the hand. She saw him look, and held it up in the lamplight, and turned it over.

The blisters had gone down. The skin of the fingers was whole again, and pink and new across the tips. But below the new skin, from the tips to the second knuckle, the fingers were a shade paler than the rest of the hand, as if somebody had washed the colour out of them a little, the way linen washes out in the sun.

"The healer says it'll stay," said Karis. "Paler. For good." She looked at it without any particular feeling, as she would have looked at a figure in a column. "It's the fourth. It's the first one that's left a mark. I've written it down."

She put the hand in her pocket and went to see to the kettle.

Ephram came at the half hour, out of breath, as if he had run up the library stair.

He stopped in the doorway and stood there with one hand on the frame, a tall loose young man in the cohort's coat with the green-taped captain's file under his arm, because he went nowhere without it. He looked at the four of them. Then he looked at the room.

"I shut grievances," he said. "There was one waiting. A first-year with a complaint about the meal rota. I told him to come back next Third-day with two complaints and I'd hear them both at once." He looked faintly appalled at himself. "I've never shut it early. Not once in two years."

"Come in," said Lira.

He came in. Then he saw the table.

Cael watched him see it. He watched the captain's eyes go along the long table, slowly, from end to end, over the turned stacks and the shut books and the squared backs of every sheet, the way they went along a floor before a bout, reading it. He saw Ephram understand that every piece of paper in the room had been turned face down, and that it had been turned face down because he was coming, and that nobody was going to say so.

Ephram did not say so either.

He took the third chair from the end of the table, and turned it round, all the way, so that its back was to the table and its front was to the window. Then he sat down in it, with his back to every paper in the room, facing Lira on the window seat and the dark glass behind her, and put the captain's file on his knees, and folded his hands on top of it.

"You can turn round," said Lira.

"I'm comfortable," said Ephram.

He did not turn round all evening.

Seln came last.

He came up the library stair without a sound, as he came up every stair, and stopped in the doorway, and did not come in at once. He stood there with his hands at his sides and looked at the room.

Cael watched him do it. He had watched Seln look at rooms for two years, at counters and sittings and in the long room at Ostrand from the one seat that saw every door, and he knew the order Seln looked at things in, because it was always the same. The door first: the brass lock, the frame, the card in its brass holder at eye height outside. Then the windows, both of them, the one over the window seat and the narrow one at the end, and what they looked out on, which was the dark and the top of the library's roof and, a long way below, the river. Then the landing behind him, and the annex door across it, and the stair going down. Then the table, with its field of turned backs. Then the people in it, one at a time.

He took perhaps a quarter of a minute over it. Nobody hurried him. At the end of it he said, to the room more than to any of them:

"It is a good room. I have been in a great many."

He came in and sat down at the middle of the bench along the table's far side, where the turned stacks were thickest, as he sat at the middle of every bench. Karis put a cup of Brom's tea in front of him. The kettle had boiled at last, while nobody was looking at it, which Lira said was the only way it ever did.

Seln looked at the cup. Then he put out his hand and turned it, a quarter turn, on the table, the way he had turned his cups for two years at every table on the hill.

Cael saw it. He saw Lira see it, and Brom.

It was the first time since the night of the gate.

---

They sang, because Lira said that you could not have a birthday without, and nobody else on the hill was going to.

Nobody wanted to start. Lira said she could not sing and proved it in two notes. Karis said she knew no birthday songs, only the ones the founders' compilers had written down, and those were mostly about drowning. Ephram said, with his back to the table, that he had a captain's voice and not a singing one, which was the same thing said twice.

So Brom sang.

He sang without getting up from the chair, with his sling across his chest and his eyes half shut, in a low voice that filled the small room easily, as if it were used to filling much bigger ones. It was not a birthday song. It was a harvest song from Velmere, he said afterward, that the estate's people sang at the last load every year, when the cart came into the yard with the last of the sheaves and the old women threw water on the horses for luck. It had a great many verses. Each one was about a field, and what had grown in it, and who had cut it. Brom sang every verse, slowly, all the way through. Nobody joined him, because nobody knew it, and nobody wanted him to stop.

At the last verse the tune changed. It went up, and came round again on itself, and there was a line at the end of it that everybody in Velmere evidently sang together, loudly, very nearly a shout. Brom sang it quietly.

Ephram, with his back to the table, sang it with him.

He sang it wrong. He had picked up the tune from the verses before and he came in at the end with great confidence and got the third line of it wrong, cheerfully, all on one note, and Brom stopped singing and opened his eyes and looked at him.

"Where did you learn that?"

"The first-years," said Ephram. "Somebody on the ground floor has been singing those three lines under the residence windows since the night before the year opened. He's never once got the third line right. I thought it went like that."

Cael sat at the table and looked at the back of Ephram's head and thought of the first night of the year, at his own window, with the house filling below him and somebody downstairs who could not learn a song.

He began to laugh. It hurt his ribs very much. He did it anyway.

Karis cut the cake with her left hand, because she said the right had done enough for one week. While she was cutting it Lira said them.

She had not said she was going to. She sat on the window seat with her foot on the stool and a plate on her knee and did not eat from it, and when Karis put the knife down she said them out loud, to the room, one after another, in the voice she used to give her figures to a panel.

"Next Second-day. The Current girl from Rooke's program, on the east hall's boards, rated. I'd have won it." She stopped, and went on. "The Fifth-day after that. The Ash second-year from the northern house, the one with the long reach. I don't know if I'd have won it. I wanted to find out." She stopped again. "And the lake house's exhibition. Their Blade. The one who beat the Current girl on figures at the confluence. I wanted that one most."

She looked at her plate.

"Three. Not fought. Rooke struck them this morning. They'll go on my sheet as losses, and the sheet will go to the evaluation at the end of the year with three losses on it that I never stood on a floor for." She looked up. "There. Said."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Cael found that he could not look away from her. She had not hidden them. He had expected that she would, a little, the way he would have, writing the bill small at the bottom of a page where nobody need read it unless they went looking. She had not written them anywhere. She had said them out loud, in a room full of people, on his birthday, in order, with the dates.

"I'd have stood there again," she said, more quietly. "In the cut. Where the man's foot was going to land. I'd stand there tomorrow. I'm not saying them so anybody'll say I shouldn't have. I'm saying them because they're mine and they cost something and I want them said where somebody heard." She picked up the cake at last. "That's all."

Ephram, with his back to the table, said, "Noted."

He said it the way Cael said it, exactly, in the log voice, flat and kind. Lira looked at the back of his head and laughed, once, surprised.

Then Ephram took the captain's file off his knees and opened it, without turning round, and took out three sheets of close-ruled paper, and held them up over his shoulder without looking, so that Cael could take them.

Cael took them.

It was the chart. He had known it existed for a year and had never seen it close. Every figure he had put up on the house's floors since the qualifying year, in order, in Ephram's square careful hand, one to a line, with the date and the floor and the three cards and the figure, and a little dot beside each one on a running scale in the margin, so that the dots made a line down the three pages. The line breathed. It went up a little and down a little and never once settled, and never once went past the mark at the top that Ephram had ruled across all three pages in red.

At the bottom of the third page, under the last figure, there was a gap. And in the gap, in the same square hand, small: *Excused (ribs).*

"Ninety-one," said Ephram, to the window. "Ninety-one entries since the qualifying year, and one gap this week." He was quiet a moment. "I'm not asking about any of them. I've never asked and I'm not going to start tonight." He held out his hand over his shoulder again for the sheets. "I wanted you to know somebody's been paying attention. That's all. Many happy returns."

Cael gave him the sheets back. Ephram put them in the file and shut it and folded his hands on top of it again, and looked at the window, and did not turn round.

---

He opened Hesk's parcel last, when the cake was eaten and the kettle had been filled again and had once again refused to boil.

He took the waxed thread's right end and pulled, and the knot came undone all at once, as it always did for him and for nobody else. Under the oilcloth was brown paper, dry, and under the brown paper was a satchel.

He did not look at the outside of it first. He opened the flap and put his hand in, the way he would have put his hand into any bag Hesk had made, to find out what it was for, and against the back his fingers found a pocket stitched flat and close in Hesk's waxed thread.

He knew what it was for before he had measured it with his eyes. He took Hesk's book out of his coat and slid it in. It went all the way down without being pushed and stood there with a quarter-inch to spare on every side, as if the pocket had been cut round it. Hesk had not seen the book since the day he bound it. He had cut that pocket from memory, at the far end of the country, and it fitted to a hair.

Only then did Cael look at the rest. The flap came down over everything. The strap was long enough to cross a man's body and ride on the opposite hip, so that the bag would sit still when he walked and not swing when he ran. And the buckle was not a bought buckle. Hesk's file marks were on the inside of the tongue, where only somebody who had slept in the shavings under that bench would think to look, and the frame had the little bevel Hesk put on everything he made so that it would not catch on cloth. He ran his thumb over the bevel. The leather round it was the heavy brown kind Hesk kept for instrument cases and would not waste on anything that did not deserve it.

There was a note folded small under the flap. It was on a leaf from Hesk's order-pad, in Hesk's small fast hand, with one initial at the foot. He read it to himself first. Then he read it again.

*Eighteen. A man's tools should fit the road he's actually on, not the one they promised him. Your grandmother would say I'm being unsubtle. She'd be right. Mind the shape. — H.*

He did not read it aloud. Nobody asked him to. He folded it small again and put it in the satchel's pocket with the book, against the book's back board, and buckled the flap.

Across the table Seln was looking at the satchel. He had not looked at anything else since the brown paper came off. He did not say anything about it. He looked at the strap that went across the body, and the buckle that would not catch, and the flap that came down over everything, and Cael thought, though he could not have said how he knew it, that Seln was thinking about roads.

"It's a good bag," said Brom, at last.

"It's a very good bag," said Ephram, to the window.

The evening broke up a little before the tenth bell. Ephram went first, with the captain's file under his arm, backing out of the door so as not to see the table even at the last, and wished Cael many happy returns again from the landing, and went down the stair whistling the third line wrong. Brom took the kettle. Karis stayed to turn her papers face up again, one stack at a time, in the order she had turned them over, which she said was the only way she would ever find anything in the morning. Seln had gone before any of them, quietly, while the bag was being admired.

Lira came down the library stair with Cael, step and crutch, step and crutch, with his good hand under her elbow on the turns and neither of them saying that it was the wrong way round, a girl on a crutch helping a boy with strapped ribs down a stair by letting him help her.

At the reading room's door he had felt it ask. He had felt it on every doorway since the road, and this one was a good door, oak, heavy, set square in its frame, the kind of door the new thing wanted most. *There?* He had named it under his breath. *Door. Karis's. It opens.*

Lira had heard him.

She stopped on the landing below with her crutch planted and looked up at him in the light from the stairwell lamp.

"Is it every door?"

"Every door. Doors most. It wants them." He did not know how else to say it. "I name them and it stops asking. But it goes on wanting them, a little, after."

She thought about that, standing on the stair with her strapped foot held clear of the tread.

"Then I'll name them too," she said. "When I'm with you. Out loud. So you don't have to do every one." She went down another step. "*Stair*," she said, to the stair, quite seriously. "*It goes down. People walk on it.*"

He did not laugh, because she had not said it to be funny. He said it after her, under his breath, *stair*, and the new thing, which had not asked about the stair at all, was quiet anyway, as if it had been told something it was glad to know.

They went on down. At the foot of the stair, in the library's dark lower hall, she said *door* to the street door, and he said it after her, and they went out into the cold.

---

He wrote it at the sill as soon as he was back in his room, with the satchel on the sill beside him and the house going quiet round him, door by door.

The fountain went on in the court below. The glass of the window was cold, and the cold came through it onto the leather of the satchel where it stood against the pane, so that when he put his hand on the flap it was cold too, like a hand that has come in from outside. The ribs were a low steady ache. The arm under its binding itched, which the healer had said meant it was mending. The new thing in him was quiet. Once, when the latch of the window settled in its keeper as the wind moved the frame, it asked; and he told it *latch*, and it was quiet again.

He took the book out of its pocket and opened it to a clean leaf and wrote the inventory, because a birthday was as good a day as any to count what he carried.

*Eighteen. The eleventh. Hesk's satchel on the sill. The book fits it.*

*What I carry: eight confirmed.*

*Wind-adjacent — Lira, the burst. Pressure-adjacent — the Bronze from Ardenmere, the push. Iron-adjacent — Brom, the read, which found the fourteen. Compression-adjacent — Reydan, the give. Ember-adjacent — Karis, the spark, two contacts and no more. Shadow-adjacent — the stair, sealed, rent from waking. Storm-adjacent — Daeva, the lane; moved twice this week, a quarter hour and then forty minutes, and settled. Anchor-adjacent — the hand with no face; idle by naming; wants doorways.*

*Under all eight, the still place I don't count.*

*Still open. Still real. Patience.*

He looked at the list for a long time. Eight lines and a ninth that was not a line. Seven names he could have written a letter to, and one he never could.

Somebody knocked at the door.

It was the tenth bell; he had heard it go from the lecture range a minute before. He knew the knock. It was one knock, not loud, the way Seln knocked at the wing's inner door when he wanted the Magisters to know he was there without being asked in.

He opened the door. Seln stood in the corridor in his coat, with his hands at his sides and nothing in them.

"The Fourth-day stands," said Seln. "Your line's the fourth on the board."

"I read it."

"I know you did." Seln looked past him, once, at the room: at the sill, and the lamp, and the satchel standing against the cold glass with its flap buckled down. Then he looked back. "I wanted to say it, not leave it on a board. That's all."

"Thank you."

Seln nodded. He did not say goodnight. He did not say happy birthday, or anything about the cake, or the song, or the bag. He turned and went back along the corridor without a sound, the way he went everywhere, and at the corner he did not look back.

Cael shut the door.

He went back to the sill and sat with the book open and the pen in his hand, and the satchel against the glass, and the cold coming through it. He thought of the chalk cut, and the fourteen, and the band of skin between a hood and a scarf. He thought of a case set down on this sill six days ago, beside this book, and a man who was not sure. He thought of a leaf from an order-pad at the far end of the country, and the three words on it that Hesk had now written to him twice, a few weeks apart, about two different things that were perhaps the same thing.

He wrote one more line, under the inventory, and blotted it, and put the book back in its pocket in the satchel, and buckled the flap.

*Tomorrow, Seln's annex. Hesk says mind the shape. I think tomorrow is the shape.*

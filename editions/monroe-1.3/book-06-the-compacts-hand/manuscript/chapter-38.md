# Chapter 38 — Thresholds

The worst of the next morning was that the hill looked exactly the same.

He had half expected it not to. He had lain awake for an hour after the tenth bell with the satchel on the sill and the court dark below, and some part of him had believed that the house would look different in daylight, now that he knew what he knew. But the frost was on the flags as it had been yesterday. The fountain went up and came down. The porter's boy was chalking the day's names on the Crown yard's boards, and two Mire second-years were arguing at the refectory door about whose turn it was to fetch the bread, and the coats at the foot of the road changed on the bell. Not one thing on the whole bluff had moved an inch to make room for what was on the shelf in Karis's room.

He went to the wing at the second bell for the week's floor sheet, because it was the Fifth-day and he always did.

The queue was four long. A first-year girl stood at the front of it with a folded paper in both hands, holding it the way you hold a thing you have been told twice not to lose. She was small, with a scarf wound three times round her neck against the cold and a Wind badge on her coat so new its pin still shone. The desk clerk was busy with the day book, so it was the teaching assistant who rose from the copying table to take it from her, as he did when the clerk was busy.

"From my station," she said. "They wrote that you wanted it for my file, so my mother sent it on."

"The house's copy," said Seln. "Thank you." He unfolded it on the counter and read it, and Cael watched him read it. It was a station certificate, the ordinary kind, with a registry seal at its foot and three lines of a clerk's writing above. Seln read the three lines once at his counter pace, neither slow nor quick. Then he blotted the foot where the girl's mother had signed for it, though the ink was days dry, and folded it again along its old folds, and wrote a line in the wing's ledger, and put the certificate in the drawer under the counter where enrollees' station papers went.

"Mm," he said, to the ledger.

The girl said thank you and went out into the cold with her hands free and her scarf trailing.

Cael stood in the queue and looked at the drawer.

There would be a certificate in it for every enrollee on the hill, four hundred and thirty of them. There would be one for Lira, from wherever she had been read, and one for Brom from the Velmere district, and one for Karis. There was no certificate in it for him, because the plate at Denvash had lit with a word and no station had ever written him down since. Every other paper in that drawer was three lines in a clerk's hand, and some of the three lines were what a station had read, and some of them were what an order had told it to write. There was no way on earth, standing at a counter in a queue, to tell which.

He could have spent the rest of the year that way. He saw it quite clearly as he stood there. He could look at every badge on every coat, every Ash and every Current and every Stone at every table in the refectory, and wonder what had been taken off its margin. He could read the whole hill as a drawer full of wounds and walk up and down it all year with that in his face, and Seln would see it at the counter in a week, and so would the coats, and so would anybody whose whole work was sorting.

He decided, standing there, that he would not.

It was a decision; he felt himself make it, the way he felt himself set his feet before a burst. He did not stop knowing. He only put the knowing where he put the band, somewhere it could be carried every day without being looked at. The girl with the new badge was a Wind first-year with her scarf trailing. That was what she was this morning. If it was not all she was, that was not a thing he could mend from a queue, and it was not a thing he was going to do to her by looking.

"Hesk-ward," said the desk clerk. "Your sheet."

He took his sheet. Under the Fifth-day, in Seln's hand, it still said *excused (ribs)*. In wing three that morning the band bouts would go on without him, three cards and a figure, the Ash instructor and the Mire and Gault, and somewhere in the residence Ephram would be ruling his chart and leaving a second gap. Cael folded the sheet and put it in his coat.

At the copying table Seln turned a leaf and did not look up.

Brom's sling came off at noon.

He came out of the infirmary with the linen triangle folded small in his good hand and his left arm hanging at his side, and stood in the covered walk opening and closing the left hand very slowly, the way a man tries a hinge that has been painted shut.

"A week of nothing heavy," he said, "and nothing over my head. She says I'm to let it remember where it lives." He opened the hand again and looked at it. "It's forgotten. You'd think it would remember a thing like that."

He went to the old frames all the same, after the noon meal, because Ephram was there. Cael went with him and stood at the wall and watched. Brom did not lift anything. He stood beside the frames with his arms folded, the left one carefully, and watched Ephram work through the long frame's sets one after another without a word, the way they had stood together at those frames all year. Ephram did not ask about the shoulder. At the end of his sets he wiped his hands, and looked at Brom's left arm for one second, and nodded at it as if it had passed some test he had set it privately, and went.

"He measures everything," said Brom, when the captain had gone.

"So do you."

"So do I." Brom unfolded his arms. "I don't mind being measured by somebody who's going to be right."

---

Lira asked it on the north wall at dusk, as Cael had known for a day that somebody would, and that it would be her.

She had come out along the wall at the slow careful pace the ankle allowed, and sat down beside him on the coping by the third buttress with her right foot out straight along the stone. Below them the valley was going blue. The river had dropped since the flood and run back into its banks, and the ferry was working again at the landing, small and black on the grey water. At the foot of the road the two coats stood apart from each other as they always stood, and while Cael watched, the bell went in the lecture range and two more came up the road from the town and the first two walked down it. Nobody hurried, and nobody looked up at the wall.

"Why are we still here?" said Lira.

She did not say it angrily. She said it the way she had said it once before, in a whitewashed dairy with a slit window, as if it were a question in a lesson that had come up on the board and was owed an answer.

"I'm not asking to be difficult," she said. "I'm asking because I sat on that bench last night and listened to you all read forty of them out, and I've been doing my drills in my head all day, and I can't make them go." She looked at the coats. "That seat down there runs on it, and so does the evaluation. Every sitting we go to, we're sitting in the room and answering it, and it's the thing in the case. They want to read you again. We know now what reads you." She pulled her coat closer. "My carters said the stamps stop four days east, loaded, or three days light. I've been carrying that in my pocket since the dairy. We could be past the last station by the Seventh-day after next if we left on the Seventh-day."

"We could," said Cael.

"So why are we still here?"

He did not answer at once. Brom had come along the wall behind them while she was talking, and stood now with his back against the buttress and his hands in his coat, and did not sit, because sitting on cold stone with one good arm was a thing he said he would do next week.

"Both roads," said Brom. "I said it in the dairy, and I've two boxes roped under my bed, and that's not nothing." He looked down at the ferry. "I'm not saying stay. I'm saying I've been packing for a fortnight and I've not told anybody what for, and I'm tired of it."

Lira turned and looked up at him.

"You've been packing?"

"Since the gate." He did not look at her. "Somebody had to."

Cael sat between them on the coping and looked at the coats at the foot of the road.

"The first thing I believed about all this," he said slowly, "was that the text would hold, that the people failed and the text didn't. I wrote it on the first night of the year, and it died on the Ostrand road. The room was never the system." He moved his hand on the cold stone. "Last night I found out what the system is. It's a form, three sentences that seventy-one people signed for coal. It's not a room I can walk out of, because it's not in a room. It's in every station between here and the coast." He was quiet a moment. "Knowing that doesn't make leaving cheaper. I thought it would, and I don't know why I thought it would. It only makes it clearer what we'd be leaving."

"That's not an answer," said Lira.

"No."

"I'll take it," she said. "For tonight."

Karis came out along the wall at the second bell after dusk, which was when she always came when she came at all, with her coat buttoned wrong and a volume of the code under her arm and her hair coming down at the back. She stopped beside Brom, and looked at the three of them, and saw at once what they were talking about, because Karis always could.

"Don't," she said. "Don't say it again. Not till I've priced it."

"Priced what?" said Lira.

"Leaving. What it costs, to the day and the line; not what it feels like, but what the code writes down." Karis shifted the volume under her arm. "I know what happens if a respondent walks out in the middle of a proceeding, because I read the coastal default to you in the dairy, and you all remember the word. I don't know what happens to the rest of us, or to the house, or whether there's any day in the year when it's different." She looked at Lira. "Give me two days."

"One," said Lira.

"Two."

"One and a half."

"Two," said Karis, "and you can have the half back afterward with interest." She turned and went back along the wall the way she had come, with the code under her arm, toward the lit window at the top of the library.

Lira watched her go. Then she laughed, once, quietly, the first time she had laughed since the infirmary.

"She's going to find it," she said. "Whatever it is."

"She always does," said Brom.

---

Lira crossed the east hall in front of Rooke on the Sixth-day at dawn, and Cael went with her as far as the door.

She could not drill. She went every dawn regardless and sat at the rail with Rooke's red drill-book and watched the program work under the two lamps, because she said a drill watched was half a drill done, and that she would rather be half than nothing. This morning she did not go to the rail by the wall. She stood a moment in the doorway with her hand on the frame. Then she let go and walked across the painted floor between the white crosses and arrows to where Rooke stood at the far rail with his arms folded, in front of the whole program, at her own pace.

Cael watched her from the door. She did not limp, or do anything a stranger would have seen. But she was thinking about every step, and he could see her thinking about it, in the set of her shoulders and the way her eyes stayed a yard ahead of her feet on the boards. If he could see it from the door, Rooke could see it from the rail.

She reached the rail and stopped.

Rooke looked at her feet, and then at her face.

"Not yet," he said.

"I know," said Lira.

He nodded, and turned back to the floor, where the Current girl was waiting on the four-count with her hands up. "Again. On the four," he said, and the program went on.

Lira walked back across the hall the same way, thinking about every step, and came out past Cael into the grey dawn of the covered walk, and did not say anything for a while.

"I knew before he said it," she said at last. "I knew at the third step." She pulled her scarf up. "I wanted him to say it out loud, so I'd stop wondering, that's all. Now it's said, and I can stop."

Bracken brought the Greyvane leaves up the library stair himself on the Seventh-day, at noon, all four flights, which he had not done since the birthday parcel.

He came into the reading room with a flat packet under his arm wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with the Greyvane archive's grey wax, and stood in the doorway in his spectacles and weighed it on his palm out of habit before he held it out.

"Nine days on the road," he said. "Registered, under seal, with the archive's compliments and an invoice for the copying that I have paid and entered. The first spend on the house's first budget line for an enrollee's research." He gave it to her. "It came before the recess, as they promised. I note it because the archive at Greyvane has never in my thirteen years kept a promise about a date, and I wish it to be on the record that it has kept one."

Karis signed his receipt with her right hand, the pale fingers steady on the pen. Then she broke the seal and folded back the oilcloth and looked at what was inside for a long moment without touching it, the way she had looked at the leaf.

Nine sheets, each with a seal impression taken in fresh wax from an old die and a line of signatures copied under it in an archivist's careful hand. The founders' hands, and the registry seals that had been set beside them on the charter, one for every year the charter had been renewed.

She took out the grey notebook and turned to the page near the back where she kept the things that were not finished, and found her own heading, *Greyvane: the founders' hands*, with three blank lines ruled under it that had been blank since the day the budget was voted. She filled them in, one line at a time.

*Received, the house's ninety-first day. Under seal, entire.*

*Nine leaves. Seals of known years, one to a renewal.*

*Paid.*

She drew a single straight line through the heading, so that it could still be read.

Bracken had watched her do it from the door. He had a way of watching a form being filled in properly that Cael had seen at the counter for two years, a small stillness, as if the room had briefly come into tune.

"Registrar," said Karis. "May I borrow the enrollment book? The old volume. The one before yours."

Bracken looked at her over his spectacles.

"For a citation," said Karis.

"I don't ask what for," said Bracken. "I ask when it comes back."

"Tomorrow. Before the second bell."

"Then it's lent." He nodded the small nod he gave a form that had been filled in properly, and went back down the stair, and twenty minutes later the porter's boy came up it with the old volume in his arms like a baby he had been told was valuable.

---

She found it that night.

The five of them sat in the reading room under the shaded lamp, with the case on its shelf between the grey boxes and the lamp in the window turned up as high as every night, and Karis laid it out for them on the bare cloth in the order she had found it. She had the code's second volume open on the left and the old enrollment book open on the right, and a single sheet of her own between them with five lines on it.

"Three things," she said. "Then the door. Then the price."

She put her finger on the code.

"First. A respondent who leaves the jurisdiction while a proceeding is pending is absent. You remember the coastal letter. The seat doesn't need him to have run. It needs him not to be there. Forty days after the close of the last sitting he failed to attend, it enters him." She did not need to read the line. "*Respondent absent from the jurisdiction; entered as fled.* That's Cael. The fourth sitting closed on the seventy-fifth day. If he were gone, it would enter on the hundred and fifteenth, long before any ruling."

"Second." She turned two leaves. "A chartered house whose enrollee is entered as fled, during a proceeding brought against his enrollment, is entered as having harboured the flight. It's one line on the house's charter. It doesn't close the house; it sits on the charter for the next inspection, and the inspection reads it." She looked at the table. "Withrow's charter folder, the one with the remedy column."

Nobody said anything.

"Third. The enrolled associates of a respondent entered as fled." She did not look at Lira or Brom. "Every enrollee of this house whose file names him, in a minute or a squad list or a delegation. A note on each file: *associated with a respondent entered as fled.* No penalty, only a note, on three files." She paused. "His makes four."

Lira's hand had gone flat on the bench beside her.

"So we can't go," said Brom.

"Not now. Not without the word on four files and a line on the house's charter, and not without leaving Withrow with that in her folder for the rest of her tenure." Karis turned to the enrollment book. "That's the price of now. Here's the door."

The old volume was open at a page near its middle. The ink had gone brown. The hand was not Bracken's; it was older, rounder, with long tails on the letters.

"A ruled matter is not a pending matter," said Karis. "The code's very particular about it, in a section about enrollees under orders that haven't yet taken effect. Once the seat has ruled, the proceeding is concluded. If the ruling revokes an enrollment, the revocation takes effect on a date, and until that date the enrollee is still an enrollee in standing, under an order. And an enrollee in standing may withdraw." She put her finger on a line in the brown ink. "And the house enters it in these words."

She read it aloud.

"*Withdrawn in the week following the district seat's ruling, before the date of its effect. Resigned in good standing.*"

"Who was it?" said Lira.

"A fourth-year, two registrars ago, in a guild matter ruled against him. He went home to his father's trade, I think, but the book doesn't say." Karis closed her hand over the line, not quite touching it. "It doesn't matter who. What matters is the words. The house has written them before, and nobody questioned them, and they stood."

Cael looked at the brown ink under her hand.

"So there's one door," he said.

"One." Karis looked at him. "It opens on one day, the day of the ruling, and stays open until the revocation takes effect, and then it shuts. And it only opens at all if we lose. If the seat rules for the house, there's no door, because there's nothing to resign from." She sat back. "Until that day there's only the corridor: every sitting attended, every form filed on time, every bout in wing three at the band, and every first-year at that counter handing in a certificate, and you standing behind her in the queue with your face doing nothing." She was quiet a moment. "Perfectly, until the ruling. If we put one foot wrong before it, the door we'd use afterward turns into the word on four files."

"So we don't run," said Lira slowly. "We walk."

"We walk. Perfectly."

"Corridor," said Brom.

Seln had said nothing all evening. He sat at the middle of the far side with his hands flat on the cloth and his cup turned, and when they looked at him he moved one finger.

"I am not enrolled," he said. "My door is a different shape." He did not say what shape. "Walk yours."

Karis drew one more line on her sheet, under the five, and wrote beside it in small letters *for now, and provisionally*. She closed the enrollment book very gently, so as not to crack its spine, and put it on the corner of the table by the door, where she would see it at the second bell.

---

They went to the east hall on the First-day night after the tenth bell, the four of them, on Lira's key, to learn what the new thing saw.

Not to use it. Cael wrote that first.

He wrote it standing at the rail under the first lantern, on a clean leaf of Hesk's book, before anybody had taken a step onto the painted boards, and held it out, and the other three initialled it one after another. Brom's B with its bar through. Lira's L, very upright. Karis's K, written with the right hand, the pale fingers holding the pen as steadily as they ever had.

*Perception only. Nothing laid. Nothing fixed. Every* There? *named, aloud if need be. If anything fixes, Brom counts. If Brom counts past one, we stop.*

"And we stop anyway at the first bell," said Lira, "because I'm cold, and I'm not allowed to move about to get warm."

She had the bench by the rail, with Rooke's red drill-book on her knee for something to hold and her right foot out straight on the boards. Karis had chalk and her grey notebook and her coat buttoned right for once. Brom had gone up the gallery stair and drawn every shutter down the length of the north wall, as Lira had drawn them on the first honest floor of the year. Now he stood in the hall's great doorway, with one of the two tall doors standing open a hand's breadth onto the dark of the passage and his right hand on its edge.

Two lanterns. The painted crosses and arrows going away across the planed floor into the half-dark. The frames along the east wall on their heavy springs.

Cael walked out into the middle of the floor and stood there, and shut his eyes, and let it look.

He had never let it look before. Since the road he had spent every waking hour answering it, *no* and then names, a word a thing at every threshold, so that it would stop asking. He had never once stood still and let it ask about everything at once and not answered any of it, only listened to the asking.

It was like opening a window on a night full of small sounds.

The hall came to him as places where things were resting. The frames along the east wall stood on their feet on the boards, and each foot was a place, a small sure point where weight met wood and stayed. The lantern on its hook by the rail hung, and its hook in the beam was a point, and the point was asking. The bench Lira sat on had four legs, and the legs were four points in a square. The bar across the foot of the gallery stair was two points and a line. The great door had its hinges, three of them, all points, and along its edge, where it stood open against nothing, a long empty place that the new thing leaned toward with its whole attention, the way a dog leans toward a door with somebody behind it.

And between the points, faint, there were lines.

Not lines anybody could see. He could not have said where they were with his eyes open. But with his eyes shut he could feel the hall hung together by them, foot to foot and hook to beam and leg to leg. It was like a scaffold built round a house that has not been built yet, every rest in the room joined to every other rest. It was not a picture of the room. It was a picture of every place in the room where a thing could be held.

He stood in the middle of it and understood the woman on the Ostrand road.

He had fought her for four exchanges and never once seen what she saw, and now he did. She had stood in the cut and seen this, every rest and every line, a whole road strung with places a thing could be held. She had laid her points where his body was about to come to rest, because she could see the rests before he reached them. It was not magic; it was this, done well, for years, by somebody who had learned it as he had learned to read a floor.

He did not like how easy it looked.

"Lira," he said, with his eyes shut. "You're sitting with more weight on the left."

There was a pause.

"I'm sitting on a bench," said Lira.

"You've got your right heel on the boards and almost nothing on it. Everything's on the left foot and the bench." He could feel it, the two points where her feet met the floor, one heavy and sure and one hardly there at all, a point that barely asked. "You told Rooke it was better."

"It is better." Another pause. "It's not that much better."

He heard her shift, and the faint point grew a little heavier, and then went faint again.

"Don't," she said. "That's cheating. You can't read my feet with your eyes shut."

"I'm not reading them. It's asking about them."

"Then tell it to mind its own business."

He laughed, and it hurt the ribs only a little now, and the new thing went on asking about her feet. He named them for it, aloud, very quietly, because she was listening. *Lira's feet. One's mending. Leave them be.* The asking about her feet stopped.

Brom moved his weight in the doorway. Cael felt it before he heard it: a rest lifting, and another place on the boards, half a pace to the left, where a foot was about to come down, lit faintly a breath before the foot came. He opened his eyes in time to see Brom's boot come down there.

"It knows where you're going to stand," he said. "Before you stand there. A breath early, not more."

Karis wrote it down without a word.

The door was Brom's idea.

He had stood in the doorway all this while with his right hand on the edge of the open door, because somebody had to be in the door, and he had noticed something that Cael had not.

"It hasn't asked about this one," said Brom. "Has it."

Cael turned and looked at him. He let it look at the door.

It did not ask, and that was the strange thing. The great door stood open a hand's breadth, a tall oak leaf on three iron hinges, a threshold, the very thing the new thing wanted most of anything in the world. It had asked about every door he had passed through for a week, and it did not ask about this one at all. It knew the door was there. He could feel the hinges as points and the edge as a long place. But it was quiet about it, the way it went quiet after he had named a thing.

"No," said Cael. "It hasn't."

"Watch," said Brom, and took his hand off the door.

*There?*

It came instantly, so fast and so hard that Cael's breath caught, the whole of the new thing turning toward the open edge of the door like a needle to north. *There? There?*

"Put it back," he said.

Brom put his hand back on the door.

The asking stopped, not slowly but at once, the way a dog stops barking when somebody it knows comes through the gate.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"It's already held," said Karis quietly. "That's it, isn't it? It wants to fix the door so the door will stay, but a door with a hand on it is staying already. Somebody's holding it, and there's nothing left for it to do."

"So I'm a doorstop," said Brom.

"You're a very good doorstop," said Lira from the bench.

"I'm the best doorstop on the bluff." Brom took his hand away again, deliberately, and Cael felt the *There?* come up like a hound's head, and Brom put his hand back, and it went down. "There. I can do it all night, and I've done harder work for less."

Cael stood in the middle of the painted floor and looked at Brom's hand on the edge of the door, the big square hand that had held twelve feet of road against two men on the Ostrand road and held a girl up in a doorway for twelve seconds. Something in him went quiet that had nothing to do with the new thing.

It did not ask about a door somebody was holding; it only wanted to hold what nobody was holding. He did not know yet what to do with that. He wrote it down anyway, after, on the leaf under the rule: *A held door isn't asked about. B.'s hand. It only wants what nobody has.*

---

Karis's part came last, because it cost her something and she wanted it measured.

She knelt on the boards between two of the frames with her chalk, and drew a line across the floor from the foot of one frame to the foot of the other, about four feet long, straight and white. Then she sat back on her heels and held out her left hand over one end of the line, palm down, close to the wood.

"Left hand," she said, before anybody could ask. "The right's done enough. These are the smallest I own, a wick's worth, and I'll count them aloud." She looked at Lira on the bench. "You count them too. If I say a number you didn't, tell me."

"One," said Karis.

A point of fire sat on the boards at the line's end. It was no bigger than the head of a pin, blue at its root and yellow at its tip, standing up perfectly still on the bare wood the way a candle flame stands in a room with no draught. It did not spread, or even scorch the board, but simply sat there, burning on nothing.

"Two," said Karis, at the other end.

And the new thing asked about the chalk.

It asked as it asked about a door. Not about the flames themselves, which moved and were not at rest; about the line between them. Two points that stayed where they were put, and a line drawn from one to the other, white on the boards: to the new thing that was a threshold. It leaned toward the chalk line between the two small fires exactly as it leaned toward an open door, with its whole courteous attention. *There?*

"It wants the line," said Cael. "It thinks it's a doorway."

"Two points and a line between them," said Karis. "That's all a doorway is, to it, isn't it? Jamb and jamb and the space between." She wrote it. "Name it."

*Chalk*, he told it. *Karis drew it. Nothing comes through it but air.*

It went quiet.

She put the two flames out with a closing of her hand, and stood, and moved the line. She drew it again two feet further from him, and lit its ends again, and counted three and four, and the new thing asked, and he named it. She moved it two feet further and counted five and six, and it asked a little less surely, as a voice is less sure across a wider room. At ten feet he named it, and Karis wrote *10* in her notebook and drew a ring round it.

At twelve feet she lit the seventh and the eighth, and the asking was very faint, like somebody speaking in the next room with the door shut. At fourteen she drew the line and did not light it, and asked him, and there was nothing at all.

"About ten," she said. "Sure to ten, faint at twelve, nothing at fourteen." She wrote it, and under it, *For now.* "That's what it can see. That's not what it can reach. Nobody's measured that, and nobody's going to tonight."

"Eight," said Lira from the bench. "You said eight. I counted eight."

"Eight," said Karis. She looked at her left hand, front and back, in the lantern light, and the fingers were their ordinary colour. She wrote in the notebook in her small upright hand, *Eight, left, smallest. Not an instance.* "Not one. Good."

She went back to the line at twelve feet, to put out the last two flames, and it happened then.

The chalk line at twelve feet lay on the boards between its two small fires, and the new thing, which had been faint about it, was suddenly not faint. It leaned. It leaned the way it had leaned in his own doorway with Lira coming through it on her crutch, when he had been halfway through saying *no*. Something in him began to go out of him toward the line, quick and quiet as a breath, to set a point at one end of it and a point at the other and tell the space between them to stay.

"One," said Brom from the door.

"*Chalk*," said Cael aloud, hard, to the whole of it. "*Karis drew it. On a floor. Nothing comes through it but air. It's nothing. It's a line.*"

It stopped.

It stopped where it was, halfway out of him, and came back, and lay down, the way the clerk at his elbow had once read an answer on a form and blotted it and gone home. The chalk lay on the boards. Karis's two small flames stood either side of it, burning on nothing. Nothing in the air between them had been told to stay.

Brom did not say two.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. Then Karis closed her left hand, and the two flames went out, and the hall was only two lanterns and the painted floor and the chalk.

"That's the bill," said Cael, and his voice was not quite steady. "Behind the eyes, like the naming on the first morning. Not bad."

Lira had a stub of pencil out. She wrote on her palm, under the lantern, and turned her hand so that he could see it. *Bill.* And under it, smaller: *Brom, one. Stopped.*

"We stop," she said. "We said we would."

They stopped.

Karis wiped the chalk lines off the boards with her handkerchief, all four of them, so that Rooke's program would find the floor as it had left it. Brom went up the gallery stair and opened every shutter again down the length of the north wall. Cael put out the lanterns one after the other and stood a moment in the dark of the hall, where the new thing asked about nothing. He had told it what everything was.

Lira was at the great door when he reached it, with her hand on its edge, holding it open for him.

"*Door*," she said, before he could. "*Rooke's. Oak. It's held. You can go through.*"

He went through.

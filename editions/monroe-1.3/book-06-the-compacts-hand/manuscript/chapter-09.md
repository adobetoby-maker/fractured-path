# Chapter 9 — Eleven Pounds

On the thirteenth night of the year the fire-watch's oil book took another line in Bracken's own hand.

*Library, top floor. Lamps late. On the registrar's authority, and at his charge.*

Cael saw it in the morning, on his way past the hook by the records-hall side door. Bracken signed that book every morning and almost never wrote in it. Now he had written in it twice in a week. He stood and looked at the page longer than a page of lamp-hours deserved, and then he went up to the top of the library and found out what the lamps had been burning for.

Karis had written the integrity section in a night.

She was asleep when he came in. She was sitting upright in her chair at the long table with her arms folded on the wood in front of her and her head on her arms, and the pen had rolled out of her hand and stopped against the inkwell. The lamp at her elbow had burned itself dry and gone out. In front of her, squared into one stack so neat that it might have been cut with a blade, lay the night's work in her finding hand. The girl at the outer table across the landing had put a shawl over Karis's shoulders and then, apparently, not dared to do anything else. She sat at her own table with her hands in her lap, guarding the door from inside the room.

"Since the eighth bell," the girl whispered. "She didn't stop. I filled the lamp twice. Then I fell asleep, and when I woke up she'd stopped." She looked at the stack as if it might go off. "She said not to wake her unless the building was on fire."

"Is it?"

The girl considered this seriously. "No."

So he sat down at the window and waited, and after the second bell Karis lifted her head off her arms, looked at the dead lamp, looked at the stack, and said in a perfectly ordinary voice, "Ninety-one."

"Good morning."

"Ninety-one." She sat back and the shawl slid off her shoulders, and she did not notice it go. "Every time anybody has ever called on the integrity provisions since the registry began writing it down. I've read every one. I found them in the registry digests. Bracken's press has the oldest digests and the hall has the rest, and Seln's sheaf told me which year each section first appears in, which saved me a week." She laid her hand on the stack. "Eighty-four of them are about things. Frames. Stations. An assessor who took money in a district I won't name. Two panels whose minutes argued with themselves. A records office that lost a whole quarter's declarations in a flood and then tried to pretend it hadn't."

"And the other seven?"

"Seven are about people." She looked at him. "Every one is about something a person did. A false declaration. A bought result. An evaluator who sat on his own nephew's panel. Seven times in the registry's whole history, somebody has pointed these provisions at a human being. Not once was it about what the human being was."

He came and sat across from her. The forty pages lay between them.

"So the paper we're answering is the first," he said.

"The ninety-second. And the first of its kind." She moved the stack an inch toward him. "Read it. Then come down to the counter with me. There's somebody I want to show something to."

Bracken was at his counter, adding the house's lamp-oil accounts for the week with a pained expression, when Karis laid one leaf of the integrity section on the wood in front of him and turned it the right way round.

It was a list. Ninety-one lines, in Karis's smallest finding hand, one invocation to a line, each with its year and its district and the section called on and a few words of what it had been about. She had set them out district by district, as the digests kept them, not by year. She put her finger beside line sixty-three, and took it away.

*Ostrand district station; sigil frame reading Stone practitioners one rank above their true rank over one month; integrity provisions, section four; filed by the registrar of Halcenvane in his fourth year in office; frame withdrawn, tested a fortnight, a seam in the casing found admitting damp, repaired, the month's Stones re-read; sustained.*

Bracken looked at it for a long time.

Then he put on his spectacles and looked at it again, more closely, and Cael watched the registrar read his own name in a list of ninety-one, in another person's hand, in a document that was going down the hill to answer a petition about a boy who stood two feet away from him on the other side of the counter.

"I'd forgotten I wrote it that way round," said Bracken at last. "*Admitting damp.* I'd have said *letting in* now." He was still looking at the line. "In my fourth year I thought a registrar ought to sound like a registrar."

"The original's in the registry's file," said Karis. "I copied the citation from the digest. There's one thing." She took the leaf back and turned it over, to a note at its foot. "In your filing, you spelt *casing* with two esses. I corrected it in the citation. Silently. It's a convention."

Bracken took his spectacles off.

"Don't," he said.

Karis waited.

"Put it back." He folded the spectacles and held them in one hand. "If the seat goes to my filing, which it may, and finds one spelling there and another in yours, it's one more thing that doesn't match. And it was my mistake. I made it at this counter in my fourth year with the frame's report in one hand and the pen in the other." He looked at her. "I'd sooner the record had it the way I wrote it. Mark it as mine."

Karis took the leaf back to the end of the counter and uncapped her pen. She crossed out the corrected word in the citation, very neatly, with a single ruled line, and wrote the old spelling above it, two esses, and after it, in brackets, the little word every copying clerk on the continent wrote after a quoted mistake, which meant *it was like this when I found it*.

She brought it back and laid it in front of him.

Bracken read it, and nodded once, and went back to the lamp-oil.

Cael understood, walking up the library stair behind her afterward, that he had just watched two people who did not say kind things to each other say one. Karis had put Bracken's own filing into the answer, as evidence that the integrity provisions were for frames; it was the strongest single line on the leaf, because it had been filed by a registrar at the very counter that was answering. And Bracken had asked for his own small error to be left standing in it, so that nobody could ever say the house had tidied the record to suit itself. Neither of them had said a word about why.

---

The house's counsel came up the hill at noon and read the forty pages backward.

She did it at the long table in her coat, with the steel rule under each line and her lips quite still, beginning at the last word of the last page and climbing. Karis sat across from her with her hands in her lap and watched her climb. Cael sat at the window. The girl at the outer table across the landing had found a reason to fill the inkwells, all of them, slowly.

It took the counsel most of the afternoon. Near the bottom of the ninth page, on her way up, the rule stopped.

"*Never,*" she said.

Karis leaned across. The counsel turned the page so that she could see it and laid the rule under one sentence. *The integrity provisions have never been turned against the nature of a practitioner, as distinct from his conduct.*

"You can't prove *never*," said the counsel. "You can prove ninety-one. A digest can miss a filing. A district can have burned its records. A seat with a mind to be awkward will ask you how you know about the ones nobody wrote down." She lifted the rule. "Say *not once in the registry's record*. Then it's a count, and it's yours, and nobody can argue with it but a man with a ninety-second filing in his hand."

Karis wrote it in. She did not argue, or explain, or say that she had meant the record. She took the correction the way Lira took a correction from the Stone on the painted floor, all at once, and moved on.

The counsel finished her climb a little before the fifth bell and set the rule across the first page.

"That's all," she said.

"That's all?"

"That's all there is." The counsel stood and buttoned her coat. "I've read a good many answers to a good many petitions. That's the best section on these provisions I've seen written by anybody, and I include my own. Put it in." She picked up her bag. "I'll be back up the hill when the rest of it's done. Send for me early. I like to read things twice."

When she had gone down the stair, Karis did not say anything for a while. She sat with her hands folded on the forty pages. Then she tore a strip off the bottom of a used sheet and wrote two lines on it, fast, and pushed it across the table to him without looking up.

*Best thing I've written. Worth exactly what a record is worth: the seat can agree with every word and turn the page.*

He read it and copied it into the observation notebook, both lines, in her section, and slid the strip back. She put it in her pocket. He understood that she had wanted it written down and had not wanted to be heard asking, and that a slip of paper across a table was the way she had found to have both.

When he looked up from the notebook, the stack had been turned over. The last page lay on top, where he would read it first, and Karis was standing at the window with her back to the room.

---

On the sixteenth day Cael took his sheet for the next Fifth-day down to the wing, because the provision's hours were lodged at the counter or nowhere, and the registry did not care what week it was.

The queue had shortened since the first days. The intake had learned the counter's hours and stopped coming in a body. A single Ash first-year stood ahead of him with a form she had filled in twice, once in pencil and once over the pencil in ink. Behind the counter the desk clerk added his column by the window. Gault's door stood shut. And at the copying table, under the shelf with the small unmarked case on it, the teaching assistant rose to take the first-year's sheet and read it from the name to the signature at his even pace, and stamped it, and tore the slip.

Then Cael's turn.

Seln took the sheet. He read it. Name; the Path line blank by rule; evaluator of record; hours; reason. The stamp came down. The slip tore along its rule.

"The Magister signs on Fifth-days," said Seln.

"Karis used the sheaf," said Cael. "All of it. Every leaf."

Seln laid the sheet on the stack at his left hand.

"The office keeps a register of everything that leaves this counter," he said, to the stack. "Forms. Slips. Copies on application. There is no sheaf in it." He set the stack's edge true. "There's nothing to have been used."

"Then I'll tell her that."

"Mm."

Cael had his fingers on the copy-slip. He had turned half away from the counter when Seln spoke again, in exactly the same voice, as if reading the next line of the same form.

"The second volume's own index runs a dozen years behind the volume. The digests are better for the late years." He had already turned back to the copying table. "If anyone should happen to be working from the second volume."

Cael went out through the wing doors and along the covered walk, and did not let himself smile until he was under the north arch.

He had thought, for two years, that Seln did not give things. He saw now that this was wrong. Seln gave things all the time. He gave forms before anybody had asked for them, and a sheaf under a door before dawn, and a correction about an index to a boy at a counter who could carry it upstairs. He gave them in a way that made thanks impossible, because there was no record of the thing and so nothing to thank him for. It was not modesty. Cael did not think it was even kindness, exactly. It was a man who had spent his life on the side of a counter where everything that passed across it was written down, making very sure that the one thing he chose to give stayed off the paper.

He thought about the quiet thing he carried, sealed, and the debt it was sealed for, which the man at the copying table did not know he was owed. He thought that it would be a very hard debt to pay to somebody who would not let anything be paid to him.

He went up the library stair and told Karis about the index. She went straight to the digests without a word. Twenty minutes later she found that one of her ninety-one, a coastal panel that had sat a member short, carried a year she had taken from the second volume's index, and the index had it a dozen years late. She ruled the year through and wrote the right one above it. It changed nothing in the argument. It went in anyway.

Bracken read the whole answer in two nights.

He read it at his counter, every leaf, from the address to the last schedule. The oil book took a third line and a fourth. He did not skim. Cael came past the records-hall windows late on the first night on his way back from the north wall, and saw him through the glass, a narrow grey shape bent over a single lamp, turning a leaf, reading it, initialling its corner, turning the next. A registrar's initials on a leaf were a promise about the leaf. Bracken did not make promises he had not read.

On the second night it rained.

It was the first rain of the year, and it came all at once a little after the tenth bell, out of a sky that had been brass-coloured and heavy since noon. It roared on the long roof of the records hall and came off the gutters in ropes, and the dust in the court turned to a smell before it turned to mud. Cael stood under the covered walk's north arch and watched it, with the fire-watch boy beside him holding his slate against his chest to keep the hours dry. Across the court, in the records hall's one lit window, Bracken did not look up.

Cael went across to him in the end, with his collar up and the rain going down his neck, because the light in that window had begun to look lonely.

Bracken did not seem surprised. He looked up from the leaf under his lamp and pointed with his pen at the stool on the public side of the counter.

"Sit," he said. "You can be useful. The house sends two copies down, one for the seat and one for the petitioner, and I've been checking the second against the first by myself with my finger, which is slow and bad for the eyes." He slid a leaf across the counter, and kept its twin in front of himself. "Read that aloud. Slowly. I'll follow."

Cael read it aloud.

It was the leaf about his Kindling. He had not known Karis had written one. It set out, in her plainest prose, the morning at Weaver's Row in Denvash: the circle of pale wood, the registrar who conducted it, the brass plate that lit late, the one bracketed word on the card, the classification line stamped where it had stood empty for fourteen years. Every fact in it was true and every fact was his, and he had never heard any of it in another person's sentences before. He read it in his own voice to a registrar in the middle of the night with the rain on the roof, and Bracken's finger moved down the other copy a line behind him, and twice Bracken said "Slower," and once he said "Again, that line," and made a small mark.

At the foot of the leaf Bracken initialled both copies in the corner, and blotted them, and set them on the finished pile.

"There was a comma missing in the seat's copy," he said. "Line nine. I've put it in." He took the next leaf off the stack. "Are you all right to go on?"

"Yes."

"Then go on."

They did four more before the rain eased. When Cael went back across the court at last, his own life was lying in two neat piles on a registrar's counter, checked and initialled, and he found that he felt lighter for having read it aloud than he had ever felt for keeping it to himself.

In the morning the court steamed. Bracken sent the porter's boy up the library stair with a message for Karis, and the message was one word long, and Karis read it on the landing and came down at once with the boy still trying to keep up.

The word was *Scales*.

They weighed it in the post room behind the counter, on the brass beam the house used for parcels going down the hill, where letters were weighed for their postage and nobody had ever weighed anything that mattered. The answer had been bound in two parts, because the binder's thread would not go through it in one, and the two parts lay side by side in a leather document wallet with the house's seal on the flap. Bracken set the wallet on the pan himself. He put on the brass weights one at a time from the box, the big ones first, and then the small, and then the very small ones that lived in their own velvet slots, until the beam came level and stayed.

"Eleven pounds," said Bracken. He bent to read the last weight. "And four ounces."

He took a small book out of his coat and wrote the number in it. It was not the house's book. Cael had never seen it before. It was a pocket ledger in black oilcloth, worn white at the corners, and from where he stood he could see a long column of entries in it, each a single line, each with a date and a figure.

"Every answer the house has filed in my time," said Bracken, to nobody, and put it away. "I like to know what they weighed."

Withrow came in while he was taking the weights off.

She stood in the post-room door and looked at the wallet on the pan, and then at Bracken, who was laying the weights back in their velvet one by one.

"The noon bag goes at the half-bell," said Bracken. "The seat takes the post. I'll put it in myself."

"How many came up our road on the eighth day?"

Bracken put the last weight away. "Three."

"Three, at a walk." Withrow came in and laid one hand on the wallet's flap, over the house's seal. "Then three go down it. The head groom and his son on the greys. Your senior clerk in the middle, with this across his saddle where the town can see it. Fifth-day, the second bell."

"That's three days inside the time."

"Good," said Withrow. "Let them count those as well."

---

It went down the hill on the nineteenth day, a Fifth-day, at the second bell.

Cael watched from the covered walk with half the house beside him. Nobody had announced anything. Nobody needed to. The head groom came out of the stable yard on the bigger of the two greys in the coat he wore at convocation and nowhere else, and his son on the other grey looking as if he had been told to sit up straight and was doing it with his whole body. Between them rode Bracken's senior clerk, a long patient man who had kept the enrollment book for nine years and had never in his life been looked at by so many people at once. The wallet was strapped across the front of his saddle under a fold of the house's own cloth, and he kept one hand flat on it the whole way across the court.

They went out under the arch at a walk.

The lodge-man stood as straight as he stood for the Chancellor. The intake crowded the north wall to watch them take the first switchback, and the second, and the bend above the trees. Lira was on the wall with them, and Brom, and somewhere behind Cael on the walk a third-year said, half laughing and half not, *Is that it? Is that the whole answer?* and somebody else said, *That's eleven pounds of it,* and nobody laughed after that.

At the foot of the road the two coats watched the escort go by them onto the river street. Neither turned his head to follow it. They looked at it as it passed, the way they looked at a cart of hay, and then went back to looking at the gate.

Karis did not come down to watch. Cael looked up at the top of the library, at the reading room's window, and she was not in it. She had gone to sleep in her chair the moment the wallet left the counter, the girl at the outer table told him afterward, with her boots still on and her key on its cord in her fist.

He wrote it that night at the sill, with the copy of the instrument beside him and the answer's first leaf lying in his memory where he could read it with his eyes shut.

*Eleven pounds four ounces, down the hill at a walk, under our own escort, three days inside the time. The best paper anyone has ever written about me. I read every leaf. I can't find a seam, and I was looking. Karis says it's perfect, and in two years I have never seen her call a thing perfect that wasn't.*

*I believe her. I also believe the corridor. Tonight I can carry both of them, one in each hand. I haven't yet had to find out what happens when I need a hand free.*

---

The north wall was warm under him from the day's sun, and the valley was going blue, when Lira came along the top of it from the east end.

She walked the coping itself, not the path beside it, as she always did; it was narrower, so it was quicker. Her hands were still taped from the evening drill and she had a heel of bread in one of them. She broke it in two without looking, gave him the larger half, and sat down beside him with her legs over the drop.

"Brom's wearing out a word," she said.

He knew which.

"He says it at the frames. Under his breath, between sets, like a man counting." She bit the bread. "He did it twice yesterday and once this morning, and he doesn't know he's doing it. Brom never says anything twice unless he hasn't finished chewing it." She looked at him sideways. "So you're going to give it to me. All of it. Small."

He gave it to her backward, the way the house's counsel read a paper.

He started at the end, with the morning after, because that was where a person actually lived. The ferryman with a letter, the guild hall with a letter, the clerk with a sealed file and no key. A daughter at Withrow's counter every week for a year, asking whether anybody had begun the review, and nobody ever obliged to begin it. Then the three steps of chapter fourteen in Bracken's voice that would not let itself be a voice, and the third word that nobody had ever had to stamp. Then the six slips of paper laid out down the long table like stones across a stream, each a door, each real law, each opening toward the next and never back the way you had come.

He did not tell it well. He told it in order, which was the only way he knew.

Lira ate her bread and watched the river. When he had finished she brushed the crumbs off her tapes, one hand and then the other.

"Does it have an outside?" she said. "Your corridor."

"Karis says every door opens one way."

"I heard you." She was quiet a moment. "They had a slate at Fenmark."

He waited.

"In the examination hall. Three examiners at a table, and a slate in a wooden frame between them." She held her hands a foot apart. "They chalked it while I fought. Afterward the one in the middle copied it fair onto the form, and wiped the slate with his sleeve so the form would have no crossings-out. I remember thinking how careful that was." She put her hands down. "Every line on that form was correct. Wind. Copper. My name spelt right. Every step in the book, in the book's order. It was the room that was wrong. I've never been allowed to say both of those at once and have anybody listen. I only had *angry*, and angry's a very poor word. It's all one size."

"Corridor," said Cael.

"Corridor." She tried it slowly, the way she tried a new step on the painted lines. "I could have used that four years ago, near enough."

The ferryman was lighting the lamp on the landing's post. The two coats there turned their faces from it, as they always did when the flame went up, so as not to be blinded for the hour that followed.

"Here's the other half," said Lira. "There was no door out of that hall but the one they pointed at. I went out of it with the form in my pocket, and for a year after that I knocked on guild doors. I asked six of them for a letter and walked to two, and every one of them read the form, and every one was polite." She turned her head toward him. "Then I came to Ardenmere with nothing left to knock on, and one evening I heard a noise like somebody hitting a boiler with a chain, three streets off, and went to look. Vell's yard. Nobody sent me there. It wasn't on any form. It was round the back of everything, where the town kept what it didn't want to look at, and that turned out to include me."

She stood up on the coping, all at once, the way she came up off a cross.

"So I believe Karis," she said. "Every door opens one way. I've stood in that building. But there's usually a yard out the back." She looked down at him. "Not that anybody draws it on the plan."

She went off along the top of the wall toward the residence, quick and narrow in the dusk, and he sat with the rest of his bread and watched her go until she dropped off the coping at the far end and was gone.

He wrote one line before the light went: *Lira: a slate wiped clean, a year of polite doors, then a yard nobody drew.*

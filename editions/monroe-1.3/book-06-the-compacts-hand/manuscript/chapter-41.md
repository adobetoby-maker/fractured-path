# Chapter 41 — The Empty Hill

They came back up the bluff the way they had gone down it, by five ways, and nobody on the road could have said they had ever been together.

Cael came last again, by the ferry. The boat was low in the water with the term's last passengers, enrollees going home with their trunks roped and their boots tied round their necks by the laces, and he stood at the rail among them with his hands in his coat and let them talk over his head about mothers and roads and what they would eat first. Nobody looked at him twice. At the landing the two coats stood apart from each other by the bollards, as they always stood, and watched the trunks come off; they were counting trunks today, and he saw the nearer one's lips move.

He walked up the bluff road behind a carter's boy leading a mule with three trunks on its back, and let the mule set the pace.

At the top the house was emptying: the outer court was full of carts, and the lodge-man stood at his window with the arrivals book turned to its other face, the departures, writing a name for every trunk that went under the arch. Third-years shouted to one another across the flags about the next term as if it were a country. Somebody had left a cracked practice lath leaning against the capped well with a paper on it that said *FREE*, and nobody had taken it.

He went through to the north wall, because that was where he went, and sat on the coping by the third buttress, and watched the carts go down.

It took them until the light was going, a slow line down the switchbacks with the trunks swaying and the wheels loud on the turns, and at the foot of the road the coats stepped back against the chandler's wall to let them by. On the river the ferry made its crossing, and came back empty, and crossed again full. Four hundred and thirty enrollees lived on the bluff, and by dusk forty-one of them were left.

Four of the forty-one had signed their slips together, at the wing's counter, that morning before they went down.

The slips were the house's ordinary form for the recess, half a sheet, with a printed line for the enrollee's name and a printed line under it that said *Remaining in residence. Reason:*. Lira had written *the program*, and Brom *the frames*, and Karis had written *research standing, per council*, in her small upright hand, with the date of the council's sitting after it, because she could not leave a reason uncited. Cael had stood at the counter with the pen for a while and then written *the proceedings*.

It was the plainest reason of the four. Since the year's eighth day the registry had had a petition before its seat in the town below, asking that he be stood in a frame and read again. If the frame could not read him, the code had a chapter for that: his enrollment struck out, and after it, a letter at a time, everything else with his name on it. And a respondent who left the hill while the thing was pending would be entered as fled, with a note on three friends' files to say whose friends they were.

Seln had taken the slip from him across the counter, and read the one line, and blotted it.

"Mm," he had said, and put it with the others.

That was all. Nobody had asked the four of them whether they would go home for the recess, and nobody had needed to. The fifth was staff, and had no slip to sign.

Lira came out along the wall while the last carts were still on the upper switchback, walking at the careful pace she had kept all week, and sat down on the coping beside him with her right foot out along the stone. She had come up from the town on the program's market cart with the tape and the chalk, three hours before him, and she had plainly been waiting to see him come.

"Well?" she said, low, though there was nobody within forty yards.

"He went out by the front door. You saw."

"I saw. I mean after. On the ferry." She pulled her coat round her. A thin cold rain had begun to come in sideways off the river, hardly more than a wetness on the wind. "Did anybody look at you differently?"

"Nobody looked at me at all. They were counting trunks."

"Good." She watched the carts for a while. "He never looked at you either. Two hours in that room, and he looked at everybody else exactly as long as he looked at the stove. I timed it. He was doing it on purpose, and it was the kindest thing I've ever seen anybody do with their eyes."

"I know."

"Six," said Lira, to the valley, as if she were trying the number on. "Six people carrying one thing. I've only ever kept a secret among three before, and I thought three was a lot." She got up, with her hand on the buttress. "I'm going in. It's wet, and I'm not allowed to be heroic about anything until Rooke says."

When the last cart had gone down he took Hesk's book out of the satchel and opened it on his knee in the last of the light. He did not write anything about Ostrand at first, but sat with the pen over the page for a long time and thought about what could go in a book and what could not.

Then he wrote three lines.

*Seventh-day. Down by five ways, up by five. A man read something in a borrowed room and thanked the room, not us, and went home.*

*Six now. That's the whole of it.*

*The recess starts tomorrow. Nobody goes home.*

Six people now knew what was in the case on Karis's shelf, and the sixth was an officer of the Compact who had read it through that afternoon and carried nothing away but the knowing. What it was, Cael did not write. It was not a thing that went into a book.

He blotted the page and read it back. It was true and it said nothing, and he understood with a small cold turn of the stomach that he had written it the way Seln wrote a return. He did not cross it out.

In his room, Hesk's clock on the shelf said ten minutes short of the eighth bell, and the lecture range had rung the eighth while he was on the stair. It had lost its usual minutes since the morning, in its usual direction. He set it right and wound it, and lay down, and was asleep before he had finished counting the things he did not have to do tomorrow.

---

The hill was a different place with nobody on it.

He found that out on the First-day morning, crossing the outer court to the refectory, when the fountain went up and came down and he could hear it do both. On an ordinary morning you could not hear the fountain at all; there were too many boots on the flags and too many voices under the covered walk, and somebody always shouting for somebody from a window. This morning there was the fountain, and the wind coming over the north wall off the river, and a pigeon on the lodge roof saying the same thing over and over, and nothing else.

The refectory had one table lit, for the cooks had put all forty-one of the house's remaining enrollees at the long table nearest the kitchen door, so that they would only have to carry the porridge one way, and the rest of the hall stood empty under its high windows with the benches turned up on the boards. The forty-one sat close together at their one table, as people do in a big room, and talked in low voices.

Lira was there already, in her program wraps, eating fast. She had been on the east hall's floor since before the first bell, or not on it, exactly, but at its rail, with Rooke's red book on her knee, watching.

"Rooke says the program doesn't stop for the recess," she said. "Rooke says the registry doesn't stop for the recess, so why should he." She pushed the honey toward him. "There are six of us. Me, the Current girl, the Force, the Stone, Rooke and the clerk. The clerk counts as one of us because she says so."

"And the ankle?"

"Is an ankle." She ate. "Ask me in a week."

Karis came in late with her coat buttoned wrong and a volume of the code under her arm, and sat, and opened the volume beside her bowl, and read it while she ate without once looking at what she was eating. Brom came in after her with Ephram, and the two of them sat at the end of the table with their elbows touching and ate enough for six and said almost nothing.

Cael had not known Ephram was staying.

"Somebody has to keep the frames honest," said Ephram, when he asked. "The springs go if nobody works them for a fortnight. I've seen it." He wiped his bowl with his bread. "Also I like the hill better with nobody on it. Don't tell the cohort."

Brom looked at his own bowl and said nothing. His left hand had gone to his coat, flat, over the inside pocket, the way it went a dozen times a day, and Cael did not think he knew it had.

After the meal Cael went to the wing, from habit, and found the counter shut and Seln at the copying table behind it anyway, with the lamp lit and a ledger open, copying.

"There is always a ledger," said Seln, without looking up.

Cael stood at the counter a moment longer than he needed to. He had come for the week's floor sheet, as he always came on a First-day, and there was no floor sheet, and there would be none for fourteen days. Wing three was locked, with its trestle bare and its three cards in Gault's drawer, and nobody on the hill was going to write down a figure for him for two weeks.

He had spent a year wishing for exactly that, and now that he had it he did not know what to do with his hands.

He tried the records hall next, for no better reason, and found Bracken at his counter in his spectacles with the daybook open, exactly as he would have been on any working morning of the term, and the three glasses on their tray, and the counter clock ticking.

"The hall keeps no recess," said Bracken, before he could ask. "Enrollees go home. Paper does not." He turned the daybook round on the counter so that Cael could read the morning's entry, which was a thing he did only when he was pleased with it. *Recess, the first day. In residence: forty-one enrolled, by slip. Post: the small bag. Nothing sealed.* "Nothing sealed," he repeated, and turned it back. "I have written that at the foot of the day for a week running, and I find I look forward to it. Go and be idle somewhere. It is what a recess is for, and I am told it does not come naturally to everybody."

He went to the frames in the end, because Brom and Ephram were there, and stood at the wall and watched them work. Ephram ran his sets on the long frame, one after another, in the measured way he did everything, while Brom stood beside the short frame with his arms folded and watched the springs. Once, Brom put his good hand on the short frame's bar and pressed, and felt it give, and nodded, and took the hand away. Neither of them spoke for the better part of an hour.

It was the most restful hour Cael had spent since the first night of the year, and he could not have said why.

---

Hesk's letter came up on the Third-day, in the recess bag, which was a quarter the size of the term bag and which Seln sorted at the post-room trestle in eleven minutes by the clock over the door.

Cael read it on his sill in the grey afternoon, with the window open a crack because his room was always too warm in the afternoons, and the wind coming in off the river and moving the corner of the page.

*Your letter came, and the satchel fits you, which I knew it would, since I measured it on a book and not on a boy.*

*The strap will go before the buckle does. When it goes, cut a new one from a side of harness leather and not from anything sold as strap, and keep the old buckle. A good buckle outlasts three straps and a bad one outlasts none. I put that bevel on it so it would not catch your coat, and if anybody offers to replace the buckle with a better one, they are wrong about what better means.*

*There is a ship's clock on the bench, in for cleaning, that gains a minute in the day. The owner is a captain who wants it to lose instead, so that he arrives in port feeling ahead of himself. I have told him I can make it keep time and that is all I will promise. He is thinking it over.*

*The widow's dog has stopped barking at me. It sleeps on the step now, in the sun, across the door, so that I must step over it in the morning. I have decided this is worse.*

*The stove draws. The shop is the shop. Eat something green.*

*— H.*

He read it twice, the second time slowly, the way he would have read it at the long bench with Hesk across from him pretending not to watch his face. Then he folded it along its folds and put it in the pocket of the satchel, against the back board of the book, with the note from the birthday.

There was nothing in it, and that was what Hesk's letters were for: a buckle and a captain and a dog asleep across a door, and the stove, and something green, and not one word about registries or polite men or what anybody might be building. Hesk wrote from his bench and stayed at the height of his bench, and the letter came up the bluff like a smell of shavings and reminded Cael that there was a whole country in which people's dogs slept on steps.

He got out paper and wrote back the same afternoon, before he could think better of it. He told Hesk that the hill was empty, and described the refectory's one table and the fountain you could hear, and Brom and the captain at the frames, keeping the springs honest. He wrote that Lira's ankle was mending, and that it rained most days, and that Karis had read the whole of a code volume over her breakfast without spilling a drop on it, which Hesk would think was the most remarkable thing in the letter.

He did not write anything he could not have read aloud to the coats at the foot of the road, and he found, when he had finished, that it was a long letter all the same.

He took it down to the post room himself and gave it to Seln for Bracken's pouch, and Seln read the direction.

"Denvash," he said, and put it in the pouch without another word.

---

The argument came on the Fourth-day night, in the reading room, and it was the first real one any of them had had since the case was opened.

It began with paper, as most things in that room did. Karis had cleared the cloth at the table's near end and laid out on it, in rows, a set of slips in her small upright hand. Each slip was a heading, and under each heading was a column of citations in pencil, and at the top of the first row, on a slip by itself, she had written *If leave is given.*

"The second paragraph," she said, before anybody asked. "*Of the seat's hearing, and who may be heard.* The advocate of record heard at the close, by the seat's leave. The house's counsel thinks the seat will give it, if it's asked properly and early." She squared the first row with the side of her hand. "So I'm writing a closing. In bones, for now. The flesh after."

Lira was in the window seat with her right foot up on the cushion and Rooke's red book on her knee. She had been reading the book, or looking at it, and now she looked at the slips.

"What for?" she said.

Karis did not look up. "To close."

"I know what a closing's for." Lira put the book down. "I mean what for, now. We know what reads him. We know the seat runs on it, and so does the evaluation, and the re-evaluation's the delivery, whichever way it goes. Your bones are going to stand up in front of a hood that already knows what it's going to say." She said it carefully, because she was fond of Karis and Karis was not looking at her. "I'm not saying don't. I'm asking what it's for."

Karis's pencil stopped.

"It's for the record," she said.

"Whose?"

"The record." Karis put the pencil down. "The minute. What goes up under the hood and comes back initialled and stays in the registry's presses for as long as there's a registry. If the seat rules against him, it should have to rule against that." She touched the first slip. "Against every word of it, on paper, in its own minute."

Brom had been sitting on the bench by the door with his kettle on its shelf beside him, unlit, and he spoke now, slowly, which meant he had been thinking about it for some time.

"I can't read my docket anymore," he said.

They looked at him.

"The district's confirmation. *Outcome anticipated: advancement, per title.* I used to take it out at night and read it." He did not take it out now. "It's a clerk's round hand. It's a good sheet. It says three assessors and a sigil frame will find me an Iron, and they will. And I look at it now and all I can see is a form." He rubbed his thumb along his knee. "It's not that I think it's false. I think it's true. I just can't see it anymore without seeing the drawer it'll go in."

Nobody answered him for a moment.

"That is what knowing costs," said Seln.

He was at the middle of the far side, where he always sat, with his hands flat and his cup turned, and he had not said anything all evening.

"I filed true returns for fifteen years," he said, "knowing what they were for. Every word of every one of them was right. You get used to it." He moved one finger on the cloth. "You should not."

Cael had been sitting on the end of the bench under the lamp with the satchel on his knees. He had not meant to say anything; he had meant to listen, and to let Karis and Lira have it out, because they would have it out more honestly without him. But the words had been in him since Ostrand, and since before Ostrand, since the stair after the four findings, and he found that they were coming whether he meant them to or not.

"I don't believe it anymore," he said.

Karis looked at him.

"The text. I wrote on the first night of the year that it had held me every time the people failed. I believed it. I believed it in the dairy at full strength." He made himself go on. "The road took the room away, and the case took the rest. I've stopped believing the text is ground. It's a floor somebody laid over something, and they lay it where they like." He looked at the slips. "I don't believe it, and I'm going to go to every sitting anyway. Every one. On time, in my good coat, with my face doing nothing."

"Why?" said Lira. She did not say it unkindly. She wanted to know.

"Because of the coastal man," said Cael. "The one who wrote his letter and went home. The default quoted him. His own words, in the second paragraph, as the reason." He looked at Karis, because she had read it to them in the dairy. "If I stop going, they don't have to prove anything. I prove it for them. I walk out of the room and they write down that I walked, and that's their whole case, signed by me." He shook his head. "I'm not going to hand them that. If they want me in a frame, they'll have to put me in it with their own hands, in their own minute, with Karis's closing standing in front of it."

"And if they do?" said Brom, from the bench. "Put you in it?"

"Then there's a ruling. And Karis's door opens, for as long as it opens." Cael did not look at the corner of the table where the old enrollment book had lain for a night. "I'm not saying what we do then. I'm saying what I do until then. I sit in that room every Fifth-day they call, and I let them win the only thing they can win, slowly, on paper, where it can be read afterward."

"That's a long time to sit in a room you don't believe in," said Brom.

"Seln sat fifteen years."

"Do not take me for a pattern," said Seln, quite mildly, and turned his cup a quarter turn.

Karis had gone quite still.

"Then what am I writing?" she said. Her voice was very level. "If it isn't ground. If the closing is a speech to a floor somebody laid where they liked." She looked at her slips, and Cael saw that her pale fingers were pressed flat on the cloth. "I've built everything I am on paper being worth reading properly. You've just told me it isn't."

"I didn't say that."

"You said the text isn't ground."

"I said I don't stand on it anymore." He held her eyes. "I didn't say it's not worth writing. They'll read your closing, and the minute will have it, and when they rule they'll rule against it in writing. That's not nothing. It's just not ground."

She was quiet for a long moment.

"No," she said at last. "It isn't nothing." She picked up the pencil. "All right. Then I'll write it as if it were ground. I'll write every line of it as if the seat could be moved by it, because that's the only way I know how to write a line." She drew a short straight line under *If leave is given*. "That's my cost. I'll pay it."

"You'll hate it," said Lira.

"I already hate it. I've hated it since the leaf." Karis did not look up from the slip. "I'm good at things I hate. Ask anybody who's seen me index."

Lira looked at her for a moment across the room, at the bent head and the hair coming down at the back and the pencil already moving again. Something went out of her shoulders that had been in them since she asked the question.

"Then I'll climb as if it were a ladder," she said, and picked up Rooke's red book, and opened it at the page she had been on. "If you're going to be stubborn about it, I'm not going to be the only one in the room who isn't."

Brom got up and lit the kettle. It took a long time, as it always did, and then it refused to boil, as it always did, and he stood over it with his hands on his hips and told it what he thought of it in a low voice, and Lira laughed, and the room let its breath go.

Nobody had decided anything. Cael sat with the satchel on his knees and understood that nobody was going to, not tonight and not for a long while. It had only been said.

---

On the Seventh-day he walked the lodge with Seln.

They had done it every Seventh-day since the inventory, and the recess did not change it, for the lodge-man let the wing read the arrivals book on a Seventh-day, as he had let it for longer than anybody remembered, and Seln read it as he read everything, from the back.

The road column was the last column on the page, kept since the year of the thieves on the order of the chancellor before last, one line at every bell, for whatever came up the bluff road or went down it. In term it ran to three or four entries a bell, and this week it ran to one or none. *Carter, flour. Post, the bag. None. None. The registrar's man, to town and back. None.*

And at the foot of every day, in the lodge-man's square hand: *Two at the road foot, changed at the bell. Two at the landing.*

"They have not thinned," said Seln.

"No."

"They have not doubled either." He turned back a page. "In the term before the cut, I could read them weekly by the grade of the man. A junior on the market day, a senior on the sitting day. Since the cut, they are all senior, every bell, and they do nothing." He put his finger under *None*. "Nobody at the gate. Nothing in the post that should not be in it. No new face in town at the inn on the wharf. Not a letter, not a knock, not a rider."

"The quiet you read before."

"The same quiet. Read again." He closed the book and gave it back to the lodge-man through his window with a word of thanks. Then he walked with Cael out under the arch and along the outer wall, where the wind came up the bluff off the river, and spoke to the wall rather than to him. "They have not gone. They have stood down. There is a difference. A man who has gone has decided he was wrong. A man who has stood down has decided he does not need to do anything, because what he wants is coming to him by itself." He walked a little way. "The corridor is on schedule. Every sitting sits. Every form is filed. Nobody on this hill has put a foot wrong, and so nobody has to come up the road and make one."

Cael walked beside him and said nothing. Below them, at the foot of the road, the two coats stood under the eaves of the chandler's warehouse with the wet coming off the roof in front of them in a bright line. One of them had a tin cup. They were older men than the pair who had stood there at the start of the year, grey at the temples, heavy in the shoulder, and they had the patience of men who had been told that the work was nearly over and needed only to be seen through.

"I would do the same," said Seln. "In their chair. I have done the same."

That night Cael wrote it in the book at his sill, under the lamp, in the log voice, so that he would have it exactly.

*Seventh-day of the recess's first week. The road column: none, none, the post, none. Four coats, senior, every bell. No third move. Seln's reading of the silence, unchanged: why spend a team when the seat will deliver him at the year's close.*

*They aren't waiting for us to do something. They're waiting for us to do nothing, for a very long time, perfectly. So are we.*

He looked at that last line for a while, and then he left it.

When he had blotted it he went to the window. Across the dark court the wing's counter was shut, and behind it one lamp was burning at the copying table, as it burned every Seventh-day night of the year.

He knew what was being written under it. Seln had told him on the wall that afternoon, in the counter voice, without being asked, as a man might mention what he meant to have for supper.

"This week's will say that the subject remained in residence for the house's recess, with three associates, on slips lodged at the counter. That he went down to the town on the Seventh-day before it, as the hill does. That he was not observed on any floor, there being no floor in a recess. Nothing further to report." He had walked a few steps. "Every word of it true. I will write another next week."

"And the week after."

"Until the year closes. A pattern that changes is a thing somebody writes down."

The lamp in the wing burned a while longer. Then it went out, and Cael stood at his window in the dark and thought about a cupboard under a table, and a row of bundles in it, and the thickest of them at the end with the name of this hill on its label, one sheet thicker tonight than it had been this morning.

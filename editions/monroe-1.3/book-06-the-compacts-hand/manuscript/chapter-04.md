# Chapter 4 — The Honest Floor

Lira unlocked the east hall at a quarter to the eleventh bell on Sixth-day night, with Rooke's key on its string, and the three of them went in and shut the door behind them.

It was dark inside and smelled of new paint and old sweat. Brom had brought two lanterns and a jug of water, and he set the lanterns on the floor at either end of the painted lines and turned them up, and the white crosses and arrows came out of the dark across the boards like the bones of something. Lira went up the gallery stair two at a time and along the gallery, and Cael heard her drawing the shutters on the high windows one after another, all the way down the north wall, until there was no light in the hall but theirs.

She came back down wiping the dust off her hands.

"Karis says she's reading," she said. "She'll take the figures at breakfast. And if anybody breaks anything, she wants to know which thing, and she wants it in writing, in that order."

"Which thing first," said Brom, "or writing first?"

"Which thing. She was very clear." Lira sat on the bottom step of the gallery stair to tape her wrists. "She says writing first is how you end up with a very neat page about the wrong bone." She tore a strip with her teeth. "And before you ask, I asked. Rooke lent me the floor after the tenth bell, any night I want it. He said he didn't want to know what for."

They had not done this since the road. On the road there had been no floor, and in the recess there had been the flag, and for eleven weeks nobody had wanted to see what the suite would do when it reached for one thing and got another. Lira had raised it at supper, on the evening of the fifth day, with her elbows on the table and her voice low. *It's settled. You wrote it settled. So show us.* Brom had said nothing, which from Brom was agreement, and Karis had said she would come if it were any other night of the week, and it was not.

So here they were, three people and two lanterns on a floor somebody had painted for another purpose entirely, at an hour when the rest of the house was asleep or pretending to be.

Brom came over before anything else and laid his hand on Cael's right side, low, where the Shield's forearm had shut on him the morning before. He pressed with two fingers, and Cael breathed in through his teeth.

"Shield?" said Brom.

"Shield."

"You let him."

"I let him."

Brom took his hand away and said nothing about it. He looked at Cael for the space of a breath, with the look he gave a floor before he stood on it, and then went to the far end of the painted lines and began unrolling the wraps for his forearms. It was all the comment Brom would ever make, and Cael felt it settle on him like a coat.

"Rules," said Lira from the stair. "Mine. I've been thinking about them all week, so you don't get to change them." She tore the tape with her teeth. "No band. No rotation. No rate. Whatever you've got, you use it, and whatever it costs, you tell us, and we write it down. Nobody's watching. Nobody's charting. If it goes off the top of a page, there isn't a page."

"And the quiet thing?"

"Stays shut." She did not look up from the tape. "That's not a rule. That's a promise, and it's yours, not mine."

He nodded. That one he had made himself, in a back room at Norhold, with the three of them watching, and it was not a rule of any floor; it was older than floors.

"Then the rest," said Lira, and stood, and rolled her shoulders, and the left one came round as easily as the right. "Me first. I want to see the one I haven't seen since the ring."

---

She ran the four counts at him.

She ran them the way the Stone had taught her, on the white crosses, from the first cross to the fourth, and she did not hold back. The entry. The second step with its long lie of weight on the front foot. The third count, in which nothing happened, which was the hardest of the four to watch because nothing happening was exactly what a fighter could not afford to believe.

On the fourth she burst, off the wrong foot, at the wrong angle.

In wing three, in front of the cards, he would have read her heel and gone with the read and been a fraction late, as an honest Iron would be against a Silver drill, and let the boards say so. Here there was no wing three. The read gave him her heel half a beat early, and he did not wait for anything. He burst into the angle she had lied about. Two bursts, one on the other, the second off the landing of the first, so that he was not where her burst ended. He was beside it.

She came out of her burst and he was there.

Lira stopped dead on the boards. Her face did something he had not seen it do in a long time: it went entirely still, and then it lit from the inside, like a lantern somebody has turned up.

"*There* you are," she said.

"Again?"

"Again."

They ran it nine times in all, and he lost count of his own bursts somewhere about the seventh, because he had not been keeping them; that was the rule. She kept hers. Three in each bout, Rooke's rule, never a fourth, even here. *I'm not breaking Rooke's rule for you,* she said, when he asked. *I'd have to explain it to him, and he'd write one word on my sheet, and it would be the wrong word.* So she had three bursts and he had as many as he wanted, and she still took two of the nine off him. Both times she did it on the third count, the empty one, by doing nothing at all for one beat longer than he could believe anybody would.

He felt the bill come in somewhere after the sixth burst. The thin line from the crest of the hip toward the knee, the one he could have drawn on himself with a pen. At the seventh it widened. At the eighth it went down into the long muscle at the front of the thigh, and stayed there, and began to sing. Five free on these boards. Everything past five was borrowed against the morning, and the morning would collect it.

He sat down on a white cross with his hand on his hip.

"Bill?" said Lira, squatting in front of him.

"Hip, crest to knee, the usual road. The eighth went into the thigh. The ninth, if there was a ninth, went somewhere I'm going to find out about at the first bell." He pressed the place with his thumb. "I went past six at Norhold, in front of the whole city. That cost me about what this did, and I was fighting a Gold who wanted me to last."

"That was one bout." Lira wrote on the back of her hand with a stub of pencil. "This was nine. And tonight you weren't fighting somebody who wanted you to last. You were fighting me." She looked at what she had written. "I made you spend."

"You made me spend."

She sat back on her heels and looked at the painted floor as if she had just been given it. Then she said, more quietly, "He was right. The Stone. He said the drill was only worth anything against somebody who could see it coming. Everybody here can't see it coming. You can." She stood. "I'm going to want to do this again."

"So am I," said Cael, and found that he meant it.

---

Brom had finished wrapping his forearms. He stood at the far end of the painted lines with his feet a shoulder's width apart and his weight sunk into them, and he looked, in the lantern light, like something that had been built there and the hall built round it afterward.

"Give," he said.

Cael went down the floor to him and set his own feet and held up his left forearm across his body.

Brom's Path was Iron Skin, and he could go hard, not all at once and not for long, but enough. When he hit something, he hit it with a forearm that had stopped being entirely flesh, and the something knew. Cael had taken one of those forearms on an honest arm once, long ago at Ardenmere, before he had the give. He had not been able to lift a cup with that hand for three days.

Brom hit him.

It came at him like a beam swung on a rope. And it went nowhere. The give took it in the forearm, all of it, as a pocket takes a dropped coin, and kept it. Nothing went up the arm, and nothing went down through the hip into the floor. Brom's forearm stopped against his as if it had struck a sack of wool, and Brom looked at the place where it had stopped with his eyebrows up.

"Again," said Cael.

Brom hit him again, harder, and the give took that one too, and the hum came up in the bone, deep and low, and began to fade.

The third came before the hum had faded. The give took that too, but Cael felt it take it, the way you feel a cup take water when it is already more than half full. The hum did not fade this time; it stayed in the bone and grew a little, and his fingers began to tingle at the ends.

"Again," he said, and Brom looked at him, and hit him a fourth time.

The give took the fourth. It took it the way a full cup takes one more drop: it held, and the surface stood up over the brim, and trembled. The hum went up his arm as far as the elbow and stopped there, waiting. Cael lowered the arm and stood with his teeth together until the elbow let it go.

"That's the edge," he said, when he could. "Not the strength. The speed. It's a cup. I can pour it out again, but not as fast as you can fill it. Space them out and I could take you all night. Four in a row and the fifth goes somewhere."

"Where?"

"I don't know. I'm not going to find out on you."

Brom considered this, unwrapping and rewrapping the end of one forearm, which was what he did when he was thinking. "At Norhold she was filling it with fire," he said. "From across a ring. Faster than me."

"And it went somewhere."

"Mm." Brom set his feet again. "Push, then."

This was the one Cael had shut his hand on in wing three. He set his right palm flat against Brom's chest, below the collarbone, and Brom set himself to it, the way he set himself against anything, with his weight down in his heels and his knees soft and his whole body arranged to be the thing that did not move.

Cael gave him the push.

Not the flat hand. The real one, the one he had taken from the Bronze at Ardenmere and given to a Gold at Norhold, at fighting load. It went out of his shoulder and down his arm and through his palm into Brom as if he had put the whole of himself behind a cart on a hill.

Brom slid.

His planted feet went back along the boards a foot and a little more, with a long dry squeal of leather on pine, and stopped. He looked down at his feet, and then he looked up, and he was smiling, which Brom did perhaps three times in a year.

"Again," said Brom.

Cael gave him four more. At the fourth his right shoulder spoke, as it always spoke at the fourth. At the fifth it said something else, longer and lower, and he knew he would be counting it on the stair in the morning. Brom slid each time, less each time, because each time he learned a little more about where to put his weight, and at the fifth he slid no more than a hand's breadth.

"Five," said Brom. "Like her."

"Like her."

"Your shoulder."

"Counting it."

Lira, writing on her hand: "Shoulder, five, the fifth counted." She looked up. "Spark?"

He laid two fingers against the iron of Brom's forearm, where the skin had gone hard and dark under the wraps, and gave it the spark. There was a tick like a nail cooling, and the copper smell rose up between them.

Brom looked at his forearm. "Hm," he said, which was a review.

---

"Now the one we came for," said Lira.

She went to the north end of the painted lines and Brom went to the south, and they stood twenty feet apart with Cael between them in the middle of the floor, among the white crosses. The lanterns at either end threw their shadows long across the boards toward him, so that he stood where two shadows met.

"Both of us," said Lira. "Get past."

He knew the shape of it before they moved. Brom would come straight and slow and would not be where Cael wanted him to be. Lira would come fast and from the wrong side, with three bursts and the four counts and the empty third. Between them there was no room on the floor at all, only the place where their two reaches overlapped.

So he made room.

He asked the air to lean, along a line between them, from where he stood to the gap past Brom's left shoulder. Fifteen feet of it, a third deep, and the air tipped like a board stepped on at one end, and his ears closed and opened. Down at his feet the dust in the cracks of the boards woke up and ran, pale, along the lane toward Brom.

And the read went with it.

He felt it go. He had meant to keep the read on Brom's body, on the heels and the hips and the weight going down. Instead, the moment he laid the lane, the read went into the lane itself. It read the air: how hard it leaned, how far it ran, where it would let go. It was a beautiful read. It told him exactly where the end of the lane was. And it told him nothing at all about Brom.

He stepped into the lane and it carried him, fast, toward the gap past Brom's shoulder.

Brom was not there. Brom had moved half a beat before, and the read had not seen it go, because the read was looking at the air.

The forearm came across Cael's chest like a door shutting in a gale. He went down on the boards on his back, and the lane let go above him, and all the breath he owned went up into the dark of the gallery and stayed there.

Brom stood over him, looking down.

"You weren't looking at me," said Brom.

"No." Cael lay on the painted floor and looked at the shuttered windows. "I was looking at the road."

He had known it. He had written it in the year's ledger at Norhold, in the guesting-house, before the road home: *at Gold it will do one or the other and not both.* He had learned it in the ring, which was a bad place to learn things. Now he had learned it again on a floor where the worst that could happen was Brom, and he lay there and let the knowing go all the way down into him, past the place where he knew things to the place where he did them.

The read could take a body or it could take the air. It could not take both at once. Not at Gold. Not yet.

He sat up.

"Again," he said. "I'm going to lay it first, before I need it, and then give the read back to you. The lane doesn't need watching once it's down. You do."

Brom went back to the south end without a word, and Lira was already at the north.

The second time, he laid the lane while they were still walking to their marks. It ran from his feet to the gap past Brom's left shoulder, fifteen feet, a third deep, and the dust ran down it and gathered at the end. Then he took the read off it, deliberately, the way you take your hand off a dog you have told to stay, and sent the read to Brom.

Brom came. His weight went down into his right heel half a beat before he moved. The read had it.

Cael stepped into the lane.

It carried him down past the place where Brom's forearm was going to be and was not yet. Lira's burst came off the wrong foot at the wrong angle and met nothing; she had read him going one way and the lane had taken him a third faster than any feet could. He came out of the end of the lane past Brom's shoulder, behind both of them, and stopped.

Then the cost came in.

It was not in the hip and it was not in the shoulder. It was behind the eyes, a heaviness, as if somebody had laid a warm hand over the inside of his skull. And everything else he carried went slow with it. He reached for the Wind, out of curiosity, the way you test a stair, and it came, but it came late: a beat late, the way a dog comes when it has decided to come in its own time. He counted. For ten counts after the lane, everything answered him a beat behind his asking. At eleven it was gone, and the hand had lifted off the inside of his head, and the Wind came when he called it.

Lira had turned round, and Brom had turned round, and they were both looking at him.

"What did that cost?" said Lira.

He told her exactly: behind the eyes, ten counts of everything slow, gone at eleven. She wrote it on her hand under the shoulder and the hip, small, because she was running out of hand. Then she stopped writing and looked up at him.

"You wrote it settled," she said. "The flag. You wrote nothing pulls."

"Nothing does." He had been turning it over himself since the count ran out. "Pull was the wrong thing answering. The push when I called the Wind, the read when I wanted my feet. That's gone. This is the right thing answering late." He thought back to the second floor in the dark, and the long while he had stood waiting for a pull that never came before he called anything at all. "At dawn I stood still after the lane until I was sure. I must have stood through the whole ten without counting them. Tonight was the first time I reached for anything inside them."

Lira looked at her palm. "So the flag's down, and this is the bill."

"The flag's down. This is the bill."

She wrote *bill, not flag* under the ten counts, and underlined it.

"Ten counts," said Brom, "in a real bout."

"Is a long time."

"Is a long time," Brom agreed.

Cael sat on a painted cross and thought about the notice.

He had it by heart. Every notice he had ever had he knew by heart, because each of them had found him somewhere with nothing else to look at, a yard, a stair, a shut room, and the one from Norhold on one knee on a stone floor, with a healer holding his hand. *Note: pressure-differential component; corridor-seeding component. Short range.* At the time it had read like a clerk's description of somebody else's coat. Tonight he thought he could put his hand on each line of it. The lane was the corridor; he seeded it, and it grew where he put it, and stayed while he left it alone. The tilt of the air, the ears, the dust running downhill, that was the pressure. Short range was fifteen feet. And the notice had said nothing at all about the read going into the air and leaving the body behind, or about ten counts of everything slow. Notices never told you what a thing cost. They only told you what it was. The cost you found out on a floor, with friends, if you were lucky, and in front of eight thousand people if you were not.

They sat down on the floor afterward among the white crosses, the three of them, with the jug of water going round and the lanterns burning low. Lira lay on her back with her taped hands on her stomach and her eyes shut. Brom sat with his legs out in front of him, unwinding his wraps slowly, a turn at a time, rolling each length up as he went.

And in the gap, it came.

He did not feel it begin. He was sitting on the boards with the jug in his hands, and then he was a little less than that, thinner, a place the lantern light slid over on its way to the wall.

"One," said Lira, without opening her eyes.

*Hold.*

He gave his attention to Brom. To Brom's hands, rolling up the wrap, turn after turn, the big careful fingers smoothing each length down before the next went over it. He watched the wrap grow into a neat tight roll the size of a fist. Watching Brom was not hiding, and the thinness went out of him like breath.

"Back," said Lira, still with her eyes shut.

"How did you know? You weren't looking."

"I didn't need to look. The room went quieter." She opened her eyes and turned her head on the boards. "That's the first this week?"

"The second."

"It's going down."

It was going down. It went down a little every month, and it had never stopped. Rent from waking, every day, spent or not. He had never used it, not once, not even in the last corner of the hardest fight of his life, when it had offered itself to him like a hand held out. It was sealed for a debt, and the debt was to a quiet man who sat at the middle of the bench and cut his bread into squares, and who did not know it was owed, or what for. Nobody had told him yet. The four of them had decided that at Norhold, on the night of the seal. Not yet. When it could be told whole.

He sat with that for a while, and did not say it, because the other two knew it as well as he did.

Brom set the rolled wrap down on the boards beside him, square to a painted cross.

"That's what goes up to wing three," he said. "Every Fifth-day. All that. And you take it as far as the door and leave it there and go in with the rest."

"Yes."

"And you'll do it again next Fifth-day, and the one after."

"For as long as the year asks."

Brom picked up the second wrap and began to roll it.

"Then once a week," he said, "you come here. Here, with us, and you take all of it out of the bag and use it, every piece, and we write down what it costs." He pressed the wrap smooth with his thumb. "So you don't forget it's yours. A man who keeps a thing shut long enough starts to think the shut is the thing."

Lira turned her head on the boards and looked at Brom for a long moment.

"That's the most you've said since Norhold," she said.

"I was saving it." Brom finished the roll and set it beside the first. "Once a week."

"Once a week," said Cael.

Lira held up her hand to the lantern and read it aloud, squinting at the pencil marks running across her palm and down onto her wrist. "Hip, crest to knee, the eighth into the thigh. Shoulder, five, the fifth counted. Forearm: four in a row, the fourth to the elbow; it's a cup, not a wall. Spark: one, on Brom, who said hm. The lane: fifteen feet, a third deep. First time, the read went into the air and Brom put him on the floor. Second time, lane first, read second, through. After: behind the eyes, ten counts slow, gone at eleven." She lowered the hand. "One drift. Held." She looked at her palm. "Karis is going to make me copy this out three times because of my capitals."

"She'll make you copy it out four times," said Brom, "if you tell her the bit about the floor."

"I'm telling her the bit about the floor first," said Lira. "Which thing, then writing." She sat up. "Bed. Somebody's hip is going to send its bill at the first bell, and I want to be awake to hear about it."

---

The hip sent its bill at the first bell, with interest, as promised.

He took the residence stair one step at a time on Seventh-day morning and went down to the north wall to let the sun get into it. It was a rest day. The house was mostly asleep, or pretending, and the Crown yard lay empty under the long wall, where the porter's boards stood chalked fresh with the year's names. Down in the valley the two coats stood at the foot of the road and the two at the landing, and none of them looked up.

He sat on the coping by the third buttress with his leg out straight, and after a while he noticed Bracken.

The records hall stood across the court from the wall, its long roof and its row of tall windows facing east toward the valley. In the highest window, the one at the north end that lit the stair to Bracken's rooms, a man was standing. He had a small brass glass in his hand, the kind a ship's officer carries, and every so often he lifted it and looked through it down the valley, toward the place where the bluff road came up out of the trees. Then he lowered it, and stood, and lifted it again.

Cael watched him do it four times. Then he got up, and took the long way round the court so as not to come at the records hall from the direction of the wall, and went in by the side door and up the stair.

Bracken was still at the window when Cael reached the landing. He did not turn round.

"Good morning," said Bracken. "Your hip is bad. I heard you on the stair."

"Good morning." Cael stood beside him and looked down the valley. From up here you could see the whole of the bluff road's last two switchbacks, and the bend below them, and a long stretch of the river road beyond the bridge, all of it pale and empty in the early sun. "You were at the lecture range's windows on First-day."

"I was."

"And this is the seventh day."

"It is." Bracken lifted the glass again, looked, and lowered it. He was quiet for a while. Then he said, as if Cael had asked a question, which in a way he had: "The entry they wrote on you at Norhold had one word at its end. *Referred.* A referral is a letter, in my trade. A letter that has gone up a road gets an answer that comes down one." He turned the glass over in his hands. "I've kept this office for thirteen years. I've never yet seen an answer come down a road that I didn't see coming a minute before it reached the gate. I see no reason to begin now."

"How long do they usually take?"

"Longer than this, for a small question." Bracken put the glass in his coat. "This is not a small question."

He did not say anything else, and Cael did not ask him to. They stood together at the high window for a while longer, a narrow grey man and a boy with a bad hip, looking at an empty road in the sun. A cart came up out of the trees on the lowest switchback, loaded with hay, a farmer walking at the horse's head. Bracken took the glass out again, and looked, and put it away.

"Hay," he said.

That night Cael wrote it in Hesk's book under the honest floor's costs, which Lira had copied out for Karis three times in capitals and once more for him.

*Seventh-day. Bracken at his high window with a ship's glass. Seven days he has watched the road, and he says the question isn't small. I believe him. I have never known him to round a number.*

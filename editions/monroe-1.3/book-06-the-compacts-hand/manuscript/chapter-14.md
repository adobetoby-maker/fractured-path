# Chapter 14 — Standing Distribution

The seal had gone in crooked.

Vastin held the petition up to the window and tilted it until the light lay along the wax. The Archmarshal's mark was all there, and the ring of small letters round it, but its left edge was shallow, almost a ghost, where the press had bitten at a slant. It was the press's fault. He had screwed it to the corner of the desk himself on his first morning in this room, nineteen years ago, in a hurry and out of true, and had never straightened it. In nineteen years it had never mattered.

He found he was asking himself whether a seal with a faint edge could be challenged.

It could not, and he knew it could not; he had sat on a hundred inquiries into a hundred seals and never seen a petition refused because a press was old. But the question had come up in him on its own, from somewhere under the morning, and an evaluator who will not listen to a foolish question has only taught himself to stop hearing his instruments. So he put the petition down and went looking for where the question had come from.

It had come from the ninth line of a printed list.

The list was on a slip pinned to the bundle that had come up in the morning file. *For the evaluator's file, per standing distribution.* Under those words were printed the offices that received such things as a matter of course: the district seats, the stations' office, the archive, the weather clerks, eleven offices in all. The ninth line was his. Senior evaluator, the Compact. Between the stations' office and the weather clerks.

Under the slip was the instrument itself, and at its last leaf, where petitions keep the table of authorities they rely on, there were nine lines. Eight were sections of the code. The seventh, in a clerk's even type, was his. *Advisory of the Compact's senior evaluator upon the fielding clause (adopted).* He had not needed to turn to the paragraph; he had written those three sentences in this chair. Somebody had filed his title between a section on frames and a section on discretion.

At the bottom of the bundle was the minute of a first sitting at Ostrand. It was dated yesterday.

He had rung for the clerk then, he remembered, and asked whether anything had come up with the bundle: a covering letter, a line from anybody, a question. The clerk had looked through the wallet twice. "No, sir. Only the slip." Vastin had thanked him and sent him down, and sat on with that answer, which was the whole of the matter in four words.

Down in the paved court the porter was folding his paper bag along its old creases. The pigeons had been fed late, and were still walking about on the stones in the sun, as if they suspected there might be more.

His left hand lay on the blotter beside the petition. Some years ago it had begun to dislike mornings; lately it had added evenings, and it did not much care for noon. He opened it and closed it, twice, slowly, and watched it do as it was told. Three trays stood at his right in the order he had kept since before he had a desk worth the name, *in*, *held*, *done*, and the petition belonged in none of them yet.

He opened the lowest drawer at the far end of the long cabinet, where he kept the few things in the room that were his, and took out a letter he had kept for some years without ever deciding why.

*Will my father's name ever come off the list?*

The hand was careful, and had plainly practised on other paper before it trusted itself to this sheet. Her father had been a district officer on the coast who took money to enter a rank in a station book that no frame had read. There had been one other, before him: a hill-station keeper who let a cracked housing read high for six years and covered it with false returns. Both findings were his, both sound, both sent down this stair; and somewhere below, in an office whose door he had never opened, the rest had followed. He had answered the girl himself, with one word, because a longer answer would only have given her another sentence to wait on.

He put the letter back. The seal on the petition was his own, faint edge and all, and it would carry what was under it. A fear with a name is a thing a man can work beside.

He rang for the clerk.

---

It was evening before he saw the blot.

He had gone out to the outer office to sign the day's dispatch ledger, as he did each evening. The line for his petition was there in the clerk's tidy upright hand: the date, the number, and in the routing column *Registry seat, Ostrand, institutional proceedings*. But at the head of the routing column, just before the first letter, there was a small round blot, where a pen had been held over the page a moment too long before it came down.

He remembered the moment. He had been at the inner door. He had seen the pen stop, and had said the route aloud so that the man would not have to ask it, and had given no reason. A reason carried down a stair in somebody's head stops being the Archmarshal's petition and becomes the Archmarshal's quarrel. The pen had come down.

A quarter of an hour later the clerk had come back up with the dispatch slip and laid it by the crooked press, where slips went, and then he had not gone. He had stood with his hands at his sides looking at the slip, and then at the Archmarshal's left hand open on the blotter, and then at nothing. Vastin had waited, and nothing had come, and after a while the man had nodded and gone out to his own desk. But he had left the inner door a hand's breadth wider than he usually left it, and twice before noon Vastin had heard his chair creak as he turned in it. A clerk who watches is a clerk who has noticed there is something to see.

Nine years of that hand, and it had never blotted a line. In nine years the outer office had only ever sent paper out of this room one way, back to whoever had asked for it. A desk like this did not send a finding toward a proceeding that had not called for one, and the clerk's pen had known it before the clerk did.

Vastin signed beneath the blot and went back into his room and took down the letter-book.

He did not need it. He knew what he would find. But he went through it anyway, the way a man going out on a long road checks a buckle he knows is fastened. There they were: four acknowledgments in forty years, pasted in where they had come, each from a seat that had not sent for his finding and had received it anyway. A district's, from a dozen years back, thanking him without rancour for a finding that had cost the district its own petition. A hill station's, whose frame had come out of its housing on his evidence. A guild hall's. A sponsorship quarrel so old the guild's name had faded. Every one of them had answered inside a fortnight.

He closed the book. It had not steadied him. Each of those four seats had wanted to know something.

What steadied him, a little, was the sheet from the east room.

It was in the boy's file in the same drawer, the clerk's ruled page from the room twelve feet by fourteen on the bluff. The first thing his eye went to, as it always did, was not any of his questions but the line near the foot where one was written going the other way, the boy's to him: *What is the Compact evaluating? Me, or the provision?* The second thing was the line beneath his own eleventh question. He had ruled it himself, before the interview, for a twelfth, the way he always ruled one more line than he expected to use. It was empty.

He knew what would have gone on it. The boy kept a private book of records and had said so: *so I can't lie to myself later*. The obvious question was what the book said. He had looked at it across four feet of table for less than a second and not asked it, because a private book was not in the record, and an officer of his height who reaches for whatever is in reach has begun to collect. He had once spent ten years helping to take a collector apart.

He took a scrap of paper and wrote a sentence on it, to see whether it was true.

*A careful evaluation leaves the proceedings nothing of the evaluator's to use but his manners.*

It was true. They had used his manners. They were in the seventh line of a table of authorities.

He struck the sentence through and put the scrap in the stove, and put the ruled sheet back in the file, and went home.

---

On the third evening there was a pinned note on a copy in his *held* tray, and he had moved the copy twice.

*Second frame reading: within tolerance.* The note was in a young man's hand, a hand still deciding what shape it meant to be. It was pinned to the copy of a station's Kindling entry, four years old, for a girl in the northern district: her Path, her first rank, in a station clerk's ordinary writing.

The junior who had pinned it had come up the too-steep stair that afternoon with the girl's file under his arm, nervous and good, and laid it out on the desk because Vastin had made him lay it out himself. Her floor work this season had run on ahead of what her station had written of her, by a margin he had measured four ways and could not make go away. "She doesn't do anything wrong, sir," the junior had said. "That's what I keep coming back to. She does everything her Path says she should. She just does it as if the station had written down somebody a year younger." He had turned his notes round so that Vastin could read them. "I measured it with the frame, and by the clock, and by her bouts, and against the tables. It's inside tolerance every way I measure it. I still don't like it."

They had gone through it for an hour: the station's line, his notes, the tolerances for a girl of that age and Path. Everything was sound. She had grown faster than a station had guessed.

Under the station's line, in the junior's hand, was the recommendation they had settled between them: *Continue observation.* The hand was still deciding what shape it meant to be, and seeing it, Vastin had remembered a smoking stove in a district house a long time ago, and his own file under his own arm, and a senior evaluator, long since a name on a plaque, telling him the thing every evaluator is told once: *Write what the instrument says. What you feel about it is information about you.* He had handed the same sentence on that afternoon, in other words. It had been easier to say than to hear.

He did not write anything about the copy. He put it back where it was.

He turned down the lamp.

The next morning, because he could not settle to the tray, he went down the too-steep stair and across the paved court to the house's frame shop, which he did perhaps twice a year.

It was a long low room behind the porter's lodge that smelled of oil and cut brass. A station frame stood in the middle of it with its housing off, the way a clock stands with its back off, all its insides showing. The mender was an old man in an apron with a lens screwed into one eye, and he did not stand up when the Archmarshal came in, because he had his hands inside the frame and it would not have been safe.

"From the river station, sir," he said, without looking round. "The one that reads slow in the damp. They want it put right."

"Is it wrong?"

The mender took his hands out, slowly, and wiped them, and considered the question with the respect due to it. "No, sir. It's telling them it's damp. Every reading it gives on a wet morning is a fraction late, and the fraction's always the same, and if they wrote it down they'd know it to the hair." He tapped the housing. "They don't want it to tell them about the damp. They want it to read the same wet or dry. So I'll put a shim in, and it'll stop telling them, and they'll be happier, and it'll be a worse frame." He put his hands back in. "I've said so in my note. They won't read the note."

Vastin stood a while and watched him work. Then he went back up the stair, and did not think about the frame again for most of the day. He found it in his mind that evening as he put the copy back in *held* one more time: a true instrument, and men who would rather it said something easier.


---

There was a wrapper in the waste-basket, and he did not remember throwing it there.

It was the outer wrapper of a seat-courier's packet, heavy grey paper stamped across in red, *BY SEAT COURIER. BY HAND.* He looked at it for some time. Then he looked at the boy's folder lying shut on his blotter, and opened it, and found on top of his own nine leaves a single sheet of registry-seat stock with a seal pressed into it.

He did not remember putting it there either.

He went back for it, a step at a time, as he would have made a witness go back. A tread on the stair that had not been his clerk's tread: single steps, quick, the whole flight, no pause at the top. The clerk in the doorway with the packet held out in both hands. His own face, arranged. Then the two sentences. Then, it seemed, the folder, and the window, and a long while of the porter and the pigeons that had gone into him without leaving any trace at all.

*The proceedings' evaluation seat is fully constituted. Supplementary findings are not contemplated by the schedule.*

He read it again, to be sure it said what he remembered. It did.

By noon there was a refusal in *done* that had taken him a quarter of an hour to write, where such things usually took him a quarter of a minute. A guild hall had asked for more time to answer a finding on its own assessors, and he had said no; but he had said it at length, giving the hall the section it had failed to meet, the officer it might apply to instead, and the date by which it should. He had signed it with his name entire.

Under it in the tray lay the first sheet he had started, with a single half-line on it, struck through corner to corner so that it could still be read. *The hall's request for further time is not contemplated by*

He did not throw that sheet away. He had taught that line to every junior he ever had.

Then he took a slip of paper and wrote a question on it.

*Minute of the 40th: evaluation seat, to be called. Refusal of the 44th: fully constituted. Who sat down in those five chairs in four days, without asking this office for the file?*

He did not try to answer it. He pinned it to the denial, and closed the folder over both.

---

"Will you be getting home tonight, sir? It's set in to rain."

The clerk had said it from the doorway at the sixth bell, with the day's wallet against his chest, looking not at the desk but past it at the window, where the evening had gone close and grey. It was the only thing he had ever said in that room in nine years that had nothing to do with paper.

Vastin had looked up from the plain sheets he was writing on. "Later than usual," he had said. "But yes."

The clerk had gone down without another word, and come back a few minutes later, unasked, with a candle already lit in a tin dish, and set it on the corner of the desk by the crooked press. The house put out the lamps on that floor at the half-bell after six. In the morning Vastin found the candle in the consumables ledger, entered in the clerk's tidy hand against the Archmarshal's name and not the floor's. He had not told him to put it there. He left it as it was.

He opened the lowest drawer to see that the folder was in it. It was. It had a tab, and the tab had a heading in his own hand, the only one of its kind he had written in forty years, because every finding he had ever made had come with somewhere to be sent.

*Pending a proceeding worth sending it to.*

He did not take the nine pages out. He had read them through once last night by the candle, with the rain starting on the glass, and then he had done a thing he did not often do: he had read the last paragraph aloud to the empty room, quietly, to hear whether it would stand being spoken.

"Offered to the proceedings at Ostrand under the code's two provisions; declined without citation as outside their schedule. The schedule itself is not disputed. One thing only is set down. A schedule that leaves no room for the one personal assessment of the subject the Compact holds was drawn for a purpose the evaluator was never told and cannot name from his desk. Where the outcome of an evaluation is settled before the evaluator arrives, it is not the subject who is being measured."

It had stood.

The rest he could have said without the pages. That the proceedings had in their hands already the one honest instrument there was, three years of the record, kept by officers who could see. That a re-evaluation whose answer was known in advance was a procedure wearing an evaluation's coat. That when the standard protocol fell silent in front of this boy the silence was the protocol's own. That the protocol could not read him. Somewhere in the middle was a sentence he had spoken at inquiries for forty years about frames, *the instrument is not at fault; it has told you where its reach ends*, and last night, for the first time, he had written it about a person, and sat with his pen lifted for a long while before he let it stand.

He had gone home at the ninth bell, later than usual, as he had said he would. The porter had been at the lodge door with his lamp, looking out at the rain, and had said "Late, sir," in the voice of a man noting a thing for the record, and Vastin had agreed that it was. The street had been empty and loud with water. He had walked it without hurrying, with his collar up and his left hand in his coat, and he had found that he was not thinking about the seat, or the five chairs, or who had sat down in them. He was thinking about a boy standing still in a frame that had stuttered and gone dark, three days on the bluff, not moving, not asking what it meant, waiting to be told what the instruments had found. And he was thinking that, of all the officers who had stood in rooms with that boy, he was the one who had written down what that silence meant, and that nobody was going to be allowed to read it.

It was not a theory. He did not hold with theories about subjects. It was only a thing he had noticed, walking, and he let it walk beside him to his door, and left it on the step.

Now he closed the drawer. At the head of the first page, under the subject and the basis and the date, on the line the sheet kept for the name of whoever a finding was meant for, he had written nothing at all.

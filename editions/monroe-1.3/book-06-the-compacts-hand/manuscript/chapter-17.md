# Chapter 17 — Under the Gallery

Wing three had a gallery after all.

Cael had fought in the room every Fifth-day for a year without once looking up at the west wall above the door. There had never been anything there worth looking at: a narrow timber balcony, a rail of dark old oak, and behind the rail a stack of spare benches and broken frames that the porters had been putting there since before he came up the hill. The house had built the gallery in the days when wing three was its examination room, so that visiting assessors could look down on a candidate without standing on the same boards. Nobody had visited for a long time. The benches had gone up into it one at a time, the way things go into a loft.

On the Fourth-day morning the benches were gone. The porters had swept the boards up there and rubbed the rail, and in the corners where the broom had not reached, the dust lay in soft grey drifts that showed exactly how long it had been. Four people stood at the rail.

He knew two of them. The pair from the east hall, in their travelling coats, each with his board. The one who wrote stood at the left. The one who did not write stood beside him with his hands behind his back. The third was a woman in the blue-grey of the lake house, with a folding board open on the rail in front of her and three pencils laid out on it in a row. The fourth was an old man in a fur collar who had brought a stool with him and sat on it, so that only his head and shoulders showed above the oak, like a judge at a country fair.

The rest of the room was what it always was. Gault at the trestle in the middle of the north side, with the instrument case set down very straight beside him. The Ash instructor on his right with her book against her chest, the Mire instructor on his left with a pencil behind each ear. The desk clerk at the trestle's end with the wing's book open. And at the east end of the room, at the rail under the high windows, the cohort, more of them than usual, with Ephram in the middle holding his soft-folded chart.

Rooke stood beside Ephram.

He was not on any list for wing three. He had never come to a provision bout in the year Cael had been fighting them. He stood at the rail with his arms folded and his eyes on the gallery over the west door, and he did not look at Cael at all.

Cael went to his chalk at the south end. He did not take Hesk's book out of his coat. The line had been written at the sill the night before, and he had it by heart.

The Current came in from the south door.

She was a third-year from the movement floor, Iron Rank Four by the porter's wall, a tall girl with long legs and a quiet face, who glided the way water goes over a weir: smooth, and a little faster at the end than you expected. He had watched her in the Crown yard. He had never stood across a floor from her.

She came over and shook his hand, and looked up once, quickly, at the gallery.

"Is that for you?" she said.

"I think so."

"Then I'm sorry about the first exchange," she said, "because I'm going to take it." And she went to her chalk.

Gault turned the glass.

"Exchange," said the desk clerk.

She opened the way the textbook said a Current should open, which was the way she always opened: two glides in, the second one bending out wide to his left, so that the strike came off the end of the curve from a place he had not been looking.

He gave her the read.

It went out under thought and came back with her weight. On the first glide she carried it in her back hip, which was right. On the second, half a beat before the bend began, her weight moved into the outside of her front foot, and he knew where the curve would end before she did.

And he could see the other thing, as clearly as he had seen it at the sill: Lira's answer, one burst, low, *along* the bend instead of across it, and himself standing at the end of the curve with his palm out when the Current arrived.

The book said the first exchange was hers.

He did not burst. He stepped the way an honest Iron steps against a Current, a fraction late, and met the end of her curve with his forearm up. Her strike came off the bend and took him across the forearm, flat and fast, and the give was there, Reydan's give, waiting to drink the whole of the blow into the arm and keep it. He shut it. He let the blow go on up the arm the way it would have gone in anybody, into the elbow and then the shoulder, and felt it arrive there like a door slammed somewhere in a house.

"Touch," said Gault. "Current."

Nought to one.

Up in the gallery a pencil moved. He did not look. He did not need to; the read did not reach that far, but he had stood in enough rooms to know the sound a board makes when a man leans his wrist on it to write.

He walked back to his chalk with the forearm ringing and set his feet.

Now, with the arm ringing, he felt what the line had meant when he wrote it. Every exchange from here would be read twice: once by three cards that had watched him for a year, and once again tonight, by lamplight, in an inn by the river, against a bound book of every bout he had ever fought in public. He was walking across a floor in another man's boots, exactly in his footprints, while the man's tailor watched from the stairs.

"Exchange," said the clerk.

---

She came in the same way. Two glides and the bend.

This time he used the read and not the burst. As her weight went into the outside of her front foot, he walked, at an ordinary walking pace, to the place where the bend would end. It was three strides. Nobody could have called it anything but timing. When she came off the curve he was already standing there, a little to the side of where she meant to arrive, and she saw him and checked in the middle of her glide, which a Current hates to do, because the whole of their Path is in not checking.

She checked well. She cut the glide short and turned her strike into a sweep, low, at his front knee.

He spent the first burst.

Low and lateral, off the left foot, to her outside; the boards came up under him eight feet on and the landing beat held him there, behind her sweep, at her back. His right hand was on her shoulder blade before the sweep finished.

"Touch," said Gault. "Provision."

One to one.

On the east rail somebody in the cohort let out a breath. Ephram's pencil moved on the soft-folded paper. Rooke did not move at all.

The Current shook her head at herself as she walked back to her chalk, and Cael saw her lips move: counting. She was a good fighter. She was already working out what she would do differently, and she would do it in the third.

She did.

She came out of her chalk without any bend at all. Straight glides, short and quick, one after another, driving forward on a line that pointed at his chest, so that there was no curve for the read to tell him the end of. A Current going straight was a slower Current, and she knew it, and she paid the speed to take the bend away from him. She walked him back. Four strides. Five. He took the ground she asked for, because the line said nothing about the third and because a careful Iron gives ground to a straight Current until the Current runs out of floor.

He ran out of floor first.

The west wall came up behind his heels. He felt it before he saw it, the change in the air at his back, and with it something else: the gallery was directly above him now. He could hear the old man's stool creak. If he looked up he would be looking straight into four faces, and they would be looking straight down at the top of his head.

He did not look up.

The Current set her last glide. He felt her weight go into it through the boards, the read giving him the half-beat before her hip committed, and in that half-beat he could have done any of four things that the house's book had never seen. He did the one the book had seen. He spent the spark.

He spent it late, as the line said: as her lead hand came in, two fingers laid on the back of her wrist for an instant and gone. The copper smell came up off his hand. Her wrist jumped as if a wasp had found it, and the strike went wide, past his ear, into the whitewash with a slap. She was not touched. There was nothing for the cards. But her glide had broken, and she had to step back off the wall to set it again, and while she was setting it the glass ran out.

"Exchange," said the clerk. "Fourth."

Cael came off the wall with the smell of copper on his fingers and the whole front of his left arm aching from the first exchange. Up in the gallery the lake house officer picked up her second pencil.

She had learned twice now, and she came out for the fourth as if she had decided what kind of fighter he was.

She shortened everything. A single glide, and then a step, and then a single glide, so that her weight was never anywhere long enough for the read to give him much. It was clever. It was the cleverest thing anybody had done against him in wing three since the Shield had learned to turn on the ball of his foot, and it took away the timing that a strong Iron would have relied on. A strong Iron would have had nothing left.

He had one burst left in the line.

He stood still and let her come. Glide. Step. Glide. On the third glide her front hip told him which way the strike would go, a quarter-beat early and no more. He went inside it. He did it with the second burst, but he spent it small, a short low burst of a stride and a half, so short that from above it would look like a quick step with good knees behind it and not like a Wind fighter's burst at all. That was a thing he had learned this year and had never written down anywhere except in the rotation's private margins: how to spend a burst so that it looked like less than it was.

He came up inside her strike, beside her, and laid his palm on her shoulder as she turned.

"Touch," said Gault. "Provision. Two to one."

The Current stood a moment with her hand on her shoulder where his palm had been. Then she laughed, short, the way people laugh when a sum comes out right after a long time, and came across and shook his hand again.

"I was sure you'd go round," she said.

"I nearly did."

"No, you didn't," she said, and looked at him a moment longer than she needed to, and went to get her kit.

---

The four visitors came out of wing three by the west door with their boards under their arms and went across the outer court toward the east hall, to see the afternoon's Silver drills from its gallery, and at the foot of the gallery stair they found Rooke.

He was on one knee on the bottom step, threading a chain through the stair's rail. It was a long chain, the kind the porters used on the cart-shed doors, and he had already run it twice round the newel post. As the four of them came up he drew the last link through, set a padlock in it, and snapped the padlock shut. Then he stood up and hung a card on the chain, square in the middle, where it would be read by anybody who came to the stair. The card was in his square capitals. *PROGRAM FLOOR CLOSED TO OBSERVERS. BY ORDER.*

Cael saw it from the covered walk, where he had gone with his coat over his aching arm, and he stopped to watch.

The man from Rhagen who wrote things down said something courteous. Cael could not hear it, but he could see the shape of it: a visiting officer's surprise, a question about the house's custom, a slight lift of the board to show that he had signed the lodge book like everybody else. The man who did not write said nothing. The lake house officer looked at the card and then at her three pencils. The old man in the fur collar had brought his stool, and he set it down on the stones and sat on it, as if to see what would happen.

Rooke answered with one word. Cael did not hear that either, but Ephram, who had come out of wing three behind the visitors and was close enough, told him afterward what it was. It was *No.* The Rhagen man had then asked, very politely, by whose order, since the card said *by order*, and Rooke had looked at him for a while and said, "Mine," and that had been the second word. Then Rooke turned his back on them and went in at the hall's side door, and a moment later the shutters began to close along the gallery's high windows, one after another, all down the north wall, from the inside.

The four officers stood on the stones for a little while. Then they went away toward the gate, the old man carrying his stool.

By the noon meal the whole house knew. The program would drill under lamps from now on, four of them, set on the painted floor at the corners of the crosses.

It was only then, over his soup, that Cael let himself go back over the end of the bout, because the chain had come first in his mind and the figure second, and he found that he wanted to know why.

Gault had added the three cards in his head, and again on the back of his hand. *Twenty-two and two-thirds. Entered.* The clerk had written it in the wing's book. Up in the gallery four pencils had written it too, and Cael had heard them. Ephram had come down the room from the east rail with the soft-folded chart and entered it below the Blade, in his cramped square hand, *gave her the first, bad arm*, and had not drawn a question mark beside it. Then he had looked up past Cael's shoulder at the man from Rhagen.

"They've written the same number I have."

"It's the number."

"I know it's the number." Ephram had folded the chart along its old creases. "Two years I've kept this, and nobody's ever once agreed with me about you. Now four strangers from four houses write down what I write, in the same minute, and I find I don't like it at all." He had tucked the chart into the captain's file. "First time my chart's been right, and I'd give a good deal to have it wrong again."

That was why the chain had come first. The figure was right. Ephram's chart was right. The four strangers' boards were right. It was all exactly right. And it was all going to travel down the bluff road tonight in four satchels to four houses, and be laid beside four bound books, and agree with them; and Rooke, who did not explain anything, had looked at the gallery for the whole bout and then gone and found a chain.

The Current found him first, before Gault did, in the passage behind wing three, with her kit over her shoulder and her face still red from the floor.

"You went short," she said. "On the fourth."

Cael looked at her.

"Nobody goes short off a glide-and-step. You go round it or you take it. I've had that pattern taken off me by a Blade and a Stone and a Shield and once by a girl from the river academy who was much too good for me, and every one of them went round." She shifted the kit. "You went in, and you went *short*. Like a step. I didn't know you could burst like a step."

"I didn't burst," said Cael. "It was a step."

She looked at him for a long moment with her quiet face. Then she nodded, slowly, the way somebody nods at a figure they have decided not to dispute out loud.

"All right," she said. "It was a step. I'd like to learn that step some time." And she went off down the passage toward the south door. He stood there with his coat half on and felt the cost of the band arrive all at once, from a direction he had not been watching: not the strangers in the gallery, who had seen exactly what he meant them to see, but a third-year from the movement floor who had been close enough to feel it.

Gault came out of his office into the passage behind wing three while Cael was still putting his coat on over the throbbing arm. He had the wing's book under his arm and the instrument case in his hand, and he stopped, which he did not usually do.

"There will be a line about observers in the minute of today's hours," he said. "I have written it. *Four officers of other houses present in the gallery, by the lodge book. The provision's demonstration conducted as on every other day.*" He looked at Cael over the case. "That second sentence is true. I wished it to be on the page in case anyone later wonders whether it was."

"Thank you, Magister."

"Don't thank me for writing down what happened." Gault shifted the case to his other hand. "That is the only thing I am for." He went on down the passage, and then, without turning round, added, "Your forearm. Put cold on it. I have written that down too."

Lira found him on the north wall at dusk without any apples.

"He's shut it," she said. "He didn't tell me why. He doesn't tell anyone why. He told the clerk to keep the book open and write *none*." She leaned on the wall. "So now there's a ledger in a closed room with *none* in it, every day."

"How was it under the lamps?"

"Dark. Fine. The crosses are still there; I can find them." She flexed her burst hand. "I just can't see my heel coming any more. You can't read a lamp the way you read a morning."

Rooke came along the wall below them on his way to the gate lodge, and stopped, and looked up, which he did not usually do. He had heard. Cael could tell from his face that he had heard, and that he had expected to hear it, and that he had come along the wall at that hour so that he would.

"Listen to it," he said.

Lira leaned over. "Coach?"

"Your heel. If you can't see it coming, listen to it. Boards talk before a foot's all the way down. Under lamps you'll hear it a quarter-beat early, same as you saw it in the morning." He looked at her a moment longer. "It's a worse way and a slower one. It's also the one you'll have on a floor that nobody's lit for you." He went on toward the lodge.

Lira stayed where she was, leaning on the wall, with her eyes half shut, listening to nothing in particular.

"He shut my light," she said after a while, "and then he came out in the cold to tell me how to do without it." She opened her eyes. "I can't stay angry at him. It's very inconvenient." 

He wrote a second line under her name in the column that night. *The east hall shut and shuttered by Rooke's order, on account of who was watching me. She trains under lamps. She says it's fine.* He looked at the last three words a long while before he blotted them.

There was a knock while the ink was still wet: the porter's boy, to say that Karis wanted them in the reading room, all of them, now, and that she had said *now* twice.

The first thing Cael saw on the long table was a signature.

It was at the foot of a short covering note, in brown ink, and it was the Chief Adjudicator's own, written large and a little unsteadily, by a hand that had shaken once at a window in the west tower and evidently still did. Karis had the note under her palm and the rest of the courtesy copy squared beside it: the adjudication office's whole file from Norhold, the match's record, the nine panel charts, and on a sheet by itself the entry Cael had last seen hanging on a nail by the north tunnel. *Result: stoppage, Daeva, fourth exchange. Performance rating: unscorable under standard. Referred.*

She lifted her hand off the note so that they could read it.

*The registry having taken up this office's referral, the office sends the referred file in full, for the proceedings to use. It is the whole of the office's finding. The office has nothing to add to it, and asks that nothing be added to it in the office's name.*

Cael read the third sentence first, without meaning to, and then went back for the other two, and thought of four satchels going down the bluff road that afternoon with a figure in each of them that was right.

"Another true number," he said, "going somewhere its maker didn't send it."

"It's a man saying *not me*," said Brom.

"*Not in my name*," said Karis. "That's better. *Not me* is a feeling. This one's under a signature, and it'll be read into the minute with the file, and it'll be there as long as the registry keeps a minute."

"Could he have kept any of it back?" said Lira. "The charts? Any of it?"

"No," said Karis.

She did not say anything else. She did not need to. They looked at the shaky signature for a while, all of them, and then she squared the copy and put it in the reading room's own press, and turned the key.

---

Karis had told them in the coach what an exhibits sitting was, on the way down the river road that morning, with the house's two cases on Brom's knees because he would not let them ride on the roof.

"Nothing will happen," she said. "That's what it's for. The clerk reads in every exhibit we filed, one at a time, number, title, date, where it came from, the line of receipt. Nobody argues. Jent conceded every one of them before the first sitting. So the clerk reads, and the seat receives, and at the end of it everything we've proved is in the minute." She looked out at the water. "I want you to watch the room, not the clerk. I want to know whether anybody listens."

"Will they?" said Lira.

"I don't know. That's why I want you to watch."

By the fortieth minute a third of the gallery had gone somewhere else behind its eyes.

Cael noticed it before he noticed anything else about the second sitting, because he had been watching the benches instead of the clerk for some while by then. Halfway along the second row a correspondent had laid his board on the bench beside him and folded his arms and shut his eyes. At the back a woman in a market shawl had got out a half-knitted stocking. Two clerks from the river guild were playing some game on their knees with a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper. Nobody was angry and nobody had stopped believing anything. They had come down the market street to see a case and somebody was reading them an inventory. At the front benches not one pencil had moved for nine minutes.

He looked down at his knee, where his own pencil had been keeping the tally under the table, a stroke for each item and a figure for each minute, and read backward what the room had stopped listening to.

It was the house's exhibits, all of them, read into the minute by the seat's clerk in the order the counsel had asked, each with its number, title, date, where it came from and its line of receipt. The clerk had one pace and kept it for everything. A short item, eight or nine seconds; one with a certifying officer's line to recite, twenty or a little more. Bracken's index to the enrollment file, with Bracken's name in its certifying line: fourteen strokes' worth. The Greyvane ruling, the hearing that had kept Cael on his feet in a city he had never wanted to see: twenty-one. Karis's renewals table, rebuilt in a single silent hour with two dates on every row: eleven. The integrity section, forty pages, a night without sleep, ninety-one invocations each traced by hand to its own page: eight seconds. Its title had taken longer than its reading.

And in among the strokes, a mark he had made when Jent rose, the only time Jent rose all morning, to enter one exhibit of his own by its number: the file from Norhold. The clerk had read the covering note into the minute with it, all three sentences, in the registry voice, exactly as Karis had said he would. Cael had watched the third one go into the record. *Asks that nothing be added to it in the office's name.* The hood had received it, as it received everything. Jent had sat down.

When the clerk read the last number and its line of receipt, Cael had the total on his knee before the senior chair did. A hundred and seventy-three items. Fifty-one minutes and a little over. All of it unopposed. Not one word disputed by any person in the room, because Jent had conceded in writing before the first sitting that every exhibit was what it said it was.

He thought of three sheets he had seen on Bracken's counter that morning before the coach went down. Each had Withrow's name at its foot and the ink still wet: releases of the house's answer, entire, to three faculties of registry law that had written to ask for it to teach from. Across the bottom of the top one, under her signature, she had added a line in her own hand, *For teaching. Let them argue.* Under that, smaller, in Bracken's upright capitals, someone had written *REGISTRAR'S RESERVATION NOTED*, and then, smaller still and in the same hand, *and overruled.*

He looked at the correspondent asleep on the second bench, and thought of three lecture rooms full of second-years being made to read every page of it, and found that it helped.

*They read the whole truth about me into the record this morning,* he wrote that night, *and nobody stood up to argue with a word of it, and a third of the room went to sleep. It was the dullest hour of the year.*

*Gault's minute for yesterday says the demonstration was conducted as on every other day. Umber's note says nothing is to be added in his name. Withrow wrote "Let them argue" across the bottom of a release. Three people, three lines, none of them meant for this year. Somebody is going to open those minutes long after the seat has ruled and the weeklies have stopped printing, and find that every honest person in the room left one sentence there for them.*

*I'd like to know who that reader is. I'd like to be worth their trouble.*

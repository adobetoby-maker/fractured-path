# Chapter 31 — Four Exchanges

The count was slow, and the four men moved on it.

Cael had seen drills like it on the second floor, when Rooke wanted a team to learn to arrive. You did not run at your mark. You walked, and the count told you how long you had, and you timed your stride so that you came onto your mark on the last word, at the same moment as everyone else came onto theirs. Four men arriving together round a coach were not four men. They were a ring. A ring could be drawn tight by one more word.

The lead did not move. He stayed by the cart's wheel with one hand lifted, keeping the time, and sent the other four in. They came from the two ditches and the two ends, one at each corner of the coach, unhurried, with their staves still at their belts and their hands open. *One.* Their feet touched the chalk at the same instant. *Two.* The lead's voice went along the cut like a man calling a dance.

Lira went on two and a half.

He saw her go out of the corner of his eye, because he was already moving the other way. She did not go at any of them. She went to the place behind the coach where the nearest man's next stride was going to come down, and she was standing in it, feet set, when his foot arrived. He came onto her instead of onto his mark. It was not a blow. It was worse than a blow, for a man in a drill: it was the wrong ground, a beat early, and he stumbled into her and off her and lost the count.

*Three.*

Her second burst took her across the coach's tail and up the north camber to the ditch side, low, so fast that the dust she left behind her hung in the air in the shape of the road she had taken. The man coming up out of the ditch found her where his third stride should have been and stopped dead with his hands up.

The lead's voice did not falter. He said *four*. But there were only two men on their marks for it, and a ring with two men in it is a pair of men.

That was all Cael saw. He did not see the third burst or the fourth. He had his own road to take, and it went straight up the crown at the woman in the hood.

---

She had not moved since she came out of the hollow.

She stood on the flat patch of crown halfway between the coach's horses and the cart, three-quarters turned, with her hands loose at her sides and her weight even. She was not looking at him. She was looking at a place on the road about a pace in front of him and a little to his left. He did not understand that until later.

He went at her because she was the key. Every one of the fourteen was hers. He had felt them through his palm and he had felt whose they were, the way you can tell, without being told, that all the letters in a bundle are in one hand. If she was down, or moving, or frightened, she could not lay a fifteenth. He did not mean to hurt her. He meant to make her move.

He spent the Wind at ten paces.

It was a good burst. He had been holding the band down so long that the full thing came out of him almost with relief: the gather low in the belly, the hip loading like a drawn bow, and the shove at his back. The road came up under him. Six feet out from the place he had left, it stopped.

Not the road. Him.

He hit something in the air in front of his chest. It was not a wall, because a wall would have had an edge, and a surface, and a feel. It was a single point, about the height of his breastbone, and it did not give at all. The burst went into it and stopped there, all of it, the way a cart stops when its wheel finds a post in the dark. He went back off it onto his heels. His breath went out of him. The landing beat locked his legs where they stood, the same short pause it had always held him in, and for that pause he could do nothing but stand on the chalk with the point six inches in front of him and know it was there.

She had laid it while he was in the air.

He saw now where she had been looking. She had been looking at the place he was going to be.

He went left. He went round the point, low, with his weight forward and his left foot reaching for the crown. And his foot came down a hand's breadth short of where he had sent it, on nothing, on the air above the chalk, and stayed there. The point under it held his boot like a step. His knee took the jolt. He was standing on one leg on the road and on the other leg on air, and the air was harder than the road.

She had not lifted a hand. She had not moved her feet. She had not even turned toward him.

He came off the point and went back. He had to think.

*She doesn't hit*, he thought. *She takes.*

That was the first thing. Every fighter he had ever faced had come at him with something, a burst or a course or a lane or a fist. They had put a thing into the space between them and asked him to deal with it. She did the opposite. She took the space away. Every step he meant to take, she got there first and took the place he meant to put his foot, and left him with one less step in the world than he had had a moment before.

She was reading him. That was the second thing, and it made him cold all down his back. In the outer court he had watched her get onto a horse without the half beat, the little gathering of the body that tells you a person has decided to move. Somebody had trained it out of her. And because she had none, she could see it in everybody else. She saw his half beat. She saw where his weight was going before his weight knew it. She laid her point there and waited for him to arrive.

Somewhere at the back of him, in its sealed place, the quiet thing from the stair stirred, the way it always stirred when he was cornered, and offered to take him out of every eye in the cut.

He did not look at it. It stayed where it was.

Behind him something hit something with a sound like a sack of grain dropped off a cart.

He turned his head for the length of one breath. Two of the four had given up on the ring. They had seen where their work was, and it was not at the coach. It was in the channel along the south bank, under the lip, where the frame left its one way open to the culvert, and where Brom was standing in it.

The first of them had gone at him square. He was a heavy man, nearly Brom's height, and he had come down the channel at a run with his shoulder low. Brom had not stepped into him or away from him. He had simply been there, where the man was going, with his feet set and his arms loose and his chin in. The man had arrived. The sound had been the man arriving.

He was on the chalk now, two yards back up the channel, on one knee. Brom had not moved a foot.

That was one.

Cael turned back to her.

He had backed off two steps to think, and the two steps were gone.

He found that out when he went to take a third, and his heel came down against air. There was a point behind him now, at the height of his calf, where there had been nothing when he walked through that place. She had laid it while he was looking at Brom. He had not even felt her do it.

So that was the third thing. She did not take anything back.

Every point she had laid since he came at her was still there. The one at his breastbone. The one under his left foot. The one behind his calf. He could not see any of them. He could feel each one if he moved toward it, the way you feel furniture in a dark room by walking into it. And she was not lifting them to lay new ones. She was laying new ones as well. The space round him was the space round him minus three, and in a moment it would be minus four.

She did not need to hurry. She had all morning. She had laid fourteen before he ever came into the cut.

He tried the push.

He put his right palm flat against the point at his breastbone, where his chest had found it, and leaned, and then did not lean. He gave it the push at fighting load, the thing he had taken from the Bronze at Ardenmere, the thing he had given a Gold five times in one sitting at Norhold.

The point did not move.

He did. He went backward off his own push as if he had shoved at the side of a house. It threw him two yards along the crown to the north, faster than he could have gone on his feet, and that was useful. For a moment he was out of the pocket she had been building round him. He took it. He used the second push the same way, against the same point from a new side, and it threw him again, west, toward the coach. The third threw him back east. The point stayed where it was every time. It was the most solid thing on the road. He was using it the way a swimmer uses the side of a bath.

The fourth push came, and the right shoulder spoke, low, the way it always spoke at the fourth.

He stopped.

Four pushes and she had not taken a step. Four pushes and he was still in the cut, with three points round him and the woman on her flat patch of crown, and every push had moved nobody but him.

He tried to make her move them.

He went wide, fast, toward the north ditch, as if he meant to run along it past her to the cart. She laid a point in front of him at the ditch's edge. It was further from her than any she had laid yet, and he saw what it cost her. She was still for an instant. Not the stillness of a woman waiting. The stillness of a woman doing something, the way a person goes still to thread a needle. Half a second. Less. Then she was waiting again.

He turned before he reached the point, and came back, and went wide the other way, toward the south. She laid one there. Half a second.

*Range costs her*, he thought. *Not much. Something.*

He went wide a third time, north again, and she did not lay anything at all. She simply watched him go, and when he turned to come back, the place he was turning into had a point in it, close to her, cheap.

She had learned him faster than he had learned her. She had stopped biting.

---

He had a moment, then, because she let him have it.

He looked round the cut for the length of a breath. Lira was on the ground.

She was at the edge of the north ditch, where the camber fell away into it, sitting in the chalk with one leg out in front of her and her hands flat behind her. She had spent four bursts. He had not seen the third or the fourth, and he did not know which had put her there. He knew only that her right foot was turned at an angle no foot was meant to hold, and that she was holding very still. One of the four stood over her, a yard off, with his stave across his body in both hands. He did not touch her. He did not need to. He stood between her and the coach and looked at her, and she looked back at him.

Custody. Not assault. They did not want her. They wanted her out of the way.

At the near horse, Karis had her right hand flat in the air a hand's breadth above the animal's foreleg, as if she were warming it at a fire.

He knew what she was doing as soon as he saw it, because he had heard her explain the difference on the night of the gate, counting the words off on her fingers. Ternhall's anchors were her own, three footholds that drank the heat of a contact down into a floor. The Lattice Path was lines strung across the air. These were neither. These were points, and they held. But something ran between them. When she had put her hand out to the horse's leg she had found it, the way you find a rope across a dark yard: a line, taut, between the point fixed against the horse's foreleg and one of the fourteen in the bank. And she had put her heat into it.

It frayed.

He could not see it. He saw the horse's leg shift, a finger's breadth, as if somebody had cut one strand of a halter. He saw Karis's face, set and white, and her right hand, and the skin of her fingers going red from the tips down. She moved the hand an inch along the line she could not see and put her heat into it again.

The man Lira had stopped at the ditch stood two yards off from her at the off horse's head, with his stave held across his body in both hands. He had been given the coach, plainly, once the ring was gone: the horses, the driver, whoever stood by them. He was watching Karis's hands. When the second strand went and the horse's leg shifted again, he took one step toward her and then stopped, as if he had remembered his orders, and only watched. Custody. He was not there to touch anybody who was not wanted.

The coachman sat on his box with his reins in his hands and could not let go of them. He was talking to his horses in the same low steady voice he always used. His hands were held where they were, in the air in front of him, as if he were driving a coach that was not moving.

Brom was still in the channel.

The second man came at him while Cael was watching. He was lighter than the first, and quicker, and he had learned from the first. He did not come at Brom square. He came at the left side, low, and at the last stride turned and drove his shoulder up into Brom's left shoulder from underneath, with his whole weight behind it, the way a man drives a post into a hole.

Brom took it.

Cael heard it. He heard Iron Skin hold, a flat dense sound like a hammer on an anvil with a cloth over it. And under that sound, smaller, he heard something else, a click and a give, and saw Brom's left arm drop an inch at the elbow and not come back. Brom's face did not change. His feet did not move. The man came off him and went down on the chalk and stayed down.

That was two.

Brom stood in the channel with his left arm hanging a little and looked at the culvert, and the gully, and the cart. He did not look at his shoulder. He had told the kitchen once that a man who looks at a hurt only teaches it to be looked at.

---

Cael went at her a third time, because he had one thing left he had not tried, and it was the fastest thing he owned.

He would lay a lane.

He would put it down the north side of the crown, past her, along the camber, from where he stood to the cart, fifteen feet of leaning air. She could lay her points where he was going to be. Let her try it at the speed a lane went. He would be past her before she had finished threading the needle.

He set his feet and asked the air to lean.

It leaned. He felt the whole cut give a little along one line, as a board gives when you step on its end. His ears closed and opened. The chalk dust woke up on the crown and ran in a thin white stream along the line he had laid, east, past her, toward the cart.

And the read went out of him.

He had known it would. It was the rule. At Gold weight the read took the air or the body, one or the other, never both; you laid the lane first and then you gave the read back, and it came back to you in a breath or two if you let it. He had no breath or two. He stepped into the lane's downhill with the read still out in the air somewhere and nothing to tell him where her points were but his own memory of them.

The lane carried him. The road came at him faster than his feet had any right to bring it.

Eight feet on there was a point at the height of his ribs.

He did not feel it until he was in it. He went into it at the lane's full speed on the right side, low, and the whole of his weight and the whole of the lane's went into one place at the bottom of his ribs. It did not give. He did. He heard himself make a sound. Then he was off the lane and off his feet and rolling, across the crown, down the south camber, over and over, with the chalk and the grit coming up at him white, and he put his left arm out to stop himself because there was nothing else to put out.

The road took the skin off it. He felt it go from the wrist to the elbow, a long hot scrape, as if somebody had drawn a rasp up the inside of his forearm. He came to rest on his side at the bottom of the camber, a few yards short of the channel, with his face in the dust and his right side one solid ache.

And then the bill came in.

Ten counts. It always came after a lane, since Norhold, and it came now: everything he owned answering a beat late. The right thing, slow. *Bill, not flag*, Lira had written in her palm on the honest floor. He tried to get his hands under him and they got there a beat after he asked. He tried to turn his head to find her and his neck turned a beat after that.

One.

She laid a point.

He felt it go in, near his left shoulder, against the chalk.

Two. Another, at his feet.

Three. Four.

He lay there and counted them in as they came, one for each of his slow counts, because counting was the only thing left that came when he called. Five. Six. She was not hurrying even now. She was laying them the way a mason lays a course of bricks, each one on the last, close round him, closer. Seven.

On the eighth count he got his right hand to the nearest of them, flat, and gave it the spark.

It went out of him the way it always did, like a word. The flint smell came up off the chalk. And he felt the point fray, a little, as the horse's line had frayed under Karis's hand: one thread of it, gone. Then the rest of it held. The spark needed something to burn, and the point had almost nothing in it to burn, and he had two contacts in him and no more.

Nine. Ten.

Eleven, and the bill was paid, and everything answered when he called it.

He was on his knees on the south camber, with his left arm on fire from wrist to elbow and his right side not working properly, and he gave the read back to himself, at last, slowly, and let it go down through his knees into the road.

---

There was a cage round him.

He felt every bar of it. They were not the fourteen; the fourteen were still out in the cut where they had always been, at the mouths and in the banks and on the milestone. These were new. They were close. They stood round him at the height of his knees and his shoulders and his head, a ring of points two feet off on every side, and above him, and on the chalk under him. He counted them twice. Nineteen.

And there was one more, and it was not on the ground.

It was against the front of his coat.

He had been carrying it since the first exchange. He understood that now, kneeling. When his chest had found the point at his breastbone she had not only stopped him. She had laid a second point against him, against the cloth over Hesk's book, so lightly that he had taken it for the bruise of hitting the first one. It went where he went. It had gone with him on every push and every turn and down the camber. It was the first point of the cage, and every other point in the cage was laid from it, and to it, and through it, the way every stitch in a row of knitting is worked through the stitch before.

He could feel the lines now, faintly, through the read. They ran from the point on his coat out to the nineteen round him, and from the nineteen out to the fourteen in the cut, and from all of them, all of them, back to her. She stood on her flat patch of crown fifteen feet away with every line in the cut running into her two loose hands like the reins of a team.

Three more, and it would close. He could feel exactly where. Three gaps, one at his left shoulder, one at his right hip, one over his head. When those three were laid, the cage would stop being a cage and start being a hold. A person inside a closed hold did not walk to a culvert. He was carried there, quite gently, by people who did not need to touch him at all.

She would lay them in the next three breaths. She had all the breaths she wanted.

He went down the line. He did it in the time it took to draw one breath, the way he went down a rotation page before a bout.

*One. The conditions are not to be made.* He had not made this road. The river had made it, and somebody who read rivers.

*Two. A reach only inside a fight the other begins, in earnest, awake, and on purpose.* She had begun it. She had been beginning it since before the coach came out of the ferry. Nobody had ever been more in earnest. And he was awake, as awake as he had ever been, on his knees in the white dust with his arm on fire.

*Three. The whole cost on paper first.* It was on paper. It was in the book against his chest, under her point, on a leaf with four sets of initials and a line in capitals pressed through to the back. At Silver weight. Brom for the minute.

He did it with his eyes open.

He knelt in the cage and held still, all of him, in front of four exchanges of her. That was all it had ever been. Not taking anything. He turned everything he had left toward what he had watched her do, and kept it there, open. Subtraction. The points laid where his half beat said he would go. The stillness of a woman threading a needle at the edge of her reach, half a second's price for range. The accumulation that never gave anything back. The stitch against his coat, and everything worked through it. He had paid for every one of those in his own body on this road, and he knew them now as well as he knew the stair to his own room with the lamps out.

They came together.

It was quiet. It did not come down on him like the storm had come, all at once and too heavy. It came in like a key going into a lock it had been cut for. And then he could see.

Not with his eyes. He could see the lines.

Every course in the cut stood out to him as plainly as the chalk dust had stood out along his lane: taut grey threads in the air, from point to point to point, a great slack net laid down the whole length of the road, closed at both ends, open in one channel where Brom stood. He saw the nineteen round him, each with its line to the next. He saw the one against his coat, the first stitch, with every other line in the cage worked through it. And he saw how it was held. It was held from inside, from where it touched him. It was the same thing as the point at his breastbone and the one under the horse's leg and the ones that held the coachman's hands. It was a thing set against a surface and told to stay.

From inside, he could tell it not to.

He let it go.

It went the way you let go of a breath you have been holding without knowing it. The point against his coat was simply not there any more. And because every other point in the cage had been laid through it, they went after it, one after another, in the order she had laid them, unthreading round him in a quick soft ripple like a row of stitches coming out when the first is pulled. The nineteen went. Their lines went out to the fourteen in the cut, and the fourteen went too. He felt them go down the road behind him like a rope paying out over a wheel: the three across the west mouth, the two in the south bank's lip, the three high in the north bank, the parapets, the milestone.

The near horse threw up its head and stamped, and stood on all four legs again as a horse should. The coachman's hands came down into his lap with the reins in them.

Karis looked at her own hand in the air above nothing, and took it back.

She felt it in her hands.

He saw that. He was still on his knees and he saw her feel her whole morning go out through her fingers, fourteen points laid before light and every one she had laid since, all of them at once. She did not stagger. She did not make a sound. Her two loose hands closed, slowly, as if somebody had pulled a rope out of them faster than she could hold it.

She lifted two fingers of her left hand.

The team broke off in four heartbeats.

He counted them, because it was the last thing he could do. On one the man standing over Lira turned his back on her and went for the north bank, and the one at the off horse's head let go of his stave with one hand and went after him at a run. On two the two men in the channel were on their feet and going, the second holding his own arm, past Brom, who let them past, into the gully beyond the culvert. On three the man at the cart was gone behind it, and the ropes on the north bank were taking weight. On four there was nobody on the road at all but the four of them and the coachman and the horses.

And her.

She was last. She stood on her flat patch of crown for one more moment, with her hands closed, and looked at him.

It lasted one second. He could see nothing of her face but the band of skin between the hood and the scarf and the two eyes in it. They were grey, and steady, and not angry. He had braced for anger. It was not there. It was the look of somebody who has spent her whole life on one craft and has just watched it done backward, in front of her, by a boy on his knees in the dust, and is trying to see how.

Then she turned and went up the north bank on the rope, hand over hand, without the half beat, and over the top into the grass, and was gone.

He knelt in the road. The wind came along the cut from the west and blew the chalk dust off the crown in a long white skirl and carried it away east over the culvert.

Brom was walking toward him down the channel, with his left arm hanging.

The minute was coming. Cael could feel it coming, everything in him beginning to lean toward the new thing, as a crowded room leans toward somebody who has just come in. He did not try to stand. He knelt in the white dust and let it come, and Brom got there first, and stood over him, and did not say anything at all.

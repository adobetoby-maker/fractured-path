# Chapter 2 — Heavy

The first thing Cael saw from the gallery was a red book.

He had come up the east hall's gallery stair on the second morning before the first bell, because whatever Rooke had built in here over the recess he had built for one of the four, and Cael wanted its shape in his notebook while it was still new enough to show its joints. He took the rail at the north end, with the high windows at his back, and looked down. Below him, at a trestle under a lamp it did not need, a clerk sat over a book bound in red board, turning a page with one wet finger.

Then he saw what the book was for. Rooke had stripped the room. The boards were planed back to bare wood, and across them, in fresh white paint, ran no rings at all, only groups of short strokes and small crosses and arrows, as if somebody had written a dance down for people he did not trust to dance it. Even the striking frames along the south wall were strangers. The padded posts stood on springs as thick as a man's wrist, rigged to throw back a Silver's blow instead of swallowing a Copper's.

Five people were working on the floor.

Rooke stood by the trestle with his board under his arm and his pencil behind his ear. At the far end two third-years went up and down a line of crosses: a broad-backed Force, gone up to Rank Seven at the year's last sitting, who moved as if he expected the floor to argue with him, and a lean Current girl who never seemed to put her heels down at all. The fourth was a stranger, and Cael knew him at once. The fifth was Lira.

The stranger was a Stone, thick through the shoulders and slow in the feet, and he stood at the near end of the painted lines with his hands folded in front of him like a man waiting outside a shop. Cael had seen him last at the wool town, on the far side of a rope, losing to Lira. He had been an Iron of the river academy then, its first entry, and she had beaten him by finding the half a beat in which neither of his planted feet was reading the floor, and going through it. Now he was a Silver, newly advanced, and he had sewn nothing on his collar to say so. Over the recess, Lira had said, he had written to Rooke and asked for the posting, and brought his academy's drill-book with him in a red board cover so that the bluff could copy it.

He had asked to come and teach the girl who beat him. Cael wrote that in the notebook before he wrote anything else.

Lira was on the line in front of him now.

The drill was a short one, and Cael had to watch it four times before he understood it.

It began with an entry, an ordinary forward step that any fighter on the hill would have recognised. Then a second step that looked like the first and was not: the weight went onto the front foot, and stayed there one count too long, so that a man watching for the burst would see it coming and set himself. Then a third count in which nothing happened at all. Then, on the fourth, the burst, from the wrong foot, at an angle the first three counts had sworn it would never take.

Four counts. Three of them were lies, and the fourth was the truth arriving where the lies had said it could not.

Lira got it wrong the first time. She burst on the third count, early, because every fight she had ever had had taught her that a third count was where you went. The Stone said nothing. He walked to the second cross and stood on it, and turned his heel a quarter, and walked back.

She got it wrong the second time. She held the third count, but she held it with her shoulders, and a held shoulder told a watcher more than a moving one. The Stone came and stood beside her and did the four counts himself, very slowly, a Silver doing a child's exercise, with nothing in his shoulders at all. On the fourth he did not burst. He only lifted his back heel a finger's breadth off the boards, and it was enough. Cael, at the rail, felt his own weight go the wrong way.

Lira watched him with her head on one side. Then she went back to the first cross.

The third time, she got it right. The fourth time she got it right at speed, and the burst went off the wrong foot at the wrong angle and carried her twenty feet across the painted lines to the far end of the group. She landed and turned, and the Stone, who had moved to meet the angle she had lied about, was standing a stride from where she was, looking at the empty boards where she was not.

He nodded once. It seemed to cost him something, and he paid it.

Rooke made one mark on his board. From the gallery it might have been any word in the language, and Cael would have staked the notebook on which one it was.

"There," said Rooke aloud. "Again. That was the drill. Now find out if it was you."

She went back to the first cross.

Cael did not count her bursts. She had asked him once not to, long ago, and he had kept to it. He counted the drill instead: the entries, the held counts, the angles, the places where her heel came up and the places where it stayed down. It was a better count, in the end. A burst was a burst. A drill was a sentence, and the sentence was what Rooke was teaching her to say.

Rooke's own rule for her was three bursts a bout, and he kept it in drill as well as on a scored floor, so most of what she ran that morning was dry. She ran the four counts without the burst, and with the burst, and dry again, and in between she stood at the end of the line with her hands on her knees and her eyes on the painted crosses as if they had insulted her. The east hall's boards gave her five free bursts in a day. She spent two in the first hour and none after it.

The Force third-year came over once and ran the drill against her. He was half a head taller than she was and six ranks higher on the porter's wall, and he went in at her heavy, the way a Force did, meaning to take the second count away from her before she could tell her lie with it. She told it anyway, and he went the wrong way. He laughed out loud, surprised, and the laugh rang round the empty gallery.

"Again," said Rooke. "Both of you. That laugh was free. Next one costs."

---

At the water break she came to the foot of the gallery stair and sat on the bottom step with a tin cup, and Cael came down and sat a step above her.

Her collar was dark through. Her hands were taped to the wrist, and she rolled them, one and then the other, the way she did when she wanted something to do with them. She held her left arm a little close to her side. It had not hurt her since the team trial at Norhold; the healers had taken the wrap off for that day and never put it back. But the arm still remembered being careful, and some mornings it carried itself close out of habit, the way a man who has lost a tooth goes on chewing on the other side.

She did not say anything for a while. She looked at the floor: the white lines, the crosses, the arrows, the frames on their new springs, the clerk at the trestle turning a page of the red book, Rooke beside him with his pencil. The whole hall had been rebuilt over the recess for one purpose, and the purpose was sitting on a stair with a tin cup.

"It's heavier than it looks from the rail," she said. "Don't write that down."

"Noted," said Cael.

He did not write it down. He kept his hands on his knees and the notebook in his coat. She saw him not do it, and her mouth moved a little at one corner, and she looked back at the floor.

That was the whole of what she said about it. It was more than she had said in two months of breakfasts.

He sat with it there on the stair and turned it over, the way he would have turned a guard somebody had shown him, looking for where it would give.

Since Fenmark Lira had kept a whole vocabulary for the registry, and he had heard all of it, on roads and benches and the steps of waystations, and there was not a kind word in it. It was a language for things withheld. It had never needed a word for a thing handed over, because nothing had been. He realised, sitting a step above her, that this was the first word she had ever had to find for a gift, and that she had picked the one that told the truth about it before she let herself be glad.

"The Stone," he said instead. "He asked for this."

"He wrote to Rooke in the first week of the recess. Rooke showed me the letter." She drank from the cup. "Two lines. He said he'd fought me and lost and wanted to know how. He said the river academy could spare him for a year if somebody here would feed him." She turned the cup in her taped hands. "He's a good teacher. He doesn't explain anything. He just stands on the cross I should have been on and waits for me to notice."

"Is it working?"

"You saw." She put the cup down on the step. "Rooke says the sitting doesn't read the board. It reads the rating. I'm Iron Rank One on the porter's wall, and I'll stay Rank One on it, and it doesn't matter. When the advancement evaluation comes, they'll put me on a floor in front of a panel and mark what I do, and if the marks sit where Silver sits, that's where I go. Not up the column. Across it." She pressed the tape flat at her wrist. "The rank's a certificate. The rating's a figure. Rooke says nobody in the Silver bracket ever asked to see anybody's certificate."

"And the figure?"

"Isn't there yet." She said it without any weight on it, the way she would have named the time. "It will be."

He knew the figure as well as she did; he had kept her figures in his notebook since the wool town.

Five judges marked a bout in three columns, execution and control and effect, and each column could run past ten, with ten for an ordinary good day at the heart of Silver. Strike the highest and the lowest judge in each column, average the three that are left, add the columns together, and you had a rating. Thirty was par for a Silver. Twenty-eight was the floor of the bracket, and her figures all last year had run from twenty-five to twenty-seven, Silver-touched, at the very top of what the board called strong Iron. Two points, give or take. Two points was a long way to climb by rating alone, and he did not know of anybody on the hill who had climbed it in a single year.

"Two points," he said.

"Two points." She looked at the painted floor. "The Stone says the first one's the drill. He says the second one's me."

The clerk at the trestle rang a small bell. The Force third-year was already back at the far crosses, swinging his arms. Lira stood and stretched until her back cracked, and then she put her hand in her pocket and took out a key on a string and showed it to him.

"Rooke gave me this. I'm first in every morning, so I'm the one who opens up." She swung it once and put it away. "It's the first key anybody here ever gave me that wasn't to my own room."

She went back down onto the painted floor. Rooke was already saying something to her, and she was already shaking her head, and the Stone was already standing on a cross.

Cael climbed back to the rail and watched until the bell, and wrote nothing at all until he was out on the covered walk with the hall behind him. Then he wrote only the date, and the word *key*, and under it the shape of the drill, four counts, three lies and the truth.

---

He met the assistant before he met Karis.

She was a second-year with ink on her collar and an armful of registry digests, coming down the library's top stair two steps at a time as he went up it after the noon meal with a mug of tea in each hand. The stair was too tight for two people to pass. She flattened herself to the wall to let him by and stared at him the whole time he was going past, and as he climbed on he heard her say to herself, quite quietly, *the left hand, then the right*, the way people say a thing they mean to remember.

He supposed he would find out later what it meant.

Karis was standing in her own doorway at the top, with the old compilations in their three bindings held against her chest, looking at the door as if it had said something to her.

The card was still in its brass holder, in Bracken's small upright hand, as it had hung all last year. *Reading room. Assigned: K. Dellenmoor.* What was new sat under the card: a brass lock with a heavy plate, let into the old oak by somebody who knew wood. Its key hung on a cord round her neck. The cord was too long, and the key had swung out over the top of the books.

"Do you know what wanting a room does to a reader?" said Karis, without turning round.

He waited, because she would tell him.

"It makes her kind. Not much. A doubt she lets go by. A page she doesn't turn back to. Nothing she'd ever write down, and nothing she'd ever forgive in anybody else." She went in at last and set the books down on the long table under the window, and he followed with the tea. "I've seen it happen to better readers than me. Every room I've worked in before this one was somebody else's to take back, and I always knew where the door was, and I read faster for knowing it." She turned and looked at the lock from the inside. "It's the first one I've ever wanted to stay in. So I'm going to watch myself in it. I'm telling you, so that there are two of us watching."

"What changed it?"

"Counting." She did not touch any of it while she named it. "A lock. The key. A girl who brings me digests and asks me what they mean, and gets one of her mistakes handed back every morning, only one, so that she has to go and find the rest herself. And a line in the house's accounts with my name under it." Her mouth moved a little. "Bracken says the council sat twice in the recess to find a heading for it, because the books had never had one. *Research, enrolled, standing.* Eight people, two sittings, three words."

The room itself had changed. The great map of the continent had come down off the long wall and lay rolled and tied on a shelf, and the plaster stood bare for the first time since he had known it. A new ledger lay open on the table with nothing written in it yet.

She drank half her tea standing. Then she crossed to the shelf by the door, where the house's certified copy of the tournament charter stood in its grey card covers among the regional schedules, and laid one finger along its spine, and kept it there.

He did not say anything. He had watched her hand stop at that book last year, in the deep cold, at the founding articles where a slip of her own paper stood up out of the binding. He had seen her walk past it a hundred times since.

"Old books first," said Karis to the spine, as if he had asked. "The founding text. The first consolidation. The second. Each one until I can say what it says without looking, and nothing newer on the table until then. I made the bargain with the only person I can't talk round." She took her finger away. "A note for the cold term."

She sat down, and opened the new ledger at its first page, and uncapped her pen, and that was the end of the visit. He left her tea where she could reach it.

---

By the third morning the assessment wing's queue reached the door.

The intake had found the counter. A Stone first-year stood in front of Cael holding his form in both hands as if it might go off. Ahead of the Stone, two Current girls argued in whispers about a drill neither of them had run yet, and ahead of them a Mire third-year studied the notice board with great attention so that nobody would think he was waiting for anything. Cael had a floor-allocation sheet in his own hand, because evaluation-linked hours were lodged here or nowhere, and he spent the wait reading the wall.

The rack by the door had seven pigeonholes. Last year the seventh had stood empty. This morning it was stacked with sheets of a pale grey-green, like the underside of a willow leaf, and the ink of the heading was still sharp from the press. *Research standing: floor and archive hours, by application.* At the foot of each sheet, in small type, was the wing's print number and a date, and the date was the week the council had voted Karis her line. Nobody on the hill had held such a standing before that week. The form had been waiting for her before she knew she would need it.

The counter employed a clerk who added columns and a teaching assistant who read what was coming, and the green sheets had not come from the clerk. Cael looked at them exactly as long as a third-year looks at a form he has no use for.

Behind the counter, the desk clerk sat at his ledger by the window, adding a column under his breath and losing it whenever the Current girls' whispering rose. Gault's door stood shut, with voices behind it. And behind the counter at the copying table, rising every time the queue moved to take the next sheet, the teaching assistant did the counter's work.

Above the copying table, on the shelf where it had stood before Norhold, sat a small locked case with no mark on it. It had gone to Norhold and come back without anybody seeing it opened. Nobody looked at it now, and Cael did not look at it either.

Seln took each form the same way. He read it from the name to the signature at an even pace. He stamped the received line. He tore off the copy-slip along its rule and handed it back. The Stone first-year got what the Mire third-year got, to the second. A man who read every line from every hand was a man nobody remembered reading anything in particular.

When Cael reached the counter, Seln took his sheet and read it. Name; the Path line left blank, by rule; evaluator of record; hours; reason. The stamp came down, and the slip tore clean.

"The Magister signs evaluation hours on Fifth-days," said Seln. "The schedule goes up the morning after."

"Thank you for the form."

"Mm."

That was always all of it. Cael had his fingers on the copy-slip.

Seln laid the sheet on the stack at his left hand and said the next thing to the stack.

"Standing papers on the provision come up for the ledger's half-year check," he said. "Keep the documentation current." He set the edge of the stack true against the counter. "There have been senior men at the foot of the road since the recess. The office mentions it only because the office has eyes."

He had already turned back toward the copying table. By the time Cael had folded the slip into his coat, the pen was in Seln's hand again and his head was down, and the Stone first-year had stepped up behind and was laying his form on the counter as if it were a sword he was surrendering.

Cael went out through the wing doors and along the covered walk at the pace of a man on his way to the library. He did not let himself have the sentence until he was through the north arch.

*Senior men. Keep the documentation current.*

Seln charged for words the way a ferryman charges for crossings, and he had never in two years crossed for nothing. A remark about the weather from him would have been worth a morning's thought. Four sentences in a queue, and not one of them about the form in his hand, were very nearly a letter.

---

The north wall ran along the edge of the bluff above the river, waist-high, broad enough to sit on, with the whole valley open below it. He had counted the coats from here for two years, and from windows and wagon benches before that, ever since Greyvane. He knew every place on the wall where the angle was good. That evening he took the one by the third buttress, where a crack in the coping held water after rain and nobody else liked to sit, and waited for the bell.

The heat of the day was going off the stone. Below him the bluff road went down in its switchbacks to the river street. Across the brown water, the ferry landing stood at the end of its causeway with the ferry tied up beside it for the night.

Two at the landing. Two at the foot of the road.

The bell went from the lecture range. Down in the valley, at the same moment, as if the bell had been rung for them, two more coats came along the river street from the direction of the town and took up the places at the road's foot, and the two who had stood there walked away toward the bridge without a word passed. At the landing it happened a breath later. It was done as neatly as a change of guard at a gate, and nobody on the river street so much as turned a head.

Grey coats and good boots; that had not changed. What had changed was the way they stood.

One of the pair at the road's foot was grey in the beard and leaned on a stick he did not seem to need. The other kept his hands in his coat and looked at the river. Neither had raised his eyes to the bluff since the change. The younger coats of other years had craned at the walls like visitors to a cathedral, and shifted their feet a little more each week as the place came clear to them. These two stood as if there were nothing on the hill they had not already been told about. They had the patience of men near the end of a long errand, who know the one thing they have been sent to see and do not mean to miss it.

He watched until the light went, and they did not look up once.

He wrote it on the wall, with the notebook flat on the coping and his back to the river.

*Third evening. The change on the bell, both pairs, as every year. Different men. These have no questions about the hill; whoever sent them answered all of those first. Men with no questions get sent at the end of something, not the start. You don't post your best men to learn a gate. You post them to be standing there when it opens.*

*I don't know what they're waiting for. Seln says senior men. Bracken watches the road. Vastin said a sitting.*

*Three people who never guess, and none of them has guessed out loud. I'm not going to be the first.*

He shut the book. Across the river the ferryman lit the lamp on the landing's post, and the two coats there stood in its light without moving, as patient as the post.

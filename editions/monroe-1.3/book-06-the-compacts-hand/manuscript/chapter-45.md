# Chapter 45 — The Lake House

Bracken brought the schedule to the old frames himself, on the Sixth-day, in the hour after the noon meal when he knew Brom would be there.

He came in by the yard door with a single sheet in his hand and his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, and stood a moment inside it while his eyes found the three of them: Ephram on the long frame, in the middle of a set; Brom beside the short one with his arms folded; Cael against the wall. The old frames stood in a low stone room behind the stables that had been a harness store once and still smelled of it, with two lamps, a sanded floor, and the two great timber frames with their iron bars and springs, older than anybody on the hill.

"From the district seat," said Bracken. "By this morning's bag. It is addressed to the practitioner, care of the registrar, and so I have read it, and so I have walked it across a wet yard, which I do not do for everything." He held it out. "It is the review's schedule of evidence."

Brom unfolded his arms and took it, and read it standing, without moving his lips, and then he held it a little away from him and read it aloud, slowly, the way he read anything official, as if testing each plank before he put his weight on it.

"*Ostrand district registry. Review for advancement of tier: Brom, enrolled at Halcenvane. Schedule of evidence. The panel will read, first, the practitioner's record as entered, the continental title included. Second, the house's attendance and floor sheets for the two years preceding, to be supplied by the registrar under the house's seal. Third, a reading in the sigil frame, under load, taken on the day in the presence of the three assessors. No testimony is contemplated. Sitting: the last Fifth-day of the academic year, at the second bell.*"

He lowered it.

"The second is mine," said Bracken. "I have supplied it. It went down this morning by the return bag and weighed four pounds nine ounces, and there is not a blot in it." He pulled his spectacles down onto his nose. "The first is the registry's own. The third is yours, and nobody can supply it for you." He looked at the short frame, and then at Brom's left shoulder, briefly, as he might have glanced at a column he had already added. "I take it that is what this room is for."

"It's what it's been for all year," said Brom.

"Then I shall leave you to it." Bracken turned to go, and turned back, as he always did. "Under load," he said. "I asked the district clerk what load. He wrote back that it is the assessors' to set on the day, and that in his experience they set it at what the title was won against, and a little over." He nodded once to each of them, the small nod, and went out into the yard.

The door shut behind him.

Ephram, who had not stopped his set, finished it, and let the long frame's bar come back to its rest with both hands, and went to the rack by the wall where the short frame's iron plates hung on their pegs. He took down two, and looked at them, and hung one back, and took down a heavier one in its place.

He had kept two years of somebody else's figures on three sheets of ruled paper, and he was not likely to have missed what Brom's title had been won against.

He slid the plates onto the short frame's bar, one each side, and stepped back, and looked at Brom.

Brom folded the schedule along its folds and put it into his left inside pocket, with the summons and the docket and the confirmation, and his hand stayed there a moment, over the stiff shape of the thing that was also in that pocket and had been since the forty-fifth day. Then he took the hand away and walked in under the bar.

---

Cael had watched Brom hold things for years. He had never before watched him practise it.

The short frame was a simple machine: two timber uprights, a crossbar of iron that ran in greased slots between them, and under each end of the bar a spring as thick as a man's wrist. You stood beneath the bar and took it on your shoulders, or on your forearms crossed above your head, and somebody let the catches go, and the springs drove it down. A sigil frame at a district seat was a finer thing, with a plate that read what the practitioner's Path did under the weight. This one read nothing. It only pressed.

Brom set his feet and crossed his forearms over his head, the right over the left, and Cael saw the skin change.

It always began at the wrists. It was not a colour, exactly, though the lamp found a dull grey in it, like a blade that has been oiled and put away. It was more that the surface of him stopped behaving like skin, and stopped creasing. It went up his forearms and across his shoulders under the shirt and down into his back, and where it had gone, Brom looked less like a man holding still and more like something that had been built there.

Ephram let the catches go.

The bar came down on Brom's crossed forearms with a sound like an anvil taking a hammer. The springs sang, and Brom's knees gave an inch and stopped.

Cael let the old read go out across the sanded floor.

It was Brom's own gift he read him with, taken years ago and used every day since, and it came back to him as it always did, as weight and where the weight went. The bar's load ran down through the crossed forearms and into the shoulders, the right taking a little more than the left, and from there down the long muscles of the back into the hips and through both heels into the floor. It was like watching water find its way down a roof. Nothing in the whole of Brom was pushing back against the bar; he was only giving the weight somewhere to go.

And the left shoulder was taking a little less than its share, though not by much. Brom knew; Cael could read him knowing, in the small care with which the left elbow was set.

Ephram counted under his breath, and at thirty he threw the catches, and the bar went up, and Brom stepped out from under it and let his arms down, and the skin went back to being skin, wrists last.

He stood breathing.

Ephram took a third plate off the rack, a small one, and held it up, and raised his eyebrows.

"Left side," said Brom.

Ephram hung it on the left.

They did it eleven times, by Cael's count, in the better part of an hour, and in all that time neither of them said another word that was not a number or a side. Ephram put the plates on and took them off, and watched Brom's left elbow the whole while with the level, patient attention he gave everything he meant to be right about. Twice he shook his head at nothing Cael could see, and took a plate off, and Brom did not argue. Once he added one that Brom had not asked for, and Brom looked at it and walked in under the bar.

On the eleventh the left shoulder took its whole share. Cael felt it through the floor, the weight going down both sides alike, and held, and held, to a count of forty.

The bar went up.

"The left's back," said Ephram.

It was the first whole sentence he had spoken since Bracken left, and he wiped his hands on the cloth at his belt and began taking the plates off the bar, one at a time, and hanging them on their pegs in order of weight.

Brom sat down on the bench by the wall, heavily, with his forearms on his knees. There were two long red bars across them where the iron had lain.

"They'll find it," he said. He did not seem to be speaking to either of them in particular. "That's the thing. I've known since the docket came that they'd find it, and I know now what the paper is that they'll write it on. I told you in the reading room. I can't read that sheet anymore." He turned his forearms over. "And I still want to stand in their frame and have the plate say what's true. I've been thinking about why. I think it's only that I'd like to have been right about myself, once, in front of somebody whose job it was to check."

"Lira said something like that," said Cael.

"Did she?" Brom thought about it. "She would."

He did not say what he would do with the finding afterward, but his hand went to the pocket again and rested there. Ephram, at the rack, with his back to them, hung up the last plate.

---

The lake house came up the bluff road on the Second-day in a hired wagon with a blue-grey cloth over its hoops, and half the hill was at the north wall to watch it climb.

There were five of them: an instructor in a long coat, a clerk with a board, two fighters who had come to watch and carry, and the Blade. He got down in the outer court and stood looking about him at the lodge and the arch and the covered walk with frank interest, a broad, fair, good-humoured young man of twenty or so with a wooden lath in a canvas sleeve across his back. He was Iron Rank Five, and he had beaten the Current girl on figures at the confluence, after four level exchanges, and had come and found her afterward to tell her that he had not enjoyed a bout so much all year. She had told Lira, and Lira had said that it was a very annoying thing for him to have done.

It was the last of the three bouts Rooke had struck, and the one she had wanted most.

They fought it on the Crown yard's sprung oak in the afternoon, under a white sky, with the house on the tiers. Rooke had chalked it on the yard's board himself. *Exhibition. Wind, Iron One, Halcenvane. Blade, Iron Five, the lake house. Five exchanges at most. Two touches ends it.* Three cards at the table: the lake house's instructor, the Mire instructor from the wing, and the Stone, who sat between them with his big hands folded and looked as if he had been carved there.

Cael had the east tier, with Brom on one side of him and Karis on the other. Karis had brought no notebook; she had said on the stair that she was going to watch one thing this year with her hands empty, and she sat on them to be sure.

"She's nervous," said Brom.

"She's not," said Cael. He was watching Lira's feet at the south chalk. "She's bored of being nervous. Look."

She was standing with her weight on the right.

Not evenly. On the right, plainly, the left foot only resting, the way anybody stands who has a favourite leg. She had been landing on that foot for seventeen days, in the east hall, at dawn with the program and at night without it. He had not been asked to the nights, and had only seen the stockings she threw away.

The steward's hand came down.

A Blade did not fight with his hands. He had the lath, three feet of pale ash with a bound grip, and his Path put an edge on it that was not the wood's, and a line out beyond its tip that he could read the way Cael read a floor. He committed to a cut in three beats: the heel, the hip, the edge. Once he had begun the first, the third came, and he could not take it back; that was a Blade's strength, and it was the door in him.

He began with a commitment at once, a long clean diagonal at shoulder height, to see what she would do.

She lied to it. Front foot. Back foot. He was already in his second beat and could not answer either. On his third, as the lath came through, she went, burst one, low and inside the cut, along the line of his own arm, and the end of that line was a place on the oak just behind his right hip.

Her right foot came down on it.

It came down whole. Cael saw the knee take the weight and the oak give under her and come back, the sprung floor's own gift, and she rose off it with the beat exactly where it belonged. Her palm was flat on the Blade's ribs before his lath had finished travelling through the place where she had been.

"Touch," said the steward. "Halcenvane."

The tiers made a noise, and somewhere along the west side the Current girl's voice went up over everybody's in a shriek. Brom let his breath out through his nose.

"On the right," said Karis, very quietly, to her knees.

"On the right," said Cael.

---

The Blade laughed.

He stood at his chalk with the lath across his shoulders and laughed, out loud, shaking his head, exactly as the Force had laughed at her on the painted floor at the start of the year. Then he stopped laughing and looked at the oak, at the place behind his own hip, and Cael saw him put it away somewhere to be used.

He did not commit in the second.

He stood, with the lath hanging in his right hand and its tip a few inches off the floor, and let her run the whole count at him, three lies and the truth, and did not move for any of it. He was reading. A Blade's line ran out past the end of his edge, and where it ran he knew what would cross it, a breath before it crossed.

She went on the fourth, because the count said to. Burst two.

He had the line laid already. He did not cut at her; he simply turned his wrist, and the lath came up and lay along the path she was travelling, like a bar dropped across a doorway, and she arrived on it. It took her on the forearm, the left, the one she carried close.

"Touch. The lake house."

One each. The tiers groaned, cheerfully. The Blade lifted the lath to her in a small salute, and Lira, walking back, rubbed her forearm and gave him a look that made the lake house's two carriers nudge one another.

The third was nobody's. They had each found the other's way in, and neither would walk through the same door twice. She lied and did not go; he began a commitment and she stepped out of its line before the hip, so that the edge came through empty air a yard from her. She walked him round the oak, and he turned with her, the lath low, laying lines and lifting them. Twice she might have gone and did not. She had one burst left under Rooke's rule and she was keeping it in her pocket as she had kept one in the east hall, seventeen days ago, and lost.

"Time," said the steward. "No touch."

At the table Rooke had not written anything for some while, and had his pencil behind his ear and his arms folded on the board.

The fourth.

The Blade had seen her land twice now, both times on the right. Cael could read it in the man's weight from the tier: a little turn of the front foot, a line laid early, not along her path this time but at its end. He was going to do what the Current girl had done. He was going to go and stand where her foot came down.

She ran the count. Front. Back. Shoulders. On the fourth she went, her last, along the inside of a cut he had not made, to a place on the oak that both of them could see, and her right foot reached for it.

The lath was there. It came across level, a hand above the boards, at the place where her right ankle would be when the weight went on.

She did not put the weight on.

Cael saw it and for a breath his stomach turned over, because it looked like the old thing, the touch and the step. It was not the old thing. Her right foot touched the oak and left it again, on purpose, lightly, like a hand taken off a hot kettle, and she was over the lath and down on the left, a full pace past him, with the beat under her and her back to his back.

She had not asked the foot. She had chosen.

She turned on the left and put her hand between his shoulder blades.

"Touch," said the steward. "Halcenvane. Two touches to one."

The house stood up.

It stood up all at once, three tiers of it, with a roar that sent the pigeons off the lecture range. Brom was on his feet with both fists over his head, the left as high as the right. Karis had taken her hands out from under her and was clapping them, which Cael had not seen her do since Greyvane. Down on the oak the Blade had turned round and was holding his lath out to Lira across both palms, grinning all over his face, and she was pushing it back at him, and the Current girl had come over the rail and was running at them both.

Cael stayed sitting a moment longer than the rest.

He had seen the foot touch and lift, where everybody else had seen a girl jump a stick. He looked across the yard at the table, and Rooke was looking at the same place on the oak, with his pencil in his hand at last. He wrote one word on the card. Then, which Cael had never seen him do, he wrote a second.

The panel's figures went up on the yard's board in the porter's boy's chalk. *Wind, 28. Blade, 26.* She did not look at them until the Current girl turned her round by the shoulders and made her.

---

The slate outside the east hall had a new line on it the next morning.

Rooke had put it at the top, above the week's drills, and ruled a chalk line under it, so that nobody could take it for anything that would be wiped off on the Seventh-day.

*Advancement evaluation, Silver bracket. The district seat, Ostrand. The first sitting after the year's close. Program's entrant: the Wind.*

Lira read it with her wraps in her hand, for she had come down early, and so had Cael, and the covered walk was empty but for the two of them and Rooke, who was standing a little way off with his board, looking out at the rain as though he had nothing to do with the slate.

She read it twice. Her face did not change the second time, and that was how Cael knew.

"That's after the ruling," she said.

She said it to the slate, in an ordinary voice, and it was not a question. She had a calendar in her head as good as Bracken's, and she had laid two dates on it and seen which came first.

"It is," said Rooke.

He did not turn round, but went on looking at the rain. Cael watched the back of his head and could not have said what the man knew. Rooke had eaten in the open seat at a refectory table on the day of the riders, and had struck three bouts off a card without asking how an ankle had come to be turned on the way to a sitting. He knew what a ruling could do to an enrollment, and he knew whose friend his entrant was. He had built her a hall. He had never once asked her for a promise.

"Then I'd better be ready for it," said Lira.

"You'd better." Rooke turned from the rain at last, and looked at her, and then for a moment past her at Cael, with a face that gave away exactly as much as his board did. "Twenty-eight is a figure. It isn't the figure. Wraps. Every count ends on whichever foot I call, and I'll call it late."

She went in, and at the door she looked back at the slate once, quickly, at the ruled line, and Cael saw her decide not to say whatever it was.

---

She asked him that night, on the north wall, with the valley dark below and the coats' two lanterns small and yellow at the foot of the road.

"I want the key tonight," she said. "And this time I want you."

"The honest floor? Brom's—"

"Not the honest floor. Not Brom and not Karis. The two of us." She had her knees drawn up on the coping and her arms round them. "I want the rate you never show."

He looked at her.

"You've seen it. You were at Norhold. You've stood on the honest floor since the first week of the year and written every bill on your hand."

"I've seen pieces. I've seen what one lane costs, with Karis counting. I've seen you spend five pushes at a wall of Brom so that we could find out where your shoulder starts to talk." She shook her head. "That's a ledger. I've never once stood in front of all of it at the same time, with you trying to beat me. Nobody has. Daeva has, and she's Gold, and you were on one knee at the end of it." She put her chin on her knees. "I fought a Blade yesterday who's four ranks over me, and I knew what he was by the second exchange. I've known you since Ardenmere and I don't know what you are with the lid off. I'd like to. Once. Before."

She did not say before what, and he did not ask.

"It won't be pretty," he said.

"I didn't ask for pretty."

"I'll price it first. That's the rule, and it's initialled."

"Price it, then. But don't score it." She unfolded herself from the coping. "Tenth bell. Bring the book if you must. I'm not writing on my hand."

He priced it at his sill, by the lamp, in four lines. *Night, the east hall, L. alone. Everything public, at the rate, to win. Buys: she knows. Spends: hip past five on boards; shoulder; ten counts for a lane, twelve for a third; behind the eyes. Not the quiet thing. Not the new one.*

The hall was cold, and the shutters were down, and she had lit both lanterns and chalked two marks on the painted floor thirty feet apart. She was at the south one in her wraps with her weight even, and he left his coat and the satchel on the bench and went to the north.

"No table," said Lira. "No count. We go till I say stop. If I touch you, I'll say so. You needn't."

"All right."

"Don't be kind."

He stood on his chalk and let go of the room he had been fighting inside all year.

That was what it felt like. He had lived inside the house's rate since Norhold as a man lives inside a coat, four on boards and never a fifth, two contacts, the give shut, the push a flat hand, the lane nowhere. He did not take the coat off; he let it fall, and stood there in the lantern light with all of it awake at once, and it was so much easier than holding it that his eyes stung.

She ran the count.

He did not wait for the fourth. The read had her weight before her front foot had finished its lie, deep, to the hips, and it said where the truth would come from a full beat before she spent it. He went first. Her own burst, the first thing he had ever taken, low and flat off the left foot, straight across the count; and he saw her eyes widen, because nobody had ever simply walked through it before. She burst to meet him, late. He was already down, and the landing beat gave him back, and he put the push into the boards at her landing place a hand's breadth before her foot arrived.

It was not a flat hand but the push at fighting load, the full shove of the thing, and the air over that square yard of floor went solid for an instant and then gave. Her foot came down on nothing where she had expected, and she sat down hard, with his palm already on her shoulder.

Three seconds, perhaps. He stepped back.

Lira sat on the painted floor with her mouth open.

"Again," she said.

She did not run the count the second time. She stayed back, out of the push's reach, moving on her toes along the far line of crosses, making him come to her, which was exactly right. He did not come; he asked the air to lean.

Fifteen feet of it, from his chalk to the space behind her left shoulder, a third deep. His ears closed and opened. The dust in the cracks of the boards woke and ran, pale, down the lane, and under the lanterns it threw a thin moving shadow, and he saw her see the shadow and know it. She had learned to read that band on this floor in the year's seventh week. She burst along its edge to get out from under where he would arrive.

He took the read off the lane and gave it to her, the way he had learned to, and stepped in.

It carried him a third faster than any feet. Her burst had been along the lane's edge; he came down the lane's middle and out of its end, past her, behind her, while her landing beat was still coming up through her knees, and touched her between the shoulder blades, where she had touched the Blade.

Then the bill came in.

The warm hand over the inside of his skull. Everything slow. He stood where he was and began the count, as he always did. *One. Two.*

She had turned. She was looking at his face.

*Three.*

"That's the ten," she said softly, and went.

She came straight at him, burst on burst, her second and her third with no count between them, and he called the Wind to go and it came a beat behind his asking, and he called the read and it came late, and he got his forearm up and opened the give and it opened *late*, and by then her palm was against his ribs, on the right side, exactly where they had been cracked.

"Touch," said Lira. "Mine."

*Eight. Nine.* He stood with her hand on his ribs and waited for ten, and at eleven the hand lifted off the inside of his head.

He was grinning; he could feel it, and he could not remember when he had last grinned on a floor.

"You went inside the count."

"I've written that count on my palm since the first week of the year. Did you think I'd never spend it?" She was grinning too, breathing hard. "Again. And don't lay a lane, or I'll do it twice."

After that he did not give her anything.

He let her strike, and she put three fast palms into his raised forearm, one, two, three, and the give took all three like a cup filling, and the fourth she did not throw, because she had counted that as well. He closed the distance on the third and touched her wrist, on the tape, with one bright contact of Karis's spark. She yelped and shook her hand, and by then his other palm was on her collarbone. He went past five on the boards and felt the hip begin to write its bill, crest to knee, and went on, a sixth and a seventh, and the push again, and the read never once off her.

She was very good, better than she had been yesterday in the Crown yard, and twice she found a line he had not shut and was a hand away from him.

It did not matter. He was not faster than her. He could see her understand that, somewhere in the eighth exchange or the ninth. He was only earlier. Everything she did, he had an answer for a half beat before she did it, and the answer came from a different drawer every time.

Once, when she planted both feet to change direction, the new thing lifted its head at them. *There?* He named them without breaking stride. *Lira's. Leave them.* It cost him the half beat, and she nearly had him a second time.

"Stop," said Lira.

He stopped.

She was bent over with her hands on her knees, and he was not standing very well himself. His right hip had stopped writing and begun to shout, and his shoulder had counted the pushes and had a view about the last one, and there was a slow thick ache behind his eyes that was the lane and the naming together.

She straightened up, and looked at him in the lantern light. She was not frightened, as he had been afraid, on the wall, that she would be. Her face was the one she had turned on the Blade's lath, held out to her across his palms: somebody had offered her a thing, and she was deciding whether it was hers to take.

"Right," she said. "Now I know."

"What do you know?"

"What it's going to look like." She sat down on the boards, all at once, with her legs out. "When somebody finally gets that in daylight, with a table and a yard full of people. I know what their face is going to do." She wiped her forehead with her wrist. "And I know you liked it."

He did not answer at once.

"Yes," he said.

"Good. You should. You've been walking round this hill for two years like a man carrying a full pail up a stair." She looked at the chalk mark he had left, thirty feet away. "I'm not going to tell anybody about tonight."

"Not Brom?"

"Not Brom. He'd want to know if you were all right, and you are, and I'd have to say it six times. Not Karis; she'd want it measured, and it isn't a figure. Not the captain, ever, though he'd give his chart to have stood where I'm sitting." She shook her head. "It isn't going on my hand and it isn't going in my book. Somebody ought to have seen it once who wasn't keeping score. That's all it was for. It's mine."

"The costs go in the Log. That's the rule."

"The costs. Hip, shoulder, eyes. Not the exchanges." She held out a hand, and he pulled her up, and his hip told him what it thought of that. "Not that I had one off you."

"You had one off me."

"I know I did," said Lira, with great satisfaction. "I just don't want it written down."

He wrote the bill at the bench, standing, because sitting had become a question. *Hip, crest to knee, from the sixth. Shoulder, three pushes at load. One lane: ten. A naming in the middle of it. Behind the eyes till morning, I'd guess.* He stopped with the pen lifted. Then he added, because the book was to be kept honest and this was a cost too: *It was easier than the band, by a long way. Mind that.*

She was at the great door when he had done, with her hand on its edge.

"*Door*," she said. "*It's held.*" And then, as he went through it: "You were early. Every time. I'm going to think about early for a month."

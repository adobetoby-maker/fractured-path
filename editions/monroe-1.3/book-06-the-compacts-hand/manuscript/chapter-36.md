# Chapter 36 — The Leaf in Its Sleeve

Karis untied the pink tape herself, because it was the wing's knot and she had watched Seln tie it on a hundred files across the counter and knew which end to pull.

The bundle opened on the cloth like a book with no spine. It was reports, a few sheets each, folded once lengthwise in the field service's way, the way a man folds a paper to carry it inside his coat on a horse. The hands varied, because Seln had copied each in the shape of its writer as he had copied the father's letter, and Cael could see a hurried man and a careful one, a man who pressed hard and one who barely touched the page. Every one of them was an evaluator writing from somewhere that was not a desk.

Karis read the first one to herself, and then aloud.

It was an evaluator in the east, on a station circuit, about a young man of a carters' guild whose floor work ran a full rank ahead of his file. The evaluator had measured it at a frame and again on a guild floor and again by the clock, and he wrote that the file was plainly wrong, and asked that the young man be read again. At the foot of his report, in another hand, a registry clerk's stamp had been pressed square: *Read against file. Enter at file.* And under the stamp, a month later, the evaluator again, smaller: *Continue observation.*

"He gave in," said Brom.

"He did what the stamp told him," said Karis. "That's not the same thing, not quite." She laid it aside and took the next.

There were twenty-three. She read every one, and she read the stamp at the foot of every one, and after the sixth she stopped reading the stamp aloud because everybody in the room could say it with her. A woman in the hill country whose Stone came up too heavy for its column. A ferryman's boy whose Current had something of Wind in it that his station had not written down. A girl at a guild hall in the south who did everything her Path said she should, the evaluator wrote, only as if she were older than her paper. Each report saw the same thing and asked for the same thing, and each came back stamped the same way. *Read against file. Enter at file.* Some carried *Continue observation* under the stamp in the evaluator's own hand, and some carried nothing, and one carried a single line that Karis read twice before she read it out: *Noted, and I do not like it.*

When she had finished she gathered the twenty-three together and laid her hand on top of them.

"Down," she said, "every one. Where the file and the floor disagreed, the floor was corrected to the file, and never the other way."

"Never once?" said Brom.

"Not in this bundle. There's not one report where somebody was read higher than his paper and the paper went up to meet him." She looked at Seln. "Was there one in the press?"

"No," said Seln.

Cael sat on the bench and looked at the bundle under her hand.

He had spent all year writing himself down. Every Fifth-day in wing three he had stood in front of three cards and fought at a rate the house had chosen for him, a smaller man than the one he was, entered in the house's book at a figure that breathed and never rose. He had paid for it every week and called it a cost. But he had chosen it. He had sat at a long table with the people who mattered to him and decided, in ink, how much of himself to show, and he could have stopped any Fifth-day he liked. None of the twenty-three had chosen anything. Somebody had looked at what they were and stamped the page to say they were something less, and they had never even been told there was a page.

---

The memoranda were heavier than anything else on the table, and Karis turned the first stack over before she touched a word of it.

"Bottom first," she said, "always. The top of a pile is what somebody wanted you to find."

So she started at the oldest, at the yellow end, and worked forward. The sheets were printed, all of them, each a single leaf in a registry circular's form, with a number at the head and a seal pressed at the foot and a line of signatures under the seal. Cael watched her turn them, and the paper changed as she went, from a thick soft yellow stock that had gone brown at its edges to a thinner whiter one, and then to the hard bright paper the registry used now, the paper of the instrument on his own sill. The type changed too. The oldest leaves had heavy letters with long tails, the kind on the founders' volumes Karis kept on her top shelf, and the newest had the narrow even type of every form Bracken had ever put across a counter. The seal at the foot of each changed by small degrees, a ring of letters gone and another come, the river and the scale drawn first one way and then another. The signatures changed with every issue, officer after officer, a new name every few leaves.

The words did not change.

Karis read the heading of the oldest aloud, and then the heading of the newest, and then four from the middle at random, and they were all the same.

"*Memorandum to keepers of stations and to clerks of entry. Corrective classification at Kindling evaluation.*" She put her finger under the line on the newest leaf. "And the body. It's three sentences, and it's been three sentences since this one." She touched the yellowest. "A word changes here and there, and *shall* became *is to*. A clause about the post came in when the post did. Otherwise you could lay the oldest on top of the newest and read straight through them."

"How many?" said Lira.

"Nineteen issues, reissued every ten years or so, by the look of the seals, when the officers changed or the paper did. Nineteen times somebody sat at a desk and had it set in type again, and signed it, and sent it to every station in the Compact."

Brom had been looking at the stacks without reading anything. He had his good hand on the table and his chin down, and he spoke to the paper.

"That's a form," he said.

Karis looked at him.

"I don't mean the shape of it," said Brom. "I mean what it is. You don't reissue a thing nineteen times because you're still arguing about it; you reissue it because it's how things are done. The estate had forms like that, the harvest tally and the stable book, and nobody thought about them, because that was what they were for." He moved his hand off the table. "That's a form."

"Yes," said Karis, after a moment. "You make a form so that the next man doesn't have to decide anything. He only fills it in."

Lira had pulled the yellow stack a little toward her along the cloth. She did not pick anything up. She looked at the first leaf with its brown edges and its heavy type.

"The first one wasn't a form," she said. "It can't have been. The first one somebody had to argue for."

"Yes," said Karis. "The first one is the one I want." She drew it back into its place beside the others, very gently. "Not tonight. I want it in order first, all of it, every seal against every other seal. I won't guess at the first one; I'll find it."

Cael had been reading the signatures while she talked.

He could not help it. Under every seal there was a line of them, three or four to an issue, and he went down them the way he went down the names on a bout card, by habit, and without meaning to he had counted. Nineteen issues and seventy-one names, give or take two he could not read in the yellow ones where the ink had gone brown and spread. Officers of the registry, by the titles after them: a chief clerk of entry, a keeper of the rolls, a second secretary, men and women whose offices Bracken would have known and Cael had never heard of. Not one of them appeared on two issues: ten years at a desk, and then a new name.

"Did they know?" he said. "Any of them?"

"They knew what it said," said Karis. "It says it in three sentences. You can't sign three sentences and not know what they say." She looked down the row of names as he had. "Whether they knew what it did is a different question. I don't know how to answer it from here."

"Most of them signed a reissue," said Seln.

They looked at him. He had not moved.

"A reissue is the lightest paper in any office," he said. "The words were settled before you were born. The clerk brings it in with the coal accounts and the lamp-oil order, and you sign where he points, because the last man signed there and the man before him." He looked at the yellow stack. "I have signed reissues, as every officer has. You sign them the way you sign for coal."

Seventy-one names, thought Cael, and he looked for the one that would make it simple, the cruel one, the one who had known and wanted it. There was no such name on the table.

---

The leaf was last, as it had been last out of the case.

Seln moved for the first time since he had put his hands on the table. He went round the end of the table and drew the sleeve toward the lamp with one finger, and then slid the leaf out of it himself, by its edges, and laid it on the cloth with its face up. He did not let go of it at once.

"Everything else is my hand," he said. "The orders and the records and the reports I copied, at night, over five weeks. The memoranda were spares. The press held three or four of every issue and nobody had counted them in a generation, so I took one of each. This is the only thing in the case that someone else made." He took his finger off it. "A copy of it would have been worth nothing. Everything it says is in what it is."

Karis's hands were already in her coat pockets. She carried thin cotton gloves there all through the cold term, for the founders' volumes on her top shelf, and she drew them out now and worked them on while she kept her eyes on Seln and not on the leaf, as if it would be rude to look at it bare-handed. Only when the second glove was on did she reach across and turn it toward the lamp by one corner.

Cael could see it from the bench. It was small, smaller than any of the memoranda, a single leaf of a thick grey-brown paper that had a faint grain in it like cloth. The writing on it was not printed. It was ink, in a hand Cael had never seen, upright and very old-fashioned and very clear, with a heading and a short paragraph under it. There was no seal. At the foot there was a mark that might once have been a seal before something wore it away, or might never have been anything.

Karis read it aloud. She read it more slowly than she had read anything that night.

"*Of the advisory corrections function. The function is appended to the reading and is not the reading. It does not alter what the reading shows. It advises what is to be entered, and the entry is made as advised. It is to be maintained, and its maintenance is the office's charge.*"

She stopped.

Cael waited for her to go on. She did not. She sat with her gloved hands either side of the leaf and her eyes on the last line, and her face had gone into the stillness he had seen on it once before, at a citation in the seventh paragraph of the instrument on the first night of the year, and he knew she was frightened, and he knew she was frightened accurately.

"Appended," said Lira.

"Yes."

"To the reading."

"Yes." Karis did not look up. "And it's not the reading. It says so, and it goes out of its way to say so." She took a slip of paper from the pocket of her coat and a pencil, and wrote one word on the slip, and turned the slip face down on the cloth before anyone could read it. "I'm not going to say what I think, not tonight. I don't guess past paper, and I won't start in front of the paper that would make me."

"Nobody's asking you to," said Brom.

"I know." She laid the pencil across the face-down slip. "I'm telling myself."

Cael looked at the leaf from where he sat, upside down across the cloth, the old upright hand and the worn place at its foot.

*The function is appended to the reading and is not the reading.*

He thought of Denvash, which he did not often let himself do. A district office on a cold morning, a chalk circle on boards worn into two shallow places where a generation of feet had stood, and a warden-adjunct with tired grey eyes three feet away at a desk with a brass plate let into it. Something had woken behind his breastbone, small, a point of light at the edge of his sight that he could feel and never point to. He had counted eleven seconds while the plate did not light, and then the word had come, very far away and very close, and the plate lighting late with the same word in it, and the light at the edge of his sight going out like a pinched wick.

All his life since, he had said that his Arbiter had spoken once and gone dark. He had said it to panels and wardens and Magisters, and written it in two books. It had been the one fixed fact at the bottom of every argument he had ever won. It had read him once, and said one word, and the registry had written down that word because it had nothing else to write, and he lived in the gap the word left.

The leaf said there were two things in that office, not one. There was the reading, and there was something appended to it. And there had been eleven seconds.

He did not follow the thought any further. It was not his to follow. Karis had turned her slip face down, and he left his own face down beside it.

---

Seln said it then.

He did not sit down to say it, and he did not raise his voice. He stood at the middle of the table's far side with the whole of the case laid out in front of him in the lamplight, the forty and the bundle and the two stacks and the leaf, and he said it to the room the way he had once read a form to a man in a doorway at the third hour, one plain sentence after another.

"The registry corrects them. At Kindling, when the station reads them. Downward, by a form, for as long as it has had paper to write the form on. That is what I have carried."

He did not say it again. Nobody in the room said it again that night, in those words or any others. It lay on the table among the paper, said once, and nobody touched it.

Cael watched them hold it.

Lira's hands had come together in her lap, one inside the other, and stayed there. Karis had not taken her gloves off. She sat with them resting on the cloth either side of the leaf, as if she had forgotten they were on. Brom's good hand had gone, very slowly, to the left side of his coat, and lay over the inside pocket where the summons was, and the docket, and the letter from Velmere that he had carried sealed since the forty-fifth day. He did not take anything out. He only kept his hand there, the way a man puts his hand on a wall in the dark.

Seln's hands were flat on the table, as they had been all night.

Far off across the court, the lecture range's bell gave the half hour. It was the same bell that had rung the half hour every night of Cael's life on the hill. It sounded exactly the same.

It was Brom who spoke first, a long while after, with his eyes on the forty rows.

"How many?"

Seln looked at the rows too, and answered them rather than Brom.

"I stopped estimating. Estimating felt like filing."

Brom nodded slowly, as if that were an answer, and it was.

"Where?" said Lira.

"My fourth posting, twelve years ago." Seln put one hand back on the table, as if the wood steadied him. "A district seat in an old building, a records room on its top floor with presses round the walls, the kind with doors. I was looking for a subject's travel endorsements. They were filed where they should not have been, so I opened presses until I found them, and in the fourth press I found these." He moved his eyes over the rows. "The orders were in bound runs, a few years to a volume, with the records bundled behind them, and the memoranda on the bottom shelf in a box. And at the back of the box, loose, under the oldest, that." He did not look at the leaf. "Nobody had opened the press in years; the dust said so."

"And you copied it," said Karis.

"Over five weeks, at night. The posting gave me the room's key because I was a junior who stayed late, and juniors who stay late are trusted with keys." He said it without any pride at all. "One lamp on the floor, so the window stayed dark, and a few sheets a night in the lining of my coat. The case I bought afterward, in another town, from a man who made them for surveyors. I never told him what it was for, and he never asked, and he put his stamp inside the lid because he was proud of his work." He paused. "I did not understand it, and I want that said. I copied it because it was paper that sat a little above where it should have sat, and I had been trained to notice that, and I did not know what to do with what I noticed except keep it. For a year I thought it was a district's bad habit, a careless office."

"What changed?"

Seln was quiet a moment.

"A corridor," he said, "in a district station, a year on, with green distemper on the walls to the height of a man's shoulder, and benches. I was waiting for a file. A keeper came out of the frame room with a mother and a girl, and he was pleased, the way they are when a reading is a good one, and I heard him tell the mother that her girl read strong, and read into a second family as well, and he had not seen it in years." He looked at the father's letter, folded under its clip in the third row. "A week later I was back for another file, and the station's out-tray was on the counter, and her certificate was in it, waiting to go. One family, first rank." He took his hand off the table. "That was when the paper turned into children. It had been only paper for a year."

Lira was looking at him with her chin on her fist.

"And then twelve years," she said.

"And then twelve years."

---

"Two years on this hill," said Lira.

She said it the way she said a figure to a panel, plain and exact, and she did not take her eyes off Seln while she said it.

"Two years at that counter, and 'Mm,' and your cup turned a quarter on every table. Floor schedules on three doors in that hand, every week, so neat nobody's ever read one twice. Pleasant to every first-year who couldn't fill in a sheet, and to every Magister who wanted a room, and to me, the first week, when I didn't know where the east hall was and you walked me half across the court to show me." She took her chin off her fist. "With that on a shelf over your head the whole time, behind you, where you could feel it." She was quiet a moment. "I'd like to know what that cost, being pleasant."

Seln looked at her for a long time. Cael had never seen anyone ask him a question about himself and wait for the answer, and he did not think Seln had either.

"Nothing anybody would have noticed," he said at last. "That was the price."

Lira held his eyes a moment longer, and nodded, and let it be.

Karis had taken her gloves off and laid them side by side on the cloth beside the leaf. She was looking at the yellow stack again, at the memoranda, and when she spoke it was the voice she kept for craft, a reader asking another reader how he had done a thing.

"You said it sat a little above where it should have, the orders. But they're correct. I've read fourteen tonight, and every one is right: every date agrees with its record, every station is named the way the station names itself, every clip is on the right pair." She frowned at the rows. "There isn't a slip in them anywhere. How did a junior on his fourth posting look at paper that was correct in every particular and know it was wrong?"

"My mother's ledger was always right," said Seln.

Nobody understood him, and he saw it, and he went on, which he did not often do.

"She kept the books for a grain factor on a river quay, twenty years at the same desk with the same scale outside the window. I copied her ledgers from the time I could hold a pen, because the factor paid a boy a copper a page for the fair copies, and I was the boy." He looked at the stack of orders. "Every page of her ledger had a mistake on it, a weight misheard or a sack counted twice or a figure carried wrong. And every mistake was struck through with one line, so you could still read it, and initialled, and the right figure written beside it in the margin. That was what right looked like: a page that had been near something real, and been wrong, and been put right by a person who signed for it." He moved one hand toward the memoranda, the whole two centuries of them. "This has never once been wrong. There is not a struck figure in it, nor an initial in a margin. Two hundred years of paper made by people, and not one of them ever made a mistake." He took the hand back. "It had been made to agree."

Karis sat back on the bench.

"Yes," she said quietly. "Yes, that's how you'd know."

It was past the ninth bell. The lamp in the window would be wanted out within the hour or the valley would notice a difference, and the case had to be somewhere before it was.

"I need it for five nights," said Karis, "to index it. Every seal against every seal, every hand against every hand, the stations on a map, the dates in a column. I can't do it from memory and I won't do it from notes I take out of this room." She looked at Seln. "So it stays here, or it goes back to your shelf, and I come down to the wing every night with a lamp, and somebody writes the lamp down."

Seln looked round the room in his order again, the door and the windows and the landing and the shelves. It did not take him as long the second time.

"Here," he said.

He chose the place himself. He walked along Karis's shelves until he came to the row of her grey document boxes on the second shelf from the floor, the ones with her initials on their spines, which held her Greyvane papers and had not been moved in a year. Two of them stood apart by about the width of a man's hand. He measured the gap with his eyes and then with the case, and it went in between them and stood there, dark leather between grey card, a little shorter than its neighbours. From the door it looked like one more box nobody had labelled yet.

"The room has your key," he said to Karis. "The case has mine." He touched his waistcoat pocket. "Two keys, and neither of us opens it alone."

"The assistant never comes in after the sixth bell," said Karis. "From tonight. I'll tell her I'm working on something that smells."

"Tell her it's glue," said Brom. "She'll believe glue."

They put it back in the order it had come out, and it went back faster than it had come out, because Seln did it and nobody else touched it. The leaf went into its sleeve and the sleeve into the lid's pocket. The memoranda went in, oldest at the bottom. The bundle, tied again in the wing's knot. The forty, row by row, squared. The lid closed and the small key turned with its dry sound, and Seln carried the case to its place between the grey boxes and stood it there and came back.

Brom had begun to stand. He did it the careful way he had done everything for a week, getting his legs under him first and then the rest. Karis was reaching for the shaded lamp to turn it down. Lira had her hands on the bench to push herself up on the good foot.

Seln was buttoning his coat.

"Seln," said Cael.

Seln stopped with his hand on the third button.

"Will you sit down." His voice was steadier than he had expected. "It's my turn to put something on the table."

Lira let go of the bench first and sat back down on it, slowly, and put her right foot out straight again in front of her. Karis took her hand away from the lamp. Brom looked at Cael for a long time, and then he lowered himself back into the chair by the shelf without a word, and reached over with his good hand, and turned the kettle's little blue flame up again under the pot.

They all knew what he meant. They had known since a back room in Norhold on the night before the final, when Lira had told him to cost the seal out loud, and he had.

Seln looked at the three of them, and saw that they knew, and that he did not.

He took his hand off the button. He came back to the middle of the table's far side, and for the first time that night he sat down.

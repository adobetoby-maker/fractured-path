# Chapter 30 — The Upper Ferry

The clerk from the district seat found Havel at the inn by the bridge on the Third-day, a little before noon, reading the second manual for the fourth time that week.

He was a young man in a wet hat, and he apologised three times on the stair before he got to the door, and twice more inside it. The district seat's records room was a man short, he said. Two men, if you counted the one with the fever. A letter had come in from the registrar of the academy on the bluff, about an order and a seal, and it concerned the proceedings, and the senior records officer had said that the proceedings' own records support was in the town on its days off with nothing to do, and would the junior chair be so good as to check the registers and draft the usual.

"The usual," said Havel.

"The usual, sir." The young man held out a packet in oilcloth. "With the senior's compliments. He says there's no hurry before the Fourth-day evening."

Havel took it. He thanked the young man, who apologised again for the rain and went.

Inside the oilcloth were two things. The registrar's letter, in a clear upright hand that Havel had seen once before at the foot of an exhibit list, asking that the instrument constituting a certain office be supplied, together with the seal under which that instrument was itself issued. And a sheet of thin paper with a pencil rubbing on it of a seal, taken carefully, the grey of the lead built up in even strokes until the device stood out white: the registry's own pattern, the river and the scale, inside a beaded rim.

He laid the rubbing on the table by the window and looked at it for a long time without touching it.

His right hand ached at the web of the thumb. It had ached every morning for a year. He opened it and closed it twice under the table, out of habit, though there was nobody in the room to see.

It was a good rubbing. It was also a good seal. That was the first thing he noticed and the thing he went on noticing all afternoon: whoever had cut the die had cut it properly. The river ran the right way. The scale's beam was level. The beading round the rim was even and close, and in the beading, at the bottom, where every registry die carried its cutter's number, there were four small figures that the pencil had picked out faintly, as a pencil does when it passes over something raised. He took the reading glass out of his kit and bent over them until he was sure of all four.

The usual was a letter of two sentences. He could have written it in his sleep. But the usual came after the registers, and so, because it was the order the manual gave, he went to the registers first.

He checked it three ways, because there were three registers, and he did not think of it as anything more than that.

The first was the district's own impression book. It lived in the records room at the district seat, on a slanted desk of its own under the window, a book as tall as a child and as thick as a door-step. Every die issued for use in the district went into it on the day of issue, pressed into a red wafer and pasted on the page in the order it came, with the office's name and the date beside it in the keeper's hand. Havel turned its pages for two hours on the Third-day afternoon with the clerk in the wet hat hovering at his elbow, offering to help and being thanked. He found the seat's own seal on its page, forty years old, the river and the scale. He found the fee office's, and the bridge-ward's, and a ferry company's, and a hundred and eighty others.

He did not find the rubbing's.

That proved nothing. A facilities office constituted for a proceeding might very well be a continental office and not a district one, and then its die would never have been issued here at all.

So the second was the registry's printed schedule of the seals of office, which was continental. It came out every year in a stiff grey binding, with a plate for every office that had a seal, engraved to the size of the die. The district seat kept the current year and the four before it on a shelf behind the senior records officer's chair. Havel took down the current year and went through it plate by plate, the next morning, with the rubbing beside him on a clean blotter. Then he went through last year's, in case. Then he took down the current year again and went through the index of offices, under *F*, and under *P* for *proceedings*, and under *S* for *seat*.

The rubbing was not in it.

The third was the number.

Every die the registry used was cut by one of three cutters, and every cutter numbered every die in the beading of its rim, and every number went into the cutters' issue ledger against the office the die was cut for. The ledger itself was in the west. A copy was kept at every district seat, in the strong-room, and brought up to date every quarter by the post. Havel signed for the strong-room key at noon on the Fourth-day, with the clerk as his witness, and went down with a lamp.

The ledger ran in numbers, in order, without a gap. That was the point of it. He found the run where the rubbing's four figures ought to be, and laid his finger on the line above and the line below.

The line above was a die cut for a ferry office on a southern river. The line below was a die cut for a district fee office in the east. The two numbers were one apart.

The rubbing's number was the number between them.

There was no line between them. There was no room on the page for one. The ledger went straight from the ferry office to the fee office in the same ink, on consecutive lines, as it had been written, and the number on the rubbing was simply not a number the registry had ever cut.

He stood in the strong-room with his finger on the page and the lamp hissing for perhaps a minute. Then he wrote the line above and the line below on a slip of paper, with their numbers, and the rubbing's number between them, and nothing else. He locked the strong-room and gave the key back and signed for its return.

---

The facilities office was in the district's register of offices. He had expected that too. It was entered there in a good clerk's hand: constituted this year, for the proceedings at the seat at Ostrand, by an instrument filed at the district seat under seal. The instrument was in the file. He asked for it, and it came, and he looked at it.

It was registry stock. The seal at its foot was the rubbing's seal, the same die, the same four figures in the beading. Everything about it was correct.

He drafted the usual at the senior records officer's own table, on the district seat's paper, while the senior watched the rain.

*The office named in the registrar's letter was constituted on the date entered in the district's register of offices, by instrument filed at this seat under seal. The registrar is respectfully referred to the issuing authority of the said instrument for any further particulars.*

Two sentences. They were both true. The senior read them, nodded, signed, and handed them back for the clerk to copy fair. It would go up the hill with the next bag.

The form of the usual had no line for what Havel had found. There was a line for the office and a line for the instrument and a line for where to write next. There was nowhere on it to say that the seal on the instrument was in none of the registers that seals were kept in. Nobody had ever needed such a line. A seal was a thing that was in the registers. That was what made it a seal.

He did not add a line. It was not his letter. It was the district's, and the district had a form, and the form was correct.

At the inn that evening the river was louder than he had ever heard it.

His window looked down on the bridge. A chain had gone across the near end of it at the fourth bell, he had watched it go from the strong-room stair, and now the bridge-ward's lantern hung on the chain-post and nobody crossed. Below the lantern the water stood up against the middle pier in a smooth brown hump, as if something large were resting underneath it, and went away downstream with a noise like a crowd a long way off.

He took the five sheets out from between the second manual and the third and laid them on the table in their order.

He read them through. The first went back to a pump step at Ardenmere and a form of four questions, and to the mark in a boy's file that no office would own. The fifth was a sentence on a senior seat's desk at Norhold, read upside down, closing a door that the first had found open. In between were three more. He read them all twice, slowly, the way the manual said a records officer should read anything he meant to rely on.

Then he took a clean sheet of the same kind and dipped his pen, and wrote the date, and under it the sixth entry, in the plain hand he kept for the sheets.

*The seal on the instrument constituting the facilities office named in the order presented at Halcenvane, and on the order itself (registrar's rubbing): absent from the district's impression book; absent from the registry's schedule of seals of office, this year's and last; its rim number absent from the cutters' issue ledger, which runs without a line between the numbers on either side of it. Checked three ways. Absent from every registry of seals.*

He read it back. It recorded something that had happened. It did not say what he made of it. That was the rule of the sheets, and he had kept it six times.

He laid the sixth beside the first.

A mark with no office behind it. A seal with no register under it. They lay side by side on the table in the lamplight, written years apart, in the same hand. He looked at them for a long while, and he did not make anything of them, because there was nothing in the rule that let him. They were two facts. They were on two sheets.

He understood, sitting there, that there would not be a seventh.

It was not a decision. He did not decide it. It was more as if he had come to the end of a ledger and found the last page ruled off. Every one of the six recorded something the registers could not answer, and he had always been able to write it down because the registers answered everything else, and he could lay one against the other and see exactly where the gap was. This one had gone through all three registers and come out the other side. There was nothing left to lay a seventh against. A seventh would have to be something he thought, and the sheets did not hold what he thought. They never had.

He put the six back between the second manual and the third, the same way round, and buckled the kit.

Then he sat at the window with his hands folded, the right one aching, and listened to the river until the bridge-ward's lantern went out.

---

The coach went down the bluff road in the dark with its lamps lit and the rain gone east in the night.

Cael sat with his back to the horses and watched the house go up behind them into the black, the lodge window first, then the lecture range, then nothing. The wind had come round behind the rain and was drying everything it could reach. It was cold. The coachman had his collar up to his hat and was talking to the near horse in the low steady voice that never stopped.

At the foot of the road the two coats under the chandler's eaves stood away from the wall to watch them turn. Cael saw one of them lift his head to read which way. West, along the bank road. The man did not move after that. There was nowhere he needed to go with it at that hour; he would write it on his board and give it to the next man on the bell, and by noon it would be wherever such things went.

Inside the coach nobody talked.

Brom had the two record cases on his knees, one on top of the other, with his arms round them. He had not let them ride on the roof on the day of the exhibits and he was not going to let them ride on the roof today, in mud. Karis sat beside him with the attestation wallet in her lap and her lips moving. She was saying the form over, the questions the seat's clerk would ask the maker and the enrollee and the advocate, and the answers, under her breath, the way Lira counted her bursts before a bout. Lira was asleep against the window with her coat over her like a blanket and her mouth a little open. She could sleep anywhere in a moving vehicle. She said it was the only useful thing she had learned from guilds.

Seln sat in the far corner with his eyes shut and his hands in his lap. He had sat like that in the coach on every road all year. Cael had long ago stopped thinking it meant he was asleep.

The bank road ran west along the near side of the river, high on its terrace, with the water on the left below them roaring in the dark. Twice the wheels went into standing water to the hub and the coach lurched, and the coachman talked to the horses until they leaned into it and pulled out. Once a fallen branch lay across the road and Brom got down and dragged it off into the ditch and got back in, wet to the knees, and put the cases back on his lap without a word.

The sky over the river began to go grey.

Hesk's book was inside Cael's coat, against his ribs, where it had been every day since the gate. He could feel the corner of it when the coach rocked. He thought about the leaf at the front of it with four sets of initials, and *NOBODY GOES IN AFTER HIM ALONE* pressed so hard into the paper that you could read it from the back.

He did not take the book out. He knew what it said.

---

The upper ferry crossed at the narrows, where the river ran between two shoulders of grey rock not much farther apart than the Crown yard was long.

It was a flat chain ferry, broad enough for one coach and its team and nothing else. A great chain ran from an iron ring sunk in the rock on the near side, down under the water, and up again to a winch-house on the far bank, and the ferry hauled itself along the chain by a wheel turned from its deck. The river came through the narrows deep and fast and brown and very loud. But it could not spread. There was no width for it to throw anything about in. It simply went through, as water goes through a pipe, and the ferry sat on top of it on its chain like a cat on a wall.

The ferryman came out of his hut at the coach lamps with a lantern of his own. He was an old man, thin as a rake, in a coat that had been mended so often it was more mending than coat. He looked at the coach, and at the house's mark on its door, and at the river.

"You'll be the academy," he said. "From up the far end."

"We will," said the coachman.

"Thought you might. Town ferry tied up at noon. Bridge chained at four." He went down the ramp to the deck and stood with one hand on the wheel and looked up at them. "Well, come on. She doesn't mind it. She's never minded it."

They led the horses on with their eyes covered, and the coach after them, with all five of them standing on the deck beside it because the coachman said they would ride steadier that way. The ferryman put his weight on the wheel. The chain came up out of the water in front of them dripping and went down again behind, and the deck began to move.

The old man talked to the chain while he turned. Cael could not hear what he said over the river. It was a long steady murmur with no pauses in it, the same sound the coachman made to his horses, and the chain answered it with a clank at every link that went over the wheel.

Halfway across, the ferryman stopped talking to the chain and talked to them instead.

"Road's good your side," he said, nodding at the far bank without taking his hands off the wheel. "Up the hill and through the cut and down the other side, and then it's all flat to the city. It'll be soup on the flat, mind. But the cut's dry." He gave the wheel another turn. "Only stretch that ever is. It's chalk, the cut. Drinks the rain like a sponge and blows dry in an hour, and you'll come out the far end white as millers."

"Thank you," said Karis.

"No trouble." He spat over the side, politely, downstream. "Had a load of hurdles over last evening for the farm on the hill, and that's been my week. You're the most company I've had."

On the far landing the coachman led the horses up the ramp and put them to, and they all got back in, all but one.

Seln stood on the landing with his collar up.

"I'll walk," he said. "There's a path along the bank under the road. A man on foot gets up the hill quicker than a coach does, and a man on foot by a river is a fisherman." He looked up at the coachman. "Where's the next milestone past the cut?"

"At the culvert, the far end," said the coachman. "Seven to the city, it says."

"Then I'll be at the culvert."

He did not explain. Nobody asked him to. He turned and went down off the end of the landing onto a narrow path among the willows, with the river roaring at his left hand, and in about the time it took Cael to look away and back again he was not a thing that anybody would have looked at twice. He was a grey shape among grey trees. Then he was not even that.

Cael put it away with the other things he had counted that morning. Seln had not been asked. He had chosen the road himself.

---

The road went up from the landing in two long bends, between hawthorn hedges, and then the hill closed in on both sides of it and they were in the cut.

It was a deep straight cutting through a spur of the hill that came down to the river. Somebody a long time ago had decided to go through the spur rather than round it, and had dug a road down into the chalk and carted the chalk away, and the walls were still there, white and steep and streaked with green where the water ran. On the north side, uphill, the bank stood twenty feet over the road. On the south side, toward the river, it was lower, ten or twelve feet. Toward the far end of the cut, a stone's throw short of where it opened out again, the south bank had been eaten away underneath by some flood long ago, before the road had a ditch, so that its lip hung out over a hollow of shadow, like a brow over an eye.

The road itself was twenty-odd feet wide, cambered high in the middle so the water would run off it, with a ditch along the north side. It was dry. The ferryman had been right. The wind came along the cut from the west behind them and lifted the chalk dust off the crown in little white skirls, and the horses' feet sent it up in clouds.

A skein of geese went over, very high, calling.

Cael counted the cut out of the window as they went, because he counted everything. Four hundred and forty yards, near enough, by the coachman's pace and the bends at each end. At the far end, the east end, the road narrowed to cross a culvert where a stream came down a gully off the hill and went under the road toward the river, with a low stone parapet on either side. Beside the near parapet stood a milestone. He could not read it from that distance. It would say seven.

Across the road just short of the culvert stood a cart.

It was an ordinary farm cart with its shafts down on the road and no horse in them, slewed across the crown so that it blocked most of the way. One of its wheels was off and lying flat beside it. There was nobody near it.

The coachman pulled up forty yards short and sat looking at it. Then he said something to the horses and put the brake on.

"Wheel off," he said, through the trap. "I'll see if it'll shift."

"Wait," said Cael.

He opened the door and got down into the white dust before anyone could stop him, and walked to the coach's front wheel on the south side, and bent to it as if to look at the hub. Then he went down on one knee on the road and put his palm flat on the chalk.

He sent the read down.

It went out of him through the hand into the ground the way it went into a floor, the cheapest thing he owned and the one he trusted most. It did not read chalk the way it read a man. It read weight. The road under him was carrying the coach, four tons of it through the wheels, the horses in front, the cart's dead weight across the crown forty yards on. All of that was ordinary. He knew the shape of ordinary.

And there were places in the cut where the ground was carrying weight and nothing stood there.

He counted them. He went round the cut with the read as carefully as he had ever gone round anything, and then he went round it again.

Fourteen.

Two on the milestone. One on each parapet of the culvert. Three in the north bank, high, where nobody could reach. Two in the south bank's lip. Three in a line across the road behind them, at the west mouth of the cut, where they had come in a minute ago. And three more in the road itself, between the coach and the cart, in a rough curve.

Each one was a weight with no body, pressed into the chalk at a single point, as if somebody very heavy were standing there on one toe. They did not move. They did not drain anything, the way Ternhall's anchors had drunk the heat out of Karis's contacts into a floor. They were not strung across the air like the Lattice Path's lines, to be found by walking into them. They were points, and they held.

Together they made a frame. He saw its shape as he knelt there, as clearly as he had seen the chalk court on the oak. Both mouths of the cut were closed. The west mouth behind them had three points across it, and the east had the cart and the culvert and the milestone. The north bank was too high. The middle of the road was held. There was one way through the whole cut that the points did not close, and it was a channel along the foot of the south bank, under the overhanging lip, perhaps twelve feet wide, running east past the cart's tail to the culvert.

He took his hand off the road.

"Fourteen," he said.

He said it to the coach door, not loudly. Karis's face was in the window. He saw her understand it. He saw Lira, awake now, understand it a beat after.

Then they came.

They came all at once and without any noise to speak of. A man stood up from behind the cart, where there had been no man a moment ago, and stood beside its wheel with his hands at his sides. Two more came down the north bank on ropes that had been lying ready in the grass along the top, and dropped into the ditch and stood up out of it. Two came up out of the gully beyond the culvert and walked along the parapet onto the road. Every one of them was in grey road clothes with a hood, and every one had a scarf drawn up over the lower half of the face, so that there was nothing to see of any of them but a band of skin and a pair of eyes.

They carried no blades. Cael saw that at once, because it was the first thing he looked for. They had short staves at their belts, and two of them had coils of soft rope, and the one at the cart had a pair of leather hobbles hanging from his belt, and a hood of dark cloth, folded.

And one walked out of the shadow under the south bank's lip.

She came out of the hollow at an ordinary walking pace and stopped on the crown of the road, halfway between the coach's horses and the cart, on the one flat patch where the camber levelled before it fell away to either side. She stood three-quarters turned from the coach. She had a hood and a scarf like the others. He could not see her face.

He did not need to see it. He had read her four times across forty feet of stone in the outer court six days ago, and drawn her on the oak in chalk, and written her shape in the notebook with no numbers in it. She had stood on the one level place in the court. She stood on the one level place in the cut. Every line out of the cut went past her.

The man at the cart spoke. He spoke to the coachman, not to anybody in the coach, in a flat even voice that carried along the chalk walls without being raised.

"Stay on the box. Nobody here wants you." He moved his head a fraction, toward the culvert. "The one we want walks east to the culvert. Nobody else is wanted. Nobody else need be touched."

There was no paper. There was no seal. There was no office, and no name, and no reason given, and no place to write any of it down.

Cael stood up out of the dust.

He looked at the hobbles and the folded hood and the soft rope, and the staves that had been chosen because they bruised and did not cut, and the six faces that were not faces. He looked at the channel along the south bank, twelve feet wide, running to the culvert. He thought of the gully beyond it, which he could not see into, and what a man would keep there if he had come to take a person somewhere.

"Custody," he said. "Not assault."

He said it to the four in the coach, and he said it so they would all hear the same thing at the same time. They wanted him whole. They wanted him walking, or carried, but whole. That changed what they would and would not do to him. It did not change what they would do to anyone who stood in the way.

Brom came out of the coach on the south side.

He did not hurry. He stepped down into the dust with the two record cases, and set them on the coach step, side by side, squared, and then walked forward along the foot of the south bank, under the lip, into the channel. He stopped in the middle of it. He turned round to face the cart and the culvert and the gully, and set his feet, and stood.

He did not look at Cael. He did not need to. It was one step from there to anywhere a man might want to go.

Lira got down at the coach's tail and stood with her weight on the balls of her feet, looking at the four men by the ditch and the parapet, at their feet, not their eyes. Karis came out last, on the north side, and went to the near horse's head. The horse was standing very still. Too still. Cael saw Karis put out her hand toward its foreleg and stop, a hand's breadth short, as if she had touched a wall in the dark.

The man at the cart lifted one hand.

"On my count," he said, to his own people, and began it.

# Chapter 11 — Choosing

Withrow sent for him at the ninth bell that evening, by the porter's boy, with no reason given.

When he knocked and went in she was not at her desk. She was at the glass-fronted case under the framed charter on the far wall, with the key from her chain in its lock, and she did not turn round until she had taken out what she wanted and locked the case again behind it.

It was a buff folder, thick, soft at the spine, with the house's name lettered on the front in a clerk's hand gone brown with age. Every chartered house kept one. Every inspector who came up its road read it before he looked at a single floor. No enrollee ever saw it.

She put it into his hands, and went and sat down behind her desk, and folded her own hands on the blotter, and said nothing at all.

He sat in the visitor's chair with the folder on his knees. He opened it at the back, because that was where the house's counsel would have started.

The newest leaf was the eighth day's. It was in the registry's flat grey type, sent up the road to be pinned in, because a house had to keep its own folder current with whatever the registry entered against it.

*Respondent: the academy of Halcenvane, in formal proceedings before the seat at Ostrand. Subject of proceedings: the basis on which it enrolled Caelen Hesk-ward, unclassified, its enrollee of record. Status: pending.*

Beside it the right-hand column was ruled, as every column in the folder was ruled, and empty.

He turned back a leaf, and another. The house's placings, a long thin column of meets and figures, and last year's the only one with *third* in it. Inspections, each with its date and its officer's name and its finding. And near the front, two notes of the kind no house wanted, both in hands from long before Withrow's: a fire-watch that had let its book lapse for a quarter, and a records office that had left a year's declarations unindexed. Beside each, in the right-hand column, the remedy the house had made, and beside the remedy a clearance, stamped and initialled by whoever had come to see it done. Then the charter's grant on its heavier paper, under a seal gone the colour of tea.

He turned forward again to the eighth day. The empty column there was the same width as the columns beside the fire-watch and the records office. It had been ruled by the same machine.

"The next inspector will read it the other way," said Withrow. "From the grant forward, the way they're taught. He'll come to that leaf last." She did not move her hands. "If the seat finds the house was wrong to enroll you, the finding goes into that column, and his inspection is a long one, and so is every one after it. If we have every piece of luck there is, a clerk writes *sustained* in that column one day and stamps it. The leaf is still there above the stamp. It never comes out."

She opened her desk drawer, and took out a paper, and laid it on the folder on his knees. Then another, and another, without a word between them. The program's charter, dated the first morning of the year. The page of the house's accounts with Karis's line on it. The intake's roll, longer than any he had seen pinned to a board. The tournament's sheet with the house's placing on it.

"That's what the column sits on," said Withrow. "I put every one of those beside your name myself. Nobody made me." She closed the drawer. "I'm not sorry, and I'm not asking you to be. I'm showing you the bill, once, plainly, across a desk, so you'll never have to lie awake at three in the morning adding it up for yourself."

He did not say anything. He had learned that when she spoke in that voice the right answer was to have heard.

"Now the other column."

She took the papers back off the folder, one by one, and returned them to the drawer.

"When the house first asked me to take you," she said, "I sent to Ostrand for the Greyvane transcript. I read it the way I used to read appeals, when I was twenty years a registry officer and nothing else: hunting for the trick. The place where somebody clever has made a law say what it doesn't." She looked at him across the desk. "I'm a good hunter. I didn't find one. It wasn't there to find."

"And this paper?"

"I've hunted it the same way. It's honest law. So is ours. And Karis's doors are real." She set both hands on the blotter, a hand's width apart. "That's two true things, and they point opposite ways, and I can only stand on one of them. So I've decided which. I'm going to stand on the first, out loud, in front of that seat and the broadsides and every inspector who ever opens that folder, with this whole house on my back, knowing where the second one goes." She did not lower her eyes. "Make a note of the word *choosing*."

He took out the observation notebook and turned to her pages, two years of small facts in his cramped hand: how she crossed a court, how long she spoke at convocation, how many times she had lifted a page to stop a hall's noise, and at the bottom, from the first morning of this year, *load-bearing calm*. Under it he wrote the date and the word. Then he underlined it twice, once against the notebook's edge and once freehand, the way she had underlined *discretion* in her pocket code on the procedure night, and he did not write anything else, because nothing else was needed.

She watched him do it and did not pretend she had not.

"May I ask you something?" said Cael.

"You may ask."

"If you'd known on the morning you signed for me that it would come to this, a folder and a seal and a hood. Would you have signed?"

Withrow did not answer at once. She looked past him at the charter on the wall, in its frame, with the glass a little dusty along the top edge where the porters' cloths did not reach.

"I did know it might," she said. "Not the hood. I'd never have guessed the hood. But a folder, yes, one day, and a column with nothing in it." She brought her eyes back to him. "That's what I meant when I told the hall to leave what it is where anybody can check. Somebody checks. Then you find out whether you meant it." She stood. "I meant it. I'd like that to be the last time either of us asks."

"On Fifth-day, when you get into that coach," said Withrow, "sit up straight. The town watches the coach."

---

He drew the year that night on a clean page of the notebook: a long ruled line from the first day to the last, with a tick for every Fifth-day.

He put the sittings on it as the notice gave them. The conference, past. The first sitting, the sixth tick. Brom's panel he put at the last tick, because Bracken's docket said so. Lira's first meet off the hill he put where Rooke's board in the east hall had it, six weeks on. Then he looked for the re-evaluation and found the notice's words instead of a day: *fixed by the seat in its ruling*.

Every other mark on the page was a date somebody he knew had written down. That one would be written by a hand under a hood, on a morning he could not yet see. He left a gap on the line for it, and found he had drawn the gap carefully, the way you leave a space at a table for somebody who has not yet come in.

Karis had found the other thing by morning.

She had borrowed Brom's docket out of his left inside pocket to copy its date, and Brom had stood in the annex doorway until she gave it back, which she did inside a quarter of an hour. She had the two dates on a slip, one above the other. Brom's panel, the last Fifth-day of the year, at the district seat in Ostrand. The seat's ruling, before the year's close, and there was only one Fifth-day in the year's last week.

"The same morning," said Cael.

"A street and a half apart." Karis looked at the slip. "Somebody will say it's spite. It isn't. It's a clerk with a calendar who likes his Fifth-days full."

"Is that better?"

"It's worse. Spite gets tired." She put the slip in the back of her notebook, behind the six from the long table. "A clerk doesn't."

She showed it to Brom at supper, because it was his date, and he had a right to it before anybody else made anything of it. He read the slip with his spoon in his other hand and gave it back.

"Then I'll be in town that morning either way," he said. "When mine's done I'll come up the street and sit at the back of yours."

"If you come out of yours Iron," said Lira, "and the seat's still talking, sit where he can see you."

"I was going to," said Brom, and went back to his beans.

The sittings went up on the covered walk's middle board that afternoon in Bracken's hand, under the house's one-line answer from the eighth day: *Sittings at the registry seat, Ostrand, on the seat's Fifth-days. First sitting: the sixth Fifth-day of the year.* Underneath it, smaller, Bracken had added a line of his own, which was a thing he never did on a public board. *The house will attend.*

"How many?" Cael asked at the counter.

"A dozen, if the year runs honest and nobody falls off a horse." Bracken was sorting the noon post into its pigeonholes. "It never runs honest. More."

On the Fifth-day that week he fought in wing three at the band, against a Blade.

She was an Iron Rank Five of the third year, with a long patient guard that liked to make you come to it, and a habit of holding her edge-line a hand's breadth lower than the cohort taught, so that a man who read the line by the cohort's rule stepped onto it. Cael read her weight instead of her line, and went to her slowly in the first exchange and took it with a burst off the left foot when her back heel lifted. In the second she walked him into the corner by the south door, one patient step at a time, and he spent a second burst getting out of it and came out a stride short of where he wanted to be. The third was the rotation's: it went to her, whatever it cost, and it cost him her edge-line laid across his forearm, hard, a sting that went through the wrap and stayed there for an hour. In the fourth he waited for the heel again and it came, and the touch was his.

Twenty-two and a third. Gault gave the clerk the figure. Ephram, at the rail, wrote it in his chart and did not draw a question mark, and did not look up, and Cael found he was grateful to him for not looking up.

---

On the twenty-seventh evening, at the north wall, the coats were early.

He had sat at the third buttress for the change of the bell more evenings than he could count, and it had always gone the same way: the bell from the lecture range, and on its last stroke two coats coming along the river street to take the places at the road's foot, and the two who had stood there going off toward the bridge. Tonight the new pair came round the corner of the chandler's a full minute before the bell. They stood a little way off along the river street and waited for it, with their hands in their coats, and only when it rang did they walk the last few yards and take their places.

Men who are early for something are waiting for it to happen.

On the third evening of the year he had written on this coping that men with no questions get sent at the end of something, not the start. He had not known then what the something was. He had a word for it now. The paper served on the eighth day had begun a thing that would end in a ruling, and the ruling would turn him from one kind of entry in a file into another. That was what grey coats of a senior grade were posted to stand beside: not a boy, but the moment the boy's paper changed.

*They're waiting for a status change,* he wrote on the coping in the dusk. *The proceedings will make one.*

He told the other two on the residence stair, because they had a right to it, and because he had sat on the coping long enough for the words to have set. Lira listened with her hand on the rail. Brom listened with his eyes on the stair window, which looked the wrong way for the river and showed only the court.

"Early," said Lira. "For the first time."

"A full minute."

"Then they've been told there's a date," said Brom. He did not say what date. Neither of them did. Lira took her hand off the rail and went up, and after a moment Brom went up after her, and Cael stood on the stair a little longer looking at the court, which had nobody in it.

---

Lira asked for the honest floor on the Sixth-day night, three nights before her trial, and she asked for it in a way she had never asked for anything.

"I want you to be a floor," she said.

They were in the east hall again, at the quarter to the eleventh bell, on Rooke's key. The shutters were drawn along the gallery and the two lanterns stood at either end of the painted lines. Brom had brought the jug. Karis had come this time, for the first hour, with her notebook under her arm. She sat on the gallery stair and said she would take the figures as they happened, counting each one from its start the way Cael had counted on the first night, and that nobody was to break anything she would have to spell.

"A floor," said Cael.

"The Force," said Lira. "The third-year. I'm fighting him on Second-day, in front of the rating table, and I've drilled beside him every morning for two months, and I know exactly how he fights a drill." She was taping her wrists, quickly. "I don't know how he fights me. Nobody's ever made him."

Cael knew the Force a little. A broad-backed young man who laughed when he was surprised; Rank Seven on the porter's wall since the last sitting of the old year, six ranks above Lira's one, and the best fighter the cohort had made in a straight line, without a program, since Ephram. A Force fighter's Path was a shove, and it went on contact. This one put it into the floor through his front foot. He stamped, and a beat later the boards within a stride of the stamp jumped, and went on jumping for a count or two before they settled. Then the shove came back up into him, the way it always came back into a Force fighter along the line it had gone out by, and he had to take it in his knees before he could stamp again.

"He settles," said Lira. "Every time. That's him taking his own push back. Then he stamps again, a stride further on. Settle, stamp, step. By the third stamp the floor in front of him is like trying to cross a cart while it's going." She tore the tape. "I can't get Brom to do that. Brom's a wall. Walls don't move the floor."

"I don't move the floor either."

"No." She looked up. "But you move the air. You lay a road that leans. If you lay one across my line, it's not his floor, but it's a floor that tips under me when I don't expect it, and I can learn what my feet do on it." She stood. "Lay them across me. I'll cross them on the four-count. Brom stands at the end of the line, where the Force would be, and if I get to him I touch him."

Cael looked at Brom. Brom, unwinding his wraps, looked back.

"The lane costs," said Cael. "Ten counts, every time. Behind the eyes."

"I know what it costs. I wrote it on my hand." Lira went to the first cross. "Lay one. We'll write what it costs."

He laid one.

He set it a stride in front of her and across her line, from the north wall toward the south, a road of air fifteen feet long and no wider than his shoulders, tipping toward the south. His ears closed and opened. The dust in the cracks of the painted boards woke and ran south down the lane, a pale thin stream. Then he took the read off the lane, deliberately, the way he had learned on this floor three weeks ago, and gave it back to himself, and stood.

Lira ran the four counts at the lane.

On the first count she stepped. On the second she put her weight on her front foot, the long lie, and her front foot came down inside the lane, and the lane took it. It slid her front foot a hand's breadth south, sideways, out from under her, and her whole lie went wrong with it. She did not fall. She caught herself on the back foot with a grunt and stood there off balance in the painted floor's dust, and the four-count was gone.

"Again," she said.

On the gallery stair Karis had begun to count, quietly, from the moment the dust ran. He tested the Wind at every number. It came late, and late, a beat behind his asking each time, until she said *eleven*, and then it came when he called it. Ten counts, the same bill as the first night. She wrote it down, and he laid another in the same place, and she began to count again.

She tried the second count outside the lane this time, short of it, and the third count, the empty one, with one foot in the lane and one out. The lane took the foot that was in. She stood through it. She let it slide her front foot, the way a fighter lets a push go by, and on the fourth count she burst off the back foot, the one that was still on honest boards, across the lane and through the end of it, and came out of the burst three strides from Brom with the dust still running behind her.

"Three strides," said Brom.

"Three strides," said Lira. "Short." She was breathing hard. "But I didn't fall. Again."

He laid a third.

She ran it, and it was better: the empty count taken standing, one foot in and one foot out, the lane sliding the inside foot and Lira letting it. She burst on the fourth off the honest foot, and the burst carried her a stride from Brom, and she touched his shoulder. Brom nodded. Lira turned round, grinning, to say something.

And Cael found he had lost the count.

Karis had started it as the third lane went down, as she had started the others, and he had been testing the Wind against her numbers. Somewhere about six he had stopped hearing them. Nothing had happened. The weight behind his eyes had simply stayed, warm and heavy, and the numbers had gone on past him like carts past a man asleep on a bench. He reached for the Wind out of habit, the way you test a stair, and it came late.

"Nine," said Karis, from the stair.

Late.

"Ten."

Late. Lira had turned round. She came across the floor to him fast and stood in front of him and looked at his eyes.

"Eleven."

He reached. Late.

"Twelve."

Late. He felt Lira's hand close on his sleeve.

"Thirteen."

It came on time.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Lira took the pencil stub out of her pocket and wrote on her palm, under the place where the tape stopped. "Ten, ten, twelve," she said. "Clean at eleven, eleven, thirteen." She looked up. "That's the last one tonight."

"I'm all right."

"I know you're all right. It's still the last one." She put the pencil away. "You'd only ever met the ten counts by themselves. Tonight you met them three times in a row and the third ran long. That's a new figure. We don't spend a new figure the night we find it. Rooke says that." She almost smiled. "Rooke says it about me."

Karis, on the stair, read her column back aloud without being asked: *First lane, slow through ten. Second, through ten. Third, through twelve; clean at thirteen. Not tested past three.* Then she shut the notebook, said she had what she had come for, and went up to bed.

They sat on the boards afterward among the white crosses with the jug going round.

"What did you learn," said Brom, to Lira.

Lira lay on her back with her taped hands on her stomach, looking up at the dark shutters.

"Not to put my weight down on the third count," she said. "I've been doing the lie with a real foot. On his floor the real foot is the one that moves. So I lie with a foot I don't care about, and I keep the one I'm going to burst from on wood that holds still." She turned her head on the boards. "And I go on the fourth. Not the third. Everything I've ever done says go on the third, and against him the third is when the boards are still jumping from the last stamp. The fourth is when he's taking his own push back."

"He'll laugh at you," said Brom. "When you stand there on the empty count."

"Let him," said Lira. "He laughs when he's surprised. I'd like him surprised."

Cael sat with the jug in his hands and the last warmth going out from behind his eyes, and thought about the twelve.

It was the first new thing the lane had told him since the morning it settled. He had believed the ten counts were the price, fixed, like the five free bursts on boards; a thing he could plan around, write in a minute before a fight, and pay. Now he knew they were not fixed. They grew when he spent the lane over and over. He did not know yet by how much, or whether they grew without end, or whether they came back down with rest. He knew only that a figure he had thought was a wall was a slope.

He realized he was glad. Not of the slope, but of where he had found it: on a painted floor at night, with Karis counting from the stair and Lira's hand on his sleeve and Brom standing at the end of the line, and not in front of a crowd with a Gold across the ring. That was the whole reason for the floor. Brom had said it three weeks ago, on this floor. *So you don't forget it's yours.* It was his, and it had a slope in it, and now he knew where.

They went back across the court in the dark, the three of them, with the lanterns out and the jug empty and Brom carrying both. Lira walked on Cael's left, close, the way she walked when she meant to say something and had not yet decided how.

"Are you going to tell Rooke?" she said at the residence door. "About the twelve."

"It's not his floor. It's ours. He said he didn't want to know what for."

"He said that about the key." She stopped with her hand on the door. "He'd want to know about this. Not to stop it. To know." She looked at him in the dark. "You don't have to. I'm only saying I'd want to, if it were mine."

He thought about it on the stair, and in his room, and at the window with Hesk's book shut on his knee. In the morning he went down to the east hall before the first bell and found Rooke at the trestle with the red book, and told him: three lanes in a night, ten and ten and twelve, and nothing past three. Rooke listened without looking up. Then he wrote one word on the corner of his board, and turned the board so that Cael could not see it, and said, "Good. Now don't do it again until you know why."

# Chapter 22 — Both Roads

The kettle began to sing when everybody had finished talking.

It was Brom's little spirit kettle, which had ridden down in the seat of the good chair and sat on the slate shelf beside Lira's chalk all evening over a flame no bigger than a fingernail. Lira said it only ever boiled when nobody was looking at it, out of good manners. Nobody had been looking at it for an hour. Now it whistled, thin and rising, into a room where five people had said what they had come to say and nobody had decided anything, and Brom got up out of the good chair to see to it.

Nobody else moved.

Across the table Karis's notebook lay open at the evening's page, and Cael read it upside down while Brom found the cups. He read it from the bottom up. The house's counsel read every paper that way, from the end to the beginning, because the end was where a writer put what he wanted and the beginning was only how he hoped you would feel about it. Cael had caught the habit from her in the first week of the year and never quite put it down.

The bottom line was still wet. Karis was writing it as he watched, one word in her finding hand, and she said it across the table while she wrote, without looking up, as if she were finishing a sentence somebody had begun an hour ago.

"Induction," she said. "That's what yours is. A good one."

She blotted it with the side of her hand. She did not say anything else about it, then or later, and Cael did not ask her to.

Above it, small, squeezed into the margin, Karis had written *C. — last sentence* and drawn a line beneath it.

Above that there was a gap. Three ruled lines with nothing on them, and two words pencilled into the margin beside them, with an *L.* after.

The three lines were his. She had not written down a word of what he said. She had not needed to; she had heard every piece of it before, one piece at a time, on coaches and in reading rooms, for three years.

He had started at the wrong end.

"Umber," he had said. "At Norhold. The man who kept the instrument. It couldn't score me, and he had a whole office behind him that would have liked a number, and he wrote *no number goes here* instead."

"Start at the start," Lira had said.

"Denvash," he had said, and stopped, because that was enough. Every one of them knew the brass plate.

"And between?" Brom had said, from the chair, in the voice he used for a man who has left out the middle of a sum.

So he had given them between, every paper of it, and not one piece of it had been news to anybody at that table. That was the strange part: sitting in a cold dairy setting out his own life to the four people who had lived most of it with him. They had let him. Nobody had hurried him, until near the end.

He had been halfway through his last example when Lira cut across him.

"It's not ground. It's a floor."

Lira had not got down off the table to say it, or raised her voice. She had said it to her chalk, and gone on writing on the shelf, and that was why Karis had put it in the margin with an *L.* after it. He had stopped. Then he had finished anyway, straight across the table to her: "I'm not leaving the room while the text still works."

At the very top of Karis's page, above his three empty lines, was Brom.

*Prepare both roads. Win the sittings. Keep a box.*

Three sentences. Lira had counted them on her fingers under the table and held the three fingers up, and Karis had written all three without asking to hear them again. That had been the first thing said in the room, and it had sat at the top of the page all night, and reading upward Cael saw now that everything under it had been an argument with the last two words.

What he remembered first of Brom's part, though, was the hand.

Brom had been telling Lira about strapping. Nobody east of anywhere cut it the way he liked; he would take all of it. And in the middle of the strapping his right hand had come off the table and gone to the left side of his coat, to the inside pocket, and lain flat over the cloth, and stayed there while he went on talking, as if it had gone there without him.

"That one packs itself," he had said, when he had finished with the strapping. "It's the second box that's the problem."

Lira had looked at the hand on his coat, and away from it, and gone straight on. "Shirts, then," she had said. "Strapping. And that plaque, I suppose, in the first one."

"If I leave it, you'll go back for it."

"In the dark," Lira had agreed.

And Karis had turned to a clean page.

He had his hand on that pocket now, with the kettle in the other.

Cael did not need to be told what was under the hand. A square cream envelope with a dark red seal on it, unbroken, which had come up the hill twenty-three days ago and had not been opened since. Under it, the summons, the docket, the district's confirmation with *outcome anticipated* on it in a clerk's round hand, and a sitting on the last Fifth-day of the year at which three assessors and a sigil frame would almost certainly find Brom an Iron. His family's ledger had been waiting for that line since before he could lift a practice weight. In the second box was a grandmother at the head of a long table, and a word he had never once said in the same breath as *east*.

Brom poured.

The cups went round. Lira took hers in both hands and put her boots up on the bench end, and dug her thumbs into the side of her landing hip, which was Rooke's nightly order and which she obeyed with the face of somebody paying a fine. In three days she would be on a strange floor two days north. She blew across her tea before anybody else had spoken.

"It was the night we asked," she said.

Karis wrote it, exactly like that, at the very top of the page, above Brom's three sentences, so that the page now began with the last thing anybody said. She dated it and shut the book.

They went up together. Brom went first with the chair on his shoulder and the kettle hooked on one finger. Seln picked the lamp up off the table without being asked and stood at the foot of the dairy step and held it up while each of them climbed past him into the passage, Brom, Karis, Lira, Cael, one at a time. When they were all up he handed the lamp to Lira and said good night to nobody in particular and went out by the side door into the court.

Cael, at the passage window, watched him cross it. Not toward the staff stair. Toward the dark bulk of the assessment wing on the far side of the court, where nobody had business at that hour.

Lira had stopped beside him with the lamp. She did not look at the court. She looked at him.

"Both roads," she said.

"Both roads."

She nodded, as if he had signed something, and went on up the stair with the light.

Cael wrote one line at the sill before he slept.

*Lira's chalk is still on the dairy shelf: the road east, and under it the forty days. Nobody rubbed it out. I think nobody is going to.*

---

He came in by the wing's back passage, because the side door stuck in the damp and the back way did not.

The passage was distempered green to the height of a man's shoulder, and he had never once walked it without the other one coming back to him: a corridor the same green in a town twelve years and three postings away, where a clerk had put his head out of a side door to ask whether he was unwell, because he had stopped dead in the middle of the floor and stood there. He had not been unwell. He had been understanding, all at once, a run of ledgers labelled *maintenance records*, which he had copied a year before as dull upkeep and never thought about since. He had understood, in that green corridor, whose question it had been all along. He had kept his appointment afterward, and kept it well.

The return was on the copying table where he had left it at the sixth bell, sealed and addressed, ready for the morning bag.

Seln did not light the lamp. The fire-watch's lantern hung under the north arch at that hour and threw a long pale square across the court, in at the wing's tall window and onto the counter, and it was enough. He came round the end of the counter and sat down at the copying table, in his own chair, and picked the return up and held it.

He knew what was in it. He had written it that afternoon in the hand he used for the counter's forms.

*Subject attended. Subject was examined in wing three by the evaluator of record and two instructors, in the ordinary form. Subject's figure as entered by the house. Subject's associations unchanged. Nothing further.*

Every word was true. The boy had attended; he had been examined; the figure was the house's figure, twenty-two and two-thirds, written by a clerk who believed it. Nothing further. In the morning it would go down the hill in the bag, and a clerk in an office would open it and read it and file it, and it would lie in the registry's file on the boy beside the house's own copies from Bracken's enrollment book. Two honest hands, his and the registrar's, one on each side of the file, and nothing whatever between them.

He put it down on the table, square to the edge.

Across the court a lamp moved. It was Bracken, late, going from his rooms to the records hall with a ledger under his arm, as he did two or three nights in a week when a figure would not leave him alone. Seln watched the small light cross the stones and go in at the hall's side door and appear again, a moment later, in the high window over the counter. The registrar would be sitting down now to a column he had already added twice, to add it a third time, because a man who signs a book in his own name owes it that.

He had always liked Bracken. He had never said so. Saying so would have meant explaining how carefully he had watched the man, and for whom.

The boy's window, on the residence passage, had a lamp in it too. The boy would be at his sill with the old instrument-maker's book, writing his one line. Seln had never asked to read a word of it, and never would. It was the one record on the hill he had decided, a long time ago and without telling anybody, not to know.

Forty days. He turned that over next, because it was the only thing he had put into the room that evening and he was obliged to account for it. He had corrected the researcher because she was wrong, and a wrong figure written down in a room where he sat was, in some small part, his own wrong figure; that was the plain reason, and it had been his reason at the counter for fifteen years. But that had not been the whole of it. Forty days after the fourth sitting closed, a respondent who had not come back was entered as fled. Before the fortieth day, he was only absent. Between those two words there might be a place for a person to stand. Seln did not know yet whether there was. He had wanted the girl with the chalk to have the true figure in her head when she came to look.

He had opened his eyes twice in the dairy, and the first time had been for the girl's chalk. *STAMPS STOP.* He knew that road. He knew, as a matter of his trade, what she did not: that a return like the one on the table had a route, office to office, and that the route ended where the stamps did. Past the last station there was no bag for it to go in, and no clerk to open it, and no drawer. A man could stand on the far side of that line and be watched by nobody whose watching went anywhere. He had never let himself think about it in those words before. He did not let himself go further now.

He put two fingers into his waistcoat pocket and found the key.

It was a small brass key worn bright at the bow, and it had lived in that pocket for longer than he had lived in any room. He held it on his palm in the pale square of light. Above him, on the shelf over the copying table, in the shadow, was the thing it opened. He did not look up at the shelf. He looked at the key.

He thought instead about the step.

He had packed his own box nineteen times. That was the thing nobody at the table knew about him, and it had come into his head while the big one was naming his shirts. A posting ended on a morning like any other; a letter came; a man put what he owned into one box and his papers into another and went down to the coach. He knew to the ounce what a life weighed when it had to be carried. He knew, too, that the box you packed was never the problem. It was the other one, the one that would not go in a box at all, and he had watched the big one put his hand flat over it and say so, and nobody at the table had asked, and Seln had liked them all very much for that.

The dairy step, an hour ago, with the lamp in his hand, and the four of them coming up past him into the dark one at a time. The big one with a chair on his shoulder and a letter he would not open. The researcher, who had drawn the corridor they were all standing in more truly than anyone in the registry could have drawn it, and did not know who had built the walls. The girl with the chalk, who had walked a road east in three pieces so that nobody would see her add it up. The boy last, with his receipts. He had lit each of them up one step. He had not told one of them what the step was made of.

---

There was a cupboard under the copying table, and he opened it without looking, because his hand knew where the latch was.

Nineteen bundles stood in it on their ends, tied with the office's grey tape, each one his own duplicates from one posting. He had never kept a list of them. He had never needed to. The labels told him: a town's reference, a year, a count of subjects in his small square hand. One academy's label said four. A district seat's said eleven. The newest, on the end of the row, said one, and had the name of this hill on it. Fifteen years of his trade stood in that cupboard on their ends, sixty-three subjects in all, and every page in every bundle was as true and as empty as the return on the table.

He had been proud of that once. It was in a commendation, in a drawer, somewhere.

The newest bundle was the thickest. He did not take it out. He knew what was in it, and he knew, besides, what was not in it, which was more.

Not in it: a document table in the mill-town hall, a year ago, where he had told the boy that his figures were too tidy, and watched the boy go away and come back with a season of figures that never once settled into a line. Not in it: two slips of wing paper, a year apart, which the boy had read and put on the common-room coals without a word to anyone, which was exactly right. Not in it: Norhold, where everything the boy had kept back for a whole season went out of him in a single afternoon, in front of eight thousand people, on purpose. Seln had sat in that stand and watched it go and understood that the boy had chosen the day. He had never asked him why. He did not ask subjects why.

In the registry's file on the boy, he supposed, his returns lay in the same drawer as the hearing's finding and the renewals and the Archmarshal's three sentences. If the boy ever saw them he would count them on his own side. There was nothing in them to object to. Seln had made sure there was nothing in them at all.

He reached up, once, while the cupboard stood open, and put his hand on the edge of the shelf above the copying table, in the dark, where the other thing stood. He did not take it down. He left his hand on the shelf's edge for the space of a long breath, the way a man rests his hand on a door he has decided not to open tonight.

He took his hand off the shelf and shut the cupboard.

He had not left the service. A man in his trade could not leave it and keep what he kept. So he had stayed, and filed true, and kept.

The fire-watch climbed down off his bucket at the turn of the hour, and the square of light on the counter slid sideways and went out. Seln sat on in the dark with the key still on his palm. Before he put it away he said one sentence to himself, in the voice he kept for the audit, which was the only voice in himself he believed, so that it would be on a record somewhere even if the only record was in his head.

*For fifteen years I made the same decision every morning and never said it aloud, and it was quiet. The deciding has stopped being quiet.*

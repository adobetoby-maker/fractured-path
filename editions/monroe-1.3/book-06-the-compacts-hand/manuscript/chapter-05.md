# Chapter 5 — Served

The eighth day of the year was a First-day, hot and still, and at the second bell Cael was on the covered walk with a book under his arm and nowhere in particular to be.

The house had found its feet by then. A week was long enough for that. The intake had stopped walking in groups of six and started walking in groups of two. The boards along the walk had filled up with the year's ordinary paper: the research standings, the program's floor hours, the refectory's rota, and a sheet in Ephram's square hand announcing that the captain would hear grievances on Third-day evenings in the common room and would not be held responsible for his answers. The fire-watch boy's slate hung on its nail under the north arch with last night's hours chalked down it in large careful figures, the last of them rubbed out once and made again.

Cael stopped at the slate, because he always did, and then he looked east, because the arch looked east, and saw them.

Three riders, on the last switchback below the gate.

They were coming up at a walk. Two rode a little ahead and to either side, in grey with a badge on the shoulder, and they rode the way men ride who have been told the pace and are keeping it whether it suits the horses or not. The third rode between them. He sat very upright, and across the front of his saddle, strapped tight against the horse's withers so that it could not shift, lay a case of dark leather the length of his forearm.

Couriers trotted. Every courier Cael had ever watched on that road had come up it at a working trot with his eyes on the next bend, because a courier was paid by the day and the road was long. These three walked, and kept step, the way men walk behind something they have been told not to hurry.

He looked up at the records hall. The high window at its north end was empty, and the ship's glass was gone from it. Somewhere inside, a man who had watched that road for eight days had already put the glass in his coat and gone down to his counter.

At the gate the lodge-man came out of his door and stood as straight as he stood for the Chancellor's carriage, and for nothing else. Riders on registry business did not stop for lodge-men. They passed under the arch at the same walk, still in the saddle, and the lodge-man stepped back against the wall with his hat in his hand and let them. They crossed the outer court with their hooves loud on the stones in the heat, and every head on the covered walk turned to follow them, and they went on round the corner of the lecture range toward the records hall as if nobody on the hill had a right to stop them, which was true.

Cael put his book under his other arm and went after them.

He did not run. A boy running across a court after a registry escort would have been a story by noon. He walked fast, at the pace of somebody late for a lecture, and he came round the corner of the lecture range in time to see the third rider dismount at the records-hall steps and unstrap the case from his saddle. The two outriders stayed mounted. They sat their horses at the foot of the steps, one on each side, facing outward.

Cael went past them and up the steps and in. Nobody stopped him. It would not have occurred to him to wait outside.

The records hall was cool after the court, and dim, and very quiet.

Bracken was behind his counter. He had not come down from the window in a hurry; there was no sign on him that he had come down at all. He stood with both hands resting on the counter's edge, a narrow grey man in a grey coat, and when Cael came in he looked at him once and then back at the door, and said nothing.

The rider came in behind Cael with the case under his arm.

On his feet he was a different kind of man from the two outside. He had soft hands and good boots that had not been walked in, and a clerk's grey coat kept very clean, with a small seal of office in dull silver at the collar that Cael did not know. He was perhaps fifty, with thin hair combed close and the careful face of somebody who has set a great many things down on a great many counters and has never once been set down himself. He set the case on the counter and laid his hand on it and waited.

Withrow came in from the inner door thirty seconds later.

She came down the long hall as if she had been sent for about a gutter. Cael had watched her cross courts and corridors for two years and knew the walk as well as he knew Brom's step on a stair, even and unhurried, the walk of a woman who had decided long ago that nothing would ever be allowed to set its pace. With half the hill already talking outside, he understood that she had chosen it on the stair and was holding it now the way a carter holds a nervous horse.

She stopped at the counter beside Bracken, and the officer bowed to her, to a depth that somebody had once measured, and took a card from his coat.

"An instrument of the registry, under seal," he read from the card, in a voice with nothing in it. "For joint service upon the Chancellor of Halcenvane Academy and upon the enrollee of record whom it names, the two together and in their own persons. Service to be acknowledged in both their hands. The seal to stay whole until it is." He lowered the card. "Are both parties present?"

"Both parties are present," said Withrow.

She did not look at Cael. She did not need to. The officer looked at him, once, the way a man checks a figure against the sum he was given, and looked away again.

He unlocked the case with a key from his watch-chain and took out a form first, a single printed sheet with two ruled lines at its foot. Cael had never seen its like. It was headed *Acknowledgment of Joint Service*, and under the heading there was a paragraph of small type, and under that, two lines.

Withrow signed on the first line. She signed her whole name, slowly, with the officer's pen.

Then she held the pen out to Cael without a word.

He signed beneath her. His hand was quite steady. He noticed that, and wrote it down in his head as a fact, because it surprised him a little.

The officer witnessed both signatures in his own hand, sanded them from a box in the case, folded the form along a crease that was already in it, and sealed it inside the case again. Then he took out the instrument.

It was thick. It lay on the counter in front of the three of them, a sheaf of heavy paper folded once along its length, and across the fold, holding it shut, sat a wafer of grey wax the size of a mark, pressed with a seal Cael had seen before only on the outside of other people's letters. The Compact's.

"Served," said the officer. He bowed to Withrow again, to the same depth, and to Bracken a little less, and to Cael not at all. Then he took up his case and went out. They heard him go down the steps, and a moment later they heard the three horses go away across the court at a walk, and then nothing.

Nobody touched the instrument.

Withrow broke the wax with her thumbnail and read where she stood, and nobody in the hall would have dared to bring her a chair. She read the first leaf once to herself, without a sign. Then she read it again, aloud, for the hall's book: on service, the enrollee must hear it, and the house must be able to say he had.

Cael had never heard the voice she used. Two sentences in, he knew it was not new. It was the oldest she owned. Twenty years of registry windows were in it, a clerk's voice that gives a paper to a room exactly as the paper gives itself and adds nothing, not even breath.

*To the Chancellor, Halcenvane Academy, and to the enrollee of record, Caelen Hesk-ward, unclassified, enrolled under the demonstration provision of the assay clause, jointly:*

*Be it known that the Guilds Compact, by its registry, having convened formal proceedings upon the question of the classification designated [SHATTERED] and its bearing upon evaluation integrity within chartered institutions, hereby files a formal challenge to enrollment basis against the said enrollment.*

*The challenge does not allege fraud in the enrollment's execution, nor contest the text of the provision under which it was executed. It asserts that an unclassifiable practitioner's presence within a chartered institution's evaluation architecture presents an evaluation-integrity question which the demonstration provision was not drafted to answer and cannot answer, and petitions the registry for compelled re-evaluation of the enrollee under standard Arbiter protocol, the enrollment to stand or fall upon that evaluation's result.*

*Proceedings to be convened at the registry seat at Ostrand upon a schedule of one academic year. The respondent institution and the enrollee of record are directed to answer within fourteen days.*

*Given under the Compact's hand.*

She lowered the leaf, and said the last line again without looking at it, in the same level voice.

"*Given under the Compact's hand.*" A pause, no longer than a breath. "I have signed that line myself. Nine hundred times."

The hall stayed quiet. Somewhere below the bluff the escort's hooves went round the first switchback and were gone. Withrow turned the leaf down again, edge to edge with the ones beneath it, and Cael saw her make certain her fingers would not shake before she let them do it.

"Registrar. Three copies under the house's seal: counsel, the enrollee, the reading room. Before dark."

"Before dark," said Bracken.

She turned to Cael. She did not change her voice to do it; the registry voice was the one that would hold, and she meant to keep it until she was alone.

"They've found a new door into the old question," she said. "Go and tell Karis. She'll be furious for about a minute and useful for a fortnight, and I'd like the minute spent before she has the paper, not after."

---

He took the library stair two at a time, all four flights, with the hip reminding him at every landing that it had been promised a quieter week.

The door at the top was shut. The card was in its brass holder. The new lock was locked.

He knocked, and the key turned on the inside, and Karis opened the door with a pen in her hand and ink on the side of her thumb and the look of somebody who has been interrupted in the middle of a sentence and means to go back to it.

Then she saw his face, and the look went.

"What," she said.

"A registry instrument. Under escort. Served jointly on Withrow and me in the records hall ten minutes ago." He made himself say it in order, the way she would want it. "A challenge to the enrollment basis. Fourteen days to answer. Withrow read the first page aloud. Bracken is making you a copy now."

Karis stood in the doorway and did not move.

He told her the first page. He had it nearly whole; he had been listening the way he listened to anything that would have to be written down later. *Does not allege fraud. Nor contest the text.* *Evaluation integrity.* *Compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol.* *Stand or fall.* She listened to all of it without interrupting, with her pen still in her hand, and when he had finished she looked past him, down the narrow stair, for a long moment.

"Standard Arbiter protocol," she said. "Those words? Exactly those?"

"Exactly those."

She was quiet. He knew what she was seeing, because he was seeing it too. Standard protocol meant a station: a frame in a stone room, a registry officer at its side, a practitioner standing in it while the frame read him. It was how every Path on the continent had been declared since before the registry kept books. The frame read the Path and named it, and set a tier against it, and a rank inside the tier, and the officer wrote down what the frame had said, and that was a person's classification for life. It was how Lira had been declared, and Brom, and Karis herself. It was how the frame at Denvash had been asked, on the morning of his Kindling, what he was, and had answered with one word in brackets and nothing else at all.

The demonstration provision had never asked a frame anything. That was the whole of why it held him.

"They read the hearing," she said. "Every page of it. Every page we won on." Her voice was quite level. "And then they went away and wrote a paper that doesn't touch a single one of them."

She stood a moment longer. Then she was finished with being angry, or had put it somewhere he could not see, and she turned to the table and tore the bottom off a sheet of paper and wrote on it standing, fast, in her finding hand.

"My assistant's in the lower reading room," she said, and gave him the slip. "Give her that."

He read it on the stair going down, because she would have expected him to.

*Bracken's press: the four rulings on the clause. Ask him nicely. — The code, three vols, the HALL'S, not the library's (lib. vol. 2 lacks a gathering). — Greyvane transcript, cert., my shelf, common room, grey card, K.D. on spine. — Tea. — Tell her she won't see her bed.*

The assistant was where Karis had said, at a table under the window in the lower reading room with the district digests stacked beside her. She read the slip once and gave it back to him, which he had not expected, and stood, and pushed in her chair.

"The tea first," she said. "She thinks better with it. Then the rest, in her order."

"She says you won't see your bed."

"She always says that," said the assistant, and went out past him at a fast walk. He heard her break into a run on the covered walk outside, and suspected it was the first time she had run anywhere on the hill since the intake.

---

Nobody told the bluff. Nobody needed to.

An escort had come through the gate at the second bell without dismounting, and ridden to the records hall, and ridden away again without the thing it had brought, and every person on the covered walk had watched it go by and could do that sum unaided. By the noon meal the refectory was full of people sitting close together over their soup and not eating it. Somebody with a cousin who swept the records hall had it that the thing was a summons. A little after the fourth bell a man from one of the Ostrand broadsides was at the gate lodge, asking the lodge-man what had come up the hill that morning, and the lodge-man was telling him that it had been a fine morning for the time of year, and that he had the greatest respect for the press.

Cael sat on the records-hall steps in the heat and waited, because the others would come there, and they did.

Brom came first, from the direction of the kitchens, with a wedge of cheese wrapped in a cloth and an apple in each coat pocket, none of which anybody had asked for. He sat down on the step below and began cutting the cheese with his clasp-knife into four, though there were only two of them.

Lira came a quarter of an hour behind him, still taped at one wrist, with the tape off the other trailing from her fist. "Rooke stopped the drill," she said, and sat. "He walked across the whole floor to my cross and said *Records hall. Now.* and gave my place to the Force." She took the quarter of cheese Brom held up to her. "So. Tell me."

He told them what he had told Karis. Lira listened with her eyes on the court. Brom ate.

"Fourteen days," said Lira, when he had finished.

"From this morning."

"She built the whole continent on a wall in one term." Lira bit the apple. "Fourteen days is a holiday."

Behind them the records-hall door opened and Bracken came out onto the top step with a sheet of paper in his hand. It was green: a pale grey-green, like the underside of a willow leaf. He held it out at arm's length, away from himself, the way a man holds a thing he has found on his doorstep and is not yet sure is not alive.

"This was on my counter," he said, "when I came back from the press with the rulings. Stamped. Countersigned. In order." He read from it. "*Research standing: floor and archive hours, by application. Applicant: K. Dellenmoor. Hours: the records hall, this night, from the eighth bell until the work is done. Purpose: the house's answer to an instrument served this day. Received and entered.*" He lowered it. "The stamp is the assessment wing's. The hand on the received line is the wing's. The form was built in the recess for an office that had never had a use for it until this morning." He looked down at the three of them on his steps. "Somebody has been expecting us."

Nobody said who. Nobody had to. Across the court, behind the assessment wing's tall windows, the counter would be open, and a man would be at a copying table with his head down, rising when the queue moved to take the next sheet.

Bracken folded the green sheet and put it in his coat, beside the brass glass.

"The house's answer goes on the board within the hour," he said. "The Chancellor has written it. It is one line long. I'm told that's deliberate."

Ephram found them there a little after, coming round the corner of the lecture range with the captain's file under his arm and the green tape trailing. He stopped at the foot of the steps and looked up at the three of them, and at the bread, and at the shut door behind them.

"The cohort's asking me," he said. "Forty of them, in the yard, all at once. I'm the captain. They think captains know things." He shifted the file. "What do I tell them?"

"Whatever goes up on the board," said Cael. "Nothing more."

"And when they ask what the board means?"

"Tell them it means what it says."

Ephram considered this. "I'll tell them that," he said. "And then I'll tell them again, slower, because they'll think I've left a bit out." He looked at Cael a moment longer than he needed to, with the same look he had given the empty floor in wing three, and then he turned round and went back toward the yard, already raising his voice to somebody about the proper place to stand when the captain was talking.

It went up at the fifth bell, on the covered walk's middle board, under the north arch, in Bracken's own hand. Half the house came to read it and went away disappointed, because it was not the kind of line you could make anything out of.

*Halcenvane Academy has been served, and will answer within the time allowed.*

By the sixth bell the man from the broadsides had copied it into his notebook at the gate lodge, and was riding down the hill with it as fast as a hired horse would take him. Cael watched him go from the north wall. The two coats at the foot of the road watched him go too. It was the first time in a week that either of them had turned his head.

---

Bracken's boy brought the enrollee's copy up to the residence at the seventh bell, in a grey folder under the house's seal, and Cael took it to his window and laid it on the sill and opened it at the first page.

He had heard it read. Now he read it, all of it, every leaf, once straight through from the address to the attestation. He did not stop to think about any of it. That would come later, at the long table, with the others. This was only the first reading, the one a man does to find out how long a thing is and where its weight sits, the way you lift a sack before you decide how to carry it.

Then he turned back to the first page and left it lying there face up on the sill, with the evening coming in over it through the open window.

The fountain ran below in the court. Somebody downstairs had given up on the song and was practising scales on a whistle instead, which was worse. Across the court and over the roof of the east range he could see the long row of the records hall's windows. In the last of them, at the north end, a lamp had been lit already, though the sun was not yet down.

He took out Hesk's book and Hesk's steel pen. The words had been ready since the library stair, and they went down clean.

*They filed. After the hearing I won, and the inspection, and a tournament that ran out of numbers for me, they've gone back to the shelf for one more way to ask. Fine. Their text has lost to ours before. Karis has the code open already. Note the date. The season's open.*

He sanded it, gave it a full minute by Hesk's clock, and closed the book.

Then he looked at what he had written, for a while, with the book shut.

He had meant every word. He found he meant them still. A year ago he had been a boy on a provision, alive because somebody kept writing it down, and a hearing, a panel and the continent's oldest contest had all tried their instruments on him and found they could not make him fit, and every time the text had held him. He had no reason to think this text would not.

But he noticed something, sitting at the window with the book closed on his knee, and he made himself look at it squarely, because noticing things was the one work he had never been able to stop doing.

It was the voice. Withrow's registry voice, reading the first page aloud in the records hall. He had thought, while she read, that she was using it because it was the right instrument for service. He thought now that she had been using it because it was the only one she had that would not shake.

He had never once heard Withrow need a voice that would not shake.

Down in the court a lamp-boy went past toward the records hall with his long pole over his shoulder. At the end of the east range, one after another, the records hall's windows were coming alight.

Cael put the book in his coat, and took the folder from the sill, and went down to join them.

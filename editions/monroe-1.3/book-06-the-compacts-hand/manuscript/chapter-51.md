# Chapter 51 — The Hand

Havel had written the time when the senior evaluator stood, and the time when she sat, and the difference between them was six minutes.

He looked at the two figures after the room had emptied. He had written a great many pairs of times in that book since the fortieth day, and he could not remember looking at a pair before, for it was not his place to notice how long a thing had taken. It was his place to see that anybody who wanted to know could find out. He blotted them.

"Junior," said the senior chair. He had the minute open in front of him and was running his thumb down the fresh page. "This wants to be in the porch before the ruling. That's the code, for a return. I've a tooth that won't let me climb, and the clerk's gone to his dinner. Take it up to the loft and stand over them while they copy it. Fair, one sheet, and you compare it yourself."

"Yes," said Havel.

He took the minute book in both hands. It was heavier than it looked, as a book is that has had a year written into it.

The copyists' loft was over the long room, up a ladder-stair behind the platform. It had been the factors' sample loft, and there were still hooks in the beams where fleeces had hung to be judged by the light, though four slanted desks stood under the north windows now. In a sitting week the seat hired four copyists from the counting-houses along the wharf; on a call day it had kept only one, a young woman with a quick neat hand and a smudge of ink on her chin.

He laid the book open on her desk, and showed her the place, and where it ended.

"As it stands," he said. "Every stop."

She began. He stood at her shoulder, as the manual said an officer was to stand when a minute left the records table, and read each line as she made it against the line it came from.

He had not read it yet himself; he had only heard it, and he had always known that those were different things. He had sat forty feet from the middle chair with his pen moving, and had written a time, and had kept his eyes on his own page while a voice he had worked under for years said a thing slowly into an empty room. Now he read it.

The second of the two returns he had by heart. He had read it once before, upside down, on a desk at Norhold, in perhaps three seconds, and had written it that night at the head of a sheet of his own, which lay in his kit at the inn, the fifth of six. He watched the copyist make the words, and they were the same words, and he felt nothing about them that he had not felt already.

The first he had never seen. He had carried the file it answered; he had laid that file at her elbow in a hall on a bluff, square to the edge, and gone back to the foot of the table. A return had gone into it then, and he had not read it, because a records officer did not read what was addressed to the senior seat.

*Determination: no originating authority of record.*

The copyist's pen went across it without a check, and his eyes stayed on the line when her pen had gone on.

His oldest sheet said that there was a mark in a boy's file with no office behind it. He had written that after he asked, in the proper way, and was told behind a shut door that it was not his clearance, and he had taken that to mean that somebody above him knew whose mark it was. In all these years he had wanted nothing from that mark but the name of an office. He would not have needed to be told the name. It would have been enough to know that one was written down somewhere, in a register he could not open, against a date.

The registers had been asked properly, by a seat senior enough to ask, and they had answered. They knew of no office either.

He stood very still at the copyist's shoulder. It was an answer, and he made himself be exact about that, as he was exact about a count. He had kept six sheets of things the registers could not answer, and here, in a minute, under a seal, was the registry's own hand saying of the first of them that it could not. He had not been wrong, on a pump step, when he was a younger man. He had only been alone with it.

"Sir," said the copyist.

She had stopped, with her pen lifted over the next line.

"This can't be right. *Designation predates file creation.* A mark can't be older than the file it's written in. That's like a stamp being older than its letter." She looked up at him, quite earnestly, with the ink on her chin. "I think somebody's dropped a word. Shall I query it?"

Havel looked at the line in the minute. It was in the senior chair's hand, taken down at dictation, and the senior chair did not drop words.

"It is as it was read," he said.

"But it doesn't make sense."

"It is not required to. A fair copy says what the minute says. If the minute is wrong, that is for the seat, and it will be wrong in the copy too, so that the seat can find it." He heard his own voice, level and dry, saying what the manual said. "Copy it as it stands."

She copied it as it stood. She was a good copyist, and she made the capital on *Designation* exactly the height of the one in the book.

He did not let himself go on to the thing she had seen. He was aware of it, as a man is aware of a stair he has decided not to climb. A girl from a counting-house had read that sentence once and known at a glance that it could not be so; the senior evaluator had read it out in a wool exchange, and called it a contradiction, and made no finding. She had done what her clearance allowed, to its edge, and stopped there. He knew what it was to stop there. He had been doing it for years with a kit bag.

He compared the copy, line by line, with a dry pen, and it agreed. He wrote *compared* at its foot, and the hour, and his name.

---

He pinned it in the porch himself.

The exchange's porch had a board for the seat's public record, behind a brass grille that opened with the junior chair's key. He put the sheet up with four pins, and squared it by the grille's bars, and locked it.

There was nobody on the steps. A boy came across from the trough, one of the lads the correspondents paid to watch the board, and read the heading with his lips moving: *Return of the evaluation seat.* He looked for a ruling under it and did not find one, and went back to the trough.

That was the public record. Havel stood a moment with the key in his hand and looked at his own pins. A thing he had carried between two manuals since before that boy could read was up on a wall in a market street, in a fair hand, for anybody; and the street went by. A carter passed, and two women with a basket between them, and on the far side a man in a travelling coat with a walking stick, who glanced at the board as he glanced at everything and wrote nothing down.

He had thought, when he was younger, that if it were ever said aloud something would happen.

He went back in to the records table and entered the posting against the hour.

The senior evaluator was still in the long room, which he had not expected. She sat in the middle chair below the covered platform with her file shut on her knee, quite alone, looking at nothing, like any woman at the end of a day's work. As he came down the side of the benches her right hand went to the breast of her coat and rested there flat for a moment, over the cloth, and came away.

He knew what was under it. He had seen her fold it twice, on a desk at Norhold, a sheet that was not office stock, and put it where she had never carried anything that was the Compact's; and he had gone back to an inn that night and written his fifth. There were two of them in one town again, each with paper the office had not asked for. He had thought so once before and had taken a kind of cold comfort in it.

But it was not the same now, and he made himself see why. She had not taken out the sheet in her coat, which was hers and would stay hers. She had taken out the returns, which were the registry's, and stood up in the registry's own room, and read them into the registry's own minute at a pace a clerk could follow. She had found the one door her clearance left open and had gone through it in the plainest way there was.

He had kept sheets. He did not think less of them for that, since a man did what his chair allowed. But he sat down at the records table and squared the receipt book, and he knew, as he knew when a count had come out, that they were finished.

---

At the inn by the bridge he ate his supper downstairs among the carters and went up while it was still light.

The river ran low and plain under the middle pier. He set the kit on the table by the window and unbuckled it, and took the six sheets from between the second manual and the third, and laid them out in their order, left to right, as he had laid them on the night of the seventh sitting. A mark with no office. Three between, each in its year. A sentence that shut a door. A seal on no register.

He read them through, slowly, as an officer reads anything he means to rely on. Then he did something he had not done to them before. He took the pencil from the kit's loop and wrote a small figure in the top corner of each, from one to six. They had never been numbered, for to number a thing was to say how many of it there were to be.

He did not take a clean sheet. He looked at the place where the clean sheets lay, and thought about what a seventh would say. It would say that on the fourth call day the senior seat read two returns into the minute; and that was in the minute, and pinned up in a porch. The six held what the registers could not answer, or would not, and this the registers had answered themselves, aloud. A thing that stood in the public record did not want a private sheet. That was what a public record was for.

He tied the six with a length of the red tape he used for receipts, once round and once across, and made the knot flat.

Six entries. Done.

It was a strange word to think, and he sat with it, because nothing whatever was done. The mark stood on the file as it had always stood, and the seal he had hunted through three ledgers still belonged to no office, and tomorrow at the hour after noon a ruling would be read and he would write the time. He did not understand it tonight any better than he had on the pump step. He had six facts and no leave to add them up, and he had never once tried.

But his part of it was done. He had kept them until somebody whose office it was said one of them aloud. He could not have said why that was enough, and yet it was.

He put the bundle back between the manuals, the same way round, and took out the other paper.

It was a form. He had drawn it from the district seat's stationery press some weeks ago, on the day after the eleventh sitting, and had carried it blank ever since: *Request for relief from rotation*, a single leaf with five ruled heads. He had never filled one in. In all his service he had gone where he was sent and stayed until he was fetched.

*Officer.* He wrote his name and grade.

*Present duty.* He wrote it as his order had it: *Records, junior chair, institutional proceedings at Ostrand; the seat's Fifth-days, until relieved.*

*Relief sought upon.* He considered that for some time, and wrote *The conclusion of the proceedings by ruling.* It was tomorrow, and a man might fairly ask to be relieved of a duty on the day the duty ended.

*Posting sought.* The district seat's records room was a man short. He had seen it for himself when he went there with a rubbing of a seal: an impression book four months behind, and a ledger of dies that nobody had balanced against the cutters' copies since the last officer left. He wrote *District records, Ostrand, or any records room that is short.* He did not write *out of monitoring*, for the form had no line for what a man wished to leave.

*Grounds.*

The pen stayed over it.

He knew the grounds. He had sat forty feet from a table for a year of Fifth-days and had not looked at it. He had looked at that boy before, on a pump step and at Greyvane and on a sprung floor at the bluff; he remembered every one of the looks, and had sworn off another, and had kept to it for a year by looking at his own hands. He was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hours, and he wished to work in a room where the leaves were only leaves.

None of that was grounds. He had never kept a sheet for what he felt, and a form was not the place to begin.

*The proceedings conclude. The district records room stands a man short, to the officer's own knowledge, and its impression book is in arrear.*

It was true, every word. He read it over once and signed it and dated it for the morning, and laid it on top of the kit, where he would see it when he woke, to hand in at the district seat on his way to the exchange.

The bridge-ward had lit his lantern. Havel sat at the window with his aching hand open on his knee and watched it, and thought, a little, about the young man at the far table, who had sat up straight for a year. He did not know what a ruling would do to him. He had never known what any of it would do; that had not been his to know. He found, sitting there, that he hoped the registers would be kind to him. He knew of no register that entered kindness, and he hoped it all the same.

Then the lantern went out, and he went to bed.

---

"I can see both doors," said Bracken, "exactly as I said."

They were at the head of the market street, the two of them, at the second bell on the last Fifth-day, where the cobbles began to fall toward the river. Below on the left were the four steps of the wool exchange, with people gathering on them already for a ruling that was hours off. Along a side street to the right, three corners down, a blue door stood in a plain stone front with a board beside it, and Brom and Lira were walking toward it.

Cael watched them go, because he could not go with them. He was a respondent on a call day, and though the district hall was well within the bell, a respondent did not walk into another proceeding's room on the morning of his own ruling; the house's counsel had said so, and then had said that she was sorry. So he stood with the registrar and saw a broad back in a good coat and a narrow one beside it go down a street. Brom walked as he walked to a frame, and Lira had her hands in her pockets and was, for once, not talking.

"She said nobody goes in alone," said Cael.

"She said it to me at breakfast, as though I had proposed the opposite." Bracken had the calendar card in his hand, the stiff one, and was turning it over and over without reading it. "Eight," he said. "That will be the eighth advancement docket I have seen go down from the hill. Seven have been found for the tier. I have no reason to think the district has changed its habits."

The blue door opened, and the two backs went in, and it shut.

It was the longest hour of the morning. He spent it at the trough by the exchange's corner, counting the people on the steps as they came, and stopped at ninety. Bracken went in to see the seat's clerk about the house's copy and came out again, and the older coat stood across the street under an awning with his stick.

Then Lira came round the corner at something that was nearly a run, and stopped herself, and walked, and Brom came after her at his own pace. He looked just as he had looked going in, with his coat buttoned, except that there were two red bars across his forearms where the cuffs had ridden up, darker than the wool press had left them.

"They loaded it," he said.

"And?"

"I held it." He thought about how to say the rest. "It was more than Ephram ever put on, a good deal over. They let it down on springs, and then they let it down again while I had it, which he never did, and I heard the middle one say *forty* and go on counting." He looked at his forearms. "The plate lit. I felt it under my boots. It's warm. I didn't know they were warm."

"What did it say?" said Cael.

"They don't tell you. They write. It goes to the registrar by the bag." Brom glanced at Bracken, and Bracken inclined his head. "Three of them at a table, and a clerk, and a frame the size of a hay wagon. Nobody said a word to me but *stand there* and *thank you*."

"It said Iron," said Lira. She had got her breath. "I was on the bench by the door and I watched the middle assessor's face when the plate came up. He looked like a man who'd put his hand in his pocket for a coin and found the coin he expected." She shoved her hands deeper into her own pockets. "He was right about himself, in front of people whose job it was to check. I saw it happen. I wanted somebody to have seen it who knew what it was."

Brom did not say anything to that. He stood in the market street with his forearms marked and his face quite calm, and Cael saw that it had been enough. Whatever a clerk wrote on whatever paper, a plate had come up warm under his boots and said what was so.

"The hour after noon," said Bracken, and put the card away. "I suggest that everybody eats."

---

The long room held more than it had ever held, and nobody in it made a sound.

They were standing along the walls, and the doors had been left open so that the steps could hear, and the steps were full, and past them the street. Everybody who had come down for the closing had come again, and Ephram's cohort had the ninth and tenth benches, and this time nobody had needed to threaten them.

Cael sat behind Withrow's chair with lot seventeen under his boot, and he sat up straight. It was, as the counsel had told him once, nearly the last time he would have to.

The five chairs were filled. At the records table the junior chair had his pen ready, and Jent sat with his hands folded on a shut folder and his spectacles hanging.

"The seat," said the clerk.

It came in and sat, and it had a single leaf in front of it.

"In the matter of Caelen Hesk-ward. For ruling. The fourteenth sitting."

The hood read. It read in the voice it had used all year, level and unhurried, with no more weight on one word than on another, and it paused at the end of each head so that the clerk might have it. Cael fixed his eyes on the exchange's clock over the doors.

"*The seat, having heard the parties upon the merits and at the close, rules as follows.*

"*It records that fourteen sittings were held; that the exhibits of the petitioner and of the respondent were received entire and without objection; that the statement entered under the provision for such statements was noted; and that, the certificate of the protocol being withheld, the seat proceeds upon its own authority, as the code allows.*

"*First. The enrollment of the said Caelen Hesk-ward at the chartered academy at Halcenvane was lawfully executed under the demonstration provision of the assay clause, which provision stands. The seat so held at the preliminary, and so holds.*

"*Second. The respondent institution has been compliant with its charter and with every order of this seat. No finding is made against it, and none is to be entered.*

"*Third. The integrity question raised by the petition being within the registry's discretion, and the seat being satisfied that the question cannot be answered upon the record alone, the enrollment basis is revoked. The revocation shall take effect at the close of the present academic year.*

"*Fourth. The said Caelen Hesk-ward shall be re-evaluated under standard Arbiter protocol, at the district seat at Ostrand, within five weeks of this day, upon a date to be notified. The evaluation seat constituted for these proceedings shall see to its conduct.*

"*Fifth. Upon the result of that evaluation, the provisions of the fourteenth chapter shall apply as they are written.*

"*Given under the Compact's hand.*"

Two minutes and forty seconds.

"So ruled," said the hood.

"So ruled," said the clerk, and wrote, and at the records table the junior chair wrote the time.

Cael had thought, for a year, that when it came he would feel the floor go, and he had braced for that as he braced for a landing. He sat with his hands flat on the house's table and waited for it, and the lot number stayed under his boot, and the boards stayed where they were.

Lawful. Compliant. Revoked.

He had heard those three words coming since the preliminary, when the seat had said *sound* and *whole* and *notwithstanding* in one breath. There was no trick in them. A year of Fifth-days had been walking toward exactly this leaf, and every door on the way had been real law and had opened one way, as Karis had told them on the first night. He had read it better than they did, and so had she, and it had made no difference, because it had never turned on the reading.

What he felt was not the floor going. It was a corridor ending. He had been walking down it so long that he had stopped feeling the walls, and now there was a last door, with a year's close chalked on it, and behind him nothing whatever that he needed to go back for.

The seat rose. It gathered its one leaf, and turned, and went to its own door, and for a moment the grey back was all there was of it. He had tried at fourteen sittings to learn something from how that shape sat, and had wanted a face under it, because a face could be read, and hated, and found out.

*Presiding seat per seal.* He had copied the words off a notice in the year's fourth week, and today he believed them. There was somebody under the cloth, and whoever it was would go home, and hang the hood on a peg, and tell the truth about the day. But it was not that person who had ruled. A seal had ruled. That was what the hood was for, and it was not a cruelty or even a disguise; it only showed, as plainly as cloth could, what he had been arguing with.

The door shut behind it, and two hundred people breathed out.

---

Jent came across the aisle before the benches had finished rising.

He came to Withrow first. He stood in front of her, a man of middle height in a coat with mended cuffs, and bowed, not to the registry's depth but a little past it.

"Chancellor," he said. "Your house is clear in every line of that leaf. I asked that it should be, in terms, and I wanted you to hear so from the petitioner and not from a clerk. If an inspector ever opens your folder at this year, send him to the second head."

"I heard it read," said Withrow. Her voice was perfectly even. "I shall know where to send him."

He turned to Karis, and for once he seemed to have nothing ready. He looked at her for a moment over the shut wallet.

"Advocate," he said, and that was all. He said it as he had said it into the minute at the fourth sitting, when it had been a courtesy; and it was not one now.

Then he looked at Cael, and Cael looked back and did not send the read. He had sat across from this man at a conference table in the year's fourth week and found nobody in the coat, and he knew why now. There had been nobody to find. Jent had been sent with an honest question, and had asked it honestly, and had been given the answer the code gave.

"Hesk-ward," said Jent. He nodded, the same nod he had given on a step in the rain. He seemed about to say something more, and to think better of it, and he picked up his one folder and his inkwell and went out by the side door with his thin clerk behind him. He had another docket somewhere, and he did not look back at the room.

In the stable yard Withrow stood by her carriage with one gloved hand on its door, and waited until Karis and Cael had come up, and Bracken with the seal.

"I want one thing said before the hill gets hold of it," she said. "I read losing filings for twenty years at the registry. It was my work to say why they had lost. They lost because a date was wrong, or a section was misread, or somebody had hoped a seat would not notice a thing it was paid to notice." She looked at Karis, and then, longer, at Cael. "You did not lose badly. I should like that to be the first thing either of you remembers about today, and I shall say it to anybody who asks me. There is not a line in that ruling that answers a line of yours. It could not answer them. It went round."

"It still went against us," said Karis.

"It did. You lost to a discretion, and nobody has ever beaten one of those from inside it. I knew that on the eighth day." Withrow opened the carriage door herself. "I chose to stand there anyway, and I'd choose it again, and now I can show anybody who asks me exactly how well it was done. That is not nothing. It is a good deal of what I have."

She got in. For a moment, before the door shut, he saw her face with nobody looking at it, and it was the face of a woman who has known a thing for four days and is not going to say it in a stable yard. He did not understand that then, and put it away to look at later.

---

Bracken changed the slate before he took his coat off.

He rubbed out the three lines with the heel of his hand, the two closings and the ruling, and left the fourth, because that was Brom's and not the house's. He wrote in their place, square and plain:

*Ruling given. No finding against the house. The enrollment basis revoked, from the year's close.*

He read it over, and dusted his fingers, and went behind his counter, where the lamps were lit in pairs down the length of the records hall and both his clerks were already bent over the middle table.

"Copy-sets," he said, to Cael, who had not asked. "The Chancellor's order, from the carriage. Thirty-four houses wrote to ask what this one would do if the seat went against it. They are to have the ruling in the house's own fair copy by tomorrow's first bag, with the standing entries, and no gloss. Three leaves to a set, and five sets more for the faculties." He pulled down his second glasses. "Thirty-nine. I have told the kitchen to send bread."

Seln was at the counter's far end. He had come down in the coach's corner without a word, and he stood now under the counter lamp with the enrollee's copy of the ruling laid in front of him on the wood. It was the seat's own engrossment, on registry stock, with the wax of the river and the scale at its foot; Cael had carried it up the hill in the satchel and had not been able to think what to do with it.

Seln did not read it, for he had heard it read. He held it up to the lamp by two corners and looked through it, for some time. Then he laid it flat and took Bracken's boxwood rule from its nail, and measured something at the sheet's edge, and measured it again from the other side. He turned the leaf over and ran his thumbnail very lightly round the rim of the seal, as Bracken had once done under an arch, and bent close to the last line to look at the hand.

"Well?" said Cael.

Seln laid the rule down.

"I have read paper all year for what stood behind it," he said. "A mark a sixteenth low. A lot written above the ruled line, with a mark for a word. A seal that is on no schedule. I have been reading paper that way for a good while longer than a year." He touched the leaf's corner. "This is registry stock from the first mould, and the mark sits where it should. The die has its number in the rim, and the number will be on its line in the cutters' ledger; I should be surprised if it were not. The hand is the seat's own clerk's. I have watched him write it since the fortieth day."

"So it's honest."

"It is exactly what it says it is. There is nothing behind it at all." He said it in his mild voice, as he might have reported a floor schedule. "In my trade a man is taught four grades of pressure, in their order. The record. The instrument. The knock, which is paper nobody will own. And last the hand, which is a road with no paper at all. I was taught that the hand was the worst of them, and I believed it for fifteen years." He shook his head, very slightly. "I had the order wrong. What stood in the road was a glove." He looked down at the last line of the leaf. "*Given under the Compact's hand.* That is the only true sentence of its kind I have been shown this year. This is the hand. It does not have another."

He squared the leaf to the counter's edge, and stepped back from it.

"I am sorry," he said. "I should have liked to find something. It would have been easier for you."

Cael looked at the engrossment under the lamp.

*I keep looking for the person and finding the hand.* That was his own line, from the year's sixth week, and he had gone on looking. He had looked at Jent and at a hood and down a road with chalk on it, and had wanted an arm, somewhere, that the hand belonged to.

There was no arm. There were two hundred and six honest people, and a leaf two hundred years old in a locked case, and a clerk with a good hand. He stopped looking, and it was like setting down a thing he had carried so long that he had forgotten it had weight.

At the middle table the copy-sets were coming off the clerks' pens, and Brom was squaring them.

Nobody had asked him to. He had come in behind them and stood about for a little with his coat on, a man who had held a frame that morning and had nothing to do with his arms. Then he had gone to the table's end, where the finished leaves were being laid in threes, and had taken up each three as it came, and knocked its edges true on the wood, long side and short side, and laid it on the pile with its corners exactly over the corners beneath.

Then he began to weigh them. He did it on his palm; he lifted each set a little, and let it settle, and put it down. Cael watched him do nine, and on the tenth his hand stayed up.

"This one's light," said Brom.

The senior clerk looked round. "It's three leaves."

"It's light."

The clerk took it, and counted, and counted again more slowly with a wet thumb. There were two; the standing entries had been left out of it. He went red and reached for a clean sheet, and Brom took up the next.

He had said, in a harness room, that he could not read a registry sheet anymore; that since the case he looked at one and saw only what it was a form of. He was not reading these. He was doing the one honest thing he could still do with such paper, which was to say what it weighed.

Lira was on the stair. It was the narrow stair behind the counter, the one that went up to Bracken's rooms, and she sat on its fourth step with her knees drawn up and her chin on them, in the shadow above the lamps. She had not said a word since the coach, and she was watching Brom's hands.

She did not look like somebody who had lost. He knew that face; she had worn it on the north wall when she first asked why they were still there, and on the night after a dairy. She was adding something up.

He left the engrossment where Seln had squared it, and went to find Karis. It was nearly the tenth bell.

# Chapter 19 — The Shape

When the plates had been cleared, Seln put four dried beans on the board in a row and said, "Four offices. Ask."

They were the speckled brown beans the kitchen soaked for the Second-day stew. He had taken them out of his coat pocket without any hurry. He laid them a hand's breadth apart, in front of his own place at the middle of the bench, and now he sat back from them with his hands folded and looked at none of the four people across the table.

Nobody asked anything for a moment. The refectory went on round them, loud at the far end where the first-years were.

"A drawing can be found," said Seln, to the beans. "These can be eaten. Ask."

Brom asked first.

Cael had not expected that. Brom did not ask questions at table; he answered them, when he had to, in as few words as the question would bear. But Brom leaned forward on his forearms now and put one thick finger on the board beside the second bean from the left, not touching it.

"That one."

"A provincial registry," said Seln. "The one that keeps the Velmere estate's file. Nine days ago. A query on the standing of the household, the tiers of the people in it, and the estate's registered relationships."

Brom did not move his finger.

"Does the query show at the estate's end?"

Seln answered with a question of his own, and for once he looked straight across the table to ask it. "Who reads the post at Velmere?"

"My grandmother," said Brom. "She always has."

"Then she'll read the query book as well. It lies open on the provincial counter for anybody on the estate's file, with every question in it, and who asked, and when."

Brom took his finger off the board. He nodded once. Then he sat back and folded his arms. Cael, who had watched Brom carry everything from code volumes to a hurt friend's kit without once looking as if it weighed anything, saw him look for a moment like a man carrying something he could not put down and could not shift to the other shoulder.

Lira asked second. She did not point. She said, "Which one's me?"

Seln touched the third bean. "The district seat. Twelve days ago. A query on the certification history of a Wind practitioner of this house, Iron Rank One, *for completeness of the respondent institution's file*."

"Which file?"

"There is only one."

Lira did not say anything about Fenmark. She asked Seln one precise question instead, in the voice she used at a rating table for a figure she suspected.

"Did they ask for the Fenmark sheet by name?"

"They asked for the file."

"Then they got the Fenmark sheet." She looked down at the bean and not at any of them. Everybody at that table knew what was at the bottom of her file, under four years of other people's honest lines, and nobody said it, and she did not say it either. After a moment she looked across at Cael. "Your column. Under my name. That they read it."

"Tonight."

Karis did not ask about hers. She took a folded letter out of her sleeve and laid it beside the bean at the right-hand end of the row, the one nobody had pointed to.

"Mine," she said. "The keeper of the archive at Ternhall wrote to me this morning, privately. Four lines. A district clerk came to her counter six days ago with a sealed request for my transfer file: my old standing, my privileges, the training log from the paired floor." She kept two fingers on the letter, as if it might blow away. "On the Third-day a fee office wanted to know who I was. Now somebody wants to know who I used to be." She was quiet a moment. "That's twice."

That left the bean at the far left, and Cael asked about it, because nobody else was going to.

"This house," said Seln. "Our own counter, three weeks ago. A district office asking for the sponsorship papers, *for the proceedings' record*; and in the fourth item, a schedule of every tie this house has to you. Sponsorship, floor hours, the standing that hangs on your evaluation, your travel endorsements." He touched that bean lightly. "The registrar answered it in full, and said in his covering letter that it was in full, twice."

Lira had been looking along the row the whole time, from left to right and back.

"You haven't got one for him," she said. "There isn't a bean with his name on it."

"No," said Seln. He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "That's the way to know it isn't a mistake."

Nobody asked him to explain that, and he did not.

Karis took four slips from the back of her notebook, wrote the four offices on them, one to a slip, and laid each one under its bean. Then she took a fifth slip and laid it at the end of the row, beyond the last bean, with nothing written on it and nothing on top of it.

On the stair afterward Lira asked her what the blank slip was for.

"Whoever's next," said Karis. "So far they've touched people with a seat at that table. Nobody that careful stops at the table." She reached the landing. "I've left room."

Brom had come up behind them, slower. At the turn of the stair he stopped, and Cael, a step below, saw his right hand go to the left side of his coat, where the inside pocket was, and rest there flat over the cloth. There was a square cream envelope in that pocket with its dark red seal still whole. It had come up with the post four days ago, and he had turned it over once at the table and put it away unopened, and nobody had asked. Now an office in his own province kept an open book with a question in it about his family, and his grandmother could turn its pages whenever she liked.

Brom took the hand away from the pocket and went on up the stair. Nobody asked him then, either.

---

The evening post came up at the eighth bell, and it was Seln who brought the one letter that mattered into the refectory.

He had been doing the sorts all week for the post porter, mornings and evenings, and he came in from the court with the house's bundle under his arm and one envelope held apart from it in his other hand. He did not give the bundle to anybody. He walked to the middle table, where the four of them were still sitting because none of them had wanted to go up, and he laid the single envelope face up on Karis's blank slip at the end of the row of beans, and stood back.

Cael knew the hand from across the table. Square, upright, the address set out as level as the bubble in a good spirit glass. Denvash.

Nobody touched it for a moment. Then Cael reached over and took it, and broke the fold, and read it where he sat, to himself, all of it, from the brass to the dog to the third paragraph and the single initial at the end. He read the third paragraph twice. Then he did not trust his voice, and he passed the sheet across the table to Brom, with his thumb under the third paragraph.

Brom read it aloud. He read it slowly, the way he read anything, without any change in his voice for any of it.

*Two polite men from the registry came through Denvash asking after my trade licenses and the shop's title. Wanted to know if you had any interest recorded in either. I told them my grandson's interest in this shop is that he grew up in it, and they wrote that down. Whatever they're building, it has a shape. Mind the shape. — H.*

Brom laid the sheet down, and nobody said anything, and Seln put his hand in his pocket and took out a fifth bean and set it on the slip, beside the envelope, apart from the others.

Then Cael took the letter back and read them the rest of it, the first part, the part he had smiled at. He did not quite know why he did it. Perhaps he wanted them to hear Hesk as Hesk was, a man with a bench and a stove and opinions, and not as a line in somebody's inventory.

*The brass came up from the coast at last, and it is a third dearer than last year and not as good, and the founder says it is nobody's fault, which I believe, because it never is. I have a surveyor's level on the bench that came back to me after thirty years. I made it for the man's father. The bubble has wandered a hair, and the son brought it in to be put right; and then, when I had it in pieces, he asked me to set it back the way it was, because his father laid out every boundary in the valley with it a hair wrong and the whole valley is built to agree with it now. I spent four days deciding. In the end I did as he asked.*

*The stove draws well. The shop is the shop. The widow across the lane has a new dog that barks at me in particular, and I have decided to take it as a mark of my character.*

It did not work. He heard it not work as he read it. The dog and the stove and the level went out into the room and fell on the table among the beans, and none of them could get Hesk back out of the third paragraph. He stopped reading and folded the letter.

*Grew up in it.* He had. At five years old he had slept under the long bench in a nest of clean shavings, because the stove ticked there and it was warmer than upstairs while Hesk worked late. He had learned his letters off the brass makers' plates on instruments that came in to be mended. The shop's title was one sheet of the district's paper folded in four, in a tin box under that bench with the receipts, and it had Hesk's name on it and no other. Two polite men had asked whether his was in the tin box too. And on a form somewhere, in a column under a printed heading, a pencil had set down *grew up in it*.

"Denvash," said Lira, as if she were setting it down on the table with the beans.

Karis took the blank slip from under the envelope and wrote on it, small. *Denvash. Provincial registry. Trade licences, and the title to the shop.* She laid it back under the fifth bean.

---

It was Karis who said it first, and she said it as a guess, which she almost never did.

"If I wanted to shut a man out of everything at once," she said slowly, "every door at once, so that he couldn't move a deed or carry a letter or warn a friend before the next one shut, I would want to know where all his doors were. Beforehand." She looked at the five beans. "Is that what this is?"

"Nearly," said Seln.

"Which part's wrong?"

"*Beforehand*." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "You've put it before the shutting. It's before the ruling."

Karis's pen lay on the board where she had let go of it. Cael had never seen her let go of a pen with a question still open.

"Go on," she said.

"Shut every door on one morning, yes. That's what it's for. A restriction that runs one office at a time leaks: somebody hears, somebody moves a deed, somebody's cousin carries a letter over a district line before the freeze arrives. So nobody runs one like that." He laid a fingertip on the board beside each bean in turn, not touching any of them. "Every office finds its own door first, and writes it down, and puts what it found in a drawer. Five drawers, here. Each file lawful. Each as dull as a laundry list. All five indexed under one name. And then they wait."

"For the ruling."

"For a ruling." Seln's voice did not change. "Think what five offices' clerks cost for nine weeks. An office spends that only when it already believes it knows the answer. So if the walking has begun, then somebody well above the seat has settled in his mind how the seat will rule, and has started paying for the morning after. The walking is a finding of its own. It's made by people who don't sit in the seat, and made first."

"And if the seat rules the other way? Our way?"

"Take the bad way first, it's shorter. One clerk, one afternoon, five files carried to one desk; every door shut on the same morning; and the enrollee learns what all this was for on a day when there's nothing left to do about it." He turned the cup back. "Your way, the five drawers are simply never opened together. Each office weeds its old papers in due course, as offices do, and nobody anywhere has broken a single rule."

"What do you call it?" said Brom.

"In the office we called it walking the fences." Seln looked at the five beans. "On paper, it's a pre-restriction inventory."

"How long does it take?"

"For a smith with a forge and a wife and a guild, six weeks. Ten, if the wife's people are spread about." He looked at the four in their row and the one apart. "For a boy with friends at three provincial registries, and a house that took him in, and a grandfather with a shop at the far end of the country—" He laid one finger flat on the board beside the fifth bean. "Nine weeks. They are in the sixth."

Nobody said anything.

Karis did not answer that with words at first. She reached out and moved the slips: the four office slips she pushed together into one heap under the four beans, and the Denvash slip she drew a hand's breadth further away from them, by itself, with its bean on it.

"They're not freezing him," she said, looking at the one slip. "He has nothing they could freeze. They're finding out what you'd mind." She took her hand away. "Somebody say it, so I know I'm not the only one who saw it."

"Said," said Brom.

He was looking at the letter in Cael's hand, the square level address and the old fold. He had never met Hesk. He had heard about him at a hundred tables, the stove and the spectacles pushed up on the forehead and the barometers waiting to come right, and he looked at the letter as if it were something of his own that a stranger had taken out of his coat.

"The question is what we do the day after."

Lira said it before anyone expected her to say anything, with Karis's bean turning in her fingers. Then she put the bean back exactly where it had been and explained herself, evenly, the way she read the true sum off a bout sheet after a loss. "Not whether we win the next sitting. We'll win it. They'll let us win every sitting all year; it costs them nothing. There are men in Denvash asking about a tin box. That isn't about any sitting. That's about the morning after the last one." Her chin came up. "So that's the question."

Nobody answered her.

Cael looked round the table, because looking was what he did when there was nothing to do. Karis with her palm on the notebook's cover. Brom bent forward with his elbows on his knees and his eyes on the board. Lira with the question still lying open where she had put it, and her chin up over it, waiting for one of them to pick it up. And at the middle of the bench Seln, who had walked fences like these himself for years and knew from the inside what the morning after one looked like, sitting with his hands quite still on either side of the five beans, as if the table might tip if he moved them.

The others went up in ones and twos, Brom last, with his hand on his coat. Cael stayed. After a while Seln began to put the beans back into his pocket, one at a time, the fifth last.

"You'll want to write to Denvash tonight," he said, without looking up.

"Yes."

"Write it." The fourth bean went into the pocket. "Give it to the registrar for his pouch, not to the town post. The pouch is locked in Ostrand and opened here, and I open it." He put the fifth bean away. "Letters from a house under inventory are read. Not by anyone wicked. By a clerk, for completeness, who will copy a line of it into a file. Don't give them a line." He stood, and turned his empty cup a quarter-turn on the board, and left it there. "Tell him the stove sounds well. Tell him nothing else he wouldn't want read aloud in a provincial counting-room. He'll understand. He's been minding shapes longer than either of us."

He went out at his even pace, and Cael sat a while longer alone at the long table, where the beans had been.

---

Withrow did not read the letter. She asked him to read it to her, all of it, and sat at her desk with her pen in her hand while he did, as if she meant to take a note.

He read the brass and the level and the stove and the dog, and she smiled at the dog. He read the third paragraph. Somewhere in its second sentence the pen in her hand stopped moving above the blotter, and it did not move again until he had finished, and then she laid it down very carefully, parallel to the blotter's edge, which was not a thing she did with pens.

"The girl," she said.

It was the last thing he had expected her to say. She pointed him with two fingers to the visitor's chair, and he sat, which was not usual either.

"The one who came to my counter every week for a year. You know about her. What you don't know is that she wasn't the first of that family at my counter." She took the brown pocket code off the corner of the desk and opened it near the back, and turned it round so that he could see the margin of the page, where, beside a short printed list, somebody had written in faded ink two initials and a few words. "Her father came first. On the fourth morning. He had a flour sack."

She told it from the sack outward.

"He tipped it out on my counter, and it was letters, nine of them, and he had put them in the order they'd come to his door so that I could read his week. The bank's on top, because the note on his house named his standing as its surety, and a standing under restriction is no surety. Then the transit office, to say his endorsement had run out, so that the law no longer let him cross out of his district to argue with anybody. Then his guild, which can't keep a contract with an unclassified man. Then the yard, which had given his run to someone else that same morning. And at the very bottom, the girl's school, to say her place was under review because a child's place rests on the parent's. I think he put that one at the bottom on purpose. The other four I won't give you. They were the same letter in other hands."

Cael looked at the margin. The initials were a man's. Beside them, in a younger version of Withrow's hand: *two children; the house; Force, Bronze 4; haulage, river district; 31.*

"He'd done nothing," said Withrow. "Twenty years before, a station clerk made a slip writing down his Kindling, and the one officer who could have sworn to the true reading was dead, so the slip could never be put right. The registry stood him in a frame again. Twice. Twice the frame had nothing to say. That was all." She touched the printed list beside her old note. "These are the nine headings. Employment, guild, credentials, property, debts, enrollments, travel, office, and *other relationships*. The district was short of clerks that year and I was good at it. I walked his fences myself. Seven weeks. I was proud of how careful it was."

"What did you say to him? At the counter."

"That he hadn't nine troubles. He had one, which had come to his door nine times in nine coats, and there was no counter anywhere he could stand at to answer it, because nobody had done anything to him. A restriction isn't an act. It's a condition you're left standing in." She shut the code. "That is how it was built."

"And then?"

"The last record I have of him is a toll at the ferry by the last station, eastbound, a year and a half later. That's how long it takes to get round a lapsed endorsement inside the law." She was quiet a moment. "After the ferry there's nothing. Past that line a man stops making records, and records were the only eyes I ever had. The girl stayed with her mother's people. And came to my counter."

"What will they do to Hesk?" said Cael.

"Nothing," said Withrow. "Not a hand on the door. A hand on the clock."

She let that stand a moment before she explained it.

"*Grew up in it* is what he told them, so it's what they wrote, and it's true. It goes under the ninth heading, a true line among true lines, and it waits. On the morning somebody wants the ninth heading, nobody will say a word against him; they won't need to. Any licence held by a man the registry has entered as tied to a restricted person can be called in for review. Nobody refuses it. They review it." She looked at the letter in his hand. "For exactly as long as somebody likes."

She opened the code once more at the nine headings, took up her pen, and wrote one word in the margin beside the ninth, small, under her own old note about the haulier, in the hand she had used to underline that book in her second year. Then she turned the book round so that he could read it. *Choosing.*

She did not say anything about it. She closed the code, and put it back on the corner of the desk, and said, "Go to bed."

Lira was sitting on the top stair of the residence when he came up, with her back against the newel post and her knees drawn up, waiting, though she would not have said so.

"Well?" she said.

He sat down a step below her and told her about the flour sack. Not all of it. The nine letters in the order they had come, the girl's at the bottom, the ferry toll at the last station. She listened without moving.

"And Hesk?"

"A hand on the clock."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "I wrote to him once, you know. From Greyvane. He wrote back two lines and told me to keep you out of draughts." She got up. "Put my line in the column. The Fenmark one. And then put a line in for him, too, even though he isn't one of the four. He'd want to be counted properly." She went along the landing to her door. "He'd say a column with a wrong total isn't a column."

---

He wrote it past midnight, and it came out as two columns before it came out as sentences, because that was how the night had arrived.

*Sittings: ours. Filings: ours. Every point there is to win on paper: ours, and the seat writes it down.*

*Denvash: theirs. A tin box under a bench: theirs.*

*I've stood for three years on one line, with the text on one side of it, which holds, and the people on the other, who fail me and can be read and beaten. I never once had to look for where it ran. Tonight there was a flour sack on Withrow's counter with nine letters in it, and I looked, and couldn't find it. A hand on a clock isn't a person, and it isn't text. I don't know what it is. I've never needed to.*

Then, because Seln had told him to and because he could not have slept otherwise, he wrote the other letter.

It was short. He wrote it three times. The first was angry, and he tore it across. The second was frightened, which was worse, and he tore that too, and sat a while with the pieces in his hands before he put them in the stove. The third he wrote slowly, reading every line as a clerk with a pencil would read it in a provincial counting-room, for completeness, and taking out anything he would not have wanted copied into a file.

*You were right to set the level back the way the son wanted it. A valley that agrees with itself is worth keeping agreed. You'll have known that before you spent the four days; you spent them to be sure.*

*The stove sounds well. Tell the widow's dog it has good judgment.*

*I'm well. The house is good to me. I read your third paragraph at a window, to the people I'd most want to hear it, and they heard it. I'll mind it.*

He signed it with the one letter Hesk signed his with, and sealed it, and in the morning he gave it to Bracken for the pouch, and Bracken took it without a word and put it in the pouch himself.

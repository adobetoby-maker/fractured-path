# Chapter 33 — Fixity

Brom brought the floor schedule up with his breakfast, in his head, because he could not carry a tray and a sheet of paper with one arm.

"Seln's put up next week's schedule," he said. "This morning, before the bell. A day early. He's never once put it up early." He set the tray on the end of the bed, carefully, and lowered himself into the chair, more carefully. His left arm was strapped across his chest in a sling the infirmary had made out of a triangle of linen and a great deal of disapproval. "There's one line in the First-day column. Ephram, the Crown yard, from the second bell."

"And the five?"

"Gone over to the coming Fourth-day. Five floors, five names, a quarter of an hour apart, the same as before." Brom took a piece of bread off the tray and ate half of it. "And there's a new line against wing three. *Hesk-ward: excused (ribs).* In his fair hand. I watched three people read it and not one of them read anything into it but ribs."

"Ephram?"

"Ephram read it twice." Brom finished the bread. "Then he took his chart out of the captain's file and made a mark on it, standing at the board, and put it back. I don't know what mark. A gap, I'd guess. He's a man who writes down gaps."

Cael lay back against the pillow and looked at the ceiling.

Seln had moved it three days, without a word to anybody, the morning after the road. He had read the coach's arrival and the infirmary's lamps and the five of them coming up the stair with chalk on their knees, and he had gone to the wing before the first bell and copied out the coming week's schedule fair a day before it was due, with five lines moved. He had not asked whether Cael was well enough. He had not asked anything. He had simply moved the day to where a man with strapped ribs would be able to climb a stair, and posted it on three doors, where it looked like nothing.

"The coming Fourth-day," said Cael.

"The day after your birthday," said Brom. "Lira will have told him. She tells people things when she thinks it'll save them asking." He looked at the tray. "Eat that. The rota's on the door."

It was; Cael could see it from the bed. Brom had written it on the back of yesterday's floor schedule, in his heavy square letters, and nailed it to the inside of the door with one nail through the top, at eye height, where nobody coming in could miss it.

*BROM, LIRA, KARIS, TWO HOURS EACH BY THE CLOCK ON THE SILL, AND THEN AGAIN FROM THE TOP.*

And underneath, smaller, as if he had thought of it afterward:

*SOMEBODY IN THE ROOM. ALWAYS.*

The first one that morning came at the door.

Brom had left it ajar when he came in with the tray, a hand's breadth open, and the door had swung to on its own weight and stopped against its frame without latching. And as it touched the frame, something at the back of Cael said, quietly and with great courtesy, *There?*

*No*, he thought.

It went away.

It had been doing that since the toll-bar. All the way home in the coach, all through the infirmary with its basins and its lamp and the healer's cool hands on his ribs, all the night at the sill. *There?* It never pushed. It never repeated itself once it had been answered. It simply noticed, every time one thing came to rest against another, that here was a surface and here was a thing touching it, and that it knew how to set a point between them and tell it to stay, and it asked whether he would like that done. It was the most polite thing he had ever carried. It was like having a clerk at his elbow who wanted only to be useful and had been told to ask about everything.

It asked about the door against its frame, and the cup on its saucer on the tray, and the tray on the end of the bed, and the bed's foot where it stood against the wall, and the book on the sill and the lamp on the book and Brom's boot where it rested against the leg of the chair, one after another, each as it came to his notice. *There? There? There?* And he said *no* to every one.

Each refusal cost him a little, not much, about what it costs to say no to a child who has asked for the fifth time whether it can come too. But they added up, as small things did, and by the third bell his head was tired in a way his body was not.

Karis came at the fifth bell with her notebook and her right hand still wrapped, and sat at the foot of the bed, and asked him how many.

"How many what?"

"How many times it's asked." She had a sheet of squared paper on the notebook's back board and a pencil in her left hand. "You've been saying *no* under your breath since I came in. I can see your mouth. I want a count."

He had not known his mouth was moving.

"I don't know. A lot."

"Then we'll find out." She drew a line across the top of the squared sheet and wrote the bell beside it. "Every time it asks, lift a finger. Don't say anything. I'll make the marks." She looked at him. "I can't write well with this hand. I can make marks."

So he lifted a finger, and she made a mark, and he lifted a finger, and she made another. By the end of her shift the first two lines on the squared sheet each had about forty marks on them, in rows of five with a stroke through, in her slanting left-handed scrawl.

"Forty an hour," she said. "Near enough." She looked at the sheet for a long time without saying anything. "It's courteous, isn't it."

"How do you know?"

"Because you keep nodding when you say no." She turned the pencil over. "You only do that when somebody's being polite to you and you don't want to hurt their feelings."

---

Lira saw it before he felt it.

She was in the chair for her shift a little before noon, with her strapped foot up on a stool and her crutch against the wall and Rooke's red drill-book open on her knee, and she stopped reading and looked at the sill. In the corner of it, where the porter's girl never quite reached with her cloth, a little drift of grey dust had woken up and begun to run along the edge of the wood toward the window frame in a thin stream, as if the sill had tipped.

Then his ears closed and opened, and he knew. Something large in him had lifted its head: the great slow weight that had lain quiet since the dawn on the second floor, raising itself to look at whatever had just come into the house. The air in the room changed its weight, not much, the way air does before thunder.

"Is that you?"

"It's the storm."

"Doing what?"

"Moving." He lay still and let it. It was not the pull of the old days, the wrong thing answering, the coat caught on the nail. Nothing answered him at all; he had not called anything. It was more as if a great iron chest in the cellar of a house had begun, very slowly, to shift itself along the wall to make room for another chest beside it, and he could hear the scrape through the floorboards. "It's making room."

She watched the dust run. Then she took a stub of pencil out of her pocket and wrote on her palm, which she had done on the honest floor all year.

"Bill or flag?" he said.

"*Bill, not flag*," she said, and stopped, and looked at what she had written, and rubbed it out with her thumb. "No. I don't know. It's not answering wrong. It's not answering late. It's not answering at all, it's just moving." She looked at him honestly. "I don't know which. I'll write that."

She wrote *don't know* on her palm, and the time by the bell.

This first turn was short. The scrape stopped after a quarter of an hour by Hesk's clock, and the dust lay still an inch along the sill from where it had been, and Lira wrote the figure under *don't know* and went back to her drill-book.

It came back in the afternoon and stayed. He lay flat through it and let his ears close and open as they liked, while the grey dust ran and stopped and ran again along the wood and the chest in the cellar went the rest of the way along its wall, slowly, scrape by scrape, until it had found the place it wanted and the air went back to being the weight of air.

"Forty minutes," said Karis, from the clock, when it was done; she had come up for her shift at the start of it and had not taken her eyes off the dust. "That's longer than the morning's. I think it's sitting down."

It was. It did not move again.

---

The doorway was in the middle of the afternoon.

Karis had gone, and Brom had gone to the infirmary to have his sling looked at, and for the quarter hour between their shifts Cael was alone in the room for the first time since the road, because the rota said *SOMEBODY IN THE ROOM. ALWAYS.* and the healer had said that Brom's sling came first. He did not mind. He lay with his eyes shut and said *no* to the cup on the saucer and the lamp on the book, and counted the bell from the lecture range, and waited.

He heard Lira on the stair before he saw her. Step, crutch, step, crutch, and the crutch's rubber foot on every tread like a soft knock. She came along the corridor. He heard her stop outside his door, and the door, which was still standing a hand's breadth open against its frame, swing inward as she put the crutch's tip to it.

She came to the threshold.

*There?* said the new thing, about the doorway.

He was half asleep. He had said *no* to it perhaps four hundred times since the toll-bar. He began to say it again.

It did not wait.

He felt it go. It went out of him as quick and quiet as a breath, without his leave, without his asking, without his having said anything at all but the first sound of the word *no*. It set a point against the left-hand jamb of the door, at the height of a woman's shoulder, and another against the right-hand jamb, and drew the line between them taut across the opening, and told it to stay.

Lira walked into it.

She walked into air. He saw her do it from the bed, with his eyes open now and his whole body cold. She came forward on her good foot with the crutch swinging ahead of her to take the next step, and the crutch's tip went through the doorway, and her shoulder did not. Her shoulder met the line across the doorway as a shoulder meets a door that somebody has shut in your face, and she stopped dead, and the crutch went on without her. Its rubber foot skidded on the boards, and her weight went after it, onto the strapped ankle, and she made a sound.

Brom caught her.

He had come up the stair behind her, back from the infirmary early, and he caught her under the arm with his good right hand from behind before she was halfway down, and held her up, and did not let go.

"One," said Brom.

Cael was off the bed without any memory of getting off it; his ribs would tell him about it a long while afterward. He was standing in the middle of the room looking at a doorway with nothing in it, and Lira's face on the far side of the nothing, white, with her shoulder still pressed against the air.

"Two," said Brom.

He reached for it, because he knew how, because he had done it on the road: he reached for the point to tell it not to stay, and he could not find anywhere to take hold. On the road the point had been against his own coat, and he had been inside the thing it held, and he had let it go from where it touched him. This one was not against him. It was against two pieces of painted pine, eight feet apart, with Lira on the far side of them. He was on the wrong side of it. He could feel it there, taut, and he could not get his hands round it any more than you can untie a knot from the other side of a wall.

"Three. Four."

Lira had stopped pushing against it. She stood in Brom's arm and put her open hand flat on the nothing in the doorway, at the height of her shoulder, and held it there, and looked at Cael through it.

"Five," said Brom. "Six. Seven."

He did not try again, because he did not know what else to try. He stood in the middle of his room with his hands open and looked at her, and she looked at him, and the doorway held.

"Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven."

"Twelve," said Brom.

On twelve it let go.

It let go by itself, without his asking. He felt the point leave the left jamb and the right, and the line between them go slack and simply not be there, and Lira's flat hand went forward through the doorway into the room as if somebody had opened a door she had been leaning on. Brom held her up. Her crutch lay on the boards inside the room where it had skidded.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Brom bent, slowly, with his good arm, and picked up the crutch, and gave it back to her, and Lira took it. She came in over the threshold, step, crutch, step, looking at the floor, and sat down in the chair, and put her foot up on the stool. Her hands were shaking. She folded them in her lap so they would stop.

"Twelve seconds," said Brom.

"I didn't ask it to," said Cael.

"I know," said Lira.

"I didn't ask it. I was saying no. I was halfway through saying no." He heard his own voice and did not like it. "It wasn't on the leaf. Nothing on the leaf said it would do anything I didn't tell it to."

"No," said Brom. "It wasn't on the leaf."

Lira had her hands folded in her lap. She unfolded them and looked at them, and folded them again.

"I saw your face," she said. "Through it. The whole twelve. You weren't doing it." She said it carefully, as if she were giving a figure to a panel and wanted it written down right. "You were trying to take it off me from the wrong side and you couldn't, and you were frightened, and it wasn't you. I'm telling you because you'll want to put it in the book the other way round."

He would have. He knew it as she said it.

"It was mine," he said. "Whatever did it. It came out of me."

"So does your Wind," said Lira, "and nobody blames you when it knocks a tray off a table on the oak." She put her foot more squarely on the stool. "Write that it was in you and it wasn't you. Both. They're both true, and I was the one in the doorway, so I get to say which goes first."

He shut the door, carefully, all the way, until the latch clicked. Then he sat down on the end of the bed, because Lira had the chair, and nobody said anything else until Karis came at the fifth bell with her squared paper and stopped in the doorway, which was only a doorway, and looked at the three of them, and asked what had happened.

Brom told her, and she listened. Then she came in, and sat on the floor with her back to the wall, and drew a line on the squared paper at the bell, and wrote beside it, in her wide crooked left-handed letters, *Doorway. Uncommanded. 12 s. B. counting.*

She did not say anything about it. She looked at the line for a long time, and then she drew a small box round it.

Rooke came at dusk, which nobody expected.

He stood in the doorway with his hands in his coat pockets and did not come in. He looked at Lira's foot on the stool and at Brom's sling and at the strapping round Cael's ribs where his shirt was open, and at the rota nailed to the inside of the door. He read the rota. Cael watched him read it, all of it, down to *SOMEBODY IN THE ROOM. ALWAYS.*, and then look at the doorway he was standing in, as if it had told him something he did not have words for.

"Ribs," he said to Cael.

"Ribs. The right side. Eight days, the healer says."

That was true, and small, and it was everything the leaf had said Rooke was to be told.

Rooke nodded, and looked at Lira.

"The ankle's six days in strapping," he said. "I've talked to the healer. You're off the frames and off the floor until I see you walk across the east hall without thinking about it. That's three bouts on the card. The Second-day after next, the Fifth-day after that, and the lake house's exhibition." He took one hand out of his pocket and moved it, palm down, flat, as if he were wiping a board. "I'll strike them in the morning."

"Three," said Lira.

"Three. They'll go on your sheet as not fought. That's a loss, on a program sheet. I won't pretend it isn't." He looked at her a moment longer. "It's a good ankle to lose them for. You were standing where a man's foot was going to land. Brom told me." He put his hand back in his pocket. "I'd have stood there."

He went.

Lira sat in the chair for a long while after he had gone, with her hands in her lap.

---

Brom had the watch after supper, and by the end of it the house was asleep.

He sat in the chair with his sling across his chest and his feet stretched out and his chin down, and Cael thought he was dozing. The lamp was low. The new thing asked about the cup, and the lamp, and the rota's nail in the door, and the door against its frame, and Cael said *no* to each one, aloud now, very quietly, because he had found that it was easier aloud. Every time a thing came to rest against a door, his whole body went cold and listened.

"I measured it wrong," he said.

He had not meant to say it; it came out into the dark room by itself.

Brom did not open his eyes. "Measured what?"

"The leaf. I sat at the sill with all of you on the Second-day and priced it. I wrote down everything I could think of, the first minute and a week without the band and the lane and the read and somebody in the room. I left blanks where I didn't know, the way Seln said." He looked at the door. "There wasn't a line for that. There wasn't a line that said it might do something on its own, in a doorway, to Lira, with me lying here saying *no*. I didn't think of it. I measured it wrong."

Brom was quiet for a while.

"My father had a forge," he said.

Cael turned his head on the pillow.

"At the back of the estate, past the kitchen gardens, in a shed with a dirt floor. Not the estate's forge. His. Mother's people had a smith for the horses and the gates and the real work. My father had a shed with a dirt floor and a little forge he'd built himself, because he liked iron, and he liked a thing you could make with your hands that came out the same every time." Brom moved his feet. "He'd let me in when I was small, if I sat on the bench and didn't touch anything. I sat on that bench a lot of afternoons."

"What did he make?"

"Hinges. Brackets. Hooks for the stable. Nails, once, a whole keg, for no reason I ever found out." Brom almost smiled. "He'd measure the stock before it went in. Always. On the dirt floor, cold, with a steel rule and a bit of chalk, three times, and mark it. How long, how thick, where it was going to bend. He'd take as long as it needed. Then it went in the fire."

He was quiet a moment.

"And sometimes it came out of the fire and did something he hadn't measured. It cracked along a grain he couldn't see, or bent where it shouldn't. Once a whole bar went soft at one end and he never knew why. And he'd stand there with it in the tongs and look at it, and he never once went back to the rule and said the rule was wrong. The rule was right. He'd measured it right. The fire does what fire does. Nobody blames the rule for the fire."

The lamp hissed a little.

"You sat at that sill on the Second-day with the rain on the window and measured it cold," said Brom. "Three times. With all of us. You wrote down what you could see and left blanks where you couldn't. Then it went in the fire, on a road, and it did something in a doorway that nobody could have measured, because nobody's ever had this iron before." He opened his eyes at last and looked at Cael across the dark room. "You've measured. That's the part that's yours."

Cael did not say anything for a long time.

"And the doorway?"

"The doorway's the fire's," said Brom. "We'll write it down. Next time there's a leaf, it'll have a line for doorways." He shut his eyes again. "Go to sleep. I'm counting."

---

Before he could read, Hesk had walked him down the long bench in the workshop with a hand on his shoulder, naming the tools in their racks. The scriber. The dividers. The little brass square with the nick in its heel. The burnisher, the pin vice, the files from the coarse rasp down to the fine one that Hesk called the whisper. A small boy at the end of a bench full of points and edges was afraid of all of them until somebody said what each was for, and after that they were a row of friends.

He was thinking about that bench when he woke, a little before dawn, with the window grey and Lira asleep in the chair at the end of her second watch.

His head ached from the inside, behind the eyes, in a place no healer could strap. It was the ache of a clerk who has stamped forty forms an hour since the toll-bar and has another forty on his desk. Rooke's red drill-book lay open face down on Lira's chest and rose and fell with her breathing. Her strapped foot was on the stool. The lamp had gone down to a blue bead.

Somebody had shut the window in the night against the wind, and its latch had dropped into the keeper with a small iron click that he had heard in his sleep.

The new thing asked about it. *There?*

He had no more *no* in him. He turned his head on the pillow and looked at the latch in the grey light, a curl of black iron with a thumb-piece worn bright by a hundred years of hands, and he did with it what Hesk had done with the scriber and the dividers.

*Latch*, he told it. *You lift it with your thumb, and the window opens.*

The asking stopped.

He lay still and listened for it to start again, the way you listen after a dog has barked once in the night. It did not. Refusing had always sent it off like a clerk with his paper still in his hand, not offended, coming back at the next counter. This was not that. This was the clerk reading the answer on the form, and blotting it, and filing it, and going home.

He tried the door next, cautiously, because the door was the one he was afraid of. *Door. You open it, and somebody you know comes in.*

Nothing asked.

The cup on the tray that nobody had carried down. *Cup, for tea.* The lamp with its blue bead. *Lamp.* The book under it, the chair, the nail in the rota, the stool. And Lira's strapped foot resting on the stool, and the cold went through him from his scalp to his heels, because it had not once crossed his mind that the thing would ask about a person, and it asked, softly, as politely as it had asked about the cup.

*Lira*, he told it, at once. *That's Lira. Leave her be.*

It left her be.

He went round the whole room after that, every surface, every thing that rested on another thing, in the grey light, and gave each one its name and what it was for, and each one went quiet and stayed quiet. And somewhere about the washstand he understood what he had found. Saying no had only ever been an answer to a question it would go on asking. It had never wanted to be turned away. It wanted to be told. It knew surfaces and it knew how to hold a thing against one, and it did not know what a single thing in the world was for, and every time something came to rest it asked, because nobody had ever explained.

He priced it, lying there, because that was what he did with anything new. A word a thing. A word cost almost nothing; a word was cheaper than the Wind's landing beat or the read's quiet minute after. But this house alone had more doors in it than he could count from his bed, and there were other houses, and roads with gates on them, and inns, and every door was going to need its word. It would never be paid off. It would be a small coin he put down at every threshold he ever crossed, so many times a day that one morning he would look up and find he had stopped noticing his hand go to his pocket.

Thresholds most; he could tell that already. He had named the door and it had stopped asking, but it had not stopped looking. It was told, and it accepted being told, and somewhere at the back it still leaned toward that frame the way a hound that has been sent to its basket leans toward the kitchen.

Lira opened her eyes and found him looking at the washstand with his lips moving.

"What," she said.

"It's a door," he said. "I told it."


---

Karis drew the curve that afternoon, sitting on the floor with the squared paper on her knee and the wrapping off her right hand at last, though she still held the pencil in her left.

The marks went down the sheet in their rows: forty in the first hour she had counted, then thirty-eight, thirty-six, thirty-one after the storm moved, thirty, twenty-eight. A line at the doorway with a box round it. Twenty-two through the night, by Brom's count and Lira's, which they had kept on the backs of their hands. Then the dawn, and the naming, and the counts dropped off the bottom of the page all at once: nine, four, two, one.

She joined the points with the pencil, slowly, and looked at what she had.

"Two days, near enough," she said. "From the road to the latch."

"You said nine weeks."

"I said nine weeks and I said I didn't believe it." She turned back through the notebook to the page on the Second-day, the leaf they had all initialled, where *about nine weeks, Karis's curve, not believed* stood in his own hand. She laid the squared sheet beside it. Then she turned back further, to the column she had kept for the storm since Norhold, and further, to the old figures she had copied out of the old volume, the little points low on the paper from the Wind and the read and the rest.

She did not say anything for a long while. Then she drew one more line on the squared sheet, very lightly, from the first of the old points through the storm and down again to the two days.

"Each one's come in faster than the one before," she said. "Not the storm. The storm was heavy and it came in the middle of the floor, and it's off the line. But the others. Every time there's more of you already there to make room, the next one finds its place quicker. The more you carry, the faster you take the next." She looked at the line. "That's what it looks like. That's what it looks like *now*."

She wrote two words under the line, in her crooked left hand, and drew a box round them.

*For now.*

"I don't trust a curve with this few points in it," she said. "I don't trust a curve that only goes one way. Somewhere there's a weight where it turns round and comes back up, and I haven't got a point anywhere near it." She tapped the box. "Until I have, it's two words. And they stay in a box."

Bracken came up the stair at the fifth bell with a letter in his hand.

He did not come in, but stood in the doorway, which was only a doorway, and looked at the rota on the door and the squared paper on Karis's knee and did not ask about either. He held out the letter.

"The district seat," he said. "Their answer. It came up in the noon bag." He read it out, as he read everything the house received, flat and complete. Two sentences. The office was constituted on the date entered in the district's register of offices, by instrument filed at the seat under seal. The registrar was respectfully referred to the issuing authority of the said instrument for any further particulars.

"It's what she said it would say," said Brom.

"It's exactly what she said it would say," said Bracken. He folded it once. "So I shall write to the issuing authority of the said instrument. I've begun the letter." He looked at Karis over his spectacles. "You had it a week early, Advocate. I've written that down too."

He went back down the stair.

That night, at the sill, with the day's tiredness heavy behind his eyes and Karis asleep in the chair for her shift, Cael turned to the leaf with four sets of initials and wrote beside it, in the margin, what it had cost against what they had said it would.

*Priced at Silver, and paid at Silver. Priced: the first minute, with Brom standing over me; paid exactly that. Priced: about nine weeks, which Karis didn't believe; paid two days to the idle state, and the idle state is naming. Priced: a week without the band, which I'm paying now. Priced: the lane and the read unsafe, which I haven't tried.*

*Not on the leaf: the doorway. Twelve seconds, uncommanded, with Lira in it. Brom says that's the fire's and not the rule's. I'm writing it down so the next leaf has a line for doorways.*

*Not on the leaf either: what the others paid. That has to have a line too, next time. Ankle, shoulder, hand. It was in the cut and it wasn't anywhere on the page.*

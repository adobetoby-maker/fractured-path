# Chapter 29 — The Catechism

The mould-room man answered first, because the south mill was nearer, and because he was the kind of man who answered a letter the day it came.

It reached Seln on the Third-day in the ordinary post, inside a printer's circular about a new kind of ruled ledger, folded twice and pushed down between the pages where nobody opening a circular would look. It was written in pencil, in capitals, by a man who had learned his letters late and did not trust the small ones.

*MOULD MENDED THIS YEAR. WIRE WENT AT THE TOP OF THE MARK. SEWED IT MYSELF. A SIXTEENTH LOW, I KNEW IT WOULD BE, THERE WAS NO HELPING IT. ONE RUN OFF IT SINCE FOR THE REGISTRY. WE CALL IT THE SECOND. PAID IN FULL.*

The last three words were the part Seln read twice. The man had added them on his own. He had understood exactly what was being spent, and he wanted it known that the account was closed.

Seln put the circular in the copying-room stove and watched the pencil go brown and then go.

One favour, one fact. The paper that had come up the hill to the gate had been made this year, off a mended mould, in the south mill's second run of registry stock. That was all: it did not say who had drawn it, or where it had gone. It only said that somebody, somewhere, after the mending, had ordered registry paper and been sent it.

The other half was in the west.

*Second run, south mould: thirteen lots, every one in the book, every one against an office you could find in a directory.*

*A fourteenth, drawn the same week, written in above the first ruled line, where nothing is ever written.*

*In the office column a mark where the word should be. A mark, not a word.*

*Nobody here wrote it, and nobody here will say so twice.*

The sheet was ash in the post room's stove before the bag-strap was buckled again. He stood over the little iron door with the poker until the ash broke, as she would have expected him to, and only then went back to the bench and the half-sorted bags, because a man who stops the sort in the middle is a man somebody remembers.

It was the Fourth-day. The porter's shoulder was still strapped, so the sort was still his, and he had liked it since the first morning. The post room behind the records hall's counter was a narrow place with forty pigeonholes along one wall, a bench along the other, a brass beam for weighing parcels and the stove the porter kept going from the first bell whatever the weather. In half an hour at that bench you knew where every letter in the house was going and roughly why, and nobody watched you learn it. It was the most honest half-hour of his day.

Her envelope had come up in among the house's ordinary post, addressed to the wing in a clerk's hand and no bigger than an invoice, and he had given it the wing's hole and gone on with everything else before he let himself take it out again. In nine years of owing him she had never once written to him, and when at last she did it was four lines and no signature, because a woman who kept the issue ledgers for half a continent knew to a hair what a line weighed.

So that was the paper.

A mould mended in the south, and a second run off it this year. Thirteen honest lots, gone out in the ordinary way to offices with names and doors and clerks who could be written to. And a fourteenth, the same week, off the same run, on the same stock, that had not gone into the ledger at all but over it. Written in above the line by somebody who could reach a store-house ledger in the west and open it and put a thing where nothing went, and leave a mark in the column that was only a mark.

He had seen a great deal of paper in his life. He had never seen that.

That was not true. He stood at the bench and made himself correct it, because the voice he kept for the audit would not let the sentence stand. He had seen it once, twelve years ago, in a press in a records room in an old building. He had seen paper that was entirely correct in every particular, and that sat a little above where it should have sat, and had been put there by somebody who could reach.

He did not think any further along that road. He had not let himself walk down it in twelve years except on the worst nights, and this was a weekday morning, and there was post to finish.

He finished the post.

---

Back in the wing, with the copying table under his hands and the rain on the long windows, he went down the grades. He had not said them over since he was young enough to need them, and he found they were all still there, in their order, like tools in a rack nobody had touched.

The record. Paper in a subject's file. A query, a designation, a mark in a column. It cost nothing and was never undone, and it was where everything began.

The instrument. A proceeding, in daylight, by the book. A petition and a seat and an answer, and every door in it real law. It cost a great deal and was always lawful, and it was what an office used when it wanted to be seen being right.

The knock. Paper again, but from an office nobody could place, brought to a door and read there. It was not meant to be obeyed; it was meant to see what the door did, how fast, who came, and how long the man with the glass took over the seal.

And the hand. No paper. No faces. A road, at a place where no house stood, at an hour somebody had chosen.

*Four*, she had said, and struck the floor once with the stick she never leaned on. *Learn them in that order, because they come in that order. And when you have seen the third, gentlemen, do not wait for the fourth to knock. The fourth does not knock.*

That had been the last morning, in a room over a ship's chandler's on a quay, with six young men on benches who were not yet anything, and a cat asleep in the sun on the window-ledge. She had been old even then. She had opened every lesson by telling them that nobody in that room would ever be told the whole of anything and that the sooner they stopped minding the better; she had spent the weeks between on the craft, how to read a subject, how to write a return that was true and useful and no longer than it needed to be, how to live a year in a town and leave nothing behind but a room that had been let. The grades she had kept for the end, so that they would know what the other side of the table did, and where they themselves stood.

The boy had had the record since before he could read it. He had had the instrument since the first week of the year, in a sealed packet read out by a woman standing in her own hall. Five days ago he had had the knock, at the gate, in eighty-one words, with a woman standing in the one place in the court that could not be got round.

A fourteenth lot was what the knock looked like from inside a ledger. Seln had held its ash on the end of a poker an hour ago.

That left one grade.

She had taught them the catechism on the same morning, after the grades, as if it were the answer to them.

It was four lines. Every man in the service learned them in his first year, and said them under his breath on the first morning of every posting, the way other men touched a doorpost for luck. Seln had said them nineteen times on nineteen first mornings. He had never once said them on any other morning, because there had never been any other morning that needed them.

He said them now.

He stood at the copying table in the empty wing, with the shelf above it at the edge of his sight and the rain loud on the long windows, and said them aloud, once, at the pace she had used, so that the room would have heard them if the room had been listening.

*What you carry is worth what it has not done.*

*A thing kept is a wall. A thing spent is a door, and doors open both ways.*

*The keeper is invisible. The witness is a location.*

*Nobody has ever protected a man by believing him.*

Then he stood there and listened to them, the way you listen to a bell after it has been struck, to hear whether it is cracked.

What he carried had done nothing for twelve years. It was worth exactly that. He had always thought the line meant that a thing unused kept its whole value, like a coin in a drawer. Standing there he heard what it actually said. It said the value was the not-doing. It had been worth twelve years of nothing, and it was worth nothing still.

A thing kept is a wall. It had been a wall. It had stood between what he knew and whoever would want to know he knew it, and it had kept him safe, perfectly, every day. But it was a wall round the case, not round the boy. The boy was on the other side of it with the rest of the weather.

The keeper is invisible. He had been invisible for fifteen years. The boy's people had never been invisible for a day. All year they had walked about the hill in the open and let themselves be counted, the girl on the walls with her chalk and the big one at the frames and the researcher with her lamp lit in the top window every night where the whole valley could see it. Five days ago, at the gate, the house itself had stood in the middle of its own court and read the paper out loud. Nobody on that side had hidden anything. They had simply been there.

The witness is a location. He had always heard that as a warning: a man who has seen a thing is only a place where the thing can be found, so do not be found. Now, in the empty wing, it sounded like a choice. He would rather be the place the boy could find.

And the last line.

He had believed the last line more than the other three together. It was the one that had kept him clean. You did not protect a subject by believing him; you protected him by counting what he did, and filing it true, and letting the true thing be your only argument. He had never believed anybody in his life. He had counted them.

He had counted the boy for a year. Two slips on a common-room fire. A season of figures that never once settled into a line. An afternoon at Norhold where everything held back went out in one breath, on purpose, in front of eight thousand people. He had counted all of it, and filed none of it, and somewhere in the counting, without asking his leave, it had turned into the other thing.

He believed him.

*Nobody has ever protected a man by believing him.* It was true. He had never seen it done. He was going to try.

The wing's clock went on with its slow tick over the counter. Nothing in the room changed its note. Seln stood with his two hands flat on the copying table and understood, quite calmly, that this was the largest thing that had happened to him since a green corridor twelve years ago, and that it had happened on a weekday morning between the post and the noon bell, and that nobody would ever write it down.

He reached up and took the case off the shelf.

---

He carried it across the court at dusk, in the rain, with his coat buttoned over the top of it.

He did not hurry and he did not take the covered walk. A man on the covered walk was a man out of the rain, and people looked at him in a vague friendly way as they looked at anybody sharing a dry place. A man crossing the open court in a downpour was a man with an errand, and nobody looked at a man with an errand.

He counted them anyway, because he could not help it.

A porter with a hand-cart of coal under an oilcloth: one. Two first-years running for the refectory with a coat held over both their heads: two, three. The fire-watch boy with his ladder: four. A Magister of the Ash faculty under an umbrella the size of a cart-wheel, who said, "Filthy, filthy," to nobody: five. The lodge-man's boy, sent across with a lamp: six.

At the residence door a knot of third-years was standing out of the wet arguing about a bout, and he went round the edge of them: seven to twelve. On the stair, coming down, the Current girl from Rooke's program with her wet hair in a towel, who flattened herself to the wall to let him by and said, "Sorry, sorry," as if the stair were hers to be sorry about: thirteen. Two more on the first landing. Three in the corridor, one of whom was the captain, Ephram, with his green-taped file under his arm, who nodded to him and went on: eighteen. Five more behind half-open doors as he passed, reading or mending or asleep.

Twenty-three. Not one of them looked at the thing under his coat. Why would they? The wing's assistant was going up a residence stair with something for somebody. It was the most ordinary sight on the hill.

The boy's door was the last on the second corridor. Seln knocked once, and opened it when the boy said to.

He was at the window, at the sill, with Hesk's book open and the lamp lit and the notice still pinned to the frame. He looked round, and his face did what it always did when a door opened, which was nothing, and then it went still in a different way.

Seln unbuttoned his coat and set the case on the sill beside the book.

He kept one hand on the lid and did not sit. The lamp found the brass of the lock and nothing else, because there was nothing else on the outside of it for a lamp to find, and the boy's eyes went to the lock and stayed there a moment and came back up to his face.

"I'm going to open this," he said. "Not tonight."

The boy looked at the case and then at him, and did not reach for it, and did not ask what was in it. Seln had known that he would not. It was why he had climbed the stair. When the boy spoke at last it was quietly, to the case as much as to the man, and it was not quite a question.

"You're sure."

Seln heard what the boy was offering him. It was a way back down the stair. The boy had built it in two words and held it open, so that a man could take his case and go and nobody need ever mention it again. Seln looked at it, and did not take it.

"No. That's how you'll know it's true."

They decided the rest in a quarter of an hour, standing, with the case between them on the sill and the rain going on.

Not here, said Seln. A residence room had a door anybody could knock on, and a corridor full of people who knew whose room it was. Not the wing either. The wing was his, and everybody who watched the wing knew it was his, and a lamp in it after hours was already a thing somebody wrote down.

"A room with a lock," he said. "A name on the card that isn't yours or mine. A reason for people to go up to it one at a time without anybody thinking it's strange. Somewhere a person has to be on purpose."

The boy thought about it for perhaps three breaths. "The reading room."

"The researcher's."

"It has Bracken's card on the door. *Assigned: K. Dellenmoor.* It has the brass lock and one key, and she wears the key. The old lumber room across the landing has the code on a shelf and the assistant's table, and the house has called the two rooms together the annex since Bracken had it cleared, and people go up and down that stair all day with books." He stopped. "And it's hers. Nobody on the hill would think twice about four people going up to her room. They've been doing it all year."

"Five people," said Seln.

"Five."

"Then the researcher's room." He took his hand off the case at last. "Not before the sitting. Whatever comes out of this, you don't want to be carrying it in your head into a room with Jent in it. He reads nothing, but other people at that table do. The First-day after."

The boy nodded.

"I'll put it in the floor schedule," said Seln.

He saw the boy not understand that, and then understand it, which took about as long as it took the rain to run from the top of the window to the sill.

The wing posted the floor schedule every Seventh-day for the week to come: four floors, their hours, and who had booked each hour, in Seln's fair copy, pinned up on the wing's board and the residence board and the refectory door. Everybody on the hill read it. Nobody on the hill read it for anything but their own name.

"Five lines on the First-day," said Seln. "Five floors, five hours, a quarter apart, each in one of your names. A practice booking. It's the most ordinary thing on the board. Read your own line. The floor means nothing. The hour is when you come up the stair."

The boy took the pencil from the sill and wrote something on the back of his hand, and looked at it, and rubbed it out with his thumb.

"There'll be six names in the First-day column," he said. "There always are. Ephram has the Crown yard from the second bell every First-day."

"Five," said Seln. "The sixth is the captain, and he wants the floor."

The boy almost smiled. Then he did not, and looked at the case again.

---

Seln wrote his weekly return that night at the copying table, in the old pattern, as he had written one every week for fifteen years.

*Subject attended to his course of study. Subject attended the seat's fourth sitting as directed. An instrument presented at the house's gate on the Sixth-day was declined by the house; subject was not seen at the refusal. Subject's floor work within the band of the year. Nothing further to report.*

Every word of it was true. He read it over in the voice he kept for the audit, and the voice found nothing wrong with it, which was the point and always had been. He folded it in the old way, with the subject's reference on the outside fold and the posting's mark in the corner, and put it in the satchel for the quick bag in the morning.

Then he made the duplicate, as he always did, word for word, and opened the cupboard under the copying table and put it into the thickest bundle on the end of the row, the one with the name of this hill on its label.

It was not the last of its kind. He knew that while he was tying the tape. He would write another next week, and the one after, and every week until the year closed, true and empty, in the old pattern, because the pattern was what the office expected and a pattern that changed was a thing somebody wrote down. Nothing in his life was going to look different from the outside. That was the other half of the catechism, the half she had never needed to say aloud.

He put the case back on the shelf above the copying table, where it had stood for two years and where nobody had ever once looked for it, and blew out the lamp.

---

Bracken saw the chain go across Ostrand bridge a little after the fourth bell on the Fourth-day, and came down from his rooms with the ship's glass still in his hand to say so.

He found Cael on the north wall, because it was where Cael always was at that hour, and stood beside him in the rain without an umbrella and gave him the glass. Cael found the bridge. The bridge-ward was at the near end in his oilskin, with a man from the town, hauling a chain across the roadway from post to post. Below them the river was standing up against the middle pier in a long brown hump, and the iron rings let into the stone were gone under it, all but the top two.

"The third ring," said Bracken. "They shut it to everybody at the third. Carts, horses, people on foot. Since the year the middle arch went, which was before my time." He took the glass back. "The ferryman tied up at noon. I watched him do it."

"The sitting's tomorrow."

"The sitting's tomorrow," Bracken agreed. "The seat does not adjourn for weather. I have never known it to, and the house has never in my time asked it to." He looked out at the water for a moment longer. "Come and see the coachman. He's in the stable passage with a map and an opinion."

The house's coachman had driven the delegation coach for as long as Cael had been on the hill. He was a short broad man with grey whiskers who talked to his horses in a low steady voice all day and to people hardly at all. He had a road map of the valley spread on an upturned bucket in the stable passage, weighted at the corners with four horseshoes, and he put one thick finger on it when they came in, as if he had been waiting with it there.

"Up this side," he said. "West, along the bank road. Two hours if the bank road holds, and it will, it's high. To the upper ferry at the narrows." The finger moved. "She runs in any water. Rock both sides, no width for the river to throw her about in. Over there. Then back east down the river road on the far side, two hours more, into the city by the west gate." He looked up at Bracken. "Leave at the hour before light. In by noon. Sitting's after."

"Thank you," said Bracken.

The coachman folded the map along its old folds and put the horseshoes in his pocket and went back to his horses.

That evening, in the refectory, Withrow came to the end of their table on her way out and stood behind Karis's chair with one hand on its back.

"Since the gate, no enrollee of this house leaves the hill alone," she said. "You know that. Tomorrow you are five, and the house's counsel went down to her rooms by the wharf this afternoon while the bridge was still open, and will meet you at the side door." She looked along the table at each of them, the way she looked at a page before she signed it. "I would go with you, if the seat had asked for me. It has not. It has asked for him, and the maker of the copies, and the advocate of record. Go the long way and come back the short one."

She went out.

"The long way," said Lira, to her plate.

"It's only a road," said Brom.

He came up to Cael's room an hour later with his kit bag, all the same, and turned it out on the bed.

It was what he took to every floor that was not the house's own. Two rolls of linen strapping, a roll of the stiff kind that the healers used for wrists, a tin of the porter's horse salve that smelled of tar and worked better than anything the infirmary had, a clean neckcloth, a flask, a heel of bread wrapped in a napkin, and a short length of leather with a buckle at each end that Cael had never seen him use and had never asked about. He went through all of it with his big careful fingers, as he did before a meet, and put it back in a different order.

"It's a sitting," said Lira from the window seat. "They'll want him to say *They are mine* and sign. Nobody straps for that."

"I strap for the road," said Brom.

She did not laugh at him. That was how Cael knew she had been thinking about the road too.

Karis came last, with the attestation wallet under her arm and her coat already over the back of the chair as if she meant to sleep in it. She checked the wallet's contents leaf against its contents, which she had done at supper and would do again in the coach, and then she sat on the end of the bed beside Brom's kit and looked at the sill.

There was nothing on the sill but Hesk's book and the lamp. The case had gone back down the stair under Seln's coat an hour after it came up.

Cael told them.

He told them in the order it had happened, because that was the only fair way: the knock at dusk, the case set down beside the book, *I'm going to open this. Not tonight.* What he had asked and what Seln had answered. The reading room. The floor schedule and the First-day after the sitting, five names, five quarters of an hour, and a sixth line that was only Ephram wanting the Crown yard. He did not say what was in the case, because he did not know. Nobody asked.

Karis sat very still with the wallet on her knees.

"My room," she said.

"If you'll have it."

"He asked for my room." She said it slowly, as if she were reading it off a page in a hand she did not know. "Out of every room on the hill." Then she looked up, and Cael saw that she was not frightened, or not only. "Yes. Tell him yes. Tell him I'll have the girl sent home early that day, and the kettle on, and every paper on the table turned over." She stopped. "No. Don't tell him the last part. I'll just do it."

"I'll bring the kettle," said Brom.

Lira was looking out at the rain. When she spoke it was to the glass.

"Two years he's sat at the middle of the bench," she said. "Two years, and never once put anything of his on the table. Not a cup. Not a word he didn't have to." She turned round. "Good. That's all. Good."

Brom stood and shouldered his kit bag. At the door he stopped, and put his hand flat to the left side of his coat, where the letter was, the one he had carried sealed since the forty-fifth day, and kept it there a moment, as if he were checking that the road had not already taken something.

"The hour before light," he said, and went.

Cael wrote it that night with the rain at last beginning to slacken against the glass.

*The bridge chained at the third ring. The ferry tied up. One road to the seat tomorrow, round by the upper ferry, four hours. Seln came up the stair at dusk with a case under his coat and set it on my sill. He's going to open it. He isn't sure. I believe him more because he isn't.*

# Chapter 20 — Lot Seventeen

Three nights before the third sitting, the honest floor was the only floor on the hill where anybody was allowed to see what Cael was.

They went in at the quarter to the eleventh bell, as they always did, on the key Rooke had lent Lira for the program's dawn drills and had told her he did not want to know about after dark. But the east hall was not what it had been. The shutters along the gallery had been closed since the Fourth-day by Rooke's order, and they did not need to be drawn. The chain still hung across the foot of the gallery stair with its padlock and its card, *CLOSED TO OBSERVERS*, so that Brom had to step over it to set the jug down on the bottom stair. The four lamps that the program now drilled under all day were standing at the corners of the painted crosses where the porters had left them, cold.

"Well," said Lira, looking round the shut-up hall with her wrists half taped. "The whole floor's honest now. Nobody's allowed to watch any of it."

Brom lit two of the lamps and left the other two dark, because two had always been the number. Karis sat on the gallery stair above the chain with her notebook open on her knee. Nobody had asked her to come. She had come anyway, every week since the first night, and counted.

"What do you want?" said Cael.

"The real one." Lira tore the last strip of tape with her teeth. "Everything you can't show in wing three. I've had six days of drilling under lamps, and I can't see my own heel coming any more, and I keep landing a quarter-beat late on things I could read in my sleep in daylight." She put her hands on her hips. "If I can see you coming at your true rate, in lamplight, I can see anybody coming at anything. So come."

He looked at Brom. Brom looked at the lamps and then at him, and nodded once, which meant *I'm holding the room*.

So Cael fought her at his true rate, for the first time since the first week of the year.

He let the read go all the way out and come all the way back, without stopping it anywhere short to look like timing. It brought him Lira's weight half a beat early, as it always did, and in lamplight half a beat was a long time. He used the Wind the way it wanted to be used and not the way the house's book had it. One burst, two, the second off the landing of the first; a third, low, along the wall; a fourth to take him round her lie on the four-count before her fourth arrived. On the boards in wing three he stopped at four, and never let a fifth show. Here he went to five without thinking about it, and then to six, and felt the old easy looseness come into his legs that he had not let himself feel on a floor in front of anybody since Norhold.

Lira lost the first two exchanges. She lost them cleanly and quickly and said "Again" both times before she had finished losing.

In the third she came at him with a straight palm, the hardest thing she had, from the end of a burst, and he did not move out of it. He let it land on his forearm. The give was there, Reydan's give, and for once he let it open. It drank her blow into the arm as a dry cup drinks water, and kept it, and her palm stopped against him as if it had hit a sack of wet sand. She stood there with her arm out, looking at it.

"I hate that," she said with great feeling.

"I know."

"I'd forgotten how much." She shook her hand out. "Again. And lay one."

He laid a lane.

He laid it across her line from the north wall, a road of leaning air fifteen feet long and no wider than his shoulders, tipping south. His ears shut and opened again. And on the gallery stair Karis began to count aloud, quietly, from the moment it was laid.

"One."

In the lamplight Lira could not read her heel. She had said so. But Cael, watching her come at the lane on the four-count, saw her find something else. The dust in the cracks between the painted boards had woken as the lane went down, the way it always did, and was running south along the lane in a thin pale stream. In daylight from the high windows you could hardly see it. Under two lamps set low on the floor, it threw a shadow. The whole lane showed on the boards as a faint moving band of dark, like the wake of a fish under still water.

Lira saw it on her second count. He watched her see it. Her lie went onto the back foot, outside the band, and on the third count, the empty one, she did not put a foot into the lane at all. On the fourth she burst along its edge, the length of it, not across, and came out at its end beside him with her palm already flat on his shoulder.

"Touch," said Brom, from the dark beyond the lamps.

"*Seven*," said Karis, on the stair.

Cael reached for the Wind. It came late. It came a beat behind his asking, the right thing answering slow, as it had answered every time since the first night on this floor. He stood in it. *Bill, not flag.* Lira, beside him, breathing hard, was looking down at the boards where the dust was settling out of the band and the band was fading.

"The dust," she said. "Under the lamps the dust makes a shadow. I can see the lane by its shadow."

"I saw you see it."

"*Ten*," said Karis. "*Eleven*."

The Wind came when he called it.

"Ten counts," said Karis, and wrote it down. "Same as the first night."

Lira had gone down on one knee by the end of the lane, and was looking along the boards at the last of the dust, with her face close to the floor and the lamp throwing her shadow long behind her.

"I lost my light," she said, to the boards. "Rooke shut it out on account of who was watching you. I've been angry about it for six days, all by myself, under these lamps, and I haven't told anybody, because it was the right thing to do and I knew it was." She stood up and dusted her knee. "And it turns out the lamps show me a thing the windows never did."

"That doesn't make it fair."

"No. It makes it mine." She went back to her cross. "Lay another. Not across this time. Lay it down the line, at me, and let me try to stand in it."

"We said we don't spend a new figure the night we find it."

"It's not a new figure. It's the same lane. I'm the new thing." She set her feet. "Lay it."

He laid it, once more, and she stood in it on the four-count and was pushed off her feet into the dust, and laughed out loud from the floor, and Karis counted ten and wrote ten, and then Brom said that was enough for one night in a voice that nobody argued with.

They put out the lamps by the old order, the far one first, and stepped back over Rooke's chain, and Lira locked the door behind them.

On the stair Cael thought about it, the way he thought about everything on stairs.

In wing three, four strangers had written down a figure for him that was exactly right and entirely false: the band, the house's rate, twenty-two and two-thirds. Here, on a floor with the shutters closed and a chain across the stair, there had been nobody to write anything at all, and the figure had been true. Six bursts on boards. The give opened. A lane and its bill. Every true number about him this year had been made in a locked room by four people who would never be allowed to say it aloud. And every false one had gone into a book.

He wrote it in Hesk's book at the sill before he slept. *The only honest measurement anybody has taken of me since Norhold was taken tonight, by Karis, on a closed floor, under two lamps. It will never be entered anywhere. Lira found the lane by its shadow. That will be entered nowhere either, and it's the best thing anybody learned on this hill this week.*

---

Ilsev had already folded her pencil shavings away.

She had them in a twist of paper in her coat pocket, every curl of them, so that not one should lie on the wool exchange's floor, and she was reading the topmost of her files with the patience of somebody who has read it three times and means to read it once more before anybody asks her a question. Cael watched her do it from the house's table. Her files lay on her lap-desk at the angle he would have known anywhere, never square to the edge and never a hair different from the last time: a counter at Greyvane, the east wall of a room on the bluff, the first seat of the Compact's row high over the Concourse.

She was in the middle chair of the evaluation seat, a little forward of the other four. This morning the grey covers were off all five, and the chairs under them turned out to be plain and high-backed, the kind a district buys by the dozen. Four district evaluators he had never seen filled the others, two men and two women, sitting very upright and looking at nothing.

The clerk was on his feet, reading a paper into the minute under the heading *statements entered*.

He gave the filer's name, and house, and Path, and rank. He gave the date and the line of receipt. Then, in the voice that adds nothing, he gave the text.

*I fought him. Your instruments failed him, not the other way around. Classify the instruments.*

Eleven seconds, all of it. While it went by, Cael's mind did a thing it often did in that room and went backward without asking him: to the side door an hour ago, where the young officer with the ruled board had lost his cold, and had not needed to ask the last name on his list because somebody had already copied it into the door log from the last sitting, *Caelen Hesk-ward*, so that all he had to write was the standing, *the enrollee of record, unclassified*, which took him nine seconds now that his hand knew the way. To the long floor, where Cael had crossed *nine*, *ten*, *eleven* and sat down with *seventeen* under his left foot for the third time, and known this time that he had walked to it on purpose. You could not choose the room. You could choose where you stood in it. And further back, to the ferry, where Karis had written one word on a slip and folded it twice and handed it to Brom.

"Don't open that," she had said, "till we're in the coach coming home."

The slip was in Brom's right inside pocket now, on the bench behind the house's table, where *attendance* sat. Cael could see Brom's hand resting on his coat over it. The left pocket was for Velmere, and it had a sealed letter in it.

The hood received Daeva's statement. Jent did not stir. The counsel did not stir. There was nothing anyone on that floor could say, because there was nowhere in the schedule to say it. The clerk laid the sheet on the pile of things received.

Cael felt it go through the room like a bell nobody else could hear. *Ten days ago the best evaluator the Compact owns asked to be heard, and the seat was too full. This morning the best practitioner alive has been heard, and the seat filed her on a pile.* He wrote it under the table on the back of his schedule, small. *Rank isn't the thing it listens to. I don't know yet what is.*

He did not know what he felt about Ilsev in that chair. She was the most exactly honest officer he had ever stood in front of. And somebody had set her in the middle of this, where everyone could see her; and if you set the straightest officer in the registry beside a thing, nobody will ever look past her to see the shape of what she is standing beside.

At the records table behind her, at the junior chair, the officer with the careful face wrote the time at the head of a clean leaf, and did not lift his eyes.

---

Jent stopped in the middle of a sentence.

"A record kept under the demonstration provision," he had been saying, "is not a record that this seat could—"

And he stopped, and stood quite still at the petitioner's table with his empty hands at his sides, in front of forty benches and a hood, and Cael watched him make up his mind in something under two breaths.

"No," said Jent. "I withdraw that. It overreaches, and it is false. The provision makes a record, and the record can be read; the respondent spent fifty-one minutes at the last sitting reading it in." He turned his head a quarter-inch toward the clerk. "The petitioner's point is smaller. The record is of what the practitioner has done. It is not, and does not pretend to be, a measure of what he is. The whole petition rests on that difference and on nothing wider. Clerk, take the wider words out of the minute, if you would."

The clerk drew his pen through a line.

Cael let the read go out across the floor to him while he stood there deciding. It was the oldest habit he had, older than the band, and in that room it had never once brought him anything. It came back now with the man's weight, as it always did, and the weight was where it had been all morning: settled square on both heels, unhurried, with nothing in it leaning toward the decision or away from it. No want under it. A man deciding to give away a point the way he would decide which pen to use.

Cael found that he had lifted his own pencil off the paper and not put it down. Of everything that had happened in that room in three sittings, it was the first thing he had not seen coming. A man had been winning, and had said a thing that helped him, and had taken it back because it was not true.

He looked down at his notes, and read back over what had come before it, because twenty minutes of Jent had gone by him like a river and he wanted to know where he was standing.

*Gives away the enrollment. First minute, before anybody asks.* Jent had told the hood the petition never said the enrollment was wrongly made, and that if anybody in the room had heard it that way he would like them corrected at once, so that not a minute of the seat's time went on it.

*Gives away the clause.* Law, said Jent. Untouched by the standardizing. The house's whole history of it accepted, every word, and taken for the petitioner's own.

*Gives away the paper.* The best record of an enrollment he had read in thirty years, said again for the minute; and when the clerk looked up to ask whether that was to go in as spoken, "Every word. I'd decline to soften it if I were asked." Along the front benches something had shifted. Cael had written *stir: ?* in the margin and let it be.

Below that, his notes had stopped being a list and become what was left.

*Registry's confidence.* Jent had read the integrity sections' first line aloud: they existed so that the registry might trust its own evaluations. Not for a practitioner, not for a house. For the registry.

*The discretion: whole.* Nobody disputed that the registry could order a man re-evaluated in an integrity matter. The seat need find nothing wrong with anything first.

*No reading, no trust — "by that fact and by nothing else".* If the standard protocol could not read a man, the registry had nothing it had measured to trust him on. Jent had said *by that fact and by nothing else* three times and explained it once, the way a man lays a plank across a ditch and then walks on it to show it holds.

*Describes, not evaluates.* And here had come the overreach, and the stop.

Cael sat with the notes and did a sum the way Lira would want it done on the coach going home, in things that happen to people and not in words from a code.

Everything they had spent six weeks fighting about was gone. Whether the clause was law; whether the house's record was honest; whether anybody had done anything wrong. Jent had handed all of it across the floor in his first ten minutes, and it could never be taken back now, because it was in the minute in his own voice. What was left was one question with no paper in it at all: may the registry stand him in a frame? And everyone in the room knew already what the frame would say.

Jent asked for it plainly. He did not ask the seat to find the enrollment faulty, since it was not. All he wanted was a single word from the seat, *cognizable*: that this was a question it was allowed to pick up. Once it had picked it up, the code already said what came next. A frame. The standard protocol. And the enrollment would live or die by whatever the frame said back.

He cited the adjudication office's file, once, by its page, as *the office's own finding that what the practitioner can do lies beyond the Standard's tables*. He did not read the entry on that page. He did not say whose name stood on the other side of the stoppage, or mention a crowd. He gave a page number and shut his mouth on it.

Then he sat, and unscrewed the lid of his inkwell, and laid his hands on either side of his clerk's tabbed folder, which had lain at his elbow the whole forty minutes and which he had never once opened.

---

The house's counsel turned her steel rule four times, and each turn did something to the room.

She had laid it on the table in front of her when she rose, flat, pointing at the platform. At the first turn, a quarter of the way round, the four district evaluators in the evaluation seat had stopped looking at nothing and begun to look at her. At the second, the front benches had put their pencils down. At the third, the hood had spoken for the only time that day. At the fourth the rule lay pointing back at the platform where it had started, and the gallery made the long low sound that galleries make when they have watched somebody do a hard thing well and may not clap.

Karis had slid a card along the table to Cael before the counsel stood. *NAMES. PREAMBLE. SHELF. TEXT.* Four words, one under the other, in her finding hand.

"She's going backward," Karis had breathed. "From the people to the paper. Tick them."

He ticked them.

The names came first, and they were why the evaluators had looked up. The counsel told the hood how much evaluating this practitioner had already had: the grant at Greyvane and its two renewals on paper, a season of bouts under a regional registrar's seal and the rating instrument the whole continent used, a tournament entered under a clause of its own charter, and three days of the Compact's own senior evaluator on the bluff with his instruments, after which he had gone home without writing one finding against the boy. More looking, she said, than most practitioners get in a working life; and all of it done by people who could see, sealed and minuted, across three years.

"By twenty-two officers," she said. "I'll name them."

She named them, at the pace at which a person reads names in a room where names matter, each with a grade and a house and a date. Gault's. Ilsev's; Ilsev did not look up. The Archmarshal's, in its place among the inspection's, and Cael thought he saw the stillness under the hood change at it, the way still water changes when something passes beneath. It took four minutes. In the silence he heard a cart's iron tyre grind over the cobbles in the street below the steps. The counsel turned the rule a quarter. Tick.

The preamble came second, and it was why the pencils went down.

"I'm going to read the seat the preamble to the integrity sections," the counsel said, laying her papers face down, "because I doubt anyone here has heard it aloud."

Ninety seconds, old and plain: the sections were for testing instruments. Frames and stations. Records and their keeping. Protocols and who held them. So that the registry might know which of its instruments it could trust. Nobody on the benches had ever heard it; Cael could see that in their faces. Then she gave them Karis's count, and he felt Karis go very still at his side. Ninety-one recorded uses. Eighty-four about things. Seven about persons, and every one of the seven about something a person had done. Not one in ninety-one about what a person was. A clause made to ask whether a frame could be trusted, the counsel said, could be made to ask the same of a man only by bending it until the words cracked.

That was where the hood spoke.

In a registrar's voice that might have been anybody's, it asked whether counsel's case was that the integrity sections could never lawfully reach a practitioner, or only that nobody had ever made them.

"Only that nobody ever has," said the counsel at once. "And that's enough. In all the years these sections have stood, this seat is asked to go first: to turn them on a person for what he is. A seat asked to go first deserves to hear it said in plain words. The petition never said it."

Jent was on his feet. "The petitioner has never called the use an ordinary one, and is content for the minute to record that it is new."

"The petitioner is content for the minute to record that it is new," said the counsel, and this once, the only time all morning, she said it over her shoulder to the benches. Then she turned back and gave the rule its third quarter turn. Tick.

The shelf came third and went quickly: the Greyvane ruling, and the four older rulings behind it, four houses that had gone to law against this clause with money and lawyers and lost, every one of them on the text. Tried by everybody who could afford to try it, she said, and it had held each time.

And last, the paper itself, the drafters' list. She took up the directive that had made the code standard and turned to the schedule at its back, where the men who drafted it had listed every older provision they meant to cancel, and she walked the hood down that list, item by item, slowly, because the whole case was in something that was not there. At the bottom she stopped. They had been careful men, she said. They had listed everything they meant to end. The assay clause was not on their list. And care, she said, only ever runs in one direction.

Twice while all this went on Karis passed a card forward to the counsel's elbow, and Cael, who had never once managed not to read upside down, read both. The first corrected a page number; the counsel used the right page in her next sentence without a hitch. The second said *Leave the fifth point. Don't need it.* The counsel turned it face down and left the fifth point.

She closed with the sentence she and Karis had built over a week.

"The petitioner would have the seat take one instrument's silence over the word of every officer who ever stood in a room with this practitioner and used their eyes. The seat should refuse. For three years the record has been the evaluation. Nothing in the record has moved since. Only the questioner has."

She turned the rule the last quarter. It lay pointing at the platform again, as it had when she began, and she sat.

---

Ilsev was standing.

The ruling had been read and the room had not yet worked out what it had heard, and in the gap Ilsev rose from the middle chair of the evaluation seat and asked leave to address it. She had leave. She did not open her files.

"I'd ask the seat," she said, "to state for the minute what the evaluation seat is here to do."

"To assess the conduct of any re-evaluation this seat orders," said the hood. "Its keeping to protocol. Not its result."

"Nor whether the standard protocol can read this practitioner at all."

"Nor that."

"Then I'd ask that those words be minuted exactly as the seat has said them," said Ilsev, "so that nobody can stretch this chair afterward to cover that question."

The clerk wrote it. Ilsev sat, and took up her new pencil, and wrote the same words into her own file in her own hand, and set the file back on her lap-desk at its old angle.

Cael understood her before he had finished understanding the ruling, because he had watched her do the same thing at Greyvane. A reader of texts was marking the edge of a text in ink, so that nobody could ever say afterward it had said more than it said. In her own dialect, which was the only one she spoke, she had let the whole floor know that her chair would vouch for the way a re-evaluation was run, and had been given no voice at all in whether there should be one. A re-evaluation, then. There was going to be one to conduct.

He went back to the ruling and made himself hear it in order.

The hood had read it from one leaf, written in the noon hour, in the same unhurried voice it had used for its one question.

*On the petition and the answer: the enrollment was made lawfully, and the seat so finds. The clause beneath it is in force; the standardizing directive did not reach it. The house's record is entire, and stands untouched. The house has observed its charter in every particular.*

Halfway through that the gallery had begun to move. Boards came up off knees. On the second bench a correspondent had half risen with his hat in his hand, meaning to be first through the doors to his runner. The hood had not changed its voice or its speed by a hair.

*On the question raised: the seat may take it up. How far the integrity sections reach a practitioner is for the registry to determine, and the seat determines that they reach this one far enough to be heard. The matter proceeds in full. Argument on the petition to compel the enrollee's re-evaluation under the standard Arbiter protocol is set down for the sittings that remain; the seat's ruling on it will be given before the academic year closes.*

The correspondent had sat back down with his hat on his knee.

Lawful, and going ahead. Both, in a single breath.

Cael made himself say it the coach way again, plainly, so that he would have it straight. What they had won, they had for good: no seat would ever again argue whether the clause was law or whether the house's paper was honest; that was settled under seal, in the minute, forever. What they had not won was the only thing that could still reach him. The sittings that were left would argue a single question, whether to stand him in a frame. And nobody in the room, not Jent, not Ilsev, not the four evaluators, not the hood, could pretend not to know what the frame would say.

Withrow bowed to the platform at the registry's depth when the hood rose, not a hair less. Jent did not smile, or gather his papers, or once look across the floor. He received what he had been sent to collect with the face of a man being told it would rain.

In the passage behind the selling floor, while the porters brought the house's cases down, Lira caught Cael by the sleeve. She had been on the attendance bench, and she had lost the thread twice.

"So we won?"

"Every point."

"And he's going in a frame."

"Probably."

She stood with that for a moment in the fleece smell of the old sample passage. "Then it's like a bout," she said slowly, "where the judges give you every exchange, and write down that you won every one, and then tell you the bout goes on anyway, under rules they'll pick later." She let go of his sleeve. "That's both, isn't it? That's what she wrote on the slip."

"I think so."

"I hate it," said Lira, with deep satisfaction at having understood it, and went to help Brom with the second case.

Through the far doorway Cael could see the records table. The officer with the careful face had stayed at it. He was squaring the minute-book to the table's edge with both hands, and then, when it was square, squaring it again.

---

Brom unfolded the slip at the first milestone past the town and held it up so that they could all read it.

One word, in Karis's hand.

*Both.*

Karis did not look at it. She was looking out of the window at the river.

For a while nobody said anything at all, and then everybody did at once, about the steps, and none of them agreed on the order.

Lira said the runners went first, four of them, gone down the market street in four directions before the gallery had finished coming out of the doors; she had counted them. Four towns by tonight. Cael thought of the printer in the wharf street setting the paragraph by lamplight on the old press, the one that did not smear, and finding he needed two headlines for it and had room for one. One sheet would say *VINDICATED*. The other would say *THE CASE GOES ON*. Neither would be false.

Karis said no, Jent came first; he was out of the doors before the runners and down the four steps at his ordinary pace with his clerk and his folder that had stayed shut, and he had stopped at the bottom beside the counsel and said, "You should have taken your fifth point. It was a good one."

"It was," the counsel said now, from her corner, without looking up from her minute. "I didn't need it."

"And he said," said Karis, "'That's why I'd have liked to hear it,' and put on his hat, and walked off up the street like a man going home to his dinner."

"He's the only one in twenty-six years who has ever stopped on the way down to mark my paper," said the counsel. "He gives it away because it's free to him. It's also accurate, which is the part I can't forgive." She wrote a line in her minute. Cael read it upside down: *Valid: held. To proceed: held. Both.* "He'll give more away next time. Giving things away is how that man walks forward."

The river went by. Lira kept one hand on the closed drill-book and never lifted its cover. Brom put the slip back into his right inside pocket and left his hand over it.

Then Karis spoke to the window.

"We won every point that can be won on paper."

Brom took the time he always took. "And?"

"And it's proceeding."

At the ferry the coach waited its turn on the landing. Cael looked out at the two grey coats who stood there as they always stood, changing on the bell. One of them was not looking at the coach's door, or at the faces in its windows. He was looking at the luggage rail on the roof, where the house's two document cases were strapped, and he was looking at them the way a clerk looks at a line he means to copy.

Nobody in the coach said anything about it. All of them had seen it.

Cael set the night's entry in his head while the ferry took them across. It came out at one line, and he did not try to make it longer.

*Part of me is still keeping score. The rest of me has started counting doors.*

Seln had not opened his eyes since the town. He did not open them now. When he spoke, it was to the whole coach, and not loudly.

"Now you understand the file I've been reading my whole career."

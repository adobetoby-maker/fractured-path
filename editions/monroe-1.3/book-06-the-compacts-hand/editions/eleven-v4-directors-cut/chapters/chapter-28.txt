Chapter 28 — Where the Mark Sat

[Patient, strategic narration; allow alliances, debts, and suspicion to change by degrees]

Bracken wound the counter clock every Seventh-day before the first bell, and nobody had ever been allowed to talk to him while he did it.

Seln knew that, so he waited.

The paper itself was a day gone. It had ridden down the bluff road in a saddlebag yesterday morning, after a minute and forty seconds at the lodge window in front of one man with a glass, and whatever it had to say about itself it had said then, to him, and to nobody since. Seln had worked that out at about the fire-watch's second round, sitting up in the wing with the senior clerk's fair copy under the lamp and getting nothing from it but the words, which was all a fair copy on the house's own stock could ever give.

So the question had to go to the man with the glass.

The records hall was dark except for the one lamp on the counter. Rain had not come yet, but it was in the air, and the tall windows along the east side showed nothing but grey. Bracken stood on the little step he kept under the counter for the purpose, with the clock's glass door open and the key in his hand, and turned it eleven times. He always turned it eleven times. Seln had counted it on perhaps eighty Seventh-days and had never once heard a twelfth. Then the registrar shut the glass, stepped down, put the step away with his foot, and looked up.

"Seln."

"Registrar." He had his hands in his coat pockets. He took them out. "One question, if you have a minute for it. It's about the paper at the gate."

Bracken's spectacles came down off his forehead onto his nose. He did not say anything, which was his way of saying yes.

"When you held the order up to the light in the lodge," said Seln, "where did the mark sit?"

He had chosen the words in the night, and he had chosen them so that there was only one way to answer them. Bracken was a man who answered the question that was asked. You did not have to tell him why you wanted something. You only had to ask it narrowly enough that the answer could not wander off.

Bracken took a moment. He did not look at anything while he took it. Then he put two fingers on the counter, side by side, as if the sheet were lying there under them.

"Low," he said. "A sixteenth of an inch, against our own stock. I laid the rule on it in the lodge window." He moved the fingers a fraction. "Everything that comes up the hill from the south mill carries the mark the same distance from the deckle. I've put a rule on perhaps four thousand sheets. That one sat a sixteenth low."

"Thank you."

"It's in my book," said Bracken. "Under the seal. Mark a sixteenth low against house stock. I didn't know what to make of it, so I wrote it down."

"That's the right thing to make of it."

Bracken looked at him over the spectacles for a little while. Seln let him look. Then the registrar nodded once, the small nod he gave to a form that had been filled in properly, and opened the day-book, and dipped his pen. He did not ask why. In two years he had never once asked Seln why about anything, and Seln had never once stopped being grateful for it.

He went back across the court to the wing in the half dark. The lodge-man's window was open again. The rain had got at the board beside it in the night, and somebody had already chalked ANYTHING SEALED in fresh.

A sixteenth of an inch was nothing. It was the width of a pencil line.

It was also a whole story, if you knew how paper was made. The mark in a sheet was a wire. Somebody had bent it into a shape and sewn it onto the face of the mould, and when the pulp was lifted on the mould the wire made the paper thinner there, and the thin place showed against the light. A mould lasted for years. A mark, once sewn, stayed where it was sewn. If the mark on a mill's registry stock had moved a sixteenth of an inch, then the wire had broken, or worn through, and somebody had cut it off and sewn it on again by hand. Nobody sews a wire twice in the same place.

So every sheet with the mark a sixteenth low had come off the mould after the mending. And the mould had been mended this year, or Bracken would have laid his rule on a low mark before.

A second run: sheets from the same mill and the same mould and the same pulp, for the same customer, only later.

He had sample leaves of the registry's stock pressed flat in the back of his second manual, one from every posting, because he copied things. He took them out when he reached the copying table, and held the newest to the window. The mark sat where it had always sat. He put the leaves away again.

He had the shape now. He did not have the answer.

There were four favours owed to him in the world, and he had never spent one.

He knew exactly where each of them was. A man in his trade kept that list the way a carter keeps his water barrels, full and lashed down and never once opened on the easy stretches of the road, because the one time you need water is the one time there is none to be had. A favour was not money. It could not be lent or broken into change. You spent it whole, once, and afterward the person who had owed it knew something about you that they had not known before: that you had wanted something enough to ask.

That was the price of a favour, and it was the reason he had kept four.

He went down them in his head, as he went down them perhaps once a season, to be sure they were still where he had left them. A man who kept a mould room at a paper mill in the south. A woman who kept ledgers in a store-house in the west. A ferry-master on a coast he would never see again, who owed him a crossing and would have given him a boat. And an old officer of his own service, retired to a garden, who owed him the largest thing of the four and would have paid it gladly and been ruined by paying it. That one he would never spend. He had known that since the day it was owed. Some debts are only worth keeping so that the other man can sleep.

Two, then, the mill and the ledger, because they were the only two who could answer the only two questions he had.

He sat at the copying table with the wing empty round him and the rain beginning at last against the windows, and he wrote two of them out.

The first went to a man who kept the mould room at the south mill. Eleven years ago Seln had filed one true sentence about him instead of a sentence that would have been more useful to somebody else, and the man had never known which office to thank, but he had known whom. The letter asked one thing. Was the registry mould mended this year, and if so, when, and was a run made off it after? It did not say who wanted to know. It did not have to.

The second went west.

There was a woman in the registry's western stores who had kept the issue ledgers for the paper that the whole half of the continent wrote its instruments on. Every lot that left her stores went into a ruled book against the office that drew it, with the date and the lot's number and the clerk's initial. She had owed him for nine years. He thought of her at her ledger sometimes, on bad nights, because it was a comforting thought: a person in a room somewhere keeping something perfectly. His letter to her asked one thing too. The south mill's second run this year: which lots were drawn, and by whom.

He sealed them with plain wax and no device. Then he put them in a clerk's satchel with three letters that were really the wing's, and buttoned his coat to the chin, and went down the hill.

It was the rest day, and the rain had decided to stay. The bluff road shone. At the second switchback he met a porter coming up with a sack over his head like a hood, who wished him good morning and did not look at him twice. That was the whole art of the trade. A man going down a hill in the rain with a satchel and an errand was not anybody.

At the foot of the road the two coats stood under the eaves of the chandler's warehouse, as close to the wall as they could get. They watched him come down. He let them. He had passed them perhaps two hundred times, and they had written him down perhaps two hundred times as the wing's assistant, with the post. He walked past them at the pace of a man who wanted to be dry and said, "Wet one," and the nearer one said, "It is," and that was all.

The drop was behind the counter of a ship's chandler near the bridge, where a woman sold lamp wicks and tarred twine and was not anything else that anybody could have proved. He bought a hank of twine he did not need. He put the two letters on the counter under the coins. She swept all of it into her apron without looking down.

"The quick bag," he said.

"It's Seventh-day."

"I know what day it is."

She looked at him then, for the first time in four years of twine. Then she nodded, and the letters were gone, and she was counting out his change.

The quick bag went faster than the post by a day each way, and it went to places the post did not. It would cost him a favour of its own, he supposed, one day. Everything did.

He stood a moment in the shop doorway with the twine in his hand, out of the rain, and watched the street. A dray going over the bridge with its load sheeted. Two boys with a dog. A woman under a shawl buying eels from a barrow. The bridge-ward in his oilskin at the near end of the parapet, looking down at the water against the old piers and then at the iron rings let into the stone of the middle one, the way a man looks at a clock he expects to be late. There were five rings on that pier, one above the other. The river was at the first.

Seln had lived in nineteen towns, and every one of them had a man like that, who looked at the water for a living and was right about it twice a year.

On the way back up he counted the coats again, and stood a moment at the second switchback in the rain with the twine in his pocket, and let them count him back. Two at the road's foot. Two across the water at the landing, where the ferry was still running, though it was running short, with the ferryman leaning on his pole at every crossing to hold her against a current that had not been there yesterday. The Ost was coming up.

He went in at the gate and did not look at the place in the court where the woman had stood. He had looked at it enough on the night of the gate. There was nothing in that place but flagstones, and the boy had drawn it all on the oak in chalk, and it had been washed off in the morning.

Two favours left.

On the First-day Karis read the year back to herself, one card at a time, and Cael sat at the end of the long table and listened to it.

The seat wanted the maker of the copies to swear to them, and the advocate of record to sign beside his oath. Karis had said at supper that she would not put her name to a page she had not read against the thing it was copied from, and that this was not caution but manners. So the wing's original cards had come up the library stair in two shallow boxes under the porter's oilcloth, with Seln's leave and Bracken's receipt, and the second set of Seln's copies lay open beside them. He had made two of everything. One had gone down to the seat in the house's boards. The other had stayed in the annex, against exactly this.

The assistant read the cards aloud. Karis followed on the copy with her finger.

"Fifth-day, the fourth week," said the girl. "Gault, the Ash, the Mire. Twenty-two, twenty-three, twenty-four. Figure twenty-three."

"Twenty-three," said Karis.

"Fifth-day, the fifth week. Twenty-one, twenty-three, twenty-two…"

It went on like that, and Cael had not expected it to be hard to listen to, and it was, because he knew every one of those afternoons. He had chosen each figure the night before it was earned, on a page at the back of Hesk's book, and spent the bout earning it to the decimal, and walked off the boards afterward exactly as tired as the rotation had said he would be. Hearing them read out in a row, the whole year so far, in the girl's careful voice, was like hearing somebody read your letters back to you. Every one of them was true. None of them was him.

Rain ran down the reading room's two windows. Below them the river had come up in the night. It was brown from bank to bank now, and moving fast in the middle, and there was a line of froth and sticks along the near side where it was pushing at the bottom of the river street.

"Fourth-day, the seventh week," said the girl, and stopped.

Karis's finger stopped too.

"It's different," said the girl. She held the card closer to the lamp. "The copy's got twenty-two and two-thirds. The card's got…" She tilted it. "The card's got it twice. There's twenty-three and a third, and a line through it, and twenty-two and two-thirds written over the top in another ink."

Karis took the card. She looked at it. Then she turned the copy toward the lamp and looked at the margin beside the line, and Cael saw her mouth change.

There was a note in the margin, so small that you would not have seen it unless you were looking for exactly that, in Seln's square hand. Figure corrected on the card in the desk clerk's hand, different ink. Corrected figure copied. Both seen.

"He found it first," said the girl.

"He found it first," Karis agreed. "Four nights ago, copying straight through the night after the road home. And he copied what the card says now, because that's what it says, and he wrote down that it used to say something else, because it did." She set the card back in its box very gently. "Write it on your slip. Seventh week: corrected on the original; the maker noted it. If the seat asks, we show them both, and his margin."

The girl wrote. Then she looked up at Karis with ink on two fingers.

"Is that a mistake?"

"No. It's a correction. A mistake is when nobody wrote it down." Karis turned back to the copy. "Go on. You found it. That's the whole job."

The girl went on. Fifth-day, the ninth week. Cael watched her, and then watched Karis, who had not once looked up at him. She did not need to; the year was in her hands, and she was reading it straight.

He thought about Seln at the copying table in the wing four nights ago, with the rain on the roof, finding a line through a figure on a card and copying the figure and the line both, and saying nothing to anybody, because it was not his to say anything about. Writing it down instead, small, where it would be found by the one person who was going to look.

He thought that Seln had probably been doing that for his whole life.

The notice for the next sitting was pinned to the frame of Cael's window, where he would see it every time he looked up from the sill.

The seat will sit on the Fifth-day following to receive attestation of the records produced. The enrollee of record will attend in person. The maker of the copies will attend.

He had copied it out of Karis's notebook on the night of the fourth sitting, because the seat's notice had gone into the house's book and he wanted it somewhere he did not have to ask for. In person. Every other sitting of the year had been content to have him on its door log. This one wanted his face.

It had been raining for three days. It came against the glass in long soft gusts and ran down it, and beyond the glass the court was all grey water, and the fountain was a noise in the middle of it.

Lira was on his bed with her boots off and her back against the wall and her feet crossed at the ankle. She had come in without knocking, as she always did, and sat down without asking, as she always did. Brom had the chair, which was too small for him, and it had complained when he sat in it, and he had sat in it anyway with great care, the way he did everything to furniture. Karis stood. She had a sheaf of the attestation papers under her arm and had not let go of them since supper.

Lira rolled her left shoulder. She did it slowly, forward and back, with her face turned a little toward the window, the way a person listens for a sound in a house. The rope at the confluence had laid a bruise across both her shoulder blades as wide as a hand, and he had watched it go from dark to yellow at the breakfast table over a week, a little more each morning, the way you watch a river go down.

"Clean," she said. "Both. Rooke had me on the frames this morning, and nothing talked."

"Good," said Brom.

"I'm only telling you because you were going to ask." She looked at Cael. "You were going to ask. You've been looking at my back for a week like it was a bill."

He had been, and he had not known she knew.

"So," said Lira. "What's this?"

He told them.

He had thought about how to say it all day, and in the end he said it plainly, because there was no way to say it that would make it smaller. On the night of the gate Lira had written her question in capitals in her notebook and left it lying open in the middle of the table, what do they try next, when paper won't do it, and nobody had answered it. He did not have an answer now either. But there was one thing he could do about the question before it was answered, and that was to price it.

"If it's her," he said. "If it's anybody like her. If somebody begins a fight with me, in earnest, the way she would begin one. I want it priced before, not after. I want the whole leaf written tonight."

Karis's arm came down slowly, and the attestation papers with it.

"You mean the reach," she said.

"I mean the reach."

The rain went on against the glass.

The covenant was at the front of Hesk's book. He did not need to turn to it to know what it said, but he turned to it anyway and laid the book open on the sill where they could all see it.

Three lines, in his best hand, from a long table at Halcenvane, written well over a year ago. Nothing made to happen. Only inside a fight somebody else begins. The cost on paper first. And under them, in Karis's wording from the last night at Norhold, with four sets of initials after it: Never again in the middle of a fight unless what follows has been priced first, at the weight it will come at.

"At the weight it will come at," said Karis. "We don't know the weight."

"Seln said the best tool somebody owns."

"Seln said that about where she stood. It's not a tier."

"No." He had thought about that, too. "But it's a guess, and it's somebody's guess who knows. Somebody keeping her for the hard ones isn't keeping an Iron. And if it's Gold, I'm wrong, and the leaf says so, and I'll know I was wrong before I start." He took the pen. "Silver. Call it Silver. If anybody's got a better guess, I'll write that."

Nobody had.

He wrote the heading. Priced before. If it is her, or anyone like her, in a fight somebody else begins. Then he stopped, and looked at what he had written, and put the pen down.

"Say it," said Lira.

"It's wanting it. If I write the price down tonight, I'm saying I'd pay it. That's wanting it. On the stair I didn't want anything. At Norhold I didn't know I wanted it until it was in front of me. This is the first time I've ever sat down at a table and wanted one on purpose, a week ahead, in ink."

"Yes," said Karis.

That was all she said, and he looked up at her. She was standing with the papers against her chest, and she did not look frightened, and she did not look as if she were about to argue him out of it.

"The rule says the cost on paper first," she said. "It doesn't say you have to not want it. It says you have to know what you want before you're standing in the middle of it, so it can't decide for you." She came to the sill and put one finger on the page beside the covenant's second line. "That's what this one's for. You don't get to want it until she begins. That's the whole difference."

"And if nobody begins?"

"Then it's a page," said Brom. "We've got lots of pages."

So he wrote it.

Karis gave him the first figure. She had kept a column for the storm since Norhold, the days and weeks and what had leaned in him and when, and she turned to it in her notebook and laid her palm across it while she thought. Eleven weeks at Gold for the flag to clear. The first month the worst. A Silver ought to weigh less, she said; nobody could say how much less, because there was only the one point on her curve that was any good, and one point was not a curve. She drew one anyway, lightly, on the back of an attestation leaf, through the storm and through the ones before it. Each of the earlier ones had its bill written in the old volume, a day or two of quiet in some room while the rest of him moved over to make space, and she knew those bills better than he did, because she had copied them. They were small points, low on the paper, close together. The storm stood up at the far side of the leaf by itself, like a tree in a field, and her pencil went from the little ones up to it in a long uneasy curve that she did not like and said so.

"Nine weeks," she said. "If I had to put a number on it. Something less than a flag, for about nine weeks. I don't believe it. Write it down."

He wrote about nine weeks, Karis's curve, not believed.

Then the first minute.

"At Norhold the wrong thing answered," he said. "In the middle of the floor. I called the Wind and something else came, and I went down. If I reach in the middle of a fight, that minute's going to happen again, or something like it. I won't be standing up for it. I may not know which way is down."

"Then somebody has to be standing over you for that minute," said Brom.

"Yes."

"Then that's me." He said it without moving in the chair, in the voice he used to tell the kitchen how many loaves. "Write it. Brom, for the minute."

Cael did not write it at once. He was thinking of the north rail at Norhold, and five people holding to Rooke's hand over the cap as if it were a rope, and Brom with his left shoulder low beside Lira, not going.

"There was a rule at Norhold," he said. "Nobody over the cap."

"There was." Brom looked at the window, where the rain was. "I've thought about that rule more than you'd credit. It was a good rule. It kept a hall full of people off a floor where a Gold was working. I'd keep it again tomorrow, in that hall." He brought his eyes back. "There won't be a cap. Wherever this is, if it's anywhere, there won't be a rail or a referee or a man with his arms crossed over his head. There'll be you on the ground for a minute, and whoever's standing nearest. I'm asking to be the one standing nearest. Write it."

He wrote it.

The rest went down quickly after that, because it was the kind of thing he had written many times before. A week at least without the band, so wing three to be excused on some true ground. The lane not to be laid, and the read not to be trusted, until whatever came had finished coming. Somebody in the room with him every hour of the first two days, because at Norhold the first two days had been the bad ones. Rooke to be told nothing but what was true and small.

Then he came to the part he could not price, and he did what Seln had told him on the oak. He left it blank where he would see it. He wrote three headings down the margin, Her reach. Her rate. What it costs her., and drew a line after each, and put nothing on any of the lines.

Lira took the pen out of his hand.

She did not ask. She leaned off the bed and took it, and pulled the book toward her across the sill, and wrote one more line under his in her large capitals, pressing hard, the way she always did.

NOBODY GOES IN AFTER HIM ALONE.

She gave him the pen back.

He initialled under it. Karis initialled with her small exact letters, and Brom made his mark, which was a B with a bar through it like a gate. Lira put hers last, in capitals.

Then they sat there in the lamplight with the rain running down the window and the notice pinned to the frame, and nobody said goodnight for a long time.

Priced on the Second-day, before the sitting, with four initials. Three days of rain and the river over the bottom step of the river street. Lira's shoulder: clean, both sides. She saw me counting it.

Gault carried his own notes down the hill.

He would not let the house's escort have them, or Bracken, or the coach's boot. He rode inside, on the eighth sitting's morning, with the bound volume on his knees in the house's boards and both hands laid on it, and the case with the handle set very straight on the seat beside him, as if the instruments in it might be wanted to swear to something. He was a spare, exact man with a magister's grey at his temples, and Cael had sat across a trestle from him every week for two years and had never before seen him out of a room he was in charge of. He looked at the wet streets of Ostrand going by with a kind of mild disapproval, as a man looks at a column somebody else has added.

"I have been an evaluator of record eleven times," he said, to the coach in general, as they crossed the bridge. "I have never before been asked for the notes. The figures, yes. The minute, always. Not the notes." He considered it. "I find I do not mind. I should mind very much if I had written them differently for being read."

"Did you?" said Karis.

"I did not know they would be. That is the use of not knowing."

The young officer at the side door wrote him in the log under standing as evaluator of record to the respondent's enrollment, and Gault leaned over the board and read it upside down, and nodded.

The long room was a little fuller than it had been, for somebody had heard that an officer would be called. Lira and Brom were on the fourth bench, where the house sat when it came to watch, and Lira had her right foot tucked under the left, out of nothing but habit now. Up on the platform the hood was in its place, with Ilsev and her four below it; at the records table sat the senior chair and the junior, who did not look up.

"In the matter of Caelen Hesk-ward," said the seat's clerk. "Upon the merits. The eighth sitting. Production, under the seat's order of the sixth."

The house's counsel rose, and said that the house produced, and sat, which was all she was needed for.

"The evaluator of record," said the clerk.

Gault went up to the clerk's table with the volume, and laid it down.

"Are these your notes upon the enrollee for the current academic year?"

"They are."

"Made by you, at the time?"

"Each before I left the room in which the thing was observed. Where I could not write at once, I have said so in the margin, and by how long."

"Entire?"

"Entire." Gault's hand was still on the board. "I was asked for the notes upon the enrollee. I have taken that to mean every note in which he appears, and not only the notes of his bouts. There are seventeen panel notes, one to a supervised session, with the three cards and the figure. There are also five notes that are not of any panel." He lifted the hand. "I mention it so that the seat is not surprised by the leaf for the year's seventy-sixth day."

Something moved under the hood. It was the smallest alteration, a stillness becoming a different stillness, and Cael, who had watched that grey shape for eight sittings, felt it in the back of his neck.

The seventy-sixth day. Four riders at the gate, and an order read aloud under the arch.

"The clerk will read the contents leaf," said the hood.

The clerk read it: seventeen sessions by date, each with its floor and its figure, and then the five. A note on the enrollee's excusal for injury, with the healer's line; two notes on the visitors' gallery; a note on the production of the wing's records to this seat. And the fifth, which the clerk read out in the same voice as the others, because it was written in the same hand.

"Seventy-sixth day. An order was presented at the house's gate, by four riders, in the name of an office styled facilities, for the enrollee's attendance upon an assessment. No assessment of this enrollee has been notified to the evaluator of record, by any office, at any time. I have written that down."

The long room was very quiet.

Jent had risen, and stood with his hands at his sides and his spectacles hanging, and for the first time in eight sittings Cael thought he saw the man choose his words.

"The petitioner has no knowledge of the matter in that note," said Jent. "I say so for the minute, and I say it carefully. It is no part of the petition. No instrument of the kind described was sought by the petitioner or issued at his instance." He paused. "As to the rest of the volume: the petitioner accepts it, as he said he would. He relies on none of it."

"Received," said the hood. "Entire. And entered."

The clerk wrote, and at the records table the junior chair drew the volume toward him and opened his receipt book. In the middle chair below the platform, Ilsev had turned a leaf in her file and was writing something small at its foot, and she did not look at the house's table, and she did not look at the hood.

A magister had just put into a registry seat's own minute, under its own order, in a clerk's level voice, that somebody had tried to take the respondent off his hill by a paper nobody would own. It had been received, and it would lie in the registry's presses beside the table of renewals for as long as there was a registry.

And nothing whatever would come of it; he knew that as surely as he knew the lot number under his boot. Nobody on that platform would ask whose office it was, because the district seat had already answered in two sentences that the office was duly constituted and the house was referred elsewhere. The note would be true, and entered, and it would change the ruling by not one word.

He looked along the house's table at Gault, who had come back and sat down and set his case straight. The magister's face was quite calm. He had been asked for his notes, and he had given them, all of them, the inconvenient with the rest. He had not expected it to do anything; it had simply not occurred to him to leave a leaf out.

"The second supplemental submission of the respondent," said the clerk, "upon the recorded invocations of the integrity provisions. Nineteen leaves and a table. Received and entered."

Karis ticked her card, and the counsel turned her rule a quarter turn.

On the steps afterward Gault stood a moment in the thin rain with the empty boards under his arm and looked up at the green cupola.

"They asked me whether my figures were mine," he said. "In front of forty benches."

"What did you make of it, Magister?" said Lira.

"I have written that down," said Gault, and went to find the coach.

He found Karis on the floor.

It was some days after the eighth sitting, on an afternoon of hard bright cold, and he had come up the four flights with a question about the ninth that he forgot at the door. The reading room's table stood bare to its cloth, with the chairs pushed in, and the lamp in the window was not lit, because it was day. And under the window, on the boards, with her back against the wall and her knees up and a square of planed deal across them, sat Karis, writing in pencil.

She had a lantern beside her, though the sun was on her hair, and three bundles in front of her on the floor, each wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord, and a fourth open on its cloth with its leaves in a stack and a single sheet lying on top.

"Don't step on the cord," she said.

He did not step on the cord, and sat down on the floor opposite her, since it seemed to be where people were sitting.

"What are you doing?"

"Finding out what I need a table for." She finished her line and blew the pencil dust off it. "I've had one since I was eleven. I've never asked what it does. So I've taken it away, to see what stops working." She held up her left hand and turned down fingers. "Ink stops working. You can't keep a well level on your knees, and I've spoiled a cuff finding that out, so it's pencil, and a knife for the pencil. The lamp stops working, because a lamp wants a flat place and a lantern only wants a hook. Laying six things side by side stops working. I can lay two." She looked at the bundles. "And the shelf stops working. That's the one I didn't expect."

"The shelf."

"I know where everything is in this room because of where it sits. Third shelf, grey box, behind the tab. That isn't knowing where it is. That's the shelf knowing." She tapped the single sheet on top of the open bundle. "So every bundle carries its own contents leaf now, on top, under the first fold of the cloth. What's in it, in order, with a line for each. If somebody handed me one of these in the dark in a field, I could find a leaf in it by counting."

She said in a field quite lightly, as she might have said in a cellar or on the moon.

"How long?" said Cael.

"To find a leaf? In the annex, off the shelf, eleven seconds. I had the assistant time me." She pulled a face. "Out of oilcloth, on my knees, the first day: four minutes and some. And I tore a corner. Yesterday, fifty seconds. I want it under thirty, and I want it under thirty with cold hands, so I've had the window open since noon."

He had wondered why the room was so cold.

He looked at the four bundles. They were the Greyvane notebooks, he thought, or part of them, and some of the year's papers from Norhold; he knew the grey of the boards showing at one bundle's edge. The oilcloth was new, and the cord was the brown waxed kind the post room used. She had tied each one with the same knot, a flat one that lay under the thumb, and he saw that she had tied and untied them often enough for the cord to have taken the shape.

The case stood on its shelf between the two grey boxes, where it had stood since the day it was opened. She had not wrapped that, and he did not ask.

"It's a habit," said Karis. She had followed his eyes, and she answered a different question from the one he had not asked. "A habit's a thing you build before you need it. That's all this is. I'd be a poor sort of archivist if I could only keep an archive in the one room where somebody had given me a key." She bent over the deal board again. "Ask me what you came up to ask me."

"I've forgotten it."

"Then hold this cord."

He held the cord while she folded the oilcloth over the fourth bundle, corner and corner and the long flap last, and then she took the cord from him and tied her knot, and looked at Hesk's clock, which he had not noticed she had borrowed from his sill.

"Forty-four," she said. "It loses, doesn't it? Call it forty-six."

The assistant found out the next morning, and Cael was there for that too, because Karis had asked him to carry the code's second volume across the landing.

The girl was at her table in the old lumber room, where she sat every morning from the first bell, with ink on her collar and yesterday's digest in front of her. She was a second-year, and she had come to that table at the start of the year unable to find a section without being told its number, and she could now find one from a struck heading in an index a century old, and Cael did not think she had noticed the difference.

Karis laid a sheet on the table in front of her.

It was the girl's own work, a morning's abstract of some old ruling, a page and a half in a round careful hand. She looked at it as she looked at one every morning: quickly, all over, for the pencil mark. Every morning since the year began there had been one, never more than one, a small tick in the margin beside the line that was wrong, and the two words find it. It was the rule of the room: one mistake handed back, so that she would have to go and hunt the rest herself.

She turned the sheet over. She turned it back.

"There isn't one," she said.

"No," said Karis.

"You've forgotten to mark it."

"I haven't."

"Then you've—" The girl stopped. She looked at the page again, properly this time, a line at a time, with her lips pressed together. "Did you check it?"

"Twice. Then I was cross, and checked it a third time against the roll, in case you'd been clever." Karis had her hands behind her back. "You hadn't been clever. You'd been right. All the way down."

The assistant went red from her inky collar to her hair, and did not seem to know what to do with her hands, and in the end she put them flat on the sheet, one on each side, as if it might be taken away.

"It won't happen every morning," said Karis. "I should say that. Tomorrow you'll cite a section from memory and get its number wrong, and I shall mark it, and you'll be furious. But it's happened once, and a thing that's happened once has been shown to be possible." She brought her hands out from behind her. In one of them was a folded note, and in the other a clean sheet of the house's good paper. "Now. This."

She unfolded the note, which was in her own small hand, and short.

"That's what I wrote, at the start of the year, on the heading you found. The hearing provision. Struck in the index, never repealed. Six lines, for the counsel, in a hurry." She put it down beside the clean sheet. "I want it written properly. Where the heading stands, who struck it and how you know, the two paragraphs, every recorded use. A proper note, the kind the reading room at Greyvane would keep. And I want it under your name."

"Mine?"

"You found it. You found it by being wrong about where to look, which is how nearly everything worth having gets found. I only used it." Karis tapped the good paper. "When it's done, it goes down to the registrar, and he enters it on your file with the house's stamp: a note upon a heading of the code, by an enrollee of the second year. It'll sit there under your name whatever becomes of anybody else's." She said that last part quickly. "The seat has heard that provision argued twice this year. It ought to be on record somewhere who turned it up."

The girl was looking at her.

"Why would anything become of anybody else's?" she said.

It was a good question, and she had been taught to ask them, one a morning, for most of a year.

"Files change," said Karis. "It's what they're for." She took the code's second volume out of Cael's arms and set it on the corner of the table. "You'll want that. The cross-heading is in the back. Find it."

She went back across the landing to her own room, and Cael followed her, and at the door he looked round. The assistant had not opened the volume yet; she was sitting with both hands still on the sheet that had no mark on it, reading it again.

In the reading room Karis stood for a moment with her back to him and her pale fingers pressed against the edge of the bare table.

"That's nearly everything she needs," she said. "The index, the digests, how to be wrong usefully. One more month and she won't want me for anything but arguments." She let go of the table. "I thought I'd mind that more than I do."

On the Fourth-day of the tenth sitting's week he fought in wing three for the last time that year.

He had known it was the last since the First-day, when the floor sheet came across the counter with a line on it that he had not seen before in two years of floor sheets. Supervised hours under the provision, the academic year: complete upon this session. It was in the desk clerk's hand, with the wing's stamp. The provision asked for a number of hours signed by the evaluator of record, and he had been paying them down every week since the year opened, a bout at a time. This one closed the account.

He read the rotation's line at his chalk, as he had read one before every bout since the mill town.

Fourth-day, wing three, the Mire. Take the first late. Let him have the third. Three on boards; the fourth kept. Buys: a figure a point and a half under last week's. Spends: a knee, probably.

He put the book away.

The Mire was a third-year, Iron Rank Five, a heavy quiet young man with a slow blink, and he stood at the north chalk with his feet planted wide. A Mire worked on the floor. He did not touch you with it; he only made it feel deeper than it was. Where he looked, the boards went soft underfoot, so that you sank to the ankle in honest pine and your next step came up slow, and while you were dragging it out he walked across and put his hand on you.

Gault turned the glass.

"Exchange," said the desk clerk.

The floor went soft under Cael's left foot at once. It was a very strange feeling, and a good piece of work: his boot told him it had gone into a hand's depth of something like wet clay.

His read told him otherwise.

Brom's gift did not care what a foot felt. It read weight and what bore it, and it came back from the boards under him as flat and dry and sound as it had been every Fifth-day for a year. There was no depth. There was a pine plank, an inch and a quarter thick, on a joist, and the clay was in his ankle and nowhere else.

He could have walked straight across it, and he knew that in the first heartbeat, as he had known what Lira's count would do before she spent it. All he had to do was believe the read and not the foot, and step as if the floor were a floor, and he would be standing at the Mire's shoulder while the Mire was still waiting for him to sink.

The rotation said late.

So he sank. He dragged the foot out as a man drags it out of clay, and went wide round the soft place, and was slow doing it. The Mire came across in four unhurried strides, and Cael got his forearm up in time, with the give shut, and took the palm on it and felt it go up into his shoulder like any honest Iron's.

No touch. They parted.

He spent a burst in the second, small and low, and took the touch from the side. He gave the third, as written: he let his right foot go down into a depth that was not there, and wrenched the knee a little coming out, for the look of it, and the Mire's hand was on his chest. In the fourth he found the way round, as late as he could decently leave it, and touched, and it was over.

"Two touches to one," said Gault. "Provision."

The three cards went along the trestle. The desk clerk added them, with no strike, and wrote. "Twenty-three and two-thirds."

A point and a half under last week's, and the knee ached, as he had priced it.

He stood on the pine boards in the bars of light from the high windows and waited for what he usually felt at the end of a band bout, the small sour tiredness of having done a difficult thing badly on purpose. It was there. But there was something else on top of it today, and it took him a moment to give it its name.

He was bored.

It was not quite that. He had been early; that was Lira's word for it, and she had been thinking about it for three weeks, and so had he. For the whole of four exchanges he had known what the Mire would do a half beat before the Mire did it, and had then stood and waited that half beat out, every time, so that it would look as if he had found it when a good Iron would have found it. It was a strange sort of work, for nobody had been in any danger. He had only been waiting, for a year, inside every bout.

There was a ruling coming, on a day he could count to, and after it either there was no more need of a band, or there was a door.

He looked toward the east end of the room.

Ephram was at the rail under the windows.

He had the three sheets out, unfolded, laid on the top of the rail with his forearm across them against the draught, and a pencil in his hand. He was not looking at Cael; he was writing the figure in at the foot of the third sheet, with the date and the floor and the three cards. He set the dot beside it on the scale in the margin, and then he laid his rule across the page and drew a line under the entry, a single firm line, from edge to edge.

He had never drawn a line under one before.

He counted down the three pages with the pencil's point, not moving his lips, and looked at the red mark ruled across the top of all three, and at the two gaps, one under the other, from the weeks of the ribs.

Then he turned the third sheet over and wrote something on the back of it.

It was short. Cael could see that much from forty feet, from the way the pencil moved: four or five words, and then, after a pause in which Ephram looked out of the high window at nothing, two or three more underneath. He did not hide it; he simply wrote it facing the wall. When he had done he read it over once, and his face, which was a good plain open face that had never been much use for keeping things in, did something Cael had not seen it do before. It went still. It was the face of a man who has been adding a long column for two years and has reached the foot of it and written the sum.

He folded the sheets in four along their soft old creases and put them in the captain's file, and tied the green tape.

Cael walked the length of the room to him, since there did not seem to be anything else to do.

"That's the year," said Ephram.

"That's the year."

"Ninety-eight, and two holes." Ephram tucked the file under his arm. "And not one of them over the line. I ruled that line in the first month I knew you. I thought I'd have to move it." He looked at Cael then, level, the captain's look, the one he gave a first-year who had told him half of something. It went on a moment longer than was comfortable. "I'm not asking," he said. "I told you on your birthday I wasn't, and I'm not."

"I know."

"Good." He nodded at the pine boards, the trestle, Gault closing his case. "The Mire's a decent fighter. He'll think he gave you trouble." He started for the door, and stopped with his hand on it, and did not turn round. "He didn't, did he."

It was not a question, and he did not wait for it to be answered.

Cael stood at the rail where the sheets had lain, and the wood was still a little warm from Ephram's forearm. Whatever had been written on the back of the third page was in a green-taped file going down the wing's corridor.

The request came to the Archmarshal's desk on a morning of sun, in the ordinary bag, and his clerk laid it in the tray on the right with the day's other paper.

It was the third thing from the top, and he came to it in its turn.

A single leaf of registry stock, under the seat's seal at Ostrand, the river and the scale. He knew the form. He had answered it, in one shape or another, perhaps two hundred times in forty years, and it had never once before taken him more than the time it takes to dip a pen.

In the matter of Caelen Hesk-ward. The evaluation seat constituted for these proceedings, having first given notice in the year's eighth week that certification would be required, now renews its request to the Archmarshal's office for certification of the standard protocol, as applied to a re-evaluation of the enrollee of record at Halcenvane, should such be ordered. The seat's closing sittings are set down for the year's last month. The office's certificate is requested before the first of them.

Below that the form left a space, ruled with a single line, for his answer.

He read the leaf through, as he read everything, from its head to its seal.

The first notice had come in the year's eighth week and he had acknowledged it by return, in one line, as he acknowledged every notice. He remembered writing the line, and he had meant, then, to sign the certificate when it was asked for. The protocol was sound. He had helped to write two of its sections as a young man, and had defended the whole of it before three convenings, and in all his service he had never refused his name to a thing he knew to be sound.

The protocol was sound still; he could find nothing wrong with any sentence of it.

Down in the paved court the porter was feeding the pigeons from his paper bag, as he did at this hour, and the birds had come to him in their usual half-circle. The window on the Archmarshal's left stood open a hand's breadth, and his left hand, which did not like mornings, lay on the blotter.

He did not open the lowest drawer. There was no finding to consult and no minute to draft, and he had known since a borrowed room in Ostrand exactly what he would write when this leaf came. He had said it aloud there, to five people, so that he would not be able to pretend afterward that he had not.

Now that it had come, he did not need the saying; he would have written the same if nobody had ever heard him.

He dipped the pen.

He wrote on the ruled line, in the upright hand he kept for instruments, with no heading and no reason.

The Archmarshal declines to certify.

He looked at it for as long as the ink took to lose its shine. Five words. It gave no ground and cited no section and accused nobody of anything. The form had asked a question, and that was the answer to it, and there was no line on the leaf for why.

He knew what would be done with it. The code let a seat proceed upon its own authority where a certificate was withheld; he had cited that section himself, once, long ago, when a guild hall's assessor would not sign. His line would stop nothing. The sittings would sit, and the ruling would be read, and somewhere in a minute a clerk would enter that a thing usually present was not there. That was the whole of what he could do, and he had weighed it on a post road for four days and found that it was still worth doing.

He signed beneath, his name and his office, as he had signed something over nine thousand findings, and he sanded it and shook the sand back into its box.

Then he laid the leaf in the tray marked done.

His clerk came for the tray at the usual hour. He took up the day's finished paper against his chest, and his eyes went down the top leaf once, as a good clerk's eyes do, and stopped for the space of a breath, and went on.

"For the noon dispatch, sir?"

"With the rest," said the Archmarshal.

The clerk waited a moment, as though there might be a covering note, or a word. There was not. He went down the too-steep stair with the tray, and the Archmarshal heard him go, every step, to the bottom.

He drew the next thing toward him from the tray on the right, a guild hall's query about a frame. He read it through from its head to its seal, and dipped his pen, and answered it.

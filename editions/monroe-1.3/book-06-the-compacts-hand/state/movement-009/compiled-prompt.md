## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that

- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,

# Chapter 54 — Good Room

"Exchange," said Rooke, and his hand came down for the third.

Ephram did not walk this time. He came across the oak like a man who has been shown at last what he is fighting, and means to have as much of it as there is, with the sun behind him now and the lath a dark bar against it.

Cael did not go to meet him. He stood on the north chalk, with his hip aching from the crest to the knee and seven bursts gone out of a day that gave six, and he went down the rule as he had gone down it on his knees in a chalk road.

*The conditions are not to be made.* He had not made them. A man had written four words on the back of a chart and kept them until a seat was done.

*Only inside a fight the other begins, in earnest, awake, and on purpose.* Ephram had begun it in a reading room at midnight, with five people listening, and he had grounded his lath in front of twelve hundred rather than be given less than all of it. Nobody had ever been more in earnest who did not also wish him harm.

*The whole cost on paper first.* It was on a leaf in Hesk's book, in the satchel on the east tier at Brom's feet, with capitals across the foot of it.

He did it with his eyes open.

The first cut came, heel and hip and edge, high, from the sun. He did not slip it. He put up his left forearm, as a man puts up a guard, and opened the give, and let the edge land where he could read it.

"Guard," said Rooke.

It went through the give as it had gone through before, across the two bruises that were there, and for an instant the yard went white at its edges. He had been ready this time, and he held himself open in front of it, all of him, as he had held still in a cage. It was a line; that was the first thing. A Blade did not strike with a piece of ash. He said where an edge was, a hand's breadth out beyond the wood, and it was there because he had said so, and it was as thin as a thought.

"You're letting me," said Ephram, between his teeth. He had checked, and stood a pace off with the lath raised and the heel not yet down, breathing hard, and he looked angrier than he had looked all afternoon. "I said I wanted all of it."

"I'm not letting you." It was the truth, and it was as much of the truth as there was to give him. "I'm looking at it. I've watched that edge from a tier for three years and I've never once been under it. Let me look."

Ephram stared at him. Then something went out of his shoulders, and what came into his face instead was not softer, but it was pleased.

"Then look," he said, and put the heel down.

He cut in earnest. Cael saw the whole engine of it, close, in the moment before it reached him: the heel that committed the body, and the hip that carried it, and the edge that could not then be called back by the man who had sent it. And he saw the other thing, which he had never understood from the rim. The line did not stop at the edge. It ran on, out past the lath's tip, straight as a rule, and Ephram *knew* along it; whatever was going to cross that line, he had word of it a breath before it came. That was how a lath had been lying at the end of a lane.

Cael took it on the forearm, and kept everything he had turned toward it.

"Guard," said Rooke, a long way off.

They came together.

It was quiet. The storm had come down on him like a roof, and the road had gone in like a key into a lock that had been cut for it, and this was smaller than either. It was like watching Hesk lay a steel rule along a line he had already scribed and find that it lay true.

And for the length of one breath he could see edges.

He could see every edge in the Crown yard. The coping of the rim wall stood out all round him like a drawn wire, and the joints of the oak, sixty feet of them, each a line with two sides. The risers of the tiers went up the sky in ruled steps, and the black boards had their corners. In front of him was the lath, with its true edge standing clear of the wood, and from its tip a thin bright line ran out through the air and ended on the third button of his own shirt. He knew where that line would be crossed, and by what, and it was the plainest thing he had ever known.

He did not pick it up.

It was there, new and whole, like a tool laid on the wrong side of the bench, and he left it lying, because it was not for this floor. A man had asked to be shown what he was fighting, and he was owed the fight with what Cael had carried down the steps, and with nothing that had been his own a breath ago. Cael shut it like a drawer, and the edges went out of the yard one by one, the coping last.

He had only the old read then, and it was enough, because he had stopped waiting.

Ephram's heel went down for another cut out of the last, and it was the short one, with no hip in it. Cael had it a half beat early, as he had had it in the first exchange, and this time he went.

Eight. It was low, and inside, under the lath, along the line of Ephram's own arm, with the hip shrieking and the edge passing over his back. He came up off the landing beat with his right hand out, and laid two fingers on the inside of Ephram's wrist, on the tape, and gave it the spark.

It went out of him like a word, and the flint smell came up between them. Ephram yelped, and the lath jumped in his hand, and then he was laughing again, helplessly, shaking his wrist.

"Touch," said Rooke. "The enrollee."

One each.

Ephram stood at the far chalk, soaked and grinning, with a red mark on his wrist.

He had learned. Cael could see it in how the man set his feet. He had learned in three exchanges what a rule of the house had kept from him for three years: that a lane was a line, and could be read; that the push took a heel, if the heel went down slowly; and that the boy in front of him was not quick, but early, so that no chain of cuts would close on a man who knew each link before it was forged.

So he would not chain. Cael read it in his weight before Rooke's hand had moved. Ephram was going to do the simplest thing a Blade could do: he would wait for a moment when there was only one place Cael could be, and then put everything he had into a single cut at that place, shoulders and all, the plain entry from the first year.

"Exchange."

He walked. It was the old walk, at every angle, with the lath low, but he was not walking to learn weight now. He was laying lines. He turned his wrist and there was one across the middle of the oak, level, a bar across a doorway, and he turned it again and there was a second, slantwise, from the rim wall to the first. He could not hold more than two at once; Cael saw that, and saw that he did not need to. He was building a corner out of two lines and four feet of sunk stone wall.

Cael went back before it. Nine. A burst along the wall, and the hip gave him nothing in return but pain. Ephram turned with him, unhurried, and the lines turned too, and he spent a tenth to stay ahead of them. He had never gone past nine on any floor.

He put the push into the oak under the walking heel, and Ephram stepped over the place as a man steps over a puddle. It took the shoulder four to learn that he had seen it coming. He tried it a fifth time, at the load, at the edge of his reach, and the boards jumped a foot to the left of a boot that was no longer there.

Then the wall was at his back, with a flight of steps on his right hand, and a line lay on each side of him. Ephram stood ten feet off, and his heel was up. There was one place left, and both of them knew where it was.

Cael asked the air to lean.

He laid it out of the corner, down the only gap, fifteen feet along the foot of the wall. The dust woke in the joints and ran, and his ears closed and opened. It was a line a blind man could have read, and Ephram read it.

His heel went down. It went down hard, for the end of the lane, with the hip coming after it and the shoulders dropped and all of him behind the edge. It was the plain entry, the whole weight at once; he had seen a lane once, and he knew to the foot where it would put a man down.

He could not take it back.

Cael did not step into the lane.

He had a breath, perhaps, before the bill. He took the read back off the air and went the other way, a single stride, straight at the man, inside the arc of the cut, to the place where the lath's root would pass at the top of its swing, a foot from Ephram's own shoulder. He set his feet there. He put up his left forearm as a post, and he shut the give.

He sent the load to the floor. It was Brom's gift, the first thing he had ever taken from Brom, and he had watched it practised in a harness room for a year: nothing in the whole of him pushing back, only somewhere for the weight to go, down the forearm and the shoulder, down the back, through both heels into sprung oak.

The cut arrived.

It had been meant for empty air fifteen feet away. It met a thing that did not give, a hand's breadth from where it began, with a Blade's whole committed body still coming on behind it. Cael felt the edge go into his forearm to the bone, it seemed, and stop. He felt the load go through him and into the boards, and the oak took it and held.

It could not go on, and so it went back.

He heard it. He had heard the same sound once in a chalk cut, under the noise of Iron Skin holding: a click, and a give. Ephram's right arm stopped being a thing with an edge on it, and the lath fell out of his hand and rang on the oak.

Cael's right palm was flat on his chest. He had put it there in the same beat, as he had meant to.

"Touch," said Rooke.

Ephram went down on one knee.

He did not cry out. He knelt on the pale boards with his right arm hanging from the shoulder at an angle no arm should hang at, and his face the colour of the chalk, and the Crown yard, which had been roaring, made a sound like a bellows let go.

Then the bill came in, and everything Cael owned went slow.

*One.* He tried to get down beside him and his knees bent a beat after he asked. *Two.* "Healer," Rooke was saying, in the same voice in which he had called the touch, and a spare grey woman was already off her bench and coming along the stand with her bag. *Three.* Somebody very large had come over the rim wall without troubling about the steps.

"Don't move it," said Brom. He was on his knees on the oak with one hand flat between Ephram's shoulder blades. "I've had one out. It goes back. It goes back worse if you help."

"That's the door," said Ephram.

He said it through his teeth, with his eyes shut, and Cael, slow, kneeling at last, thought that he had not heard right.

"Every Blade's got a door in him. They tell you in the second year. They don't tell you where." Ephram opened his eyes, which were wet with pain, and quite clear. "I've wondered where mine was for three years. Nobody on this hill was ever early enough to stand in it." He breathed. "Did you know it would do that?"

"No," said Cael. *Six. Seven.* The word came a beat late, and it was true. He had known that a commitment could not be taken back, and he had not once asked himself where it went if it was met. "I didn't price it. I priced my arm."

"Good," said Ephram. "I'd not have liked it if you'd known and done it anyway." He turned his head a little toward the stand. "Coach. Call it. I'm not having it set till it's called."

Rooke looked down at the two of them from the edge of the stand with his glass in his hand. Whatever he felt, his face showed about as much as his slate did.

"Two touches to one," he said, to twelve hundred people. "The enrollee."

Then the healer had Ephram's wrist in one hand and his elbow in the other, and Brom had him round the chest. She said something short, and there was a second sound like the first, and Ephram made a noise that he afterward denied.

*Ten.*

At eleven Cael could have stood, and he stayed where he was.

---

It had been waiting for him since the third exchange.

He had felt it there all through the fourth, in the place behind his eyes that belonged to nothing else, patient as a clerk. He looked at it now, on his knees on the oak, with the yard coming apart into noise above him and the healer winding linen round a captain's arm.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Blade-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: Iron.
Note: edge-declaration component; line-reading component.
Contact range.
Acquisition: directed. Engagement: adversarial, earnest — non-hostile.
```

He read it through twice.

Nine.

*Iron.* It was the lightest thing he had been handed since before Norhold, and Karis's curve had been right again, for it had come in a breath. *Edge-declaration.* That was the edge that stood clear of the wood because a man said so. *Line-reading.* It was the thin bright rule that had ended on his third button. *Contact range.* He had five lines across his forearm that said the same.

And at the top, where a name went, *unnamed*, as it had said on the road. It always said so. He had sat on a chalk bank and grieved that he would never be able to find the woman it had come from.

He knew this one's name, and where he slept, and what he whistled on a stair, and that he could not get the third line of a song right. Ephram was eighteen inches from him, having his arm bound, and he could have said it now. That was the difference, and it was the cost. He had written it down the night before at full weight, and it weighed exactly what he had written.

He read the last line a third time.

*Earnest — non-hostile.*

He had not known the notices had those words in them. The one before had said *combat*. The storm had come out of a Gold who was trying to beat him, and the road out of a woman who meant to put him in a wagon. This had come out of a man who had asked for the bout because he wished to know the truth about a friend, and who had fought with everything he had and wished him no harm in the world. Whatever kept the notices had known the difference, and it had a word for it.

"Coach."

It was Rooke's clerk, the thin anxious young man who had kept the visitors' ledger. He had come to the edge of the stand with the house's permanent book open on one forearm and the program's on the other.

"Which name, Coach? It isn't a program bout, and it isn't a ladder bout. The house's permanent column only has a head for one fighter. Ephram, or the enrollee?"

Rooke did not turn round. He was watching Brom and the healer get Ephram to his feet.

"Both. Enter both."

"Under what name for the enrollee? There's no column that—"

"As chalked."

The clerk looked up at the first board, at the two words Rooke had written there in the dark, and then he sat down at the long table and dipped his pen. He entered the bout twice in the house's book, once under each fighter, as a man does who has been told to get a thing down where nobody can rub it off.

Cael stood up, and his legs allowed it.

At the far end of the stand, Gault had not moved from beside his case. The magister was looking across the oak at him, steadily, as he had looked across a trestle every week for two years with three cards and a glass. He had signed a figure for every one of those weeks, and every figure had been earned on the house's floors, to the decimal, exactly as it was written; and he had just been shown what they had been figures of.

He did not take out his small book. For once he did not seem to need to write a thing down in order to have it.

He nodded. It was one small exact nod, the kind he gave a card that had been added right, and Cael nodded back, and found that he had to look somewhere else quickly.

The yard was a great deal of noise. He had expected that, and had not expected the kinds. The Current girl was standing on her bench with both fists in the air and tears running down her face, and seemed not to know about the tears. The assistant had her hands over her mouth. Half of Ephram's cohort were over the rail and round him, and the porter stood by his pail with his cap in his hand, blowing his nose and speaking severely to the ground. Brom walked beside the healer with the captain's good arm across his shoulders, and his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them; he had said once that a man who looks at a hurt only teaches it to be looked at.

Karis was not crying. She was on her feet, clapping, with her pale fingers flat, and her face was the one she wore when a count came out.

Lira was not crying either. She had her arms folded and her chin up, and she was looking round the tiers at twelve hundred faces, one after another. She had sat on a painted floor and told him she knew what their faces would do, and she seemed to be checking it.

At the top of the west tier the seat was empty. The grey man had gone, with his stick, to catch a bag. And at the end of the stand, by the porter's boards, there was nobody.

---

It took the Crown yard the better part of an hour to empty.

He sat on the lowest step of the east flight while it did, because the healer had told him to sit somewhere and hold his arm above his heart. She had looked at the forearm and whistled through her teeth, and felt along both bones, and said that nothing was broken and that it would be the colour of a plum by morning and stay so for a fortnight. She had bound it, and then she had looked at the way he was sitting and asked which hip. He had told her, and she had said that she supposed there was a shoulder as well.

"And behind the eyes," said Cael.

"I don't strap those," said the healer, and went after her other patient.

The town went first. It went up the tiers and out by the yard gate in a slow loud river, wool-factors and carters and clerks, and he heard his name in it, the registry's and the hill's, and a good many guesses, and let them go by. The lake house's Blade came down to the rim before he left and stood looking at the oak for some time with his lath across his shoulders; then he looked at Cael, and laughed, and shook his head, and went away.

The house went more slowly, for it did not seem to know how to leave. First-years stood about in knots on the tiers, re-fighting the fourth exchange with their hands. Nobody came down the steps to him, and that was Lira's doing, who had put herself at the head of the east flight with her arms folded.

Withrow was the last.

She had not left the registrar's chair. She sat in it, upright, with her hands in her lap, while the stand emptied round her and Bracken went down with the clerk's two books under his arm, and she went on sitting until the tiers were bare stone again and the porter had begun at the top with his broom. Then she stood.

She did not come to the steps. She looked at the oak, the whole length of it, as a woman looks at a room she has kept for a long time, and up at the first board, where four lines stood in a coach's capitals. Then she looked at Cael on his step, with his arm bound.

Her face was not the registry face, and he did not know what it was. He thought that she had seen a thing on her own oak that explained two years of an enrollee looking ordinary, and that she was adding it, quite calmly, to the things she had decided not to ask about.

She inclined her head to him. It was not the registry's depth; it was a good deal less, and it was meant.

Then she went out by the north arch, alone, and she was the last out of the yard.

On the tier above him the Current girl had come back for a scarf. She stopped beside Lira, red-eyed and hoarse.

"I've trained on this hill with him for two years," she said. "I've watched every wing-three card there was. I never saw one bit of that. Not one." She wiped her nose on her wrist. "Where's he been *keeping* it?"

Lira did not answer the question. She looked down the steps at him, and then out over the empty oak.

"That's the first time this school has ever seen him," she said.

---

Ephram came back when the light had begun to go yellow.

He came down the steps slowly, with his right arm bound across his chest in a sling of the healer's linen and his coat hung over the shoulder. Rooke's clerk was waiting by the boards with the chalk and the bout sheet.

The clerk chalked the result under the notice in the same three-line grammar the yard used for every bout. Cael and Ephram stood together while he did it. Ephram looked at the board rather than at Cael, which was the register the ritual ran in.

"Whatever you are," he said, "I said I'm glad it's on my side of the floor. At the trial. When she stopped to read you." He looked at his bound shoulder. "The floor's about to be the whole continent, and I'm not on it — I'm staying; you know I'm staying; it's the right thing for me and you'd tell me so if I asked, which is why I'm not asking." He put out his left hand. "Still glad. That's the whole thing. I made myself wait two years to fight you straight and it was worth every month, and I don't need to know what you are to know that. Go be it somewhere with more room."

Cael took the offered hand with his right.

Cael stood with Ephram's left hand still in his.

It was in him, new, housed for the moment where nothing else was, a thing with an edge and a rule to it. It had come out of the man in front of him an hour ago, and he could have said so in a dozen words. *There's something I took. You should know what you gave.*

Ephram would have listened. He would have gone still, and thought about it, and very probably said that he was glad of that as well; he had turned his back on a table of papers twice, for a friend's sake, without being told what was on them.

And then he would have known. He would have been a cohort captain at a chartered house who knew what the registry's unclassified enrollee was, and what he had carried east, and he would have known it on the day a district officer rode up to the gate, and for every day after. He was staying. He had a board with his name on it, and they would come and ask him, courteously, what he had seen.

Cael did not say it.

"You've a look on you," said Ephram.

"I'm writing something down."

"You always are." Ephram picked up his coat with the wrong hand and got it over his shoulder at the second try. "Don't make me sound clever in it. I walked into a post."

He went up the steps, slowly, and at the top he began to whistle. It was the song from the residence windows, and the third line was wrong.

---

He wrote it at the rim, sitting on the coping with his feet over the oak. He wrote in pencil, as Karis had learned to, with the book on his knee and the bound arm steadying the page.

*Nine. Taken in earnest from a friend who asked me to show him straight — so I showed him straight, and the architecture did what it does with earnest, and the notice said "non-hostile," which it has never said, because there has never been a fight like it in the log. Ephram asked for the true measurement. He got it. He doesn't know he gave me a piece of himself for it; he consented to a fight, not to the thing the fight fed. I wrote the price down before the bout, the way the covenant says, so that when it cost — and it cost — I'd have written the cost before I collected it, which is the only line between me and what the office did at a road cut and in a records hall — spending people whose names it never bothered to write down, and asking nobody anything.*

*He said "non-hostile" and the system agreed. Nine fragments and the ninth one's engagement field says the closest thing to friendship a machine knows how to file. I'll take it. It's the truest thing the instrument ever wrote about me except "no number goes here," and it was written about a fight I didn't want to win.*

He read it over once, and then set down the bills, smaller.

*Left forearm, wrist to elbow: the touch, and four more on the guard, the last to the bone or near it. Hip, crest to knee: ten on the oak, four past the six. Shoulder: six pushes at load, one past Norhold. Two lanes, ten and ten. Behind the eyes. One spark. Not the quiet thing; in a yard with twelve hundred people looking down it had nothing to offer and didn't. Not the one from the road.*

He stopped at that, and looked at it, because it wanted its reason beside it, and the log had always been more honest than he was aloud.

It had lifted its head a dozen times in four exchanges. A heel going down on oak was the plainest thing to fix in all the world, and Ephram had put his heel down thirty times; one point under that boot, set and told to stay, would have ended the bout in the first exchange. *There?* it had asked, each time, in its courteous way. *His*, he had told it. *Leave it.*

*Why not. One: it's on no paper the registry holds, and a man with a pencil had the top of the west tier. Two: it came off somebody who used it to put people in a wagon, and I've not yet found a use for it that isn't a hand on a person. Three: he asked me for a fight, and it holds people. You don't do that to a friend who has asked you for a fight. It stays in the kit.*

He shut the book and put it in the satchel, which Brom had left for him on the step with the strap done up.

He had gone out by the yard gate after Ephram left, to put his head under the pump in the covered walk. It was a plain stone gate with a dressed lintel, and he had walked beneath it a thousand times, and halfway through, something had raised its hand.

It was not the voice he knew. *There?* was a patient voice, and asked about resting things. This was quicker, and it did not ask about the lintel. It asked about the lintel's lower edge, the long straight arris of it over his head, where two faces of the stone met, and it held that edge up to him as a boy in a schoolroom holds up his slate.

*Edge?*

He had stood under the gate with a bound arm and very nearly laughed. It had taken him two days and forty proposals an hour to learn what to do with the last one, and he had a stair for this one to come up by.

*Lintel*, he had told it. *It keeps the arch up. It isn't for cutting. Leave it.*

It left it. He had felt it settle, in the place beside the other, as a second tool settles into a rack that somebody has already made room in. He supposed it would ask again. He supposed it would ask about every sill and step and table's edge between here and wherever they were going, for a day or two, and take names instead.

He sat on at the rim.

The sun had gone behind the lecture range. Below him the oak lay in its pit, pale and dry and empty, sixty feet by forty, with a few scuffs on it near the wall where a man had knelt. The porter had finished the tiers and gone, and up on the wall the four boards were going grey. He could still read Rooke's notice on the first, and beneath it the clerk's three chalked lines of the bout's result.

He had come back from the pump by the covered walk, past the records hall.

Its door had stood open, and the lamps were lit in pairs. Bracken was at his counter in his shirtsleeves, with all three pairs of glasses about him and a quire of printed forms at his elbow, on the registry's own stock, and Cael had been able to read their heading from the walk: *Withdrawal of an enrollee from a chartered house.* Bracken had counted four sheets off the quire and laid them in a row on the wood, under the lamp. He had squared each of them to the counter's edge with one finger, and then to its neighbour, and set the pen beside them.

Four. He had not looked up. They were for morning.

Seln had looked round a reading room at the top of a library once, on the evening of a birthday, with six people in it and a honey cake. *It is a good room*, he had said. *I have been in a great many.*

Cael had been in a great many himself by now. He had been in a wool exchange with a lot number under his boot, and a dairy with a slit window, and a borrowed office with nothing of anybody's in it; he had stood on a north road outside a room he would never go into; and he had been careful in every one of them.

He looked down at sixty feet of sprung oak in a hole in the ground, where for an hour that afternoon he had not been careful with anything.

It was a yard, and had no roof. He said it anyway.

"Good room."

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

## Using Status Markers

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

> **SECRET (reveals Book 9):** The Architect built the system to suppress Fractured Paths, not organize existing potential.

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.

  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”

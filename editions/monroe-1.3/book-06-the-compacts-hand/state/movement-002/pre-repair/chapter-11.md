# Chapter 11 — Choosing

Withrow sent for him at the ninth bell that evening, by the porter's boy, with no reason given.

Her office was on the first floor of the old range, at the end of a corridor nobody used except to go to it. Cael had been in it perhaps a dozen times in two years, and every time he had been told something he would sooner not have heard. He knocked. She said *Come*. He went in, and the first thing he saw was not Withrow at all.

It was a folder, lying open on her desk, turned round to face the visitor's chair.

It was a plain buff folder of the kind the registry issued, thick, worn soft at its spine, with the house's name lettered on its front in an old clerk's hand that had faded to brown. He knew what it was before he was close enough to read the lettering. Every chartered house on the continent kept one, and every inspector who ever came up its road read it first, from the front, before he looked at a single floor. He had never seen this one. No enrollee had. It lived in the locked case under the framed charter on the wall behind her, and the case had a key, and the key was on Withrow's chain.

"Sit," said Withrow. "Read it first. I'll talk afterward. You'll want to have seen it before you hear me describe it, because you've never once in two years believed a description of anything."

He sat. She did not sit. She stood at the window with her back to the room and her hands folded behind her, and looked out at the court in the last of the light, and let him read.

It was the house's life in leaves. The charter's grant was first, on heavier paper than the rest, under a seal gone brown with age. Then came the inspections, one after another, each with its date and its inspector's name and its finding. Near the front there were two notes of the kind no house wanted, both from long before Withrow's time: a fire-watch that had failed to keep its book for a quarter, and a records office that had let a year's declarations go unindexed. Beside each, in the right-hand column, stood the remedy the house had made, and beside the remedy a clearance, stamped and dated and initialled by whoever had come to see that it was done. The house's placings were there, every meet, every year, a long thin column of figures. Last year's was the only one with *third* in it.

He turned to the last leaf in use.

It was dated the eighth day of the year. It was in the registry's flat grey type, and somebody had sent it up the road to be pinned into the file, because the house was required to keep its own file current with whatever the registry entered against it.

*Respondent: the academy of Halcenvane, in formal proceedings before the seat at Ostrand. Subject of proceedings: the basis on which it enrolled Caelen Hesk-ward, unclassified, its enrollee of record. Status: pending.*

The right-hand column beside it was ruled, like all the others, and empty.

The empty column held him longer than the rest of the file had. It was the same width as the columns beside the fire-watch and the records office. It had been ruled by the same machine. And there was nothing in it, because nothing had happened yet that could go in it.

"That entry stays," said Withrow, at the window.

He looked up.

"Whatever the seat decides." She had not turned round. "If the seat finds for us on every point, that leaf stays in that file. Every inspector who comes up our road from now until long after I'm dead will open this folder from the front, and turn the leaves, and come to that one, and read that this house was once a respondent in formal proceedings about the basis on which it enrolled a boy. And then he'll read the column." She turned at last. "If we have every piece of luck there is, a clerk will one day write *sustained* in that column and stamp it. The leaf will still be there above the stamp."

"I know," said Cael.

"You didn't. You knew that the house was named. You didn't know what *named* looks like in a buff folder, in a case under a charter, for the next fifty years." She came and sat down across the desk from him, and folded her hands on it. "Now you do. That's one column of the ledger. I'm going to give you both."

---

She gave him both, and she gave them in her registry voice, which was not the voice she used at convocation and not the one she used across this desk to a third-year who had broken a window. It was flatter than either. It was the voice in which she had read the first page of the instrument aloud in the records hall, and he understood that she had chosen it because it was the voice in which a column of figures was read.

The house was the named respondent. In registry grammar that was not a courtesy, not a way of addressing the paper to someone who would see it was answered. The house had been put inside the petition beside him. Whatever the seal decided about his enrollment, it decided about the house's judgment in making it. If the seat found that the house had been wrong to enroll him, it found against the house's judgment, and the finding went into the folder. A chartered house with an adverse finding in its folder was a house whose next inspection would not be eleven quiet days of an officer declining to find anything. It would be a long inspection. It would be a hungry one.

"Here is what that finding would sit on top of," said Withrow, and laid her hand flat on the desk beside the folder, as if setting each thing down on it as she named it.

Third on the continent, from a house the continent had stopped expecting anything of. A Silver program, chartered on the year's first morning, with its own floor and its own line in the accounts and a newly made Silver who had asked to come and teach in it. A research standing that the council had sat twice to find a heading for, with a budget and a lock and a second-year assistant under it. An intake larger than any she had admitted. And the house's name written down, this year, in other people's books, in places where houses like this one were not usually written down at all.

"I built my whole tenure toward every one of those," said Withrow. "I have set every one of them down on the table next to your name. Nobody made me. I did it on the morning I signed your roster, by declining to pretend you were something you aren't." She did not lower her eyes. "I'd do it again. You should hear the price from me, once, plainly, across a desk. Then there's nothing left for you to lie awake over at three in the morning."

He did not say anything. He had learned in two years that when Withrow told you a thing in that voice, the right answer was to have heard it.

"Now the other column," she said.

She reached across and closed the folder, gently, and laid it to one side.

"Before I was a chancellor I was twenty years a registry officer. You know that. Before your name ever came to this desk I had the Greyvane transcript sent up from Ostrand, and I read it until I could have recited the finding." She sat back in her chair. "I didn't read it as your chancellor, because I wasn't. I read it as a registry officer who has watched a great many clever advocates make a great many bad clauses look good, and I was looking for the seam. The place where somebody had made the law say what it did not say." She shook her head once. "It wasn't there. The law was on your side at Greyvane. It was just on your side."

"And now?"

"Now I've read their paper eleven times. I've read every leaf of what Karis sent down the hill, and I've read her six slips of paper on the long table, and I've read the word she wrote in the minute." Withrow's voice did not change at all. "Here is what the registry officer in me sees. The law is still on your side. On every point that can be argued in front of a seat, it is on your side. And Karis is right about the doors."

She let that sit. The last of the light had gone off the window behind her, and the room was getting dark, and neither of them moved to light the lamp.

"Both of those are true," said Withrow. "The law is ours, and the doors open one way. I've known it since the night of the slips. Any registry officer would. So I have a choice about which of those two true things I put my weight on." She leaned forward and set both hands on the desk between them, flat, a hand's width apart. "I am going to keep putting my weight on the first. I am going to keep believing, out loud, in front of that seat and the town and the broadsides and every inspector who ever opens that folder, that it matters that the law is ours. I know where the corridor goes. I'm walking it anyway, with this whole house on my back, on purpose." She looked at him steadily. "Make a note of the word *choosing*."

He took out the observation notebook. He did not ask whether she wanted it written now; she had said *make a note*, and she meant now. He turned to her pages, the section he had kept on her since his first week on the bluff, two years of small facts in his own cramped hand: the way she crossed a court, the length of her convocation speeches, the number of times she had lifted a page to stop a hall's noise. *Load-bearing calm*, from the first morning of this year. Under it all he wrote the date, and the word, and drew a line under the word.

Then, because the notebook took observations and this was one, he wrote what the word was.

*She has known about the corridor since the night of the slips. She is going down it anyway, deliberately, carrying the house, and she gave me the one word for it tonight, so that it will be in this book on the day the doors begin to shut behind us. Choosing. Not a point of law. A woman telling a witness, while there is still time, what she has decided to be.*

He looked up. Withrow had been watching him write, and she did not pretend she had not.

"Go and train," she said. "Rooke says you've been haunting the east hall's rail with a face like a man pricing a horse. Good. Price it." She stood, and he stood. "And on Fifth-day, when you get into that coach, sit up straight. The town watches the coach."

---

The year's shape laid itself out over the next three days, and Cael put it together the way he put together any floor before a bout, from what he could see of it and what other people let him see.

The seat's sittings went up on the covered walk's middle board on the twenty-sixth day, in Bracken's hand, under the house's one-line answer from the eighth day: *Sittings at the registry seat, Ostrand, on the seat's Fifth-days. First sitting: the sixth Fifth-day of the year.* Underneath, smaller, Bracken had added a line of his own, which was the kind of thing he never did on a public board. *The house will attend.*

At the counter that afternoon Cael asked him how many sittings.

"Eleven," said Bracken, without looking it up. "If the year runs honest and the seat keeps its own schedule and nobody falls off a horse." He was sorting the noon post into its pigeonholes, a letter at a time. "It never runs honest. Call it eleven and expect fourteen. The ruling's before the year closes. That's in the notice, and they'll keep to it. They like a tidy year."

Karis had the two dockets laid side by side on the reading room's long table when he went up.

One was the seat's notice. The other was the sheet Bracken had carried down the refectory on the fourth night, Brom's, the district seat's docket for the review: a panel of three assessors and a sigil frame, the record read as it stood, the last Fifth-day of the academic year. She had borrowed it from Brom that morning. Brom had taken it out of his left inside pocket, and unfolded it, and handed it to her, and stood in the reading room's doorway until she gave it back, which she did inside a quarter of an hour, which was very fast for Karis.

She had copied the two dates onto a slip, one above the other, and she was looking at them.

"The same day," she said.

He looked. The seat would rule before the year's close; the notice said so, and Bracken said they would keep to it. And in the last week of the year there was one Fifth-day. Brom's panel would sit on it, at the district seat, which was in Ostrand, a street and a half from the wool exchange.

"If the district's hall were being painted that week," said Karis, "they'd put them under one roof. Brom's tier in one room, and whether you're to be allowed one at all in the next, on one morning." She put the slip in her notebook's front matter, beside the six slips from the long table. "The registry has no sense of humour. It doesn't do this on purpose. It's just tidy." She closed the notebook. "I think tidy is worse."

There was one more date on the year's board, and it was not a date at all.

What the petition wanted, at the end of all its paragraphs, was a frame: a station, a protocol, the instrument the clause had always kept him out of. The notice gave that no day. In the same grey type as everything else, it said only that any such evaluation would be *fixed by the seat in its ruling*. So there it sat, at the bottom of the year as Cael had begun to draw it in the notebook: a line with nothing on it. Every other thing on the page had a day. The sittings had their Fifth-days. Brom's review had its morning. Lira's first meet off the hill, the program's first real test against other houses' Silvers, had a day six weeks away on Rooke's board in the east hall. And under all of them, the one blank.

He looked at the blank a long while. It was not empty, he thought. It was waiting. A door with no date on it yet, but already hung, with the hinge on the far side.

On the Fifth-day between, he fought in wing three at the band. A Blade this time, an Iron Rank Five with a long patient guard, who liked to make you come to him. Cael came to him, slowly, twice, and burst out of the corner the Blade had walked him into, and gave him the third exchange because the rotation said the third exchange went. Twenty-two and a third. Ephram wrote it in his chart and looked at it, and did not draw a question mark, and did not look at Cael either.

---

On the twenty-seventh evening he went to the north wall at the change of the bell and placed the coats.

He had been counting them since the year's first night, and before that since Greyvane, and he had known from the first night that they were older and stood differently. What he had not had was the reason. He had written it down three times as a question and left it, because he would not guess. Now he sat at the third buttress with his back to the house and the valley open below him, and watched the change on the bell, the two at the landing and the two at the foot of the road, as neat as ever. And while he watched, the reason arrived, quietly, the way the read arrived on a floor, from under.

They were older because the young men's work was finished.

He laid it out for himself in order, the way Karis would have. For two years and more, the coats at the foot of every road he had lived above had been learning. Learning his hours, which bell he rose at and which he slept at. Which floors he trained on, and how often, and who with. Who he ate with, and where he sat to eat. Who came up the road to see him and who went down it. Where he walked in the evening and how long he stayed. That was work for young men: patient, dull, a little foolish, the kind of thing a junior officer was set to do so that he would learn to do it. You could see it in the way they had stood, the young ones, craning at the walls, shifting their feet a little more each week as the place came clear to them.

And then it had come clear. All of it. Every hour of his day was in a file somewhere by now. There was nothing left on this hill that anybody needed to be sent to learn.

So they had sent different men. Men who did not need to learn anything, because whoever sent them had told them all of it before they came. Men of a higher grade, who would not be wasted on gathering. The kind of men an office posts when the file is complete and it is waiting for the next thing to happen to the person in the file.

Down at the foot of the road the grey-bearded one leaned on the stick he did not need, and looked at the river, and did not look up.

Cael wrote it on the coping in the dusk.

*The coats aren't gathering anything any more. They're waiting for a status change.*

*The proceedings will make one, a year of sittings from now. Whoever posts the men at the road has already read the ruling in his head. The only thing on the continent that hasn't is the paper it'll be written on.*

---

Lira asked for the honest floor on the Sixth-day night, three nights before her trial, and she asked for it in a way she had never asked for anything.

"I want you to be a floor," she said.

They were in the east hall again, at the quarter to the eleventh bell, on Rooke's key. The shutters were drawn along the gallery and the two lanterns stood at either end of the painted lines. Brom had brought the jug. Karis had come this time, for the first hour, with her notebook under her arm; she sat on the gallery stair and said she would take the figures as they happened, in order, and nobody was to break anything she would have to spell.

"A floor," said Cael.

"The Force," said Lira. "The third-year. I'm fighting him on Second-day, in front of the rating table, and I've drilled beside him every morning for two months, and I know exactly how he fights a drill." She was taping her wrists, quickly. "I don't know how he fights me. Nobody's ever made him."

Cael knew the Force a little. A broad-backed young man who laughed when he was surprised. Rank Seven on the porter's wall since the last sitting of the old year, six ranks above Lira's one, and the best fighter the cohort had made in a straight line, without a program, since Ephram. A Force fighter's Path was a push. He gathered it and set it, into the air or the floor or a man, and he did not throw it; he put it down where he wanted it and walked away and left it there. A good Force on a floor he owned could lay his push into the boards around him a stride at a time, so that every step an enemy took came down on wood that was already moving.

"He builds," said Lira. "Like a mason. A course at a time. He settles in the knees, then he sets one, then he steps behind it, then he settles and sets the next. By the third course the floor between you is like trying to cross a cart while it's going." She tore the tape. "I can't get Brom to do that. Brom's a wall. Walls don't move the floor."

"I don't move the floor either."

"No." She looked up. "But you move the air. You lay a road that leans. If you lay one across my line, it's not his floor, but it's a floor that tips under me when I don't expect it, and I can learn what my feet do on it." She stood. "Lay them across me. I'll cross them on the four-count. Brom stands at the end of the line, where the Force would be, and if I get to him I touch him."

Cael looked at Brom. Brom, unwinding his wraps, looked back.

"The lane costs," said Cael. "Ten counts, every time. Behind the eyes."

"I know what it costs. I wrote it on my hand." Lira went to the first cross. "Lay one. We'll write what it costs."

He laid one.

He set it a stride in front of her and across her line, from the north wall toward the south, a road of air fifteen feet long and no wider than his shoulders, tipping toward the south. His ears closed and opened. The dust in the cracks of the painted boards woke and ran south down the lane, a pale thin stream. Then he took the read off the lane, deliberately, the way he had learned on this floor a week ago, and gave it back to himself, and stood.

Lira ran the four counts at the lane.

On the first count she stepped. On the second she put her weight on her front foot, the long lie, and her front foot came down inside the lane, and the lane took it. It slid her front foot a hand's breadth south, sideways, out from under her, and her whole lie went wrong with it. She did not fall. She caught herself on the back foot with a grunt and stood there off balance in the painted floor's dust, and the four-count was gone.

"Again," she said.

He waited out his ten counts. They came as they had come before: the warm weight behind the eyes, the whole suite answering a beat late when he tested it, and at eleven, clean. Then he laid another, in the same place.

She tried the second count outside the lane this time, short of it, and the third count, the empty one, with one foot in the lane and one out. The lane took the foot that was in. She stood through it. She let it slide her front foot, the way a fighter lets a push go by, and on the fourth count she burst off the back foot, the one that was still on honest boards, across the lane and through the end of it, and came out of the burst three strides from Brom with the dust still running behind her.

"Three strides," said Brom.

"Three strides," said Lira. "Short." She was breathing hard. "But I didn't fall. Again."

He laid a third.

She ran it, and it was better: the empty count taken standing, one foot in and one foot out, the lane sliding the inside foot and Lira letting it. She burst on the fourth off the honest foot, and the burst carried her a stride from Brom, and she touched his shoulder. Brom nodded. Lira turned round, grinning, to say something.

And Cael found he was not counting.

He had laid the third lane and taken the read off it and the weight had come behind the eyes, as it always came, and he had begun to count the ten. And somewhere about six he had lost the count, not because anything had happened but because nothing had: the weight had simply gone on, warm and heavy, and the numbers had stopped meaning anything. He reached for the Wind out of habit, the way you test a stair, and it came, late. He reached again. Late.

"Count," he said.

Lira's face changed. She came across the floor to him fast and stood in front of him and looked at his eyes, and then she began to count aloud, slowly, one number for each of her own breaths.

At eleven he reached for the Wind and it came late.

At twelve it came on time.

"Twelve," said Lira. She did not say anything else for a moment. Then she took the pencil stub out of her pocket and wrote on her palm, under the place where the tape stopped. "Three in one night. Ten on the first, ten on the second. Twelve on the third." She looked up. "That's the last one tonight."

"I'm all right."

"I know you're all right. It's still the last one." She put the pencil away. "You told me on this floor that you'd only ever met the ten counts once. Now you've met them three times in a row, and the third was longer. That's a new figure. We don't spend a new figure the night we find it. Rooke says that." She almost smiled. "Rooke says it about me."

Karis, on the gallery stair, had written it down in order: *First lane, ten. Second, ten. Third, twelve. Not tested past three.* She read it back aloud without being asked, so that he would hear it said by somebody else, and then she shut the notebook and said she had what she had come for and would see them at breakfast, and went.

They sat on the boards afterward among the white crosses with the jug going round.

"What did you learn," said Brom, to Lira.

Lira lay on her back with her taped hands on her stomach, looking up at the dark shutters.

"Not to put my weight down on the third count," she said. "I've been doing the lie with a real foot. On his floor the real foot is the one that moves. So I lie with a foot I don't care about, and I keep the one I'm going to burst from on wood that holds still." She turned her head on the boards. "And I go on the fourth. Not the third. Never the third, against a builder. Everything I've ever done says go on the third, and on his floor the third is the one he's already built under."

"He'll laugh at you," said Brom. "When you stand there on the empty count."

"Let him," said Lira. "He laughs when he's surprised. I'd like him surprised."

Cael sat with the jug in his hands and the last warmth going out from behind his eyes, and thought about the twelve.

It was the first new thing the lane had told him since the night it settled. He had believed the ten counts were the price, fixed, like the five free bursts on boards; a thing he could plan around, write in a minute before a fight, and pay. Now he knew they were not fixed. They grew when he spent the lane over and over. He did not know yet by how much, or whether they grew without end, or whether they came back down with rest. He knew only that a figure he had thought was a wall was a slope.

He realized he was glad. Not of the slope, but of where he had found it: on a painted floor at night, with Lira counting aloud and Karis writing it down in order and Brom standing at the end of the line, and not in front of a crowd with a Gold across the ring. That was the whole reason for the floor. Brom had said it a week ago. *So you don't forget it's yours.* It was his, and it had a slope in it, and now he knew where.

*Lane: three in a night, ten, ten, twelve,* he wrote in Hesk's book before he slept. *The count grows when I spend it close together. Not tested past three. Lira stopped it. She was right to.*

*Lira: lie with the foot you don't care about. Go on the fourth.*

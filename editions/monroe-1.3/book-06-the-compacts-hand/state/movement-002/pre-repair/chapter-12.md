# Chapter 12 — Admit

The east hall had never held a crowd before, and on the thirtieth morning of the year it held one.

Nobody had announced the trial beyond a line on Rooke's own board, chalked the evening before in his square capitals: *PROGRAM TRIAL. SECOND-DAY, SECOND BELL. LIRA / FORCE THIRD-YEAR. RATED.* That was all. But the house had read it, and the house knew what *rated* meant on that floor. It meant the first bout ever fought on the program's painted boards under the program's own seal, with a figure at the end of it that would go into the program's own book. By the half-bell the gallery was full along both sides. By the second bell the porters had opened the shutters on the high windows to let the heat out, and the light came down onto the white crosses in long bright bars, and there were people standing on the gallery stair.

Cael had the rail at the north end, where he had stood on the second morning of the year to watch the red book. Brom stood on his left with his arms folded. Karis stood on his right with her notebook shut and her pen in her hand. Ephram was three steps down the gallery stair with the captain's file under his arm, where he could see the whole floor and be seen by the whole cohort, which Cael supposed was the point. The girl from the reading room's outer table had found a place against the far wall of the gallery, with her small notebook open.

Below them on the floor stood the rating table: a trestle under the north windows with three chairs behind it. The clerk with the red drill-book sat at one end. Rooke sat in the middle with his board and his pencil. And at the other end, with a ruled card in front of him and his hands folded on the table like a man waiting outside a shop, sat the Stone from the river academy, who had asked to come and teach the girl who beat him and was now going to put a figure on her.

Rooke stood up at the second bell and said the rule, once, in a voice that carried to the back of the gallery without being raised.

"Program trial. My rule, not the region's." He held up his board as if the rule were written on it, which it was not. "Four exchanges. Each one, the table gives a flag to whoever took it, or to nobody if nobody did. Most flags takes the trial. Level after four, the table's figure decides. Three bursts a bout for the program's entrant, as on every day she's ever stood on this floor. The other fighter, what his own Path allows." He sat down. "Rated, by the table. The figure goes in this program's book. Not the registry's. Not yet."

Lira stood at the south chalk with her wrists taped and her left arm carried a little close, out of habit. Across forty feet of painted boards stood the Force.

---

He was a big young man, broad in the back and thick through the thighs, and he stood at his chalk with his feet planted and his hands loose and a pleasant expression, the expression of somebody who has been looking forward to a thing and has decided to enjoy it. He had been drilling beside Lira every morning since the recess. He knew the four-count; he had run it himself, badly, for the practice of seeing it. He had laughed once on this floor when she lied to him on the second count and he went the wrong way. He was Iron Rank Seven, six ranks above her on the porter's wall, and he was the best thing the cohort had built in a straight line since Ephram.

Cael watched him settle.

It was very small. A Force fighter's push came out of the legs, and before it came, the knees gave a little and the hips dropped a finger's breadth, as anybody's do before a heavy lift, when he wants the floor to know about it first. The read gave it to Cael from the rail without his asking. Settle, and then the push went down into the boards in front of the man's feet and stayed there.

"Exchange," said the clerk.

The Force did what Force fighters did. He did not come forward. He settled, and set his first course, and Cael felt it go into the floor from forty feet away: a strip of boards a stride wide in front of the man, across the whole width of his reach, gone heavy and live, like the deck of a barge in a current. Then he stepped forward behind it. He settled again. He set the second course in front of the first.

Lira ran straight at him.

It was the worst thing she could have done, and the whole gallery knew it, and a sound went round the gallery like a held breath being let out in disappointment. She did not run the four-count. She did not lie. She went at him in a plain hard line, burst one off the left hip, the old burst, the one she had brought out of a barn at fourteen and that every fighter on the hill had seen a hundred times. The burst carried her twenty feet and put her down on the second course.

The boards under her moved. They were not where her foot expected them; they had been pushed a hand's breadth toward her, and her landing came down on them a beat early and slid. She caught herself. She had to; she was on a floor that was trying to put her on her back. And while she caught herself the Force stepped through his own first course, which did not move for him because it was his, and set his palm on her shoulder.

Not a blow. A touch, square and plain, with his whole weight behind it, and Lira went back off the second course a stride and a half and sat down on the painted boards.

The Stone lifted his flag. Red. The Force's.

The gallery groaned.

Rooke wrote nothing at all.

Lira got up. She dusted her palms on her thighs, and walked back to her chalk, and stood there with her hands at her sides and her eyes on the boards in front of the Force's feet. She was not looking at him. She was looking at where his courses had gone down, and her lips were moving very slightly.

"She gave it," said Brom quietly.

"Yes."

"On purpose."

"Yes." Cael had seen it from the first stride. Lira did not run straight at builders. She had not run straight at a builder since the mill town. "She was counting him."

"Counting what?"

"When he settles." He watched her lips. "She needed him to build in front of her, start to finish, once, with nothing in his way. So she gave him nothing in his way."

Karis, on his other side, had opened her notebook. She wrote one line and showed it to him without a word. *First flag bought. Price: one flag on the record, in front of the table.*

"Exchange," said the clerk.

The Force settled. Set his first course. Stepped. And Lira, at her chalk, began the four-count.

The first step was an ordinary step, any fighter's, and the Force watched it come and settled again for the second course. The second step was the lie. She put her weight on her front foot and left it there one count too long, the long lie the Stone had taught her, so that anybody watching for the burst would see it coming and set himself to meet it. The Force saw it coming. He set himself. He set his second course exactly where her burst would land if it came off that front foot, a fine strong course, and Cael from the rail felt it go down and thought it was the best course the man had built all morning.

The third count, nothing happened.

It was the hardest thing in the world to watch. Lira stood on the painted floor with one foot forward and one back and did nothing at all, for a whole count, while a Rank Seven Force stood twenty feet away behind two courses of moving boards with his knees bent, waiting. The gallery made a small sound. Somebody laughed, uncertainly. The Force laughed too, from surprise, the way he always did, a short bark.

He settled for the third course.

Cael saw it. Lira saw it a hair before he did. The settle was on the man's own fourth count, his own rhythm, the same rhythm he had built to in the first exchange, start to finish, with nothing in his way. He settled on his fourth count whether she gave him anything to build against or not.

And her fourth count was the same beat as his.

She burst off the back foot. Not the front foot she had lied with, which stood on boards a course away from honest; the back one, which had never left the wood the Force had not yet touched. She burst at the angle the first three counts had sworn she would never take, low and to the right, past the end of his second course, where there was no course yet because he was only now settling to set it. She went across honest boards in a single burst and came down on them two strides from his left hip with her landing beat holding her steady, and the Force was still in his settle, knees bent, his push still in his legs, with nowhere to put it.

She touched his shoulder. Lightly. Two fingers.

The Stone lifted his flag. Blue.

The noise in the gallery was not cheering, not at first. It was the sound of a great many people realizing at the same moment that the story they had been watching was a different story. Then it was cheering. Rooke, at the table's middle, put his pencil to his board and wrote one word, and Cael could not see what it was and did not need to.

"One burst," said Karis, writing. "Two exchanges. One flag each."

The Force stood a moment where he was, looking at the place on the boards where his third course would have gone. Then he shook his head, grinning, and went back to his chalk.

---

He had learned. That was what made him the best the cohort had built. The third exchange, he did not build to his own count any more.

He settled faster. He set his courses close together, two counts apart instead of four, not as strong but quicker, and he set them not in a line in front of him but in a fan, left and right and center, so that the whole floor between them began to move, a stride at a time, everywhere. Within a dozen heartbeats there was no honest wood between Lira's chalk and his anywhere she could see.

Cael watched her look for it and not find it.

She tried the four-count once, and the lie on the second count put her front foot on a course that was not there a moment earlier and was there now, and the boards took her foot sideways, and she had to burst to get out of it. Her second burst of the bout. She came down near the east wall, beside the striking frames on their thick springs, on a stretch of boards the Force had not yet reached. He came after her. Not fast; Force fighters were never fast. He came behind his courses, settling and setting, settling and setting, a fan of moving floor pushing ahead of him across the white crosses like a tide coming up a beach.

She went back. A stride. Another. The frames were a pace behind her heel.

"She's out of floor," said Brom.

"She's not," said Cael.

She had done it on the honest floor three nights before, at the end of a lane he laid for her, with Brom standing at the end of the line. She had learned it standing in dust that ran. *Lie with the foot you don't care about. Go on the fourth.*

She stood with her back nearly against the frames and put her left foot forward onto a course. It moved; she let it. She let the boards slide her front foot a hand's breadth sideways and did not fight them, the way a fighter lets a push go by. She kept her right foot behind her on the last strip of honest wood on the east side of the hall. And she stood there for the empty third count, one foot moving and one foot still, while the Force settled to set the course that would take the last of her honest wood away.

He settled on the count. Even now, building fast, building in a fan, the man still had a count, and a fighter cannot build fast without one. Cael had seen it with the read: two counts now, not four, but a count all the same.

She went on her fourth.

Burst three. Off the right foot, the still one, straight along the line of the frames, not toward him at all but past him, so close to the frames that the nearest post rang as her shoulder brushed it. She came down eight feet on, behind the end of his fan, on boards his courses had not reached because his courses all pointed the other way. The Force was turning. A Force fighter turning on a floor he had made heavy around himself had to turn through his own courses, and they were his, and they did not move for him, but they did not help him either. He was a beat late.

She touched his back between the shoulders.

Blue.

"Three bursts," said Karis. "Her whole bout." Her pen kept moving. "One exchange to go."

Cael knew what the fourth exchange would cost before the clerk said the word.

The Force had learned twice now. He was not going to give her a count to burst on. He was not going to chase her into a corner where she could go past him along a wall. He stood at his chalk when the clerk said *exchange* and he did not come forward at all. He settled, and set a course in front of himself, and settled, and set another behind it, and another, standing still, building, until there were four courses of moving boards laid in a half-ring round him like the walls of a well, and he stood in the middle of them on his own still floor and waited for her to come.

There was no honest wood anywhere near him. To touch him she would have to cross four courses. On any one of them a landing would slide. She could not go round; the half-ring had its back to the north wall, and the gap at its ends was too narrow for anybody to get through on boards that moved.

Rooke's rule was three bursts. She had spent three.

Lira stood at her chalk and looked at the well of moving floor for a long moment. Her lips were not moving now. She was not counting him. Cael watched her decide, and saw her decide it, and saw her know exactly what it would cost.

She went.

Burst four, off the left hip, across the first course and the second in one long low flight, the old burst, the barn's burst, the strongest thing she had ever owned. She came down on the third course. It moved. She let it. She let it slide her landing a hand's breadth and turned the slide into the start of the next thing, as she had learned in the dust on the honest floor, and burst again off the moving boards before they could move her any further.

Burst five.

It carried her over the fourth course and onto the still floor at the center of the well, a stride from the Force, at a range so close that he could not set a course between them because there was no floor between them to set it in. He was still in his settle. He had built a wall to stand behind, and she had come over the top of it.

She did not hit him. She put her taped palm flat on his breastbone and held it there.

Blue.

The gallery came apart.

---

"Three flags to one," said the clerk into the noise, and wrote it in the red book.

The Stone wrote on his card. He wrote for a long time, longer than a single figure needed. Then he slid the card along the trestle to Rooke, and Rooke read it, and stood up with it in his hand, and the gallery fell quiet to hear it, because nobody on the hill had ever heard a program figure read out on that floor before.

"Execution, ten," said Rooke. "Effect, ten." He did not look up. "Control, seven. Twenty-seven."

He put the card down on the trestle.

"Ten is a good ordinary day at the heart of Silver. She was there in two columns." His voice did not change at all. "The seven is the fourth burst and the fifth. A fighter who spends past what her program sets her doesn't control her bout. She rents it." He looked at the clerk. "Enter it."

Twenty-seven. Cael had her figures in his notebook since the wool town, every one: twenty-five, twenty-six, twenty-seven at her very best last year, the top of what the board called strong Iron. Twenty-eight was the floor of the Silver bracket. She had stood a point short of it before. She stood a point short of it now. But this time the point had a name and a place, and Rooke had just said both aloud in front of the whole house.

Lira walked to the middle of the floor and shook the Force's hand. Her collar was dark through and her chest was going like a bellows, and she was standing on her right leg more than her left.

Rooke came down from the table with his board under his arm and met her at the edge of the painted lines, before she reached the rail. He did not wait for her to sit. He never did.

"Five," he said. "Against three."

"I know."

"Five free on these boards in a whole day. You spent the whole day in one bout, in front of a table." He looked at her left leg. "Landing leg."

"Hot. Down the outside, from the hip."

"Hip?"

"The fourth went into it. The fifth went somewhere further down." She did not try to make it smaller. "I'll find out at the first bell."

Rooke wrote on his board. "Two days at half work. No frames. No bursts until the leg's quiet, and you tell me when it's quiet, and I'll decide whether to believe you." He looked up. "That was cheap. Two days. You'll pay it standing on your head." He tucked the pencil behind his ear. "Five weeks from now you'll be on a road, three bouts inside four days, against Silvers who were born in programs like this one. Five bursts in one of those, and you've nothing left for the other two. Learn the difference here, where it costs two days. Not there, where it costs the meet."

"Yes, Coach."

He went back to the table without another word, and sat, and began writing in the red book in the clerk's place, as if the trial were a long time ago.

On his way to the south door the Force stopped at the foot of the gallery stair, kit on his shoulder, wraps hanging out of his coat pocket. Lira was on the bottom step of the gallery stair by then, unwinding her tapes. He stopped in front of her.

"The first one," he said. "Did you give it to me?"

"Yes."

He thought about that. He did not seem to mind it at all. "Why?"

"I needed to watch you build. All the way through, with nothing in your way." She pulled the last strip of tape off her left wrist and balled it. "To see whether you settle on your own count whatever happens, or only while you're leading."

"And?"

"Every time." She looked up at him. "Even when you're behind. Even when you're building fast. Two counts instead of four, but a count."

He stood there a moment longer with his kit on his shoulder, looking at the boards where his well had been.

"Even when I'm behind," he said, as if to himself. Then he nodded to her, a short honest nod, the way a man nods to somebody who has told him where he left his hat, and went out of the south door. Cael watched him go and knew that by Rooke's next morning drill the man's count would be a different count, and that the whole cohort would be the better for having had him beaten.

Lira came up the stair to the rail.

She had her tapes balled in one fist and the other hand pressed flat against the outside of her left thigh, where the leg was sending its bill, and her face had the look Cael had watched on it for two months of mornings and never had words for. She stood at the rail beside him and looked down at the painted floor: the white crosses, the arrows, the frames on their thick springs, the trestle where the clerk had gone back to his red book, the Stone folding his card in half and putting it in his coat. Rooke writing.

"At fifteen I'd have laughed at all this," she said. "A table. A book. A man come all the way from the river to teach me a count." She turned the balled tape in her fingers. "I'd have said I had a barn and a yard and my own two legs and that was plenty, and I'd have meant it, and I'd have been very proud of meaning it." She watched the Stone go out. "I'm better now. I can admit I want it."

Nobody said anything. Brom unfolded his arms. Karis, on Cael's other side, closed her notebook very quietly.

Lira looked at the floor a moment longer, and then she went down the stair to find her water, walking carefully on the left leg, and did not look back to see how it had been received.

They walked back across the court afterward in the heat, the three of them, behind the crowd going out.

"*Admit*," said Karis.

"I heard."

"She has never once used that word. Not in three years. Not about anything." Karis walked with her notebook against her chest. "She says things are owed. She says things are late, or wrong, or hers. She has never said she wanted something the registry could give her." She stopped on the flags. "Put it in the permanent pages. Under her name. Not the working ones."

He took out the observation notebook there in the court and wrote it, the date and the word, in the permanent section at the back where he kept the things that were not going to change. Lira was coming across the court behind them, slowly, on her bad leg, and he knew she could see him writing. She did not shout at him to stop. She watched the pen move, and let it, and that was the whole of what passed between them about it.

*The word was want,* the Log got that night. *In three years, every good thing the registry ever handed Lira she called late, or owed, or wrong. She never once called one wanted. Today, on the painted floor, a point short of the bracket with five bursts in her leg, she did. I've put the date in the permanent pages beside the date of the corridor. Whatever this year ends up costing, part of the bill is in her name now, and not in mine.*

---

That same week two envelopes came for Brom, and the house only ever saw one of them opened.

The first came on the thirty-first day, to Bracken's counter, in the district seat's grey stock with the district's own small seal. Bracken did not send for Brom. He carried it across to the refectory himself at the noon meal, for the second time that year, and this time he did not lay it on the table. He stood at the end of the middle table and read it aloud, all of it, which he had never done with anybody's paper in that room.

"*Review for advancement of tier. The panel is constituted: three assessors and a sigil frame, at the district seat. The practitioner's record is received and entered as it stands, the title included. Sitting: the last Fifth-day of the academic year.*" He turned the sheet to the light. "And at the foot. *Outcome anticipated: advancement, per title.*"

He lowered the sheet.

"*Anticipated*," said Bracken. "In thirteen years I have never seen that word on a district docket. A docket says when and where and who. It doesn't say what. It isn't allowed to." He looked at the word again over his spectacles, as if it might go away if he looked hard enough. "Somebody at the district seat sat down and decided that writing it was cheaper than pretending not to know it. I'd like to shake his hand. I'd also like to have him sacked." He held the sheet out across the table. "Iron, Brom. By the last Fifth-day. They've as good as written it on the door."

Brom wiped his hands on his napkin and took the sheet, and read it, and folded it twice along the creases the district clerk had put in it. Then he unbuttoned his coat and put it in the left inside pocket, with the first docket and the summons and the Velmere letters, and buttoned the coat again, and pressed the buttons down one after another with his palm.

"Thank you, Registrar," he said.

Bracken's eyes rested on the buttons. He did not say anything about where the paper had gone. Then he went back up the refectory between the long tables, and Brom picked up his spoon.

The second envelope came up with the evening post on the same day, and it was sealed with Velmere's wax.

Cael saw it on the post-room shelf when he went for his own letters: a heavy cream envelope, square, with a dark red seal on the flap pressed with the bar and the ring. The house had seen that seal a few times a year for as long as it had known him, on letters that came to Brom and went into his left inside pocket unopened until he was alone. Once, at Norhold, it had come on a letter of a single line.

Brom took it from the shelf himself at the eighth bell. He did not open it in the post room. He did not take it to the court, or the common room, or the bench by the north wall where he sometimes read things. He took it upstairs to his own room and shut the door.

Two days later it was still on his table.

Lira reported it at supper, as she reported everything that mattered, in the tone of somebody giving the price of bread. She had her bad leg up on a stool under the table, by Rooke's order, and the red drill-book open beside her plate, and she did not look up from the book.

"Second envelope's still on his table," she said. "Unopened. He's squared it up with the docket, one on the other, very neat. Edges level." She turned a page. "He's moved the lamp. It used to be on that side. Now it's on the other, so the light doesn't fall on them."

Karis put down her fork. "You went into his room."

"I did not go into his room." Lira turned another page. "My room is up the stair past his room. His door doesn't latch unless you lean on it, and he never leans on it. I go past it twice a day because I have to, to get to my bed, and to get out of my bed." She looked up, at last, at Karis. "I want it entered that I didn't go in. I wanted to. Both of those. Not on paper," she added, to Cael, who had reached for the notebook. "In your head. Some things don't go on paper."

He took his hand away from the notebook.

"And when he opens it," said Lira, "nobody asks. Not one of us. Not what it said, not whether he's all right, not whether he wants to talk about it. Nobody." She looked round the three of them, and her look stopped on Karis and stayed there, not unkindly. "Not even you."

Karis did not take offence. She considered it, as she considered a note in a margin.

"I wasn't going to ask him," she said. "I was going to be in the reading room with the door open, every evening, until he came up the stair or didn't." She picked up her fork again. "I learned that from him. He's done it for me more times than I could count, and never once let on that he was doing it." She ate. "I never let on that I noticed."

Brom came down the stair a little after, and the table's conversation turned without anybody turning it. They talked about the sitting at Ostrand the week after, and whether the coach would go at the first bell or the half, and whether Rooke would let Lira off the dawn drill to ride down, which he would, because Rooke let nothing stop anybody going to watch a thing they might learn from. Brom ate his supper and said six words in the whole of it, all of them about the coach. Then he went back up.

The envelope stayed on the table, in the dark, for one more night.

On the third evening he opened it.

Nobody saw him do it. Nobody at that table ever learned what it said, because he did not tell them, then or afterward. What they saw was his face, when he came down to supper an hour later than usual and sat in his place and pulled the bread toward him.

It was the face of a man being offered his whole ledger back.

Cael put his whole attention on it from across the table, and then made himself look away, because it was not his to read. But he had seen enough. It was not joy, and it was not grief. It was the stillness of somebody who has been handed a paper with everything on it he was ever priced out of: the house and the name and the standing of a family two generations Gold, which had looked once at a big careful boy at fourteen and found a rounding error. All of it, set down in a hand he knew, with a place at the foot for his own hand, and very possibly a date.

Nobody asked. Lira passed him the beans. Brom took some, and passed them on to Seln, who took some, and passed them on.

*Two envelopes, one week,* Cael wrote that night, in the margin of the Log, which was the only place the arrangement left him. *The district's says Iron by the year's last Fifth-day. Velmere's sat two nights in the dark on his table before he would break the wax. His tier and his family, both inside one week, after years of neither. He has said "Noted" to neither of them. From Brom that means the account is open and the sum is being done.*

*Wait. Don't ask. Lira's rule, and the right one.*

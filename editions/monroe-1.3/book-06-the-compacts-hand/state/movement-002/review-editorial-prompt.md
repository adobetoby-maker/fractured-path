# Chapter 8 — Keel

He slept past the first bell for the first time since the road, and woke with the copy still lying on the sill where it had spent the night.

The light had come for it in the end. It lay across the first leaf in a long pale bar, and the fountain was running in the court below, and somewhere along the residence front a door banged and a voice called for somebody's other boot. The ordinary morning of an ordinary house. He lay a while and listened to it, and counted what he could hear, and found that the counting did not help.

Fourteen days. Thirteen, now.

He got up and dressed and put the copy in its folder under his arm, because he did not want to leave it on the sill for the porter's boy to read, and went down.

Karis was not at breakfast. Her chair at the end of the table stood pushed in square, and her cup was gone from the rack, which meant she had been down before the bell and taken it up with her. Lira sat across from his place with her elbows either side of a bowl of porridge she was not eating. Brom ate for two, steadily, as if somebody had told him he would need it.

"She's been up there since the third bell," said Lira. "Brom heard her on the stair."

"I heard the key," said Brom.

"Did she sleep?"

"She says she slept." Lira pushed the bowl an inch away. "She also says the floor in her room has a slope to the west, and she's going to have it measured. So no."

He ate, because Brom pushed bread at him until he did, and then he went up.

Two porters were carrying a table across the top landing of the library when he reached it.

They had the door of the old lumber room propped open with a broken chair, and they were working the table through it on its side, sweating and grumbling at it in the low courteous voices porters used on the library stair. Behind them the room itself stood half empty. It had held, for as long as Cael had known it, the house's spare benches and a map case with no maps in it and two rolled carpets nobody would admit to owning. Now the carpets were out on the landing and the benches gone, and somebody had already swept the floor in long straight strokes and left the broom against the wall.

"Registrar's orders," said the older porter, when Cael held the door for them. "Table, two chairs, a shelf for big books. He says big books." He looked at the shelf, which was very big. "He says a girl's to sit in here and nobody's to go past her who hasn't been asked for."

"Which girl?"

"Second-year. Ink on her." The porter set his end of the table down. "She's already been and moved the chairs twice."

Across the landing the reading-room door stood open for the first time Cael could remember. Karis was inside at the long table with her back to the window and her sleeves pushed above her elbows. She had the code's three volumes laid out on her left, open, one above another like steps. On her right lay something he had not seen before.

It was a sheaf of paper, bound along its edge with tape, perhaps forty leaves. It had no cover and no title and no note. He came round the table to see it the right way up. Every leaf was ruled into two columns. In the left column, one paragraph of the instrument at a time, in order, stood the paragraph's number and its first words. In the right, under each, ran every section of the code the paragraph leaned on, by volume and chapter and section, and under each section a line in very small print saying where else in the registry's history that section had been used, and by whom, and how it had come out.

The hand was a copying hand. It was so even that it might have been printed, and it made no letter larger than any other.

"It was under the door when I came up," said Karis without looking round. "At the third bell. Pushed through flat, so it wouldn't crease." She turned a page of the code. "The porter's boy was sweeping this stair at the second bell. He says the wing's teaching assistant came up it with something under his arm, and went down it again without, and wished him good morning on the way."

Cael looked down at the sheaf. Forty leaves. The paragraphs of a paper served at the second bell yesterday, mapped against a code of three volumes, with the history of every section underneath. It would have taken a clerk with a good lamp a week.

"Thank him," said Cael.

"He'd hate that." She turned another page. "I'll use it instead. He'll know I've used it, because he'll see the shape of it in what goes down the hill, and that's the kind of thanks he'd let me give." She looked up at him at last. Her eyes were red at the rims and perfectly clear in the middle. "Sit down. You're going to be told the rules once, and then you're going to read."

The rules took less time than breakfast.

Four days to the answer's bones, said Karis. After that, the flesh, and then Bracken and the house's counsel and the weighing, and then it went down the hill with time in hand. Every leaf of the code that left the records hall came back to it at night, because Bracken would not sleep with the press unlocked and she would not ask him to. Brom would carry. Brom had already been told and had already said yes, in one word, at the foot of the stair at the second bell.

"Twice a day," she said. "Up in the morning, down at night. The volumes weigh more than I do. He says that's no trouble. He says it in the voice he uses when something is going to be no trouble for a month."

"And Lira?"

"Lira is in charge of the door." Karis wrote a section number on a slip and tucked it into the second volume. "Not this door. That one has the girl. The records hall's. The intake found out this morning where we sat last night. There were nine of them on the records-hall steps at the second bell, looking at the windows as if the code were going to come out and wave."

He did not ask how Lira was going to keep forty first-years off a set of steps. He found out at noon.

She had gone to Ephram. Ephram, as captain, still held the meal rota the two second-years had drafted on the first night, the one with a column of names and a table chosen for watching how Cael held a spoon. Lira asked him for it. She took it to the common room, crossed out the heading, and wrote a new one above it in her square capitals. *RECORDS HALL STEPS. ONE PAIR AT A TIME. NOBODY SITS. NOBODY ASKS. ANYBODY ELSE IS SENT TO THE FRAMES.* Then she gave it back to Ephram to sign, and he signed it, and pinned it up on the covered walk beside the house's one-line answer from the day before.

By the first bell after noon there was a pair of second-years on the records-hall steps, standing very straight. One was the Mire boy who looked at ceilings. The other was the girl from the short table with her small notebook, and she was writing in it.

"She's on both rotas," said Lira, at the rail of the covered walk, very satisfied. "Karis's and mine. She won't sleep for a week."

"She's watching who comes."

"Of course she is. I told her to." Lira looked at the steps. "Somebody might as well."

---

He read the keel that afternoon, because Karis put it in front of him first.

It was the Greyvane ruling. She had laid it down before anything else, before the paper she was answering had a single paragraph answered, on the bare table in the middle of three cleared feet of wood. The ruling, the certified transcript under it, and Prynn's own copy of the provision on top of both, in that small exact archivist's hand Cael had first seen on a high desk in a room that smelled of cold paper.

"Keel," said Karis.

He had watched them build barges on the slip below Ostrand bridge. He had stood on the bank on the house's free afternoons for whole hours, because the work had an order in it and he liked orders. The shipwrights did not start with the sides you could see. They laid one long timber down the middle of the slip first, end to end, and squared it, and pinned it, and nothing else went on until it was true. Then the ribs went up from it on both sides, and everything that came after hung from the ribs. A barge could lose a plank and float. If the keel was wrong, nothing you nailed to it afterward would keep the river out.

"Everything else hangs from this," said Karis. "Every argument in the answer comes back to one fact: a seat has already heard this question and settled it on the public record, and the paper we're answering doesn't say otherwise. It doesn't even try. So the first thing on the table is the place where the question was settled." She put her palm on the transcript and left it there. "And then I don't move it."

She uncapped her pen and drew a sheet toward her, to write the heading.

Then she stopped, and Cael watched her stop.

It was a small thing. Her pen came down an inch over the paper and stayed there. She looked across at the instrument lying open on the table's far side, at its first leaf, and he knew what she was looking at, because he had looked at it himself all night. *To the Chancellor, Halcenvane Academy, and to the enrollee of record, Caelen Hesk-ward, unclassified.*

"You answer a paper in its own words," said Karis. "Always. If they call a thing a carriage, you don't call it a cart. You make them argue with the word they chose, not with yours." She did not look at him. "They've given you your registry name. So the answer goes back to the same address. Halcenvane Academy, respondent, with the enrollee of record, Caelen Hesk-ward, unclassified, answers jointly. Do you mind?"

Nobody had asked him that about the name before. Not at Ardenmere, where it had been stamped on him; not at Greyvane, where it had been read out over his head; not on any form since. The registry gave him the name the way it gave a ledger a number. People said Cael.

"No," he said. "It's my name in that building. Write it the way they spell it."

She wrote it. He watched it go down in her finding hand, every letter, his own name on a heading he had not written, and found it did not feel like being named. It felt like being entered.

She ruled a line under it, and the keel was laid.

---

On the second day she put up the ribs.

There were three of them, and she explained them once, to the counsel, who had come up the hill again before noon with her steel rule in her coat pocket and sat at the table's end with her hands folded on it, listening.

The first was Karis's own finding from Greyvane, the long one, the one she had built from the standardization directive's repeal schedules a line at a time until she could say, flatly, in front of a seat, that nobody had ever repealed the clause. It had been left behind, like a child at a fair, when everything round it was swept up and carried off. The second was the house counsel's reconstruction, made the year after, by another road, through the registry's own index of surviving provisions, without reading a word of Karis's first. The third was Prynn's copy and the note on its cover, from the archive at Greyvane, which showed the clause standing in the founding text with nothing written against it in two hundred years of hands.

"Three roads to one town," said the counsel.

"Three roads to one town." Karis laid the three bundles side by side and pinned them together along their edges with brass fasteners, through the margin, so that none of them could be lifted out alone. "Any one would do. I'm not going to let the seat find out which. If they knock one down they'll find the other two standing in exactly the same place."

"And if they don't knock any of them down?"

"Then they've conceded the clause twice." Karis capped her pen. "Once in their paragraph six. Once by silence in front of these."

The counsel looked at the fastened bundle for a long moment, the way Cael had seen Brom look at a lift before he took it.

"That's not a rib," she said. "That's a wall."

At noon Brom came up the library stair with the second volume of the code on his shoulder, the way a miller's man carries a sack, and set it down on the shelf in the lumber room without a sound. The girl at the outer table stood up when he came in. She sat down again when he went out. Then she got up and moved the volume an inch along the shelf so that its spine stood level with the others, and sat down once more and went on writing.

Cael read everything that day. That was the arrangement: Karis wrote, and he read. Not for approval, she said; because it was about him, and she did not put her name to documents about people who had not read them. He read at the window with the light behind him and the pages on his knee, and when he had read a leaf he laid it face down on the sill, and Karis took it back without asking what he thought, because if he had thought anything he would have said.

Near the end of the afternoon she did something he did not understand.

She was deep in the provenance, with the directive's schedules open in front of her and her finger on a line. She did not lift her eyes from it. She reached behind her with her free hand to the pile of finished leaves and drew one out from near the bottom and held it over her shoulder toward him without turning round.

"Read that one tonight," she said. "First. Before you sleep."

He took it. It was the renewals table.

He looked at it, and then at her back, and waited for a reason. She turned a page of the directive and gave him none.

---

The renewals table was a single leaf, ruled in Karis's hand into a grid, and it had three rows.

He read it at his own window that night, with the fountain going in the court and the copy of the instrument shut in its folder on the bed behind him. Across the top of the grid ran the columns: the sitting, the panel, the day, the finding, the seal. Down the side ran the three times his place on the bluff had been written down by somebody obliged to write it. The grant at Greyvane, from the hearing. The first renewal, at Halcenvane, in the inspection year, before the Compact's delegation. The renewal at the mill town, last year, on the road.

Three rows. Every cell filled. Each day was entered in the form the registry used, and each finding was set down in the panel's own words.

He read it twice and found nothing. He had not expected to find anything; Karis did not make tables with things in them to find. He laid it on the sill and began to undress, and then stopped with one boot off and looked at it again.

Why first? Why tonight?

He sat down on the bed with the boot in his hand and thought about the inspection year. He had been in the room for the panel. He remembered it the way he remembered most rooms, by its count: Gault at the trestle with his case, the two instructors, the delegation's officer at the end of the row with a pen he never once uncapped. A Fifth-day. The panel had found, and Gault had written the minute in his own hand at the trestle while they all waited. Then the delegation's officer had asked for the minute to go to the Compact's officer of record for countersignature before it was sealed, and Gault had said that was within the inspection's rights and had written *held for countersignature* across the foot of it, and the officer had taken it away.

And the minute had come back. Cael remembered that too. He had been called to Gault's office to put his own initials on the receipt of the sealed copy, as the enrollee of record, and Gault had sealed it in front of him and dated the seal, and said, *There. Now it's a thing.*

That had not been the panel's day. That had been later.

He put the boot down and went to the drawer and took out the old volume of the Log.

It was full; there was no room in it for one word more. But every word that was in it was still in it, and he knew roughly where in its thick back half the inspection year began. He turned the pages under the lamp, past fights and figures and the names of rooms, and found the Fifth-day of the panel. *Renewal sat. Gault, Ash, Mire, three cards, found. Minute held for the delegation's officer.* And then he turned forward, a page at a time, counting the days between entries by the bells and the weekday names, until he found the other one. *Gault's office, called. Initialled the receipt. Minute sealed today, dated today. He said: now it's a thing.*

Nine days.

He went back to the table on the sill. The first renewal's row gave the day of the sitting, the panel's day, which was the day the house's own enrollment book carried in Bracken's small upright hand, because that was the day the house had seen it happen. The sealed minute itself, with its own date on its seal, had gone up the road to the registry's file and never come back down. The house had no copy of it. The registry did. And the only place on the continent where both days stood side by side was a book he had filled himself and put in a drawer.

He sat a long time with the two volumes open on his knees.

Then he looked at the table again, very closely, under the lamp. In the margin beside the first renewal's row, so faint that he had to tip the leaf to the light to be sure of it, sat a single pencil dot.

He took it up at the third bell, with the old volume of the Log under his arm.

Karis was already at the long table. She did not look round when he came in. She held out her hand behind her, palm up, the way she had held out the leaf, and he put the renewals table into it.

"Nine days," he said.

"Show me."

He opened the old Log on the table beside her elbow at the panel's day, and laid his finger beside the entry without touching it, and then turned forward to the other and did the same. She read both. She read them the way she read anything with a date in it, twice, once for the words and once for the numbers, and then she turned back to the first and read it a third time.

"Held for countersignature," she said. "I'd forgotten that was a thing they could ask for."

"Gault hadn't. He wrote it on the foot of the minute."

"Of course he did." She laid the renewals table down on the table, square, and looked at it as if it had been handed in by a student she had expected better of. Then she took the whole leaf out of the answer.

She did not footnote it. He had thought she would; a line at the foot of the table, a small number in the cell, a sentence somewhere saying the sealing fell nine days after the sitting for a stated reason. She took a clean sheet, ruled it fresh into the same grid, and built the table again from nothing. Every row this time gave two days, the sitting and the seal, and the first renewal's row gave the reason for the nine between them in eleven words. Under the grid she wrote the source for each day: the house's enrollment book for the sittings; the registry's own file, to be produced on request, for the seals; and for the gap, *the enrollee's contemporaneous record, available to the seat*.

It took her an hour. She had told the counsel the day before that she had no hours to spare that week, and he watched her spend this one without once looking at the clock.

"You could have put it in a note," he said, when she ruled the last line.

"I could." She blotted the new leaf. "Suppose I had. The procurator's clerk goes through the answer with a pen, which he will, and finds the nine days in the registry's file, which he will. And he files a single line. *The respondent's own table is inconsistent with the registry's record.* One line. He doesn't have to say anything else. After that, the seat reads every other leaf in this answer looking for the next mistake, and it doesn't matter that there isn't one." She laid the new table in its place in the pile and squared the pile's edge with both hands. "There is no such thing as a small error in a document about a person."

He looked at the old leaf, lying on the table on its own, out of the answer. The pencil dot sat in its margin.

"You found it," he said. "The first morning."

"I found that something was wrong with it." She did not pretend otherwise. "I didn't know what. The book had one day; the inspection's report gave the renewal a different one in a list at the back. I could have spent a morning in the inspection's papers finding out which was right." She turned a page of the directive's schedules, which was where she had been before he came in. "Or I could give the table to the only one of us who sat in both of those rooms, and not tell him why."

"Why not tell me?"

"Because if I tell you there's a mistake on a leaf, you'll find one. You're good at that. You'd have found a mistake if I'd given you a clean leaf and said so." She did look at him then. "I wanted to know what you'd find if you were only reading. You were in the room, both times. Everybody else who was there is a name on a minute. Gault's a signature. The delegation's officer is a seal. You're the only witness in this answer who can't be replaced." She went back to the schedules. "Nothing I've built has ever come out worse for being read first by the person it's about."

He stood by the table with the old Log under his arm and the realization arriving the way the read arrived on a floor, from under, all at once. She had not been testing him. She had been using him, the way she used the code's third volume and Prynn's copy and the sheaf with no note on it. As a source. The best she had on one question, so she had gone to it.

It was the most useful he had ever been to her. He found he did not mind the method in the least.

And on the way down the stair he remembered the counter, and the four sentences Seln had spent on him in a queue on the third day of the year. *Standing papers on the provision come up for the ledger's half-year check. Keep the documentation current.* The renewals table was the provision's standing papers, set out in a grid. Whatever went down the hill this week would sit in the registry's file beside them when the half-year check came, and a table that disagreed with the file by nine days would have been the first thing the check found. Karis had not heard Seln say it. She had kept the documentation current anyway, because that was what she did with documents.

---

On the fourth day, which was a Fifth-day, he went to wing three at the second bell, because the provision's hours did not stop for the registry.

It was a short bout and he fought it at the band. The Mire across from him was an Iron Rank Four who sank the boards in front of her a hand's depth at a time, so that wherever Cael meant to plant his foot the floor had turned to something nearer porridge. He read the boards before she softened them, and stepped where she had not yet been, and burst twice, and gave her the third exchange because the rotation's line for that day said the third. Two to one. The rib from the Shield had gone yellow and then gone, as he had told himself it would. Gault added the three cards on the back of his hand and gave the clerk twenty-four and two-thirds, and Ephram at the rail wrote it in his soft-folded chart and drew no question mark beside it.

Walking back across the court, he noticed what it had cost.

Not the burst, nor the exchange given away. Those were the band's ordinary prices, and he paid them every Fifth-day without looking at the bill. It was the hour. Up the library stair, Karis was spending a week of her life laying his name on a table one true day at a time, and he had spent this morning in front of three cards making sure the house's book held a slightly smaller version of him than the one she was writing down. The band had always been a daily expense. He had never before had to pay it in the same week as somebody else paid for the truth.

He did not know what to do with that, so he wrote it in the Log, under the figure, and left it there.

Karis came down to supper that night for the first time in four days.

She came in late, when the refectory was half empty and the porters had begun stacking the benches at the far end. She had washed her hands and the ink had not come off. She sat down at the end of the middle table in her own chair, and Brom, without being asked, pushed the dish of beans down the board to her, and Seln, at the middle of the bench, moved his cup out of its way by exactly the width of the dish.

She ate three mouthfuls. Then she put down her spoon.

"Bones," she said.

Nobody cheered. Lira put her elbows on the table and her chin on her fists and looked at her.

"And the rest?"

"Seven days." Karis picked up the spoon again. "Then Bracken. Then the scales."

"The scales?"

"Bracken weighs things," said Karis. "He's going to want the number." She ate another mouthful, slowly, as if she had just remembered that food was a thing a person did. "So am I."

Across the table, Seln cut his bread into exact squares, and buttered one, and did not look up. But when Karis said *bones*, his knife had stopped for a moment above the board, and Cael, who had been watching for it, had counted the moment, and it was a long one.

# Chapter 9 — Eleven Pounds

The fire-watch's oil book got a second line in its history on the thirteenth night of the year, and Bracken wrote it.

*Library, top floor. Lamps late. On the registrar's authority, and at his charge.*

Cael saw it in the morning, on his way past the hook by the records-hall side door, in the same small upright hand as the first. Two lines in thirteen years, both in one week. He stood and looked at the page longer than a page of lamp-hours deserved, and then he went up to the top of the library and found out what the lamps had been burning for.

Karis had written the integrity section in a night.

She was asleep when he came in. She was sitting upright in her chair at the long table with her arms folded on the wood in front of her and her head on her arms, and the pen had rolled out of her hand and stopped against the inkwell. The lamp at her elbow had burned itself dry and gone out. In front of her, squared into one stack so neat that it might have been cut with a blade, lay forty leaves in her finding hand. The girl at the outer table across the landing had put a shawl over Karis's shoulders and then, apparently, not dared to do anything else. She sat at her own table with her hands in her lap, guarding the door from inside the room.

"Since the eighth bell," the girl whispered. "She didn't stop. I filled the lamp twice. Then I fell asleep, and when I woke up she'd stopped." She looked at the stack as if it might go off. "She said not to wake her unless the building was on fire."

"Is it?"

The girl considered this seriously. "No."

So he sat down at the window and waited, and after the second bell Karis lifted her head off her arms, looked at the dead lamp, looked at the stack, and said in a perfectly ordinary voice, "Ninety-one."

"Good morning."

"Ninety-one." She sat back and the shawl slid off her shoulders, and she did not notice it go. "Every time anybody has ever called on the integrity provisions since the registry began writing it down. I've read every one. I found them in the registry digests. Bracken's press has the first forty years and the hall has the rest, and Seln's sheaf told me which year each section first appears in, which saved me a week." She laid her hand on the stack. "Eighty-four of them are about things. Frames. Stations. An assessor who took money in a district I won't name. Two panels whose minutes argued with themselves. A records office that lost a whole quarter's declarations in a flood and then tried to pretend it hadn't."

"And the other seven?"

"Seven are about people." She looked at him. "Every one is about something a person did. A false declaration. A bought result. An evaluator who sat on his own nephew's panel. Seven times in the registry's whole history, somebody has pointed these provisions at a human being. Not once was it about what the human being was."

He came and sat across from her. The forty pages lay between them.

"So the paper we're answering is the first," he said.

"The ninety-second. And the first of its kind." She moved the stack an inch toward him. "Read it. Then come down to the counter with me. There's somebody I want to show something to."

Bracken was at his counter, adding the house's lamp-oil accounts for the week with a pained expression, when Karis laid one leaf of the integrity section on the wood in front of him and turned it the right way round.

It was a list. Ninety-one lines, in Karis's smallest finding hand, one invocation to a line, each with its year and its district and the section called on and a few words of what it had been about. She put her finger beside line sixty-three, and took it away.

*Ostrand district station; sigil frame reading Stone practitioners one rank above their true rank over one month; integrity provisions, section four; filed by the registrar of Halcenvane in his fourth year in office; frame withdrawn, tested a fortnight, a seam in the casing found admitting damp, repaired, the month's Stones re-read; sustained.*

Bracken looked at it for a long time.

Then he put on his spectacles and looked at it again, more closely, and Cael watched the registrar read his own name in a list of ninety-one, in another person's hand, in a document that was going down the hill to answer a petition about a boy who stood two feet away from him on the other side of the counter.

"I'd forgotten I wrote it that way round," said Bracken at last. "*Admitting damp.* I'd have said *letting in* now." He was still looking at the line. "In my fourth year I thought a registrar ought to sound like a registrar."

"The original's in the registry's file," said Karis. "I copied the citation from the digest. There's one thing." She took the leaf back and turned it over, to a note at its foot. "In your filing, you spelt *casing* with two esses. I corrected it in the citation. Silently. It's a convention."

Bracken took his spectacles off.

"Don't," he said.

Karis waited.

"Put it back." He folded the spectacles and held them in one hand. "If the seat goes to my filing, which it may, and finds one spelling there and another in yours, it's one more thing that doesn't match. And it was my mistake. I made it at this counter in my fourth year with the frame's report in one hand and the pen in the other." He looked at her. "I'd sooner the record had it the way I wrote it. Mark it as mine."

Karis took the leaf back to the end of the counter and uncapped her pen. She crossed out the corrected word in the citation, very neatly, with a single ruled line, and wrote the old spelling above it, two esses, and after it, in brackets, the little word every copying clerk on the continent wrote after a quoted mistake, which meant *it was like this when I found it*.

She brought it back and laid it in front of him.

Bracken read it, and nodded once, and went back to the lamp-oil.

Cael understood, walking up the library stair behind her afterward, that he had just watched two people who did not say kind things to each other say one. Karis had put Bracken's own filing into the answer, as evidence that the integrity provisions were for frames; it was the strongest single line on the leaf, because it had been filed by a registrar at the very counter that was answering. And Bracken had asked for his own small error to be left standing in it, so that nobody could ever say the house had tidied the record to suit itself. Neither of them had said a word about why.

The house's counsel came up the hill at noon and read the forty pages backward.

She did it at the long table in her coat, with the steel rule under each line and her lips quite still, beginning at the last word of the last page and climbing. Karis sat across from her with her hands in her lap and watched her climb. Cael sat at the window. The girl at the outer table across the landing had found a reason to fill the inkwells, all of them, slowly.

It took the counsel most of the afternoon. Near the bottom of the ninth page, on her way up, the rule stopped.

"*Never,*" she said.

Karis leaned across. The counsel turned the page so that she could see it and laid the rule under one sentence. *The integrity provisions have never been turned against the nature of a practitioner, as distinct from his conduct.*

"You can't prove *never*," said the counsel. "You can prove ninety-one. A digest can miss a filing. A district can have burned its records. A seat with a mind to be awkward will ask you how you know about the ones nobody wrote down." She lifted the rule. "Say *not once in the registry's record*. Then it's a count, and it's yours, and nobody can argue with it but a man with a ninety-second filing in his hand."

Karis wrote it in. She did not argue, or explain, or say that she had meant the record. She took the correction the way Lira took a correction from the Stone on the painted floor, all at once, and moved on.

The counsel finished her climb a little before the fifth bell and set the rule across the first page.

"That's all," she said.

"That's all?"

"That's all there is." The counsel stood and buttoned her coat. "I've read a good many answers to a good many petitions. That's the best section on these provisions I've seen written by anybody, and I include my own. Put it in." She picked up her bag. "I'll be back up the hill when the rest of it's done. Send for me early. I like to read things twice."

When she had gone down the stair, Karis sat looking at the forty pages for a while with her hands folded on top of them.

"It's the best thing I've ever written," she said. "I'd like that minuted."

"Entered."

"And I'd like this minuted next to it." She did not look up. "I know exactly what it's worth. It's worth what a record is worth. Every argument in there is right, and the seat can read every one of them and agree with every one of them, and then turn the page." She smoothed the top leaf with her palm. "I'm not a fool about it. I just want it written down that I knew."

"Both entered," said Cael.

She looked up then. "When did you start saying *entered* like that?"

"About a week ago. In the records hall." He held up the observation notebook. "She keeps the registry's minute. Somebody ought to be keeping ours."

Karis looked at him a moment longer, as if checking a figure. Then she did a thing she had been doing since the road home from Norhold and had never once remarked on. She took the last page of the forty off the bottom of the stack, unasked, and held it out to him across the table, so that he would read the end before anybody else did. Then she turned away to the window and stood with her back to him while he read it.

On the sixteenth day Cael took his sheet for the next Fifth-day down to the wing, because the provision's hours were lodged at the counter or nowhere, and the registry did not care what week it was.

The queue had shortened since the first days. The intake had learned the counter's hours and stopped coming in a body. A single Ash first-year stood ahead of him with a form she had filled in twice, once in pencil and once over the pencil in ink. Behind the counter the desk clerk added his column by the window. Gault's door stood shut. And at the copying table, under the shelf with the small unmarked case on it, the teaching assistant rose to take the first-year's sheet and read it from the name to the signature at his even pace, and stamped it, and tore the slip.

Then Cael's turn.

Seln took the sheet. He read it. Name; the Path line blank by rule; evaluator of record; hours; reason. The stamp came down. The slip tore along its rule.

"The Magister signs on Fifth-days," said Seln.

"Karis used the sheaf," said Cael. "All of it. Every leaf."

Seln laid the sheet on the stack at his left hand.

"The office keeps a register of everything that leaves this counter," he said, to the stack. "Forms. Slips. Copies on application. There is no sheaf in it." He set the stack's edge true. "There's nothing to have been used."

"Then I'll tell her that."

"Mm."

Cael had his fingers on the copy-slip. He had turned half away from the counter when Seln spoke again, in exactly the same voice, as if reading the next line of the same form.

"The second volume's own index runs eleven years behind the volume. The digests are better for the late years." He had already turned back to the copying table. "If anyone should happen to be working from the second volume."

Cael went out through the wing doors and along the covered walk, and did not let himself smile until he was under the north arch.

He had thought, for two years, that Seln did not give things. He saw now that this was wrong. Seln gave things all the time. He gave forms before anybody had asked for them, and a sheaf under a door before dawn, and a correction about an index to a boy at a counter who could carry it upstairs. He gave them in a way that made thanks impossible, because there was no record of the thing and so nothing to thank him for. It was not modesty. Cael did not think it was even kindness, exactly. It was a man who had spent his life on the side of a counter where everything that passed across it was written down, making very sure that the one thing he chose to give stayed off the paper.

He thought about the quiet thing he carried, sealed, and the debt it was sealed for, which the man at the copying table did not know he was owed. He thought that it would be a very hard debt to pay to somebody who would not let anything be paid to him.

He went up the library stair and told Karis about the index. She went straight to the digests without a word. Twenty minutes later she found that one of her ninety-one, a coastal panel that had sat a member short, carried a year she had taken from the second volume's index, and the index had it eleven years late. She ruled the year through and wrote the right one above it. It changed nothing in the argument. It went in anyway.

Bracken read the whole answer in two nights.

He read it at his counter, every leaf, from the address to the last schedule. The oil book took a third line and a fourth. He did not skim. Cael came past the records-hall windows late on the first night on his way back from the north wall, and saw him through the glass, a narrow grey shape bent over a single lamp, turning a leaf, reading it, initialling its corner, turning the next. A registrar's initials on a leaf were a promise about the leaf. Bracken did not make promises he had not read.

On the second night it rained.

It was the first rain of the year, and it came all at once a little after the tenth bell, out of a sky that had been brass-coloured and heavy since noon. It roared on the long roof of the records hall and came off the gutters in ropes, and the dust in the court turned to a smell before it turned to mud. Cael stood under the covered walk's north arch and watched it, with the fire-watch boy beside him holding his slate against his chest to keep the hours dry. Across the court, in the records hall's one lit window, Bracken did not look up.

In the morning the court steamed. Bracken sent the porter's boy up the library stair with a message for Karis, and the message was one word long, and Karis read it on the landing and came down at once with the boy still trying to keep up.

The word was *Scales*.

They weighed it in the post room behind the counter, on the brass beam the house used for parcels going down the hill, where letters were weighed for their postage and nobody had ever weighed anything that mattered. The answer had been bound in two parts, because the binder's thread would not go through it in one, and the two parts lay side by side in a leather document wallet with the house's seal on the flap. Bracken set the wallet on the pan himself. He put on the brass weights one at a time from the box, the big ones first, and then the small, and then the very small ones that lived in their own velvet slots, until the beam came level and stayed.

"Eleven pounds," said Bracken. He bent to read the last weight. "And four ounces."

He took a small book out of his coat and wrote the number in it. It was not the house's book. Cael had never seen it before. It was a pocket ledger in black oilcloth, worn white at the corners, and from where he stood he could see a long column of entries in it, each a single line, each with a date and a figure.

"Every answer the house has filed in my time," said Bracken, to nobody, and put it away. "I like to know what they weighed."

Withrow came in while he was taking the weights off.

She stood in the post-room door with her hands folded in front of her and looked at the wallet on the pan. The registry's ordinary post went down the hill twice a day. It would have carried eleven pounds without remark, in a sack, among the house's letters to tailors.

"No," said Withrow, before anybody had asked her anything. "Not in the bag."

"It's the ordinary way," said Bracken. "The seat accepts the post."

"I know what the seat accepts." She came and laid one hand on the wallet's flap, on the house's seal. "Theirs came up our road with three riders in grey, at a walk, so that every soul on this hill would stand still and watch it come. I watched. So did you." She took her hand away. "Ours goes down the same road the same way. Our seal, our riders, our pace. Let the town stand still and watch it arrive." She turned to Bracken. "The head groom and his son, on the greys. Your senior clerk in the middle with the case across his saddle. In the house's colours. Fifth-day, at the second bell."

"That's three days inside the time," said Bracken.

"Yes," said Withrow. "I'd like them to notice that too."

It went down the hill on the nineteenth day, a Fifth-day, at the second bell.

Cael watched from the covered walk with half the house beside him. Nobody had announced anything. Nobody needed to. The head groom came out of the stable yard on the bigger of the two greys in the coat he wore at convocation and nowhere else, and his son on the other grey looking as if he had been told to sit up straight and was doing it with his whole body. Between them rode Bracken's senior clerk, a long patient man who had kept the enrollment book for nine years and had never in his life been looked at by so many people at once. The wallet was strapped across the front of his saddle under a fold of the house's own cloth, and he kept one hand flat on it the whole way across the court.

They went out under the arch at a walk.

The lodge-man stood as straight as he stood for the Chancellor. The intake crowded the north wall to watch them take the first switchback, and the second, and the bend above the trees. Lira was on the wall with them, and Brom, and somewhere behind Cael on the walk a third-year said, half laughing and half not, *Is that it? Is that the whole answer?* and somebody else said, *That's eleven pounds of it,* and nobody laughed after that.

At the foot of the road the two coats watched the escort go by them onto the river street. Neither turned his head to follow it. They looked at it as it passed, the way they looked at a cart of hay, and then went back to looking at the gate.

Karis did not come down to watch. Cael looked up at the top of the library, at the reading room's window, and she was not in it. She had gone to sleep in her chair the moment the wallet left the counter, the girl at the outer table told him afterward, with her boots still on and her key on its cord in her fist.

He wrote it that night at the sill, with the copy of the instrument beside him and the answer's first leaf lying in his memory where he could read it with his eyes shut.

*Eleven pounds four ounces, down the hill at a walk, under our own escort, three days inside the time. The best paper anyone has ever written about me. I read every leaf. I can't find a seam, and I was looking. Karis says it's perfect, and in two years I have never seen her call a thing perfect that wasn't.*

*I believe her. I also believe the corridor. Tonight I can carry both of them, one in each hand. I haven't yet had to find out what happens when I need a hand free.*

The north wall was warm under him from the day's sun, and the valley was going blue, when Lira came along the top of it from the east end.

She walked the coping itself, not the path beside it, as she always did; it was narrower, so it was quicker. Her hands were still taped from the evening drill and she had a heel of bread in one of them. She broke it in two without looking, gave him the larger half, and sat down beside him with her legs over the drop.

"Brom's wearing out a word," she said.

He knew which.

"He says it at the frames. Under his breath, between sets, like a man counting." She bit the bread. "He did it twice yesterday and once this morning, and he doesn't know he's doing it. Brom never says anything twice unless he hasn't finished chewing it." She looked at him sideways. "So you're going to give it to me. All of it. Small."

He gave it to her backward, the way the house's counsel read a paper.

He started at the end, with the morning after, because that was where a person actually lived. The ferryman with a letter, the guild hall with a letter, the clerk with a sealed file and no key. A daughter at Withrow's counter every week for a year, asking whether anybody had begun the review, and nobody ever obliged to begin it. Then the three steps of chapter fourteen in Bracken's voice that would not let itself be a voice, and the third word that nobody had ever had to stamp. Then the six slips of paper laid out down the long table like stones across a stream, each a door, each real law, each opening toward the next and never back the way you had come.

He did not tell it well. He told it in order, which was the only way he knew.

Lira ate her bread and watched the river. When he finished she brushed the crumbs off her tapes, one hand and then the other.

"They had a slate at Fenmark," she said.

He waited.

"In the hall. Three examiners at a table, and in the middle of the table a slate in a wooden frame." She held up her hands a foot apart. "They wrote on the slate first. While I fought. Then, when it was over, the one in the middle copied it out fair onto the form, and wiped the slate with his sleeve, so that the form would have no crossings-out on it." She put her hands down. "I watched him wipe it. I remember thinking how careful that was."

"And the form?"

"Was right." She said it simply. "Every line. My Path was Wind. My tier was Copper. My name was spelt properly. The examiners were examiners, and they'd done every step the book had in it, in the book's order, and then they wrote it down clean." She looked at the water. "That was the part I couldn't stand. Not the walk out. Everybody thinks it's the walk out. It was standing in front of that table knowing there wasn't one wrong word on the form, and knowing just as well that the whole room was wrong. I didn't have a way to say both in the same breath. I used to lie awake trying."

"Corridor," said Cael.

"Corridor." She tried it, slowly, the way she tried a new step on the painted lines. "I could have used that four years ago, near enough. I had to make do with being angry." She almost smiled. "Being angry is a very poor word. It's all one size."

The ferryman was lighting the lamp on the landing's post. The two coats there turned their faces from it, as they always did when the flame went up, so as not to be blinded for the hour that followed.

"Here's the other half," said Lira. "I didn't find a door in that hall. There wasn't one. I walked out of the front, because the front was where they pointed, with my form in my pocket and no idea where I was going. Three streets on, I heard a noise like somebody hitting a boiler with a chain, and I went to look." She turned her head toward him. "It was Vell's yard. Nobody sent me there. It wasn't on any form. It was just round the back of everything, where the town kept the things it didn't want to look at, and the things it didn't want to look at turned out to include me."

She stood up on the coping, all at once, the way she came up off a cross.

"So I believe Karis," she said. "Every door opens one way. I've stood in that building. But there's usually a yard out the back." She looked down at him. "Not that anybody draws it on the plan."

She went off along the top of the wall toward the residence, quick and narrow in the dusk, and he sat with the rest of his bread and watched her go until she dropped off the coping at the far end and was gone.

He wrote it before the light went entirely.

*Lira, on the wall: at Fenmark they wiped the slate so the form would be clean, and every word on the form was true, and the room was wrong. She's been carrying that without a word for it since. Now she has Karis's.*

*And she has a second word of her own, which she gave me on purpose. A yard out the back. She's started walking the walls of this thing already, the way she walks a floor before a bout, quietly, with her eyes down, looking for the place the boards give. She won't bring it to the table until she has something she can measure. Vell's yard taught her that a building has more ways out of it than the ones painted on the door. I was taught by people who only ever used the front.*

# Chapter 10 — Per Seal

The seat's answer came up the hill in the noon bag on the twenty-third day, four days after the house's had gone down, and Bracken opened it at his counter with the paper-knife he kept for the registry's letters and nobody else's.

Cael was at the counter because he had been sent for. So was Karis, with ink on her cuffs and the girl from the outer table two steps behind her carrying a notebook she had not been told to bring. Withrow was not there. She had asked to have the notice read to her in her office afterward, by Bracken, in full, and had said she would sooner hear it once from a registrar than four times from a corridor.

The notice was one leaf, printed on the registry's thin grey stock, with the seat's seal at the foot. Bracken read it to himself first, all of it, without a sound. Then he turned it on the counter so that they could read it with him, and laid his finger at the top, and read it aloud in the voice he kept for fees.

*The seat at Ostrand gives notice that it has convened formal proceedings upon the petition served on the eighth day of the year. The seat will sit on its Fifth-days, upon the schedule of the academic year, and will rule before the year's close. Counsel of record will confer with the seat's clerk upon the schedule on the twenty-fifth day. The first sitting will be held on the sixth Fifth-day of the year. Senior evaluators of the district will be called to the evaluation seat on a day to be notified. Appearing for the petitioner as procurator of record: Jent. The presiding seat: per seal.*

Nobody said anything for a moment. Behind them, in the hall, the porter's boy was sweeping, and the broom went on along the boards and stopped, as if the boy had felt the quiet and did not want to be the loudest thing in it.

"Jent," said Cael.

It was the name from the last leaf of the instrument, set under the attestation in type no larger than the rest. He had read it on the night of the procedure and passed it to Bracken, and Bracken had not known it. He knew it no better now. Cael watched him look at the four letters as if they might belong to somebody he had once met at a district sitting and forgotten, and then decide that they did not.

"Still nobody I know," said Bracken. "Which is still information." He moved his finger down to the last line. "This is the part I don't like."

"Per seal," said Karis.

"Per seal." Bracken took his finger off the paper. "There's a section in the second volume, near the back, for proceedings the registry chooses to call institutional instead of personal. The idea is that a seat in such a matter speaks for the registry as a body, and not for whichever officer happens to be sitting in it that morning. So the record names the seat by its seal, and not the officer by his name." He looked at the notice. "It's lawful. It's been in the code longer than I've been alive. I've read that section more times than I could count, because a clerk reads everything. In thirteen years at this counter I have never once seen it used."

"So a seal is going to rule on me," said Cael.

"Whichever officer has the seal in his keeping that morning will rule on you," said Bracken. "The minute will show the seal. It won't show the hand that pressed it." He folded the notice once, along the line where it had come folded, and laid it on the counter between them. "I can tell you the names of every registrar who has sat that seat in thirteen years. I can tell you which of them likes a long sitting and which of them takes his luncheon at the eleventh bell whatever the advocate is saying. I can't tell you which of them will be under the hood. Neither can anybody else in this building. That is what the section is for."

Karis had not moved. She was looking at the last line of the notice upside down, and her face had gone the colour it went when a document had done something she had not predicted and she was deciding whether to respect it.

"It's very well done," she said at last.

"It is," said Bracken.

"I hate it," said Karis, and went back up the library stair without another word. The girl from the outer table looked at the notice once, hard, as if to fix it in her head, and went after her.

Cael wrote it that night with the folded notice beside him on the sill. He had taken a copy at the counter in his own hand, every word, and he read it over twice before he put the pen down to the Log.

*The seat's notice. Sittings on its Fifth-days, the ruling before the year's close. For the petitioner, Jent, the name off the last leaf, who still means nothing to Bracken. And for the seat itself, no name at all. Per seal.*

*Every person who has ever decided a thing about me had a face, and I could read it. Pellin at Denvash, with her brass plate. Coss across a desk. Vastin with his frames. The provost who was frightened, and showed it. Umber, polishing his spectacles over a sheet he couldn't score. I have read every one of them from across a room, and every reading has been worth something.*

*They've filed the face out of it. By a section of the code nobody has used in thirteen years, the seat that rules on my enrollment has chosen to be nobody. There will be a hood, and a hand under the hood, and a seal in the hand, and the minute will only ever show the seal.*

*Given under the Compact's hand. I always read that as a way of speaking, like a ship's name on a letter. Somebody has decided to make it true.*

The coach went down the bluff road on the twenty-fifth day at first light, with the sky over the river still the colour of dishwater and the switchbacks pale in front of the horses.

Withrow's carriage went first. It was a small closed thing with a single good horse, and she rode in it alone with the house's counsel, because she said there were things a chancellor and her counsel said to each other on the way to a seat that nobody else in the house should be made to keep. The delegation coach came behind, the big one that had carried them to Norhold and back, with its driver in the coat he had worn for the whole of the last season. The five of them sat inside it with the windows down for the cool, and nobody had been told to come except Cael. The others had come anyway. Withrow had seen them at the coach door and had looked at them one after another, and then had told Bracken to enter them on the day's list as the house's attendance. That was all the asking there had been.

Seln sat at the far end of the bench by the door with a sheaf of paper on his knee.

It was bound along its edge with tape. Cael saw it, and saw Karis see it, and saw her look at it for about as long as she would have looked at a stranger's luggage, and go back to the summary. Seln turned a leaf. He did not look up. He had come on the day's list as *records support*, which was what Bracken had decided he was, and he was supporting the records by reading, with great attention, a document that the wing's register said did not exist.

At the foot of the bluff the road went along the river street to the bridge, and on the bridge the coach slowed behind a cart of barrels, and Karis put down the summary and looked out of the window at the water.

"He'll concede it," she said.

Nobody asked who. She went on to the river.

"Whoever he is. In the first minute, I think. He'll take the answer and agree with all of it. The clause, the provenance, the renewals, the ninety-one. All of it." She turned her head. It's a man moving his guard somewhere you haven't looked."

"Then where should we be looking?" said Lira.

"At whatever he doesn't concede," said Karis, and picked up the summary again.

Ostrand's registry seat was the old wool exchange, which stood where the market street ran out at the top of the hill.

Cael had walked past it a dozen times on the house's errands and never looked at it twice. It was a long stone hall raised four steps above the street on a plinth, with tall plain windows and a great double door in the middle, and in the old days, Bracken said, the whole wool clip of the valley had been sold across its floor by the bale. The trade had moved downriver to the confluence before Bracken was born. The registry had taken the building because it was large and cheap and stood at the top of a hill, which the registry liked. It still smelled faintly of sheep in the heat. When the coach stopped at the foot of the steps and the door was opened, the smell came in at once, warm and greasy and not at all unpleasant, like the inside of a new coat.

There was a joiner at the side of the building, on a short ladder, hanging a door.

It was a new door in an old wall. The stone had been cut round it so lately that the dust still lay white on the sill, and the joiner was fitting the second hinge with a mouthful of screws. Beside him a registry officer stood with a ruled board under his arm and watched the hinge go on as if he would be blamed personally if it went on crooked.

"For the sittings," said the house's counsel, at Cael's elbow. She had got down from Withrow's carriage while he was looking. "They expect people on the steps. A great many people. The front door is for the public, and the public is going to be in the way." She looked at the new door with professional interest. "They've thought about you. That's not always a compliment."

The procedural conference was not held in the hall at all. It was held in a room at the back of the building, through a passage and up a short stair, in what had once been the wool-factors' counting room and was now a district office with a long table and eight chairs and the registry's plain sigil painted on the wall above the hearth. A window at the far end looked straight down the market street, over the roofs, to the wharfs and the river and the bluff beyond it with the house along its top.

Jent was already in the room when they came up the stair, and he stood up.

He was not a big man. That was the first thing Cael counted, and he counted it because he had expected something else without knowing he expected it. He had expected size, or presence, or at least a voice. What stood up from the far side of the table was a man of middle height and middle years in a brown coat that had been good once and had been looked after since, with the cuffs mended in a slightly different brown, and a pair of spectacles on a black cord round his neck. Karis was not an advocate. She was an enrollee with an Iron Rank Three on the porter's wall and a reading room with a lock on it. Cael saw her hear the word and saw her not correct it. Jent had looked at the answer, and at the woman carrying it, and chosen the most exact word he had for the person who had made it.

He nodded to Brom and Lira and Seln as the house's attendance. Then he looked at Cael, briefly, as a man looks at a party in a matter, and nodded to him too, and went back round the table and sat down and unscrewed the inkwell.

The seat's clerk arrived a minute later, a thin young man with a schedule, and the conference began.

It was dull. Cael had been told it would be dull, and it was, and he found himself grateful for every dull minute of it. The clerk read the schedule. The house's counsel agreed to the schedule. Jent agreed to the schedule. The clerk wrote down that both counsel of record had agreed to the schedule, and read back what he had written, and both counsel of record agreed that he had written it. The sittings would be on the seat's Fifth-days. The first would be the sixth Fifth-day of the year. The evaluation seat was still to be empanelled. The clerk would notify.

Then the exhibit lists went across the table, and Cael stopped being grateful.

The house's list was eleven pages long and ran to a hundred and seventy-three items, each numbered, each described in a line of Karis's smallest hand. The counsel slid it across the table with one finger. Jent drew it to him and read the first page, every line, with his spectacles on. Then he turned to the last page and initialled the receipt at its foot and slid the list back, and did not read the ten pages in between.

His own list was a single leaf. He laid it on the table and turned it round to face them, and the counsel drew it across, and Cael read it upside down from where he sat.

There were nine items on it.

*The petition as served. The convening's minute. The clause, as certified by the registry. The standardization directive, with its schedules. The advisory upon the fielding clause, of the prior year. The adjudication office's referral of the enrollee's tournament entry. The registry code, chapter fourteen. The registry code, the integrity provisions. The registry code, the schedule of the registry's discretions.*

He did the arithmetic without meaning to. It was the first thing he did in any room with paper in it, and he could no more have stopped it than stopped reading a sign.

Nine things. Not one of them a witness. Not one of them a finding of fact about anybody. Not one of them anything a person could be asked a question about, or cross-examined on, or caught out in. Every item on the list was a published text, a thing that already existed in the registry's own files in a dozen certified copies, a thing you could not prove or disprove because there was nothing in it to prove. A man did not bring a list like that to show a seat that something had happened. He brought it to read aloud, at the right moment, from the right page.

Every one of the nine was there to be cited.

"Nine," said the house's counsel, as if confirming the number of chairs.

"Nine," said Jent. He had taken his spectacles off and was polishing them on a corner of his coat. "I'll ask the seat to take the respondent's answer into the record whole, if the respondent has no objection. Not the summary. The whole of it." He put the spectacles back on. "The summary is very good. The answer is better. I'd sooner the seat had the better one in front of it."

He laid his hand flat on the wallet that held the house's answer, which the clerk had set down at his end of the table. He did not pat it. He simply let his palm rest on the leather for a moment, as a man rests his hand on a horse he has admired.

"I've read it twice," he said. "The best-documented enrollment I've reviewed in thirty years. Genuinely." That last word went across the table to Karis, and it came as plainly as a measurement. "Any of the four older challenges to the clause would have been over before its first adjournment, if somebody had done the directive's schedules for it like that. The renewals table I have never seen done better. I'd like you to know that someone on this side of the table noticed."

"Thank you," said Karis.

Cael heard it and turned his head. In two years he had never once heard Karis thank anybody for a compliment. She took them as she took the weather, without comment, and went on with what she was doing. But she had said it now, plainly, looking at Jent. Then she looked down at the table, and Cael understood that she had thanked him because she had seen, a moment before anybody else, what he was going to say next, and wanted the thanks entered before it.

For the rest of the conference Cael read him.

He had four measures he trusted, and he ran them in order.

The hands first, because the hands told the truth when the face had learned not to. Jent's hands lay on the leaf in front of him, one either side of the inkwell, square to the table's edge. When he wrote, one hand wrote and the other held the leaf. When he stopped writing, both hands came back to where they had been. They did not go to his face, or his collar, or the cord of his spectacles. They did not tap. They did not do anything they had not been asked to do.

The breath next. It was even. It did not change when the counsel asked him a hard question, and it did not change when she asked him an easy one.

Then the weight. Cael let the read go out of him, very quietly, down through his own feet into the old floorboards of the counting room and along them under the table to the far side, the way he would never have let it go in a ring with a judge watching. It came back with the man's weight in his chair. Square. Settled, in the middle of the seat, as a sack settles. In forty minutes it did not once shift forward toward a point he wanted to make, or back from one he wanted to avoid. It sat where it had been put.

And last, the thing he trusted most of all: the half-beat. Every person he had ever read had one. When a question came that cost something, there was a sliver of time before the answer when the person decided how much of the answer to give, and you could see the deciding if you knew where to look. Coss had had a long one. Vastin had had a short one, honest, like a man checking a figure. Even Umber had had one.

Jent had none.

Cael watched for it through every question the counsel asked. Question and answer followed each other with no space between them. Jent was never fast. Just without any gap at all, because there was nothing in the man choosing between one answer and another. There was only the answer.

Four measures. Four nothings.

"I'll save the seat some of its time," said Jent near the end, when the clerk had asked whether counsel had anything further for the schedule. "And yours. The documentation isn't in issue. The provision isn't in issue. The petition doesn't need either of them to be wrong, and I wouldn't insult the respondent's answer by pretending to argue with it." He squared his leaf. "Two questions remain, and the petition stands on them: whether there's an integrity question at all, and what the remedy should be. Both are matters for the registry's discretion. I'll put them to the seat on its schedule. The respondent will answer. The seat will rule."

He screwed the lid onto his inkwell, and put it in his coat, and stood.

"Chancellor," he said. "Counsel. Advocate."

Then he looked at Cael once more, and inclined his head, exactly as far as a procurator inclines it to a party.

"Hesk-ward," he said.

He went out at a clerk's pace, and they heard him go down the short stair, and the conference was over at the precise minute the code allowed for it and not one minute after.

---

The house's counsel rode back in the coach.

She had asked to. Withrow had gone on alone in her carriage with the notice and the schedule and a face that said she would like an hour by herself before she was anybody's chancellor again. The counsel sat in the corner by the window with her bag on her knees and the steel rule in her coat pocket, and for the length of the market street she said nothing at all.

On the bridge she spoke, to the window, in the voice of somebody revising a long opinion out loud.

"He hasn't come to argue," she said. "I've faced men who came to argue. You can always tell them. Some ground they always fall back to. Some clause they're too fond of. A point they'd sooner lose the whole case than give up." She watched the river go under the coach. "He has none of that. He's come to carry a paper from one end of a schedule to the other at the pace the code sets, and lay it on the seat's table at the far end, and let the seat do what it was built to do." She turned from the window. "Every man who has beaten me in twenty-six years wanted to, and badly. This one doesn't want anything. I've no idea yet what that does to my record."

Seln, at the far end of the bench, had his eyes shut and the taped sheaf on his knee.

"You've met hundreds," he said, without opening them. "They're called clerks. You've just always had the good chair."

Lira laughed out loud, and put her hand over her mouth, and laughed again behind it. Brom's shoulders moved once. Even the counsel's mouth did something at one corner that she did not let it finish. Seln did not open his eyes. But Cael, across the coach, saw his hand lie quiet on the sheaf, and thought that the man had very possibly just made the first joke of his life in front of witnesses.

Karis did not laugh. She was looking at the summary in her lap without reading it.

"He conceded it," she said. "All of it. In the first minute. I told you he would."

"You did," said Brom.

"And it felt like winning." She folded the summary in half. "Exactly as I said it would. I said it, and I knew it, and it still did." She put the summary in her coat. "I'd like that noted too."

"Noted," said Cael, and it was.

He wrote Jent up that night at the sill, in the observation notebook first and then in the Log, and the two entries did not say the same thing.

The notebook got the measures: hands square, breath even, weight settled and never shifted, no half-beat. A brown coat with mended cuffs. An inkwell with a screw lid. *Advocate,* to Karis. *Hesk-ward,* to me. Nine exhibits, all texts, all to be cited. Forty minutes, to the minute.

The Log got what the measures meant.

*Four nothings. I've never come away from a man with four nothings before. I've come away from every one of them with something: Coss with the wanting-me-gone all over his desk, Vastin with his need for the measurement to be true, Umber with his sheet, the provost with his fear of blame. Each of them wanted one thing more than the room, and whatever a man wants more than the room is the door you go in by.*

*Jent praised Karis's work and meant every word. He gave away the whole of our answer before the clerk had sharpened his pen. Giving it away didn't hurt him. You can't lose what was never yours.*

*He's a hand. Somebody else is the arm.*

*And whoever that is has learned something since Greyvane. You can't send a man with an appetite to deal with a boy who reads them. So they've sent a man without one, and put a hood on the seat, and taken the name off the chair. Two years of reading faces, and they've answered it by sending me none.*

He read it over once. Then he went to the window and looked across the dark court to the library, and at the top of it, in the reading room, Karis's lamp was still burning, which meant the oil book would have another line in it in the morning.

# Chapter 11 — Choosing

Withrow sent for him at the ninth bell that evening, by the porter's boy, with no reason given.

Her office was on the first floor of the old range, at the end of a corridor nobody used except to go to it. Cael had been in it perhaps a dozen times in two years, and every time he had been told something he would sooner not have heard. He knocked. She said *Come*. He went in, and the first thing he saw was not Withrow at all.

It was a folder, lying open on her desk, turned round to face the visitor's chair.

It was a plain buff folder of the kind the registry issued, thick, worn soft at its spine, with the house's name lettered on its front in an old clerk's hand that had faded to brown. He knew what it was before he was close enough to read the lettering. Every chartered house on the continent kept one, and every inspector who ever came up its road read it first, from the front, before he looked at a single floor. He had never seen this one. No enrollee had. It lived in the locked case under the framed charter on the wall behind her, and the case had a key, and the key was on Withrow's chain.

"Sit," said Withrow. "Read it first. I'll talk afterward. You'll want to have seen it before you hear me describe it, because you've never once in two years believed a description of anything."

He sat. She did not sit. She stood at the window with her back to the room and her hands folded behind her, and looked out at the court in the last of the light, and let him read.

It was the house's life in leaves. The charter's grant was first, on heavier paper than the rest, under a seal gone brown with age. Then came the inspections, one after another, each with its date and its inspector's name and its finding. Near the front there were two notes of the kind no house wanted, both from long before Withrow's time: a fire-watch that had failed to keep its book for a quarter, and a records office that had let a year's declarations go unindexed. Beside each, in the right-hand column, stood the remedy the house had made, and beside the remedy a clearance, stamped and dated and initialled by whoever had come to see that it was done. The house's placings were there, every meet, every year, a long thin column of figures. Last year's was the only one with *third* in it.

He turned to the last leaf in use.

It was dated the eighth day of the year. It was in the registry's flat grey type, and somebody had sent it up the road to be pinned into the file, because the house was required to keep its own file current with whatever the registry entered against it.

*Respondent: the academy of Halcenvane, in formal proceedings before the seat at Ostrand. Subject of proceedings: the basis on which it enrolled Caelen Hesk-ward, unclassified, its enrollee of record. Status: pending.*

The right-hand column beside it was ruled, like all the others, and empty.

The empty column held him longer than the rest of the file had. It was the same width as the columns beside the fire-watch and the records office. It had been ruled by the same machine. And there was nothing in it, because nothing had happened yet that could go in it.

"That entry stays," said Withrow, at the window.

He looked up.

"Whatever the seat decides." She had not turned round. "If the seat finds for us on every point, that leaf stays in that file. Every inspector who comes up our road from now until long after I'm dead will open this folder from the front, and turn the leaves, and come to that one, and read that this house was once a respondent in formal proceedings about the basis on which it enrolled a boy. And then he'll read the column." She turned at last. "If we have every piece of luck there is, a clerk will one day write *sustained* in that column and stamp it. The leaf will still be there above the stamp."

"I know," said Cael.

"You didn't. You knew that the house was named. You didn't know what *named* looks like in a buff folder, in a case under a charter, for the next fifty years." She came and sat down across the desk from him, and folded her hands on it. "Now you do. That's one column of the ledger. I'm going to give you both."

---

She gave him both, and she gave them in her registry voice, which was not the voice she used at convocation and not the one she used across this desk to a third-year who had broken a window. It was flatter than either. It was the voice in which she had read the first page of the instrument aloud in the records hall, and he understood that she had chosen it because it was the voice in which a column of figures was read.

The house was the named respondent. In registry grammar that was not a courtesy, not a way of addressing the paper to someone who would see it was answered. The house had been put inside the petition beside him. Whatever the seal decided about his enrollment, it decided about the house's judgment in making it. If the seat found that the house had been wrong to enroll him, it found against the house's judgment, and the finding went into the folder. A chartered house with an adverse finding in its folder was a house whose next inspection would not be eleven quiet days of an officer declining to find anything. It would be a long inspection. It would be a hungry one.

"Here is what that finding would sit on top of," said Withrow, and laid her hand flat on the desk beside the folder, as if setting each thing down on it as she named it.

Third on the continent, from a house the continent had stopped expecting anything of. A Silver program, chartered on the year's first morning, with its own floor and its own line in the accounts and a newly made Silver who had asked to come and teach in it. A research standing that the council had sat twice to find a heading for, with a budget and a lock and a second-year assistant under it. An intake larger than any she had admitted. And the house's name written down, this year, in other people's books, in places where houses like this one were not usually written down at all.

"I built my whole tenure toward every one of those," said Withrow. "I have set every one of them down on the table next to your name. Nobody made me. I did it on the morning I signed your roster, by declining to pretend you were something you aren't." She did not lower her eyes. "I'd do it again. You should hear the price from me, once, plainly, across a desk. Then there's nothing left for you to lie awake over at three in the morning."

He did not say anything. He had learned in two years that when Withrow told you a thing in that voice, the right answer was to have heard it.

"Now the other column," she said.

She reached across and closed the folder, gently, and laid it to one side.

"Before I was a chancellor I was twenty years a registry officer. You know that. Before your name ever came to this desk I had the Greyvane transcript sent up from Ostrand, and I read it until I could have recited the finding." She sat back in her chair. "I didn't read it as your chancellor, because I wasn't. I read it as a registry officer who has watched a great many clever advocates make a great many bad clauses look good, and I was looking for the seam. The place where somebody had made the law say what it did not say." She shook her head once. "It wasn't there. The law was on your side at Greyvane. It was just on your side."

"And now?"

"Now I've read their paper eleven times. I've read every leaf of what Karis sent down the hill, and I've read her six slips of paper on the long table, and I've read the word she wrote in the minute." Withrow's voice did not change at all. "Here is what the registry officer in me sees. The law is still on your side. On every point that can be argued in front of a seat, it is on your side. And Karis is right about the doors."

She let that sit. The last of the light had gone off the window behind her, and the room was getting dark, and neither of them moved to light the lamp.

"Both of those are true," said Withrow. "The law is ours, and the doors open one way. I've known it since the night of the slips. Any registry officer would. So I have a choice about which of those two true things I put my weight on." She leaned forward and set both hands on the desk between them, flat, a hand's width apart. "I am going to keep putting my weight on the first. I am going to keep believing, out loud, in front of that seat and the town and the broadsides and every inspector who ever opens that folder, that it matters that the law is ours. I know where the corridor goes. I'm walking it anyway, with this whole house on my back, on purpose." She looked at him steadily. "Make a note of the word *choosing*."

He took out the observation notebook. He did not ask whether she wanted it written now; she had said *make a note*, and she meant now. He turned to her pages, the section he had kept on her since his first week on the bluff, two years of small facts in his own cramped hand: the way she crossed a court, the length of her convocation speeches, the number of times she had lifted a page to stop a hall's noise. *Load-bearing calm*, from the first morning of this year. Under it all he wrote the date, and the word, and drew a line under the word.

Then, because the notebook took observations and this was one, he wrote what the word was.

*She has known about the corridor since the night of the slips. She is going down it anyway, deliberately, carrying the house, and she gave me the one word for it tonight, so that it will be in this book on the day the doors begin to shut behind us. Choosing. Not a point of law. A woman telling a witness, while there is still time, what she has decided to be.*

He looked up. Withrow had been watching him write, and she did not pretend she had not.

"Go and train," she said. "Rooke says you've been haunting the east hall's rail with a face like a man pricing a horse. Good. Price it." She stood, and he stood. "And on Fifth-day, when you get into that coach, sit up straight. The town watches the coach."

On the twenty-seventh evening he went to the north wall at the change of the bell and placed the coats.

He had been counting them since the year's first night, and before that since Greyvane, and he had known from the first night that they were older and stood differently. What he had not had was the reason. He had written it down three times as a question and left it, because he would not guess. Now he sat at the third buttress with his back to the house and the valley open below him, and watched the change on the bell, the two at the landing and the two at the foot of the road, as neat as ever. They were older because the young men's work was finished.

He laid it out for himself in order, the way Karis would have. For two years and more, the coats at the foot of every road he had lived above had been learning. Learning his hours, which bell he rose at and which he slept at. Which floors he trained on, and how often, and who with. Who he ate with, and where he sat to eat. Who came up the road to see him and who went down it. Where he walked in the evening and how long he stayed. That was work for young men: patient, dull, a little foolish, the kind of thing a junior officer was set to do so that he would learn to do it. You could see it in the way they had stood, the young ones, craning at the walls, shifting their feet a little more each week as the place came clear to them.

And then it had come clear. All of it. Every hour of his day was in a file somewhere by now. There was nothing left on this hill that anybody needed to be sent to learn.

So they had sent different men. Men who did not need to learn anything, because whoever sent them had told them all of it before they came. Men of a higher grade, who would not be wasted on gathering. The kind of men an office posts when the file is complete and it is waiting for the next thing to happen to the person in the file.

She tried the second count outside the lane this time, short of it, and the third count, the empty one, with one foot in the lane and one out. The lane took the foot that was in. She stood through it. She let it slide her front foot, the way a fighter lets a push go by, and on the fourth count she burst off the back foot, the one that was still on honest boards, across the lane and through the end of it, and came out of the burst three strides from Brom with the dust still running behind her.

"Three strides," said Brom.

"Three strides," said Lira. "Short." She was breathing hard. "But I didn't fall. Again."

He laid a third.

She ran it, and it was better: the empty count taken standing, one foot in and one foot out, the lane sliding the inside foot and Lira letting it. She burst on the fourth off the honest foot, and the burst carried her a stride from Brom, and she touched his shoulder. Brom nodded. Lira turned round, grinning, to say something.

And Cael found he was not counting.

He had laid the third lane and taken the read off it and the weight had come behind the eyes, as it always came, and he had begun to count the ten. And somewhere about six he had lost the count, not because anything had happened but because nothing had: the weight had simply gone on, warm and heavy, and the numbers had stopped meaning anything. He reached for the Wind out of habit, the way you test a stair, and it came, late. He reached again. Late.

"Count," he said.

Lira's face changed. She came across the floor to him fast and stood in front of him and looked at his eyes, and then she began to count aloud, slowly, one number for each of her own breaths.

At eleven he reached for the Wind and it came late.

At twelve it came on time.

"Twelve," said Lira. She did not say anything else for a moment. Then she took the pencil stub out of her pocket and wrote on her palm, under the place where the tape stopped. "Three in one night. Ten on the first, ten on the second. Twelve on the third." She looked up. "That's the last one tonight."

"I'm all right."

"I know you're all right. It's still the last one." She put the pencil away. "You told me on this floor that you'd only ever met the ten counts once. Now you've met them three times in a row, and the third was longer. That's a new figure. We don't spend a new figure the night we find it. Rooke says that." She almost smiled. "Rooke says it about me."

Karis, on the gallery stair, had written it down in order: *First lane, ten. Second, ten. Third, twelve. Not tested past three.* She read it back aloud without being asked, so that he would hear it said by somebody else, and then she shut the notebook and said she had what she had come for and would see them at breakfast, and went.

They sat on the boards afterward among the white crosses with the jug going round.

"What did you learn," said Brom, to Lira.

Lira lay on her back with her taped hands on her stomach, looking up at the dark shutters.

"Not to put my weight down on the third count," she said. "I've been doing the lie with a real foot. On his floor the real foot is the one that moves. So I lie with a foot I don't care about, and I keep the one I'm going to burst from on wood that holds still." She turned her head on the boards. "And I go on the fourth. Not the third. Never the third, against a builder. Everything I've ever done says go on the third, and on his floor the third is the one he's already built under."

"He'll laugh at you," said Brom. "When you stand there on the empty count."

"Let him," said Lira. "He laughs when he's surprised. I'd like him surprised."

Cael sat with the jug in his hands and the last warmth going out from behind his eyes, and thought about the twelve.

It was the first new thing the lane had told him since the night it settled. He had believed the ten counts were the price, fixed, like the five free bursts on boards; a thing he could plan around, write in a minute before a fight, and pay. Now he knew they were not fixed. They grew when he spent the lane over and over. He did not know yet by how much, or whether they grew without end, or whether they came back down with rest. He knew only that a figure he had thought was a wall was a slope.

He realized he was glad. Not of the slope, but of where he had found it: on a painted floor at night, with Lira counting aloud and Karis writing it down in order and Brom standing at the end of the line, and not in front of a crowd with a Gold across the ring. That was the whole reason for the floor. Brom had said it a week ago. *So you don't forget it's yours.* It was his, and it had a slope in it, and now he knew where.

*Lane: three in a night, ten, ten, twelve,* he wrote in Hesk's book before he slept. *The count grows when I spend it close together. Not tested past three. Lira stopped it. She was right to.*

*Lira: lie with the foot you don't care about. Go on the fourth.*

# Chapter 12 — Admit

The east hall had never held a crowd before, and on the thirtieth morning of the year it held one.

Nobody had announced the trial beyond a line on Rooke's own board, chalked the evening before in his square capitals: *PROGRAM TRIAL. SECOND-DAY, SECOND BELL. LIRA / FORCE THIRD-YEAR. RATED.* That was all. But the house had read it, and the house knew what *rated* meant on that floor. It meant the first bout ever fought on the program's painted boards under the program's own seal, with a figure at the end of it that would go into the program's own book. By the half-bell the gallery was full along both sides. By the second bell the porters had opened the shutters on the high windows to let the heat out, and the light came down onto the white crosses in long bright bars, and there were people standing on the gallery stair.

Cael had the rail at the north end, where he had stood on the second morning of the year to watch the red book. Brom stood on his left with his arms folded. Karis stood on his right with her notebook shut and her pen in her hand. Ephram was three steps down the gallery stair with the captain's file under his arm, where he could see the whole floor and be seen by the whole cohort, which Cael supposed was the point. The girl from the reading room's outer table had found a place against the far wall of the gallery, with her small notebook open.

Below them on the floor stood the rating table: a trestle under the north windows with three chairs behind it. The clerk with the red drill-book sat at one end. Rooke sat in the middle with his board and his pencil. And at the other end, with a ruled card in front of him and his hands folded on the table like a man waiting outside a shop, sat the Stone from the river academy, who had asked to come and teach the girl who beat him and was now going to put a figure on her.

Rooke stood up at the second bell and said the rule, once, in a voice that carried to the back of the gallery without being raised.

"Program trial. My rule, not the region's." He held up his board as if the rule were written on it, which it was not. "Four exchanges. Each one, the table gives a flag to whoever took it, or to nobody if nobody did. Most flags takes the trial. Level after four, the table's figure decides. Three bursts a bout for the program's entrant, as on every day she's ever stood on this floor. The other fighter, what his own Path allows." He sat down. "Rated, by the table. The figure goes in this program's book. Not the registry's. Not yet."

Lira stood at the south chalk with her wrists taped and her left arm carried a little close, out of habit. Across forty feet of painted boards stood the Force.

---

He was a big young man, broad in the back and thick through the thighs, and he stood at his chalk with his feet planted and his hands loose and a pleasant expression, the expression of somebody who has been looking forward to a thing and has decided to enjoy it. He had been drilling beside Lira every morning since the recess. He knew the four-count; he had run it himself, badly, for the practice of seeing it. He had laughed once on this floor when she lied to him on the second count and he went the wrong way. He was Iron Rank Seven, six ranks above her on the porter's wall, and he was the best thing the cohort had built in a straight line since Ephram.

Cael watched him settle.

It was very small. A Force fighter's push came out of the legs, and before it came, the knees gave a little and the hips dropped a finger's breadth, as anybody's do before a heavy lift, when he wants the floor to know about it first. The read gave it to Cael from the rail without his asking. Settle, and then the push went down into the boards in front of the man's feet and stayed there.

"Exchange," said the clerk.

The Force did what Force fighters did. He did not come forward. He settled, and set his first course, and Cael felt it go into the floor from forty feet away: a strip of boards a stride wide in front of the man, across the whole width of his reach, gone heavy and live, like the deck of a barge in a current. Then he stepped forward behind it. He settled again. He set the second course in front of the first.

Lira ran straight at him.

It was the worst thing she could have done, and the whole gallery knew it, and a sound went round the gallery like a held breath being let out in disappointment. She did not run the four-count. She did not lie. She went at him in a plain hard line, burst one off the left hip, the old burst, the one she had brought out of a barn at fourteen and that every fighter on the hill had seen a hundred times. The burst carried her twenty feet and put her down on the second course.

The boards under her moved. They were not where her foot expected them; they had been pushed a hand's breadth toward her, and her landing came down on them a beat early and slid. She caught herself. She had to; she was on a floor that was trying to put her on her back. And while she caught herself the Force stepped through his own first course, which did not move for him because it was his, and set his palm on her shoulder.

Not a blow. A touch, square and plain, with his whole weight behind it, and Lira went back off the second course a stride and a half and sat down on the painted boards.

The Stone lifted his flag. Red. The Force's.

The gallery groaned.

Rooke wrote nothing at all.

Lira got up. She dusted her palms on her thighs, and walked back to her chalk, and stood there with her hands at her sides and her eyes on the boards in front of the Force's feet. She was not looking at him. She was looking at where his courses had gone down, and her lips were moving very slightly.

"She gave it," said Brom quietly.

"Yes."

"On purpose."

"Yes." Cael had seen it from the first stride. Lira did not run straight at builders. She had not run straight at a builder since the mill town. "She was counting him."

"Counting what?"

"When he settles." He watched her lips. "She needed him to build in front of her, start to finish, once, with nothing in his way. So she gave him nothing in his way."

Karis, on his other side, had opened her notebook. She wrote one line and showed it to him without a word. *First flag bought. Price: one flag on the record, in front of the table.*

"Exchange," said the clerk.

The Force settled. Set his first course. Stepped. And Lira, at her chalk, began the four-count.

The first step was an ordinary step, any fighter's, and the Force watched it come and settled again for the second course. The second step was the lie. She put her weight on her front foot and left it there one count too long, the long lie the Stone had taught her, so that anybody watching for the burst would see it coming and set himself to meet it. The Force saw it coming. He set himself. He set his second course exactly where her burst would land if it came off that front foot, a fine strong course, and Cael from the rail felt it go down and thought it was the best course the man had built all morning.

The third count, nothing happened.

It was the hardest thing in the world to watch. Lira stood on the painted floor with one foot forward and one back and did nothing at all, for a whole count, while a Rank Seven Force stood twenty feet away behind two courses of moving boards with his knees bent, waiting. The gallery made a small sound. Somebody laughed, uncertainly. The Force laughed too, from surprise, the way he always did, a short bark.

He settled for the third course.

Cael saw it. Lira saw it a hair before he did. The settle was on the man's own fourth count, his own rhythm, the same rhythm he had built to in the first exchange, start to finish, with nothing in his way. He settled on his fourth count whether she gave him anything to build against or not.

And her fourth count was the same beat as his.

She burst off the back foot. Not the front foot she had lied with, which stood on boards a course away from honest; the back one, which had never left the wood the Force had not yet touched. She burst at the angle the first three counts had sworn she would never take, low and to the right, past the end of his second course, where there was no course yet because he was only now settling to set it. She went across honest boards in a single burst and came down on them two strides from his left hip with her landing beat holding her steady, and the Force was still in his settle, knees bent, his push still in his legs, with nowhere to put it.

She touched his shoulder. Lightly. Two fingers.

The Stone lifted his flag. Blue.

The noise in the gallery was not cheering, not at first. It was the sound of a great many people realizing at the same moment that the story they had been watching was a different story. Then it was cheering. Rooke, at the table's middle, put his pencil to his board and wrote one word, and Cael could not see what it was and did not need to.

"One burst," said Karis, writing. "Two exchanges. One flag each."

The Force stood a moment where he was, looking at the place on the boards where his third course would have gone. Then he shook his head, grinning, and went back to his chalk.

---

He had learned. That was what made him the best the cohort had built. The third exchange, he did not build to his own count any more.

He settled faster. He set his courses close together, two counts apart instead of four, not as strong but quicker, and he set them not in a line in front of him but in a fan, left and right and center, so that the whole floor between them began to move, a stride at a time, everywhere. Within a dozen heartbeats there was no honest wood between Lira's chalk and his anywhere she could see.

Cael watched her look for it and not find it.

She tried the four-count once, and the lie on the second count put her front foot on a course that was not there a moment earlier and was there now, and the boards took her foot sideways, and she had to burst to get out of it. Her second burst of the bout. She came down near the east wall, beside the striking frames on their thick springs, on a stretch of boards the Force had not yet reached. He came after her. Not fast; Force fighters were never fast. He came behind his courses, settling and setting, settling and setting, a fan of moving floor pushing ahead of him across the white crosses like a tide coming up a beach.

She went back. A stride. Another. The frames were a pace behind her heel.

"She's out of floor," said Brom.

"She's not," said Cael.

She had done it on the honest floor three nights before, at the end of a lane he laid for her, with Brom standing at the end of the line. She had learned it standing in dust that ran. *Lie with the foot you don't care about. Go on the fourth.*

She stood with her back nearly against the frames and put her left foot forward onto a course. It moved; she let it. She let the boards slide her front foot a hand's breadth sideways and did not fight them, the way a fighter lets a push go by. She kept her right foot behind her on the last strip of honest wood on the east side of the hall. And she stood there for the empty third count, one foot moving and one foot still, while the Force settled to set the course that would take the last of her honest wood away.

He settled on the count. Even now, building fast, building in a fan, the man still had a count, and a fighter cannot build fast without one. Cael had seen it with the read: two counts now, not four, but a count all the same.

She went on her fourth.

Burst three. Off the right foot, the still one, straight along the line of the frames, not toward him at all but past him, so close to the frames that the nearest post rang as her shoulder brushed it. She came down eight feet on, behind the end of his fan, on boards his courses had not reached because his courses all pointed the other way. The Force was turning. A Force fighter turning on a floor he had made heavy around himself had to turn through his own courses, and they were his, and they did not move for him, but they did not help him either. He was a beat late.

She touched his back between the shoulders.

Blue.

"Three bursts," said Karis. "Her whole bout." Her pen kept moving. "One exchange to go."

Cael knew what the fourth exchange would cost before the clerk said the word.

The Force had learned twice now. He was not going to give her a count to burst on. He was not going to chase her into a corner where she could go past him along a wall. He stood at his chalk when the clerk said *exchange* and he did not come forward at all. He settled, and set a course in front of himself, and settled, and set another behind it, and another, standing still, building, until there were four courses of moving boards laid in a half-ring round him like the walls of a well, and he stood in the middle of them on his own still floor and waited for her to come.

There was no honest wood anywhere near him. To touch him she would have to cross four courses. On any one of them a landing would slide. She could not go round; the half-ring had its back to the north wall, and the gap at its ends was too narrow for anybody to get through on boards that moved.

Rooke's rule was three bursts. She had spent three.

Lira stood at her chalk and looked at the well of moving floor for a long moment. Her lips were not moving now. She was not counting him. Cael watched her decide, and saw her decide it, and saw her know exactly what it would cost.

She went.

Burst four, off the left hip, across the first course and the second in one long low flight, the old burst, the barn's burst, the strongest thing she had ever owned. She came down on the third course. It moved. She let it. She let it slide her landing a hand's breadth and turned the slide into the start of the next thing, as she had learned in the dust on the honest floor, and burst again off the moving boards before they could move her any further.

Burst five.

It carried her over the fourth course and onto the still floor at the center of the well, a stride from the Force, at a range so close that he could not set a course between them because there was no floor between them to set it in. He was still in his settle. He had built a wall to stand behind, and she had come over the top of it.

She did not hit him. She put her taped palm flat on his breastbone and held it there.

Blue.

The gallery came apart.

---

"Three flags to one," said the clerk into the noise, and wrote it in the red book.

The Stone wrote on his card. He wrote for a long time, longer than a single figure needed. Then he slid the card along the trestle to Rooke, and Rooke read it, and stood up with it in his hand, and the gallery fell quiet to hear it, because nobody on the hill had ever heard a program figure read out on that floor before.

"Execution, ten," said Rooke. "Effect, ten." He did not look up. "Control, seven. Twenty-seven."

He put the card down on the trestle.

"Ten is a good ordinary day at the heart of Silver. She was there in two columns." His voice did not change at all. "The seven is the fourth burst and the fifth. A fighter who spends past what her program sets her doesn't control her bout. She rents it." He looked at the clerk. "Enter it."

Twenty-seven. Cael had her figures in his notebook since the wool town, every one: twenty-five, twenty-six, twenty-seven at her very best last year, the top of what the board called strong Iron. Twenty-eight was the floor of the Silver bracket. She had stood a point short of it before. She stood a point short of it now. But this time the point had a name and a place, and Rooke had just said both aloud in front of the whole house.

Lira walked to the middle of the floor and shook the Force's hand. Her collar was dark through and her chest was going like a bellows, and she was standing on her right leg more than her left.

Rooke came down from the table with his board under his arm and met her at the edge of the painted lines, before she reached the rail. He did not wait for her to sit. He never did.

"Five," he said. "Against three."

"I know."

"Five free on these boards in a whole day. You spent the whole day in one bout, in front of a table." He looked at her left leg. "Landing leg."

"Hot. Down the outside, from the hip."

"Hip?"

"The fourth went into it. The fifth went somewhere further down." She did not try to make it smaller. "I'll find out at the first bell."

Rooke wrote on his board. "Two days at half work. No frames. No bursts until the leg's quiet, and you tell me when it's quiet, and I'll decide whether to believe you." He looked up. "That was cheap. Two days. You'll pay it standing on your head." He tucked the pencil behind his ear. "Five weeks from now you'll be on a road, three bouts inside four days, against Silvers who were born in programs like this one. Five bursts in one of those, and you've nothing left for the other two. Learn the difference here, where it costs two days. Not there, where it costs the meet."

"Yes, Coach."

He went back to the table without another word, and sat, and began writing in the red book in the clerk's place, as if the trial were a long time ago.

On his way to the south door the Force stopped at the foot of the gallery stair, kit on his shoulder, wraps hanging out of his coat pocket. Lira was on the bottom step of the gallery stair by then, unwinding her tapes. He stopped in front of her.

"The first one," he said. "Did you give it to me?"

"Yes."

He thought about that. He did not seem to mind it at all. "Why?"

"I needed to watch you build. All the way through, with nothing in your way." She pulled the last strip of tape off her left wrist and balled it. "To see whether you settle on your own count whatever happens, or only while you're leading."

"And?"

"Every time." She looked up at him. "Even when you're behind. Even when you're building fast. Two counts instead of four, but a count."

He stood there a moment longer with his kit on his shoulder, looking at the boards where his well had been.

"Even when I'm behind," he said, as if to himself. Then he nodded to her, a short honest nod, the way a man nods to somebody who has told him where he left his hat, and went out of the south door. Cael watched him go and knew that by Rooke's next morning drill the man's count would be a different count, and that the whole cohort would be the better for having had him beaten.

Lira came up the stair to the rail.

She had her tapes balled in one fist and the other hand pressed flat against the outside of her left thigh, where the leg was sending its bill, and her face had the look Cael had watched on it for two months of mornings and never had words for. She stood at the rail beside him and looked down at the painted floor: the white crosses, the arrows, the frames on their thick springs, the trestle where the clerk had gone back to his red book, the Stone folding his card in half and putting it in his coat. Rooke writing.

"At fifteen I'd have laughed at all this," she said. "A table. A book. A man come all the way from the river to teach me a count." She turned the balled tape in her fingers. "I'd have said I had a barn and a yard and my own two legs and that was plenty, and I'd have meant it, and I'd have been very proud of meaning it." She watched the Stone go out. "I'm better now. I can admit I want it."

Nobody said anything. Brom unfolded his arms. Karis, on Cael's other side, closed her notebook very quietly.

Lira looked at the floor a moment longer, and then she went down the stair to find her water, walking carefully on the left leg, and did not look back to see how it had been received.

They walked back across the court afterward in the heat, the three of them, behind the crowd going out.

"*Admit*," said Karis.

"I heard."

"She has never once used that word. Not in three years. Not about anything." Karis walked with her notebook against her chest. "She says things are owed. She says things are late, or wrong, or hers. She has never said she wanted something the registry could give her." She stopped on the flags. "Put it in the permanent pages. Under her name. Not the working ones."

He took out the observation notebook there in the court and wrote it, the date and the word, in the permanent section at the back where he kept the things that were not going to change. Lira was coming across the court behind them, slowly, on her bad leg, and he knew she could see him writing. She did not shout at him to stop. She watched the pen move, and let it, and that was the whole of what passed between them about it.

*The word was want,* the Log got that night. *In three years, every good thing the registry ever handed Lira she called late, or owed, or wrong. She never once called one wanted. Today, on the painted floor, a point short of the bracket with five bursts in her leg, she did. I've put the date in the permanent pages beside the date of the corridor. Whatever this year ends up costing, part of the bill is in her name now, and not in mine.*

That same week two envelopes came for Brom, and the house only ever saw one of them opened.

The first came on the thirty-first day, to Bracken's counter, in the district seat's grey stock with the district's own small seal. Bracken did not send for Brom. He carried it across to the refectory himself at the noon meal, for the second time that year, and this time he did not lay it on the table. He stood at the end of the middle table and read it aloud, all of it, which he had never done with anybody's paper in that room.

"*Review for advancement of tier. The panel is constituted: three assessors and a sigil frame, at the district seat. The practitioner's record is received and entered as it stands, the title included. Sitting: the last Fifth-day of the academic year.*" He turned the sheet to the light. "And at the foot. *Outcome anticipated: advancement, per title.*"

He lowered the sheet.

"*Anticipated*," said Bracken. "In thirteen years I have never seen that word on a district docket. A docket says when and where and who. It doesn't say what. It isn't allowed to." He looked at the word again over his spectacles, as if it might go away if he looked hard enough. "Somebody at the district seat sat down and decided that writing it was cheaper than pretending not to know it. I'd like to shake his hand. I'd also like to have him sacked." He held the sheet out across the table. "Iron, Brom. By the last Fifth-day. They've as good as written it on the door."

Brom wiped his hands on his napkin and took the sheet, and read it, and folded it twice along the creases the district clerk had put in it. Then he unbuttoned his coat and put it in the left inside pocket, with the first docket and the summons and the Velmere letters, and buttoned the coat again, and pressed the buttons down one after another with his palm.

"Thank you, Registrar," he said.

Bracken's eyes rested on the buttons. He did not say anything about where the paper had gone. Then he went back up the refectory between the long tables, and Brom picked up his spoon.

The second envelope came up with the evening post on the same day, and it was sealed with Velmere's wax.

Cael saw it on the post-room shelf when he went for his own letters: a heavy cream envelope, square, with a dark red seal on the flap pressed with the bar and the ring. The house had seen that seal a few times a year for as long as it had known him, on letters that came to Brom and went into his left inside pocket unopened until he was alone. Once, at Norhold, it had come on a letter of a single line.

Brom took it from the shelf himself at the eighth bell. He did not open it in the post room. He did not take it to the court, or the common room, or the bench by the north wall where he sometimes read things. He took it upstairs to his own room and shut the door.

Two days later it was still on his table.

Lira reported it at supper, as she reported everything that mattered, in the tone of somebody giving the price of bread. She had her bad leg up on a stool under the table, by Rooke's order, and the red drill-book open beside her plate, and she did not look up from the book.

"Second envelope's still on his table," she said. "Unopened. He's squared it up with the docket, one on the other, very neat. Edges level." She turned a page. "He's moved the lamp. It used to be on that side. Now it's on the other, so the light doesn't fall on them."

Karis put down her fork. "You went into his room."

"I did not go into his room." Lira turned another page. "My room is up the stair past his room. His door doesn't latch unless you lean on it, and he never leans on it. I go past it twice a day because I have to, to get to my bed, and to get out of my bed." She looked up, at last, at Karis. "I want it entered that I didn't go in. I wanted to. Both of those. Not on paper," she added, to Cael, who had reached for the notebook. "In your head. Some things don't go on paper."

He took his hand away from the notebook.

"And when he opens it," said Lira, "nobody asks. Not one of us. Not what it said, not whether he's all right, not whether he wants to talk about it. Nobody." She looked round the three of them, and her look stopped on Karis and stayed there, not unkindly. "Not even you."

Karis did not take offence. She considered it, as she considered a note in a margin.

"I wasn't going to ask him," she said. "I was going to be in the reading room with the door open, every evening, until he came up the stair or didn't." She picked up her fork again. "I learned that from him. He's done it for me more times than I could count, and never once let on that he was doing it." She ate. "I never let on that I noticed."

Brom came down the stair a little after, and the table's conversation turned without anybody turning it. They talked about the sitting at Ostrand the week after, and whether the coach would go at the first bell or the half, and whether Rooke would let Lira off the dawn drill to ride down, which he would, because Rooke let nothing stop anybody going to watch a thing they might learn from. Brom ate his supper and said six words in the whole of it, all of them about the coach. Then he went back up.

The envelope stayed on the table, in the dark, for one more night.

On the third evening he opened it.

Nobody saw him do it. Nobody at that table ever learned what it said, because he did not tell them, then or afterward. What they saw was his face, when he came down to supper an hour later than usual and sat in his place and pulled the bread toward him.

It was the face of a man being offered his whole ledger back.

Cael put his whole attention on it from across the table, and then made himself look away, because it was not his to read. But he had seen enough. It was not joy, and it was not grief. It was the stillness of somebody who has been handed a paper with everything on it he was ever priced out of: the house and the name and the standing of a family two generations Gold, which had looked once at a big careful boy at fourteen and found a rounding error. Karis spent the house's money for the first time on the thirty-fifth day, and she made Cael come down to the counter to watch her do it.

Karis had not touched it in five weeks. She laid the request on Bracken's counter, filled in, in her finding hand. ## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

### Archmarshal Vastin

**PROVISIONAL (character arc)**

The Compact's senior enforcement officer. Age 51 at Book 4. Silver-tier, Iron Wall Path — exceptional defense and institutional authority. Appears in Book 5 as a legitimate authority figure, becomes the Compact's direct antagonist in Book 6, and by Book 9 has switched sides when he understands what the Architect's will is actually doing.

His arc: the man who enforced a corrupt system for legitimate reasons, and what he does when he understands the system is far more corrupt than he knew.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

## The Arbiter System

**LOCKED (existence); SECRET (true nature)**

Arbiters are experienced as personal spiritual entities — small glowing sigils, unique to each practitioner, that appear at Kindling and remain accessible throughout a practitioner's life for advancement evaluation and Path guidance.

**SECRET:** Arbiters are not spiritual entities. They are interface nodes to the Architect's underlying system — an ancient constructed architecture that pervades Valdris below the level of human perception. The Architect designed and deployed this architecture before recorded history. Every Arbiter in Valdris is a terminal to a single system.

**SECRET:** The Arbiter system has a deliberate flaw: it cannot evaluate [UNBOUND] / [SHATTERED] architecture. The Architect built in an automatic flag and elimination protocol rather than an evaluation pathway — they did not believe a Fractured Path practitioner could survive long enough to require one.

---

## The Quieting

**PROVISIONAL (mechanism); SECRET (source)**

First observed in Arc 3. Ancient sites in the edge territories where Path abilities cease functioning — where Arbiters go silent, where Path declarations cannot be invoked, where practitioners experience their potential as inaccessible. The Quieting spreads across Arc 4.

**SECRET:** The Quieting is not a natural phenomenon. It is the Architect's preserved will beginning to prime Valdris for a systemic reset — a reversion of the Path architecture to its original design parameters, which would eliminate all current practitioner classifications and rebuild the system from scratch. The Architect's reset protocol treats current practitioners as acceptable collateral.

**SECRET:** The Quieting cannot affect Cael's Fractured Path because the Fractured Path does not run through the Architect's system. It is prior to it.

### Observable mechanics — PROVISIONAL, on the page from Book 7 (Void Roads), 2026-09-04

What the edge territories call *quiet ground* and what Karis names *the Quieting* (B7 Ch15). Everything below is shown, measured or paced in Book 7's prose; nothing about SOURCE is disclosed or theorized there.

- **Perimeter:** a perfect circle. Paced with cord at the Fallow Ring: sixty-two cords of twenty metres and twenty strides — a two-hundred-metre radius (B7 Ch10). Same radius at the Long Stair and the Drowned Hall (the Compact's own survey ledger carries the radius line). **The fourth site is twice the radius — four hundred metres — and cordoned in numbered Compact iron a pace apart; not entered (B7 Ch23).**

Every Arbiter in a party fails identically, at the same stride, one way and back the same, regardless of Path or tier (B7 Ch10–11, Ch18, Ch21).

## Tide Path — PROVISIONAL (defined in Book 7, 2026-09-04)

Flow architecture. Where Ember declares ignition and Iron Skin declares density, Tide declares *current* — the perception and redirection of internal energy flow, the practitioner's own and, at contact, another's. Civil face: healing (restoring flow so the body does the rest). Diagnostic technique: **the reading** — contact, both hands, the healer's current run through the patient's architecture ("listening with my hands to how you're built"). Two modes, taught before either is load-bearing (B7 Ch7): the *surface* reading (hands on the hurt, seconds) and the *deep* reading (both hands, whole architecture, minutes, patient still and uninjured, at a mending's cost). Costs and limits: contact range only; healing spends the healer's reserve at roughly the rate of the damage repaired — no crowds, no self-healing; a Tide practitioner **cannot heal what she cannot read**; combat use is a *stall* (a hand on a wrist, a breath, the declaration doesn't render) at brutal cost — Oryn has done it three times in her life. Inside quiet ground: nothing.

**Cael's Tide-adjacent fragment (B7 Ch13):** partial; Iron; self and contact; the first fragment that does nothing in a fight; engagement field *clinical*; **the standing rule, in Oryn's hand and his: ask first, every time.** Consistent with B2's "Tide-adjacent" anomaly label and Book 15's "Oryn's Tide Path burns out."

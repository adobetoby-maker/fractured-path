# Chapter 6 — The Records Hall

The fire-watch kept an oil book. Every lamp lit on the hill after the tenth bell went into it, by building and by hour, so that the bursar could see at the year's end where the house had burned its money in the dark. Bracken signed it himself every morning, and had done for thirteen years, and in all that time the records hall had never once had a line in it.

At a little past the eighth bell he took the book down from its hook by the side door, opened it on the counter at the night's page, and wrote in his small upright hand.

*Records hall. Lamps late. On the registrar's authority, and at his charge.*

Then he hung it back on its hook and went to light the lamps.

There were six along the long table, on iron stands, and two on the counter, and one over the door to the locked press. He lit them all. When he had done it the hall looked like a room Cael had never seen before: warm at the middle and black at the edges, the tall windows gone dark and blind, the long table laid down the length of the floor like a road with lamps along it.

Four of them sat at it.

Bracken had the head of the table, nearest his counter, with the registry code in front of him in its three bound volumes, stacked in order with the spines toward him. Beside them lay the four bound rulings from the press. Karis had the near side, with her copy of the instrument open flat and her notebook open beside it and her pens laid out in a row along the edge of the table like a surgeon's knives. Her assistant had sat at her elbow until the tenth bell, writing whatever she was told to write, and had then been sent to bed and had gone protesting all the way to the door, in a whisper, so as not to disturb the room.

Cael had the foot of the table, with the observation notebook. He had nothing to do there and he knew it. But it was his anatomy that was being taken apart on that table, and he had been learning since his Kindling that a man should be present for his own anatomy.

The fourth chair was the counsel's.

She had come up the bluff road at the ninth bell, in a hired chaise from Ostrand, with one bag. Cael had never seen her before. She was not the counsel who had come up the hill in the inspection's carriages, the one who had talked, by every account, from the bridge to the gate, and had signed for her chests with a face like a cat in a bath. That counsel had been the Compact's, and this one was the house's. She had held the house's retainer for eleven years, and it had been a condition of the retainer from the first day that her name should appear on nothing she advised, only the academy's. So on the bluff she had no name. She was simply counsel, the way the river was simply the river.

She was a small grey woman with very straight shoulders and a short steel rule, the kind a joiner uses, which she laid under each line of the instrument as she read it, so that her eye could not slip to the next before it had finished with this one. She did not talk while she read. When she did talk, she spoke in short complete sentences with nothing left over at the end of them, as if each one had been costed in advance.

Withrow did not sit.

She came and went. She came down from her office to the table, and stood at its head behind Bracken's chair for a while, reading over his shoulder, and went back up. She came down again with the inspection year's finding in its folder, and laid it at Karis's elbow without a word, and went back up. She came down a third time with a tea tray, and nobody remarked that the Chancellor of Halcenvane was carrying a tea tray, and she set it at the table's foot by Cael and went back up.

The fourth time she came down, she was carrying a book.

It was a small book, in a brown cloth binding gone pale at the corners, and Cael knew at once from the size and the colour of it that it was a registry book. Every registrar on the continent had been issued one like it at the start of his service: the procedures volume of the code in a pocket copy, to carry on circuit. Bracken had one in his coat. This one was older than Bracken's and had been carried further.

Withrow opened it at a page near the middle that it opened at by itself, and laid it on the table beside Bracken's great third volume, open at the same place. Then she went and stood at the head of the table with her hands folded in front of her and did not say anything.

Cael leaned across and read the open page upside down.

It was chapter fourteen. The print was the same as the great volume's, only smaller. But the margins of Withrow's copy were full. There were notes in pencil and notes in ink, in a hand that was hers and was not, quite: the same letters, made by somebody younger and in more of a hurry. Most of the notes were dates and the names of districts.

He counted before he read, because counting came first.

One clause on the page had been underlined twice. The first line was in black ink, thin and very straight, made against a rule. The second, under it, was in a brown ink that had faded toward rust, and was not straight at all. Beside the second underlining there was a bracket in the margin, and nothing written in the bracket.

"You're counting my pencil marks," said Withrow from the head of the table, without turning round. "Go on. Ask about them."

"What did you underline?"

She came back down the table and laid a fingertip in the margin by the line. It did not tremble. Cael noted that, because he had expected it to.

"*At its discretion,*" she read. "Part two. *Suspension of practitioner status pending review, such review to be initiated by the registry at its discretion.*" She lifted her fingertip off the page. "The black line I drew in my second year at the registry, with a rule, very neatly. I was young. I thought *discretion* meant the registry would be allowed to be merciful, if it chose. I thought it was a kind word." She looked at the brown line. "I underlined it the second time nine years later, with no rule at all, sitting on a coach going home from a district I'd rather not name. By then I knew what it actually meant."

"What does it mean?"

"That nobody is obliged to begin." She said it quite simply. "The review can begin whenever the registry likes. Tomorrow, or in a year, or never. There is no date in it, and no officer named who must set one." She looked at the faded bracket. "There is a difference between a penalty and a condition, Cael. A penalty is a thing that is done to you, and then it is over. A condition is a place you are put, and nobody is obliged to come and let you out." She looked past him, up the table, to Bracken. "Registrar. Count me the dates in that chapter. Any date at all. Any limit of days."

Bracken went through it at the pace he went through everything, a line at a time with his finger running under the print, and it took him a while, and nobody at the table hurried him.

"None," he said.

"None," Withrow agreed. She closed her brown book and put it under her arm. "I'll be upstairs."

She went. They heard her on the stair, at her ordinary pace, all the way up.

---

Cael counted the instrument before anybody read a clause of it aloud.

There was nothing else for him to do at that table, and counting was the work he trusted. A paper had a floor plan like any building. You walked round it first. You learned its size and the number of its doors before you went in and started reading the writing on the walls.

Nineteen leaves, written on one side only, on a heavy cream paper with the registry's mark pressed into the corner of each. Thirty-seven numbered paragraphs. Three schedules annexed at the back, each with its own small heading. The paragraphs were short, most of them a few lines, and the longest was the ninth. The schedules were longer than any paragraph.

Then he counted words. He did it with a pencil, in the margin of the observation notebook, a stroke for each one, and he went through the whole instrument twice, because a count you have only done once is a guess.

*Discretion* appeared nine times. *Evidence* appeared three times, and two of those three were in the phrase *no evidence is required to be adduced*. The word *fraud* appeared once, in the second paragraph, in a sentence whose whole purpose was to say that there was none. The instrument cited eight provisions of the registry code by chapter and section, and the convening's own minute twice, by date. It cited nothing else at all. Not a ruling. Not a precedent. Not a witness.

Schedule two was a list of the file's standing entries. Cael read it with more care than the rest, because it was a list of himself. It was written in the registry's own grammar, the grammar of declarations, every entry with a heading for Path and a heading for tier and a heading for rank, as every practitioner's file on the continent was written. Under Path, on his, there was the one bracketed word from the frame at Denvash. Under tier there was a short ruled line with nothing above it. Under rank, the same. He had seen his file's front sheet once before, at Greyvane, and it had looked like this then: a form built to hold an answer, holding the shape of a question instead.

The entries ran down under it in order: his Kindling at Denvash, the Greyvane hearing and its finding, the provision with the stamp and its renewals, and the tournament's entry, every one of its eleven words. And between the hearing and the provision, given its full title as if it were the most ordinary thing on the page, the line he had known was on his file since the inspection year, and had never once seen written down in front of him.

*Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4.*

It was cited and not argued from; no paragraph leaned on it or so much as named it. It sat in its schedule like a stone in a field that the plough has been driven round.

He looked at it for a long moment, and then he went on, because it was not what this night was for, and he could feel Karis watching him from across the table, and he did not want her to have to decide whether to ask.

The last leaf had the attestation on it, above the seal's impression. It was in the same type as everything else and no larger. *Given under the Compact's hand.* Under it, smaller, a line he had not heard read aloud in the records hall that morning, because Withrow had stopped at the first leaf. *For the petitioner, the procurator of record.* And a name.

None of them had heard of it. Bracken said so, when Cael passed him the leaf. He said he knew the name of every procurator who had stood before a registry seat in this district for thirteen years, and of most in the next two districts as well, and this was not one of them.

"Which is information," he said, and handed it back.

Cael wrote the tallies down in a column, and drew a line under them, and looked at them.

What they said, before a single clause had been read aloud, was that somebody had written nineteen leaves without expecting to spend any time at all on proving anything. Nine discretions to three evidences. A man who expects to have to prove a thing writes the word *evidence* on every page, because he will be standing in front of a seat with his evidence in his hands. A man who writes *discretion* nine times expects to be standing in front of a seat that will simply choose.

He wrote that under the line. *They don't expect to prove anything. They expect to be agreed with.* Then he passed the column down the table to Karis, and she read it, and put it with her notes without a word, and he realized he had just done a piece of the night's real work.

---

The counsel read it backward.

Cael charted it from the foot of the table, because he had never seen anybody read a document that way. Most readers start where a paper starts, and she did not. She turned to the last numbered paragraph, the thirty-seventh, laid her steel rule under its first line and read that first, and then the thirty-sixth, and then the thirty-fifth. She went up through the instrument from its end to its beginning, a paragraph at a time, the rule moving up the page one line at a time like a man climbing a ladder.

At the tenth paragraph she stopped and made a note. At the fourth she stopped and made another. When she reached the address on the first leaf she did not read it at all. She closed the instrument and laid the rule across it.

"Why backward?" said Cael.

She looked at him down the length of the table as if he had asked her the price of something, and then decided that it was a fair question and he could have the answer for nothing.

"The end of a paper is what the writer wants," she said. "The middle is why he says he should have it. The front is the history he tells you so the why will sound reasonable." She tapped the rule. "If you read from the front you are reading his story, in his order, and by the time you reach the end you have already half agreed to it. If you read from the end you know what he wants before he has had a chance to tell you why. Then you can watch him build the why."

"And the history? At the front?"

"Recitals." She said the word as a joiner might say *green timber*. "That is where a nervous drafter puts his conscience. In twenty-six years I have won most of what I've won in the recitals. The other side recites some piece of history to make his case sound fair, and the piece of history gives me something he needs and did not notice he was giving." She turned the instrument over and opened it at the first leaf again, without looking at the address. "In this paper the recital runs to four sentences. On a paper of this weight I would expect forty. Whoever drafted it knew that somebody would read it backward, and he left me nothing in the front to read."

Bracken, at the head of the table, had laid the four bound rulings out in a row.

"You'll want these, then," he said.

They read all four before midnight.

They were not long. The provision's whole history at law would have fitted on a single shelf of a single press, and it did. Bracken read the headings aloud and Karis read the findings, and the counsel listened with her eyes shut and her rule across her knees.

The first was a river house's, from a long way south. It had enrolled a ferryman's daughter whose Path had come to her late, at a station that could not settle on what it was, and a richer house upriver had gone to law to say she had no business being taught. The finding was a dozen lines long and said, in effect, that the clause said what a house might do, and the house had done it, and so the girl would stay.

Karis read it out in her flat finding voice, and Cael found he had stopped writing.

He had not known there had been anybody else. He had known about the clause for two years, and about the four rulings in Bracken's press for nearly as long, and it had never once occurred to him that the rulings had people in them. A ferryman's daughter, a long time ago, standing in some hall in the south while two houses argued over whether she could be taught. A station that could not settle. He wondered whether she had kept a book. He wondered what had become of her after the finding, and whether anybody had ever written that down, and knew that nobody had, because findings did not have room for afterward.

"Her name's on it?" he said.

Karis looked at the heading. "Her initials. And the house's." She read them out. They meant nothing to anybody at the table. "It was a long time ago."

He wrote the initials in the notebook anyway, small, at the edge of the page.

The second ruling was a mason's guild that had put an apprentice into its school by demonstration, and a rival guild had challenged the form of the panel that watched him. It was sustained. The last two were much older, the paper gone brown and soft at the edges, the parties' names worn down to the guilds they had belonged to. Both had been sustained. Bracken closed each cover as Karis finished, and laid it back in its row, square to the table's edge.

"That is the clause's whole history at law," said the counsel, when the last brown cover had closed. "Four houses went to law against the text, and the text beat every one of them." She opened her eyes. "And Greyvane. Greyvane's hearing went at the text too, by another road, and lost the same way. Five."

"This one doesn't go at the text," said Karis.

"No." The counsel picked up her rule. "This one was written by somebody who read all five and decided not to write a sixth."

Karis took the first finding.

She did it standing, which she did when a thing was going to be minuted, and she did it with the instrument's second paragraph flat on the table under one hand and the Greyvane transcript open under the other.

"They allege no fraud," she said. "Read it." She read it. "*No fraud, irregularity or want of form is alleged in any act by which the enrollment was made.* That's the whole of it. Not in the filing. Not in the hearing. Not in the stamp or its renewals or the house's certificate or the tournament's entry. They don't touch one of them." She lifted her hand off the transcript. "Every story the broadsides printed last year was about a trick. The secret register. The hidden Path. The staged hearing. A clerk paid off. Every one of those stories was a road to a fraud, and this paper has walked past the end of every road without looking down it. Greyvane shut those doors on the record, and they've read the record, and they know the doors are shut." She sat down. "That's the first finding. They don't waste a word on anything they could lose."

The counsel took the second, and she did it in the voice of somebody reading out the weather.

"Paragraph six," she said. She laid the rule under it. "*The provision by which the enrollment was made is acknowledged to be valid, and to have survived in law the standardization directive. Neither is put in issue.*" She lifted the rule. "That is our case. That is the whole of what I would have spent this year proving to the seat. Whether the clause is still law. Whether the directive killed it. Whether a house can still enroll a practitioner by demonstration on a clause out of the founding text. They have given it to us. In the sixth paragraph. In writing. Under their own seal." She laid the rule down. "In twenty-six years nobody has ever handed me my best ground in the first paper they filed."

"Is that good?" said Cael.

The counsel looked at him.

"When a man gives you the field," she said, "look at what he has kept for himself."

Karis had already turned the page.

She read the seventh paragraph to herself. Cael watched her read it. He watched her finger move along the first line and the second, and stop.

Her finger stopped on the page, and her whole body went still around it.

He knew that stillness. He had seen it at Greyvane over a shelf, and in the reading room over the founding articles, and at Norhold over a charter, and every time it meant that she had found the thing on the page that the page had been built to hide. He counted it, because he could not help counting it. One, two, three. It went on past eight, past ten. At twelve she moved.

---

She did not read it. She turned the instrument round on the table so that it faced the rest of them, and put her finger on the paragraph, and took her finger away.

The counsel laid her rule under it and read it aloud.

*7. The petitioner notes, and adopts as its own, the advisory entered in the prior year by an evaluator of the Compact upon the fielding clause of the tournament charter, which reads in full: "The clause says what it says. The enrollment is lawful on its face and adjudicated at public record. If the Compact wishes practitioners of this kind not to compete, it should write different rules — not misread the ones it has."*

Nobody spoke.

"That's his," said Karis. Her voice was very level. "The Archmarshal's. Vastin's. Three sentences. You told us at Norhold there were three sentences of advisory, Cael, and none of us had ever seen them." She looked at the paragraph. "Now we have. They've quoted the whole of it. With approval."

"*The clause says what it says,*" said Cael slowly.

"I know." Karis did not look up. "I said that. On the bench under the thorn at the first bend, the morning the entry went down the hill. *It says what it says all the same.* I said it to you." She laid her palms on the wood, one either side of the instrument. "He said it to them. In writing. He told the Compact, in the plainest words any evaluator ever put on a form, that the text holds and that if they don't like what it holds they must write different rules."

"And they have," said the counsel.

"They have." Karis's face changed then.

Cael had been watching that face for three years. He had seen it angry and amused and bored and cold, and once or twice, very late, tired enough to be kind. He had seen it at Norhold on finals day in the moment the stoppage was called, and that had been the worst. He had never seen it look like this. It was not anger, and it was not cold. She was looking at a printed paragraph on a page of cream paper as if the page had moved.

"They haven't argued with the text," she said. "Not one word. They've agreed with it. They've taken the most honest thing anybody in the Compact ever wrote about you, from the most honest man they have, and they've done exactly what it told them to do. So here are the new rules, written, and the warrant they give for writing them is *him*." She took her hands off the table. "I want it minuted that this is the first thing in this paper that frightens me. Put in your minute that I said so at the seventh paragraph, before I'd seen the eighth."

"Minuted," said the counsel.

She had a page at her elbow now, Cael saw, that had not been there at the beginning of the night. At its head, in a small exact hand, she had written *Halcenvane: the Compact's petition. Working minute. Privileged.* She wrote Karis's sentence on it, word for word, and the time by the hall's clock, and blotted it.

Cael put it in the observation notebook as well, word for word, since word for word was the only way that notebook took anything. Then, underneath, he wrote the other thing. It had come to him while Karis was speaking, quite clearly, like a sum adding itself at the bottom of a column.

*Vastin, in the room full of clocks: the people who want this to stop are not going to use official channels much longer. This is the most official paper I have ever held. Nineteen leaves, a seal, an escort at a walk, and a form of joint service I had never seen in my life. So either he was wrong, or this is the last official thing.*

*And they've made his own honest sentence carry it. He said write different rules. They're telling the seat they're only doing what the honest man advised.*

He looked at it for a while.

He found he was not frightened, not yet. He was something closer to cold. It was the feeling he had had on the morning of his Kindling at Denvash, standing in front of a frame that had nothing at all to say about him and was perfectly calm about it. He knew from the feeling that he would be frightened later, when there was time, and that it would come at him in the dark at the window and not here at the table, where there was work.

He looked up, and Karis was looking at him across the lamps.

"Go on," he said. "There's more of it. You said so."

"There's a great deal more of it," said Karis, and turned the instrument back round to face herself, and found the ninth paragraph with her finger. "And none of it gets any better."

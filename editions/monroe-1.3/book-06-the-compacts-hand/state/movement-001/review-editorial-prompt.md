# Chapter 1 — Nothing Pulls

The house had filled again by dark, and Cael could hear every room of it from his window.

For two months the bluff had been nearly his. There had been the porters and the kitchen and the fire-watch boy, and Bracken in the records hall with his door open, and the four of them and a handful of others who had nowhere better to be. Now the wagons had been coming up the road since noon. Doors banged along the residence front. Somebody below was singing the same three lines of a song over and over, getting the third line wrong each time. The fountain in the court ran on under all of it, as steady as ever, but it was no longer the loudest thing on the hill.

He had Hesk's book open on the sill and Hesk's steel pen in his hand. Hesk's travelling clock sat at his elbow, an hour fast, as it had been since the day it came out of its pine case. He had never set it right. It was easier to subtract an hour than to explain to Hesk why he had changed it.

The old volume was in the drawer, full. He had written its last line on the last ferry, and there was no room in it for one word more. Everything went into Hesk's book now.

He ruled a margin and wrote the interval shut.

*Two months since the ferry. Closed here, because there's nowhere else left to close it.*

*The house: full again tonight. Somebody downstairs can't learn a song.*

*Lira: Rooke opened the Silver program on the first morning of the recess, and she has been on its floor every dawn since. She doesn't talk about it at table. That's new. Since Ardenmere she has told me about every floor she ever stood on.*

*Brom: the summons from the district seat is still in his coat, buttoned in beside the letter from Velmere. On the last night at Norhold he called it the recess's problem. The recess ends tomorrow.*

*Karis: the reading room has a lock on it now, fitted in the fifth week, and a key on a cord round her neck. She takes it off to sleep and puts it on before she puts on her shoes.*

*Seln: at the copying table every working day of the recess, and at the middle of the bench every evening. Nobody has said a word about the bench. Nobody needs to.*

*Ephram: cohort captain from tomorrow. He has spent the recess learning the names of every first-year on the intake list, in order, so that he can pretend not to know them.*

He stopped there and looked down into the court for a while. A first-year was dragging a trunk across the flags on its corner, too proud to ask for help. Then he went on.

*The coats at the bottom of the hill: two at the ferry landing, two at the foot of the road, changing on the bell. Same arrangement as last year. Different men. The two I knew from the bridge never came back up the valley. These are older and stand differently, and they don't watch the gate the way men watch a gate they're learning. They watch it the way you watch a kettle you already know will boil.*

*Vastin said there would be a sitting after the houses went home. The houses went home two months ago. Nothing has come up the road. I don't know whether that's good news or slow news.*

*What I carry, at the year's open: seven, and the one I don't count. Daeva's storm still has its flag on it. Nine of ten, this last week, the Wind came when I called and nothing else came instead. Nine isn't ten.*

*Seventeen. Enrolled on a lawful basis. Third on the continent, with an entry no instrument could finish. Open the year.*

He blotted it with sand, the way Bracken did, and tipped the sand back into its paper. Below him the singer reached the third line, missed it, and started again.

---

He woke an hour before light and knew at once that he would not sleep again.

There was a pull in him somewhere, faint, like a coat caught on a nail as you go through a door. He had felt it every morning since Norhold. Some mornings it was in the hip and some mornings in the chest, and some mornings he could not have said where it was at all. He lay and listened to the house breathe for a while. Then he got up and dressed in the dark and went down the residence stair in his stockings, carrying his boots.

The second floor stood under the middle roof, across the court and up one flight, and at that hour it was black. He lit the lantern by the door from the porter's tinderbox and turned it low. The boards ran away from him into the dark, plain pine, a little stiffer than the sprung oak of the Crown yard and a great deal less forgiving, laid long ago by somebody who must have believed that the best way to teach a fighter about falling was to make him do it on something hard. On oak he had six free bursts in a day. Here it was five, and he had stopped being surprised by that a long time ago.

He sat on the bench by the door and put his boots on, and laced them, and did not hurry.

Then he ran the suite, in the order he had learned each thing, because that was the order he trusted.

The Wind came first, Lira's burst. He set his feet at the old chalk mark by the north wall and felt the gather come low in the belly, the leading hip load like a drawn bow, and the shove at his back like a door slammed by a draught. The far boards came up to meet him twenty feet on. He landed, and the landing beat held him where he stood, the same short locked pause it had held him in for four years. He had spent four years trying to make it shorter. It was no shorter.

He walked back and did it again. Two. Three. A thin line drew itself through the hip toward the inside of the knee, as it always did at the third, and he named it and went on. Four. Five. The hip took the fifth and kept the receipt.

He did not run a sixth. On this floor the sixth would be paid for in the morning, and he had a long day in front of him.

He stood a moment with his hands on his hips and listened to himself. Nothing had come when he called except the thing he had called. Five of five.

The read came next, Brom's read, the cheapest thing he owned and the one he trusted most; it had never once failed him on a floor. He knelt and laid his palm on the boards and let it go out of him and down. It did not read wood the way it read a man. It read weight. The floor told him where it was carried and where it was not: a joist running under the north wall, sound; one under the middle that had been sistered with new timber a few years back and was proud of it; a soft place by the far door where water had once come in under the sill. The building above him leaned its loads into the walls, slow and patient, the way old buildings did. He took his hand away and the read came back to him clean.

That was two.

The push next, the thing he had taken from the Bronze at Ardenmere. There was a striking post in the corner, wrapped in old canvas. He set his right hand flat against it and leaned, and then did not lean; he gave it the push. The post shuddered on its foot, and the canvas whumped, and a little dust came down from the beam above. He gave it four. At the fourth the right shoulder spoke, low, the way it always spoke at the fourth. In public he never showed more than a flat hand at a plate. At Norhold he had given a Gold five in one sitting, and his shoulder had counted the fifth for a week afterward.

He did not give the post a fifth. He did not need to know what he already knew.

Three.

Reydan's give was harder to try alone, because it needed something to hit him, and the only thing on the floor willing to do that was the heavy bag hanging from the beam on a chain, a sack of sand and rags the size of a calf. He drew it back to the length of the chain, let it go, and put his left forearm in its road.

It came at him like a falling door.

And it went nowhere. The blow came into the forearm and stopped there, all of it, as if he had caught it in a pocket. Nothing went back up the arm into the shoulder. Nothing went down into the hip. The bag hung against his arm for a heartbeat as if it were puzzled, and then swung away on its chain with the dull creak of a gate. His forearm hummed, deep in the bone, and the hum faded slowly. That was the bill, and it was small. It had always been small, until somebody hit it too hard or too often.

Four.

The spark was Karis's, and he used it only by her leave. She had given him that leave a long time ago, standing, and never taken it back. There was a copper basin on the windowsill that the porters filled for the floor in the hot weeks. He emptied it out of the window into the gutter and set it on the bench, and laid two fingers against its rim. The spark went out of him the way a word goes out of a mouth. The copper ticked. A smell came up from it like a struck flint, and the rim was warm under his fingers. Two contacts a bout was the rule in public. It was a house rule, not the registry's, and at Norhold he had broken it once, on purpose. He set the basin down.

Five.

He stood in the middle of the floor and did not hurry the sixth.

The quiet thing from the stair he did not try. He never tried it; it was not a thing that needed calling. It came by itself in the gaps of the day whether he called it or not, and he had paid rent on it every day since he took it, from the moment he woke to the moment he slept, spent or unspent. It was sealed. It would stay sealed until the debt it was sealed for was spoken, and nobody had spoken it yet.

That left the last. Daeva's storm.

He had carried it eleven weeks.

It had come to him in the fourth exchange of the hardest bout of his life, in front of eight thousand people, out of the hands of a Gold who had begun the fight herself, and who had looked at him afterward on the stone as if he had asked her a question nobody had ever asked her before. The notice that came after it was the first that had ever said *Gold*. It was also the first that had ever warned him that what it gave him might not hold. *Stability: provisional — architecture under load.* He had read those words in a healers' corridor with his left side strapped from shoulder to hip, and he had felt them every day since.

Gold was heavy. Everything else he carried had been shifting to make room for it, slowly, the way the furniture in a house shifts when somebody carries a great iron chest in through the front door. For weeks after Norhold he had reached for the Wind and got something else. Some mornings it was the push, and his whole body shoved at nothing. Once, on the road, it was the read, and he had stood in a waystation yard knowing the exact weight of the water butt and going nowhere. The healers had said a month. His own count had said more, and his own count had been right.

He went to the north wall and turned and faced the length of the floor.

The lantern by the door gave the boards a little light, and beyond the light they ran into the dark. There was dust on them, the fine pale dust of a floor nobody had swept since the recess began. He looked at the dust.

Then he asked the air to lean.

He had never found better words for it. Daeva had her own words, and they were not his. To him it was like putting a hand flat against the morning and pressing, and feeling the whole of it give a little, along one line, the way a board gives when you step on its end. The air in front of him tipped. Fifteen feet of it, no more, in a narrow road no wider than his shoulders, and it reached perhaps a third as deep as hers had reached. His ears closed and opened, and the dust on the boards woke up. It ran, in a thin pale stream along the floor, down the length of the lane, as water runs down a gutter, and gathered against the far edge of it in a little drift.

He stepped into the downhill of it, and the floor came to him faster than his feet had any right to bring it.

Fifteen feet. Then the end of the lane, like the end of a moving walkway at a fair, and he was his own weight again and stopping. Behind him the air let go, and his ears closed and opened once more.

He stood still and waited for the pull.

This was the moment it always came. After the lane, every other thing he carried had pulled toward the place the lane had been, like iron filings toward a lodestone, and for a minute or two nothing answered true. He waited for it. He waited for the coat to catch on the nail.

Nothing caught.

He turned round, slowly, and set his feet, and called the Wind.

It came. The gather, the hip, the shove at his back, the boards coming up twenty feet on, the landing beat holding him. Only the Wind. Nothing came with it and nothing came instead of it. He stood in the landing beat with his breath short and his hip charging him for a sixth burst on a five-burst floor, and he was smiling in the dark like a fool.

He called the read, kneeling. Clean. He gave the post one push, flat, just to hear the canvas answer. Clean. He let the bag come at his forearm once more, and the give took it and kept it, and the bone hummed, and nothing else in him so much as stirred.

He stood there a little longer and did what he always did after a run, which was to lay what he had done beside what the record said he could do, the way a clerk lays the day's takings beside the ledger.

The record was a public thing now, and it was large. Before Norhold the registry had held five of his seven, and only three of those had ever been shown on a scored floor, at rates the house had chosen for him: six bursts on the oak, two contacts of the spark, a read that looked like good timing, and a push that never went past a flat hand. Norhold had changed that for good. On finals day nine judges and three scribes had written down the Wind past six, and the push at a fighting load, and Reydan's give taking a Gold's fire, and the spark past its two contacts, and something none of their columns had a name for. Every one of those was on paper now in the tournament's own hand, and paper did not forget. But the house did not fight on finals day. On the bluff, in front of the wing's panels, he still fought at the rate the house had always shown, and a little under it, and never over it, and he scattered the figures inside that room so that they would breathe like an honest fighter's. Lira called it the band. It was the oldest daily expense he had.

What he had just done on this floor was what the band was for hiding.

Then he sat down on the boards in the middle of the floor and let himself have it.

No notice came, and none ever would. Notices came when something began; nothing had ever come to tell him a thing had finished. He had known that, and he found he had half expected one anyway, as a boy half expects a letter on the morning of a fair. The floor was quiet, and the lantern had burned down a little. Somewhere outside a cockerel was arguing with the light, and the light was winning.

He knew then, sitting on the boards, that he had been afraid. Not of the bout, and not of the notice, and not of Gold. He had been afraid, for eleven weeks, that the house in him would never sit true again, and that he would spend the rest of his life reaching for one thing and getting another, on floors where getting the wrong thing would cost somebody else as well as him. He had not let himself write it down, because writing it down would have made it a finding. He let himself feel it now, all of it, because it was over.

Settled.

---

He took Hesk's book out of his coat and wrote it on his knee, with the pencil he kept in the spine.

*First-day, before light. The second floor. The full suite in order. Wind, five of five; the read, clean; the push, four, flat at the last; the give, the bag twice; the spark, one contact on the porters' basin; the lane, fifteen feet, a third deep, the dust ran. Then the Wind again, after the lane. It came alone.*

*Nothing pulls.*

*Eleven weeks. The flag is down. Settled.*

He looked at the next line for a while before he wrote it, because he knew Hesk would read the book one day and he knew what Hesk would say about leaving things out.

*Relief. Writing the word because it's the true one. Hesk says the book keeps the whole account or it isn't an account.*

He read it back. Then, under it, he wrote the other thing, the thing the relief had been standing in front of.

*So now it can be priced.*

The minute from the bluff was in the front of this book, in his best hand, three lines long: nothing made to happen; only inside a fight somebody else begins; the cost on paper first. And under it, in Karis's wording, with four sets of initials, the line they had added at Norhold after the bill came in. *Never again in the middle of a fight unless what follows has been priced first, at the weight it will come at.* At Norhold nobody had known the weight. Now he knew it. Fifteen feet, a third deep, the ears, the dust. Eleven weeks of everything leaning. That was what Gold cost, on paper, at last.

He found he did not mind owing it. He minded only not knowing what he owed.

He closed the book and stayed where he was, on the boards, because there was no reason yet to move.

And in the gap, it came.

He did not feel it begin. He never did. One moment he was a boy sitting on a floor in the grey light. The next he was a little less than that: not gone, only thinner, a place the eye would slide over on its way to somewhere more interesting. He knew it only by the absence it left, like a stair that is not there in the dark.

*Hold.*

He did what he had trained himself to do in every gap of every day since the stair. He gave a corner of his attention to somebody. There was nobody on the floor, so he went to the window. Below, under the covered walk's north arch, the fire-watch boy was standing on his upturned bucket with his slate, chalking the night's last hour. He had his tongue between his teeth. He made the figure, and looked at it, and rubbed it out with his cuff, and made it again more carefully.

Watching the boy was not hiding. A person who was awake to one other person was visible to everybody. The two halves of the thing in him would not run together; he could not be the most watchful creature on the hill and the least noticed at the same time. Not yet. The thinness went out of him like breath from a cold window.

The rent was paid for another morning. It always was.

He did not write that down, because there was nothing new in it to write.

---

The great hall of the lecture range held the whole house at once, and by the second bell it was nearly full.

He sat where he had sat for two years, fourth row, north side, with the pillar at his shoulder. From there he could see the whole faculty bench and only half of the platform, and half of the platform was all anybody needed. He counted the hall as it filled. It was the first thing a room would tell you without being asked.

The intake was larger this year. It sat in its block near the back, very upright, and it did what intakes always did, which was to watch the older years come in and try to learn from the way they walked which of the distances between them mattered and which were only habit. But this intake had a second thing to watch, and it watched that harder.

It watched the delegation.

Lira came in with her hair wet and her collar dark from the east hall, and he saw forty heads turn to follow her down the aisle. Brom came in with a heel of bread in each hand, and a different forty turned for him. Karis came in as the bell finished, on the minute, with her grey notebook already open, and a few heads turned for her too: the ones who read transcripts. He had arrived early and alone, and the intake had been watching him since the door. He could feel it on the back of his neck like sun.

Ephram came down the row behind and dropped into the seat at Cael's back. He was carrying the captain's file now, thick and new and tied with green tape, and he carried it the way a man carries a saddle he has not yet paid for, a little too carefully.

"There's paperwork about you," he said. "Did you know?"

"What paperwork?"

"Two of the second-years came to see me last night." Ephram undid the tape. "They have a list. Every meal of the term, two names against it, and a table chosen from which the pair of them can study how you hold a spoon. They wanted it countersigned by the captain, as a cohort activity."

"Did you sign it?"

"I told them they'd need a supervising faculty member and a floor-hours stamp from the assessment wing, and that the wing only stamps on Fifth-days." He turned a page. "They've gone to find out whether that's true. It'll take them a week. By then you'll have changed where you sit."

"I never change where I sit."

"I know. I'm counting on them not knowing." Ephram found the page he wanted and smoothed it down. "Captaincy is mostly that. Knowing what people don't know, and letting it take them a week."

Lira dropped into the seat on Cael's left and took one of Brom's heels of bread without being offered it. Brom sat beyond her and did not seem to notice the loss. Karis took the end of the row and wrote the date at the head of a clean page.

Withrow walked out onto the platform with one sheet of paper, and did not look at it.

She did the house's ordinary business first. She did it in about the time it takes to tie a pair of boots, and nobody remembered most of it afterward. Rooms. Hours. A new instructor for the Current cohort. A wall on the north side to be pointed, and the north side to keep away from it. Then, in the same voice and at the same speed, she said that Coach Rooke's Silver-bracket program, which had run through the recess on the house's credit, was from this morning a standing track of the academy, chartered, with its own floor and its own line in the accounts.

The hall made a noise. It was not loud at first, and then it was. It came up from the intake's block and the third-years' benches at once, and somebody near the back stamped, and then a great many people were stamping.

Withrow stood and let it run.

Cael watched her do it. In two years he had never once seen her wait for applause. He had seen her stop it, more than once, with a look or a lifted page. This morning she stood with her hands on the lectern and waited, as if the noise were a cart that had to finish crossing a bridge before she could go on. When it had crossed, she went on.

He looked along the row. Lira was looking at her own hands, very hard.

"We went to Norhold," said Withrow, "and came home third, and you have all heard me say so, and you will not hear me say it again." She let a breath go by. "Here is the year instead. It is quieter work and it is harder work. Third is a figure now, written down in other people's books, and a figure in other people's books is a thing other people may try to rub out. We keep it the way we earned it. We are exactly what our papers say we are, every day, in front of whoever is looking." Her eyes went nowhere in particular, which with Withrow was always somewhere. "I am told the looking has not stopped. Good. Let it look. The research standings post on the covered walk this morning. The program's floor hours post at noon. Go to your assignments."

The hall broke up into its hundred smaller noises, and went out of both doors at once.

Cael did not get up. He sat with the notebook on his knee and wrote one line under the date, because he had been trying for two months to find the right words for what had happened to her voice since Norhold, and he had them now.

*Withrow: load-bearing calm. Her house was weighed against the continent and came back with a number she can stand on. She stands on it.*

"You've got that face on," said Lira. "The one where you've found a word."

"I've found a word."

"Is it a good one?"

"It'll hold weight."

She took that as a yes. She was already on her feet, pulling at the strap of her kit bag, and her eyes had gone past him to the door. "Rooke wants us back on the line at the fourth bell. He wants to see what I've forgotten since the first bell." She went, with Brom's bread in her fist.

---

Cael stayed where he was a little longer and watched the platform empty.

Rooke had stopped Withrow at the foot of the steps. He was saying something short to her, and she was answering shorter. Gault was writing on the back of his hand, which meant he had lost his pencil again. And Bracken was not at the faculty bench at all.

He was at the east end of the hall, by the tall windows, alone, standing with his hands clasped behind his back and his head turned a little to one side, the way a man stands in a doorway at night when he thinks he has heard a step on the stair and is waiting to hear it again. From those windows, Cael knew, you could see the bluff road as it came up out of the trees on its last two switchbacks. You could see a rider on it a full minute before the gate could.

There was nobody on the road. Cael could see that much from here, through the glass, over the heads of the last people going out. The road lay pale and empty in the early heat, and the dust on it had not been raised since the last of the wagons.

Bracken stood and looked at it anyway.

"Twice," said Karis at his shoulder. She had come back along the row without his hearing her. "Once before the bell, and now."

"I saw."

"He looked at the road the way you look at a page you've already read, to see if the words have changed." She held her notebook shut against her chest with both arms. "Bracken never looks at anything without a reason. He'd think it a waste of the eyes."

"Then he has a reason."

"Yes." She considered the registrar's back. "And he hasn't told it to anybody, which means it's either not his to tell or not ready to be told." She turned toward the door. "I'll be in my room. I've a lock to get used to."

Cael watched Bracken a moment longer, and the registrar did not move. Then a porter came up the side aisle with a question, and Bracken turned from the window and answered it, and walked out of the hall with him, and the window was only a window.

Cael wrote it in the notebook. *Bracken at the east windows, twice before the third bell, the road empty both times.* He did not know what it meant. He had learned that the things he did not yet understand were exactly the things worth writing down, so that later he could see when they had started.

Then he put the notebook away and went down into the heat of the quadrangle, where the whole house was crowding round the boards in the covered walk, and the year was beginning in the ordinary way, as if nothing in it were waiting.

# Chapter 2 — Heavy

The east hall had been a plain room with a gallery when Cael last trained in it. Over the recess Rooke had turned it into an instrument.

On the second morning he was on the gallery stair before the first bell. Whatever Rooke had built in here, he had built it for one of the four, and Cael wanted its shape in his notebook while it was still new enough to show its joints. The stair was unlocked. He went up it quietly and took a place at the rail at the north end, where the light from the high windows would be behind him, and looked down.

The floor had been planed back to bare wood and marked again in white paint.

It was not marked in rings, the way the Crown yard was chalked. It was marked in short lines and small crosses and arrows, laid out in groups across the whole length of the hall, so that from above it looked like the plan of a dance written down by somebody who did not trust dancers. Along the south wall the striking frames had been rebuilt. They were padded posts set on springs, and the springs were new and thicker than any Cael had seen on the hill, so that a frame would give back what it was given at a Silver's weight and not a Copper's.

At the head of the floor stood a trestle with a lamp on it, though the windows were already grey, and a clerk sat there with a book bound in red board open in front of him.

There were five people on the floor.

Rooke stood by the trestle with his board under his arm and his pencil behind his ear. Two third-years were working through a line of crosses at the far end. One was a Force, broad in the back, who had gone up to Rank Seven at the year's last sitting and moved as if he expected the floor to argue with him. The other was a lean Current girl who never seemed to put her heels down at all.

The fifth man was a stranger, and Cael knew him at once.

He was a Stone, thick through the shoulders and slow in the feet, and he stood at the near end of the painted lines with his hands folded in front of him like a man waiting outside a shop. Cael had seen him last at the wool town, on the far side of a rope, losing to Lira. He had been an Iron of the river academy then, its first entry, and she had beaten him by finding the half a beat in which neither of his planted feet was reading the floor, and going through it. Now he was a Silver, newly advanced, and he had sewn nothing on his collar to say so. Over the recess, Lira had said, he had written to Rooke and asked for the posting, and brought his academy's drill-book with him in a red board cover so that the bluff could copy it.

He had asked to come and teach the girl who beat him. Cael wrote that in the notebook before he wrote anything else.

Lira was on the line in front of him.

The drill was a short one, and Cael had to watch it four times before he understood it.

It began with an entry, an ordinary forward step that any fighter on the hill would have recognised. Then a second step that looked like the first and was not: the weight went onto the front foot, and stayed there one count too long, so that a man watching for the burst would see it coming and set himself. Then a third count in which nothing happened at all. Then, on the fourth, the burst, from the wrong foot, at an angle the first three counts had sworn it would never take.

Four counts. Three of them were lies, and the fourth was the truth arriving where the lies had said it could not.

Lira got it wrong the first time. She burst on the third count, early, because every fight she had ever had had taught her that a third count was where you went. The Stone said nothing. He walked to the second cross and stood on it, and turned his heel a quarter, and walked back.

She got it wrong the second time. She held the third count, but she held it with her shoulders, and a held shoulder told a watcher more than a moving one. The Stone came and stood beside her and did the four counts himself, very slowly, a Silver doing a child's exercise, with nothing in his shoulders at all. On the fourth he did not burst. He only lifted his back heel a finger's breadth off the boards, and it was enough. Cael, at the rail, felt his own weight go the wrong way.

Lira watched him with her head on one side. Then she went back to the first cross.

The third time, she got it right. The fourth time she got it right at speed, and the burst went off the wrong foot at the wrong angle and carried her twenty feet across the painted lines to the far end of the group. She landed and turned, and the Stone, who had moved to meet the angle she had lied about, was standing a stride from where she was, looking at the empty boards where she was not.

He nodded once. It seemed to cost him something, and he paid it.

Rooke wrote a word on his board. Cael could not read it from the gallery and did not need to. Rooke had been writing one word at a time on Lira's sheets for a year.

"There," said Rooke aloud. "Again. That was the drill. Now find out if it was you."

She went back to the first cross.

Cael did not count her bursts. She had asked him once not to, long ago, and he had kept to it. He counted the drill instead: the entries, the held counts, the angles, the places where her heel came up and the places where it stayed down. It was a better count, in the end. A burst was a burst. A drill was a sentence, and the sentence was what Rooke was teaching her to say.

Rooke's own rule for her was three bursts a bout, and he kept it in drill as well as on a scored floor, so most of what she ran that morning was dry. She ran the four counts without the burst, and with the burst, and dry again, and in between she stood at the end of the line with her hands on her knees and her eyes on the painted crosses as if they had insulted her. The east hall's boards gave her five free bursts in a day. She spent two in the first hour and none after it.

The Force third-year came over once and ran the drill against her. He was half a head taller than she was and six ranks higher on the porter's wall, and he went in at her heavy, the way a Force did, meaning to take the second count away from her before she could tell her lie with it. She told it anyway, and he went the wrong way. He laughed out loud, surprised, and the laugh rang round the empty gallery.

"Again," said Rooke. "Both of you. That laugh was free. Next one costs."

---

At the water break she came to the foot of the gallery stair and sat on the bottom step with a tin cup, and Cael came down and sat a step above her.

Her collar was dark through. Her hands were taped to the wrist, and she rolled them, one and then the other, the way she did when she wanted something to do with them. She held her left arm a little close to her side. It had not hurt her since the team trial at Norhold; the healers had taken the wrap off for that day and never put it back. But the arm still remembered being careful, and some mornings it carried itself close out of habit, the way a man who has lost a tooth goes on chewing on the other side.

She did not say anything for a while. She looked at the floor: the white lines, the crosses, the arrows, the frames on their new springs, the clerk at the trestle turning a page of the red book, Rooke beside him with his pencil. The whole hall had been rebuilt in twelve weeks for one purpose, and the purpose was sitting on a stair with a tin cup.

"It's heavier than it looks from the rail," she said. "Don't write that down."

"Noted," said Cael.

He did not write it down. He kept his hands on his knees and the notebook in his coat. She saw him not do it, and her mouth moved a little at one corner, and she looked back at the floor.

That was the whole of what she said about it. It was more than she had said in two months of breakfasts.

He sat with it there on the stair and turned it over, the way he would have turned a guard somebody had shown him, looking for where it would give.

Lira had a word for everything the registry had ever done to her, and every one of the words was a grievance. Wrong, she said, and slow, and blind, and once, of the station that had read her, draughty. Those were words for things that had been taken from her or kept back. This was a word for a thing put into her hands. He realised, sitting a step above her, that in her whole life nobody had ever put anything into them, and that she was finding out, on a painted floor, what it weighed.

"The Stone," he said instead. "He asked for this."

"He wrote to Rooke in the first week of the recess. Rooke showed me the letter." She drank from the cup. "Two lines. He said he'd fought me and lost and wanted to know how. He said the river academy could spare him for a year if somebody here would feed him." She turned the cup in her taped hands. "He's a good teacher. He doesn't explain anything. He just stands on the cross I should have been on and waits for me to notice."

"Is it working?"

"You saw." She put the cup down on the step. "Rooke says the sitting doesn't read the board. It reads the rating. I'm Iron Rank One on the porter's wall, and I'll stay Rank One on it, and it doesn't matter. When the advancement evaluation comes, they'll put me on a floor in front of a panel and mark what I do, and if the marks sit where Silver sits, that's where I go. Not up the column. Across it." She pressed the tape flat at her wrist. "The rank's a certificate. The rating's a figure. Rooke says nobody in the Silver bracket ever asked to see anybody's certificate."

"And the figure?"

"Isn't there yet." She said it without any weight on it, the way she would have named the time. "It will be."

He knew the figure as well as she did; he had kept her figures in his notebook since the wool town. Five judges marked a bout in three columns, execution and control and effect, and each column could run past ten, with ten for an ordinary good day at the heart of Silver. Strike the highest and the lowest judge in each column, average the three that are left, add the columns together, and you had a rating. Thirty was par for a Silver. Twenty-eight was the floor of the bracket, and her figures all last year had run from twenty-five to twenty-seven, Silver-touched, at the very top of what the board called strong Iron. Two points, give or take. Two points was a long way to climb by rating alone, and he did not know of anybody on the hill who had climbed it in a single year.

"Two points," he said.

"Two points." She looked at the painted floor. "The Stone says the first one's the drill. He says the second one's me."

The clerk at the trestle rang a small bell. The Force third-year was already back at the far crosses, swinging his arms. Lira stood and stretched until her back cracked, and then she put her hand in her pocket and took out a key on a string and showed it to him.

"Rooke gave me this. I'm first in every morning, so I'm the one who opens up." She swung it once and put it away. "It's the first key anybody here ever gave me that wasn't to my own room."

She went back down onto the painted floor. Rooke was already saying something to her, and she was already shaking her head, and the Stone was already standing on a cross.

Cael climbed back to the rail and watched until the bell, and wrote nothing at all until he was out on the covered walk with the hall behind him. Then he wrote only the date, and the word *key*, and under it the shape of the drill, four counts, three lies and the truth.

Karis's room was at the top of the library, four flights up a stair so narrow that two people could not pass on it, and he went up after the noon meal with a mug of tea in each hand.

He found her in the doorway.

She was not going in and she was not coming out. She was standing in the doorway with a stack of books against her chest, the founding-era compilations in their three different bindings, and she was looking at the door.

The card was still in its brass holder at eye height, as it had been all the year before, in Bracken's small upright hand. *Reading room. Assigned: K. Dellenmoor.* Under the card, new since the recess, there was a lock. It was a good lock, a brass one with a heavy plate, set into the old oak by somebody who had known how. Its key hung on a cord round her neck. The cord was too long, and the key had swung out over the top of the books.

"They've given me a line in the accounts," she said, without turning round. "Did you know that? Not a grant. A line. The council voted it in the sixth week. It's the first line the house has ever put in its books for an enrollee's own work. Bracken told me it took them two sittings to find the right heading for it, because there wasn't one."

"What heading did they find?"

"*Research, enrolled, standing.* Three words. It took eight people two sittings." She shifted the books. "And the lock. And the girl."

"The girl?"

"My assistant. A second-year. Ink on her collar every day of her life." Karis went into the room at last, and set the books on the long table under the window, and he followed her in with the tea. "She goes at registry law the way I did at her age, which is to say all night and mostly wrong. I correct her once every morning. One mistake, no more. If I corrected all of them she'd learn to wait for me to do it."

The room had changed again. The great map of the continent was gone from the long wall, rolled and tied on a shelf, and the wall was bare plaster for the first time since he had known the room. On the table, in the order she had just laid them, stood the old compilations. Beside them was a new ledger with nothing written in it yet.

She did not sit down. She stood at the table and counted the room aloud, touching nothing, the way she would have read out the contents leaf of a bundle before she untied it.

"A door," she said. "A lock in the door. A key. A line in the accounts with my name under it. A girl who brings me digests and asks me what they mean." She stopped. "Every room I've ever worked in belonged to somebody who could take it back. The shelf at Ternhall. Prynn's back table at Greyvane, which was Prynn's, and she let me know it every morning, kindly. Even this one, last year, was lent. I've always known where the door was, and I've always read faster because of it." She looked at the lock. "It's the first one I've ever wanted to stay in."

"That sounds like good news."

"It's a finding," said Karis. "I haven't decided what kind." She turned the key on its cord between two fingers. "A reader who wants to keep her room starts reading a little more kindly. Not much. A word here, a doubt there, nothing she'd ever write down. I've seen it in better people than me." She let the key go. "So I've told myself I'm going to watch for it. I'm telling you as well, so that there are two of us watching."

He did not write it down; he gave her the tea.

She drank some of it standing up. Then she went to the shelf by the door, where the house's certified copy of the tournament charter stood in its grey card covers among the regional schedules, and she put one finger on its spine, and left it there, and did not take it down.

He knew what was in it. He had watched her hand stop at the same place last year, in the deep cold, by the founding articles, where a narrow slip of her own paper stood up out of the binding. She had not opened it then. She had not opened it on the road home, or in the recess, though he had seen her go past the shelf a hundred times.

"The provisions section," he said.

"Stays on the shelf." She took her finger off the spine. "Old books first. "I didn't ask."

She flattened herself against the wall to let him by, and stared at him as he passed, and he heard her say to herself, quite quietly, *the left hand, then the right*, as if she were committing something to memory.

He supposed he would find out later what it was.

On the third day he went down to the assessment wing with his floor-allocation sheet, because anybody whose floor hours were tied to his evaluations had to lodge the sheet at the counter, and the counter was the counter.

The queue was longer than it had been last year. The intake had found the wing. A Stone first-year stood in front of him holding his form in both hands as if it might go off. Ahead of the Stone, two Current girls were arguing in whispers about a drill neither of them had run yet, and ahead of them a Mire third-year was reading the notice board on the wall with great attention so that nobody would think he was in a queue at all.

Cael used the queue the way he used every queue. He read the room.

The rack of forms stood against the wall by the door, seven pigeonholes in a row. Six he knew. The seventh had stood empty all last year, and now it was full. The sheets in it were green, a pale grey-green like the underside of a willow leaf, and the heading was set in the wing's plain type. *Research standing: floor and archive hours, by application.* There had been no such standing on the hill until the council voted Karis her line in the accounts. Somebody in this wing had built the form for it in the same week, before anybody had asked to use one.

Cael knew who. He looked at the green sheets exactly as long as a third-year looks at a form he does not need, and no longer.

Behind the counter, the desk clerk sat at his ledger by the window, adding a column under his breath and losing it whenever the Current girls' whispering rose. Gault's door stood shut, with voices behind it. And behind the counter at the copying table, rising every time the queue moved to take the next sheet, the teaching assistant did the counter's work.

Above the copying table, on the shelf where it had stood before Norhold, sat a small locked case with no mark on it. It had gone to Norhold and come back without anybody seeing it opened. Nobody looked at it now, and Cael did not look at it either.

Seln took each form the same way. He read it from the name to the signature at an even pace. He stamped the received line. He tore off the copy-slip along its rule and handed it back. The Stone first-year got what the Mire third-year got, to the second. A man who read every line from every hand was a man nobody remembered reading anything in particular.

When Cael reached the counter, Seln took his sheet and read it. Name; the Path line left blank, by rule; evaluator of record; hours; reason. The stamp came down, and the slip tore clean.

"The Magister signs evaluation hours on Fifth-days," said Seln. "The schedule goes up the morning after."

"Thank you for the form."

"Mm."

That was always all of it. Cael had his fingers on the copy-slip.

Seln laid the sheet on the stack at his left hand and said the next thing to the stack.

"The office observes that the coats at the foot of the road were changed in the recess, for senior men." He set the edge of the stack true against the counter. "The office has no opinion about coats. It notes that an enrollee whose standing rests on documents may wish to keep his documentation current."

He had already turned back toward the copying table. By the time Cael had folded the slip into his coat, the pen was in Seln's hand again and his head was down, and the Stone first-year had stepped up behind and was laying his form on the counter as if it were a sword he was surrendering.

Cael went out through the wing doors and along the covered walk at the pace of a man on his way to the library. He did not let himself have the sentence until he was through the north arch.

*Senior men. Keep the documentation current.*

Seln had read the bottom of the road in the same week Cael had. He had drawn the same line under it, and he had spent one sentence of it across a counter, in front of a queue, in the voice he used for floor hours. Cael had been keeping a private tally of that man's words for two years, the way a carter tallies freight by the pound. By that tally, one sentence at a counter was worth a speech from anybody else. Two was a sermon.

---

He counted the coats that evening from the north wall, at the change of the bell.

The north wall ran along the edge of the bluff above the river, waist-high, broad enough to sit on, with the whole valley open below it. He had counted the coats from here for two years, and from windows and wagon benches before that, ever since Greyvane. He knew every place on the wall where the angle was good. He took the one by the third buttress, where a crack in the coping held water after rain and nobody else liked to sit.

The heat of the day was going off the stone. Below him the bluff road went down in its switchbacks to the river street. Across the brown water, the ferry landing stood at the end of its causeway with the ferry tied up beside it for the night.

Two at the landing. Two at the foot of the road.

The bell went from the lecture range. Down in the valley, at the same moment, as if the bell had been rung for them, two more coats came along the river street from the direction of the town and took up the places at the road's foot, and the two who had stood there walked away toward the bridge without a word passed. At the landing it happened a breath later. It was done as neatly as a change of guard at a gate, and nobody on the river street so much as turned a head.

Grey coats and good boots; that had not changed. What had changed was the way they stood.

Last year, and the year before, the coats had been young men, or young enough. They had looked up at the walls. They had watched the gate as if they were trying to learn it, and every week they had learned a little more of it and shifted their feet to show it. They had been men sent to find out.

These did not look up. One of the pair at the road's foot was grey in the beard and leaned on a stick he did not seem to need. The other stood with his hands in his coat and looked at the river. Neither of them had glanced at the bluff since the change. They did not need to, because they had not come to learn the hill. Somebody had learned it for them, from somebody else's notes, before they ever came up the valley. They stood the way men stand at the end of a long errand, waiting for the one thing they have been sent to see.

He watched until the light went, and they did not look up once.

He wrote it on the wall, with the notebook flat on the coping and his back to the river.

*Third evening. The change on the bell, both pairs, the same as every year. The men are not the same. The young ones were sent to learn. These were sent already knowing. You don't send your best men to watch a gate. You send them to be standing at it when it opens.*

*I don't know what they're waiting for. Seln says senior men. Bracken watches the road. Vastin said a sitting.*

*Three people who never guess, and none of them has guessed out loud. I'm not going to be the first.*

He shut the book. Across the river the ferryman lit the lamp on the landing's post, and the two coats there stood in its light without moving, as patient as the post.

# Chapter 3 — The Band

On the fourth evening the rota found him at supper.

The refectory ran the length of the old range under the bell tower, three long tables and a short one under the windows, and the house had always sat where it liked. Cael sat where he always sat, at the end of the middle table by the pillar, with his back to the wall and the door in front of him. Lira was across from him, eating as if somebody might take the plate away. Brom was beside her with two plates. Karis had a book propped against the salt.

Seln sat between Brom and Karis, at the middle of the bench.

He had sat there every evening since the first waystation on the road home. Nobody had said a word about it then and nobody said one now. He ate slowly, with his elbows in, and he cut his bread into exact squares before he buttered any of it, and when Brom pushed the dish of beans along to him he took some and pushed it on to Karis without looking up. It was the most ordinary thing in the room. Cael counted it anyway, every evening, the way a man counts heads at a door at the end of a long day.

At the short table under the windows, two second-years were watching him eat.

They were not good at it. One of them was a Mire boy who kept glancing up and then down again as if the ceiling had spoken to him. The other was a girl with ink on her collar, and she was much better. She did not glance at all. She ate her supper, and turned the pages of a small notebook beside her plate with one finger, and every so often she wrote a word in it without lifting her eyes from Cael's hands.

"Your assistant," he said to Karis.

"Yes." Karis did not look up from her book. "She asked me last week how a person learns to observe. I told her to choose a subject who would never notice he was being watched, and to keep a page on him for a month." She turned a leaf. "She chose you. I've entered it as tomorrow's error."

Lira laughed into her cup and had to put it down.

"Left hand, then the right," said Cael. "I heard her on your stair."

"That's how you hold a cup," said Karis. "You test the weight with the left and lift with the right. I've known it for two years. She found it in three days." She looked up at last, across the room at the girl with the ink on her collar, with something in her face that Cael could only have called pride if he had been willing to be told off for it. "She's going to be very good. I'd like it noted that I'm annoyed."

Ephram came past with his tray and stopped to see what Lira was laughing at, and was told, and went on to the far table shaking his head. "I said they'd need a floor-hours stamp," he said over his shoulder. "Nobody listens to the captain."

Bracken came in at the end of the meal, which he never did.

He ate in his own rooms over the records hall, from a tray, at an hour that suited the hall's lamps rather than the house's bell. In two years Cael had seen him in the refectory perhaps four times, and each of those times he had been looking for somebody. He came down the room now between the long tables without hurrying, a narrow grey man with ink on the knuckles of his right hand, and he had a single sheet of paper in his hand.

He was carrying it himself, and Bracken did not carry forms. He had clerks for forms, and boys, and a pigeonhole by the records-hall door for anything that could wait until morning. What Bracken carried himself, across a courtyard and down a refectory, was something else.

He stopped at the middle table across from Brom, and laid the sheet on the board between the plates, and turned it so that Brom could read it the right way up. Then he put one finger on a line near the top, and took the finger away, and waited.

Brom put down his spoon.

Cael read it upside down. He could not help that; he had been reading things upside down across tables since Denvash.

*Ostrand district registry. Review for advancement of tier, mandatory upon a continental title. Brom, enrolled at Halcenvane; Path, Iron Skin; tier, Copper formal. A standard panel is convened by this docket. Sitting: the last Fifth-day of the academic year, at the district seat. The panel will read the practitioner's record as it stands.*

"Three assessors and a sigil frame, in a room at the district seat about the size of our long room," said Bracken. He said it to Brom, in the voice he kept for reading a fee aloud. "The summons they handed you on the road gave the district ninety days to convene a panel. This sheet convenes it. On paper, as of this morning, well inside the ninety. The house wrote in the recess and asked that the sitting itself be listed at the end of the year, so that you'd go before the frame with a year's work behind you and not a recess's idleness. The district said yes." He paused. "Districts say yes to that sort of letter. They like a champion to look well in their own building."

Brom read the sheet. He read it as he read anything that cost him, once and all the way down, without moving his lips.

"Seven times in thirteen years a sheet like this has come across my counter," said Bracken. "Seven times the panel found for the tier the title was won at. They have no other road. Your name is cut on a staff in the middle of the continent where any carter can read it, and a district office that read you lower than that staff would be arguing with Norhold in public, which no district office has ever had the stomach for." He looked straight at Brom then, which he did not do often, with anybody. "You'll hear a certain word said about this all year, in corridors, by people who think you can't hear them. I'd sooner you heard it from me first, at a table, to your face. It is a formality. You'll go into that room Copper and come out of it Iron. Your family's ledger closes."

There was a little silence at that end of the table. At the next table along, somebody dropped a spoon.

Brom folded the sheet in half, and in half again. He did not put it in his kit bag, where the plaque was, wrapped in two shirts. He unbuttoned his coat and put it in the inside pocket, the left one, where the summons had ridden since the road and where the Velmere letters lived. Then he buttoned the coat again, and pressed the buttons down with his palm, one after another.

"Thank you, Registrar," he said.

"Don't thank me. It's a docket." Bracken picked up nothing, because there was nothing to pick up, and turned to go. Then he turned back, as if he had forgotten something. "The last Fifth-day of the year," he said to the table at large. "Somebody write it down. I'd hate for the house to be told I let it slip."

He went back up the room between the long tables, and out.

Brom picked up his spoon and went on with his supper. After a while he pushed the beans along to Seln, who took some and pushed them on.

Lira caught Cael up on the covered walk afterward, under the lamps, where the house was going past them in twos and threes toward the residence.

"You saw where he put it," she said.

"Inside pocket."

"The left one." She walked a few paces. "He keeps things in two places, Brom. Did you know? The things he's won go in the kit. The plaque. Rooke's sheets. The bout cards from the wool town, every one, in a tin. The things about Velmere go in the pocket. The letters. Her one line." She did not say whose. "And now that."

He had not known. He had been counting Brom since Ardenmere and had never once counted which pocket.

"So he doesn't think he's won it," Cael said slowly.

"He doesn't think it's about winning." Lira put her hands in her coat. "It's about them. A panel's going to sit in a little room and tell a house in Velmere what their boy's worth, and the house is going to read it, and he's going to have to stand there while they read it." On the fifth morning the wing signed his hours, and he went to wing three to fight in front of them.

Until this year the provision's supervised hours had been signed in the east and north halls. But over the recess Rooke's program had taken the east hall for its own, and the assessment wing had moved all of the provision's bouts into a hall of its own to make room, the third of its three. Everybody called it wing three. It was a long plain room behind Gault's office, with whitewashed walls and old pine boards and a row of east windows high up, so that in the mornings the light came in over the heads of the people at the trestle and lay along the floor in bars.

Gault sat at the trestle in the middle of the north side with his case beside him on the bench. It was the case with the handle, the one he carried his instruments in, and he had set it down very straight, as he always did, as if it might have opinions about the bench. On his right sat the Ash instructor with her book held against her chest. On his left sat the Mire instructor, a square calm woman with a pencil behind each ear. Each of the three had a card in front of them, ruled into three columns: execution, control, effect. The desk clerk from the counter sat at the end of the trestle with the wing's book open, to write the figure down when there was one.

At the east end of the room, at the rail under the windows, stood Ephram.

He had a sheet of paper folded in four in his hand. It had been folded and unfolded so many times that it had gone soft at the creases, like cloth. He did not open it. He leaned on the rail with it, and nodded to Cael, and said nothing at all, which for Ephram was a kind of formal dress.

The Shield came in from the south door, stretching his arms.

He was a third-year, Iron Rank Five, a long-armed young man with a patient face and a guard that Cael had watched in the Crown yard for two seasons without ever having to stand in front of it. A Shield's Path put a hard smooth place in the air in front of his forearm, a plane no wider than a door's panel, and he could turn a blow off it the way a roof turns rain. The good ones walked it forward. This one was a good one.

He came across the boards and held out his hand, and Cael shook it.

"They say you fight a different bout every week," said the Shield. "Which one am I getting?"

"Whichever one you make me fight."

The Shield thought about that, and grinned, and went to his chalk.

Cael went to his own, at the south end, and took Hesk's book out of his coat before he set his feet. The page was already written. He had written it the night before, under the lamp, as he had written one before every bout since the mill town. It was the rotation, the list he had built by a stove a year ago after Seln told him across a counter that his figures were too tidy. Scatter, built on purpose and costed in advance. Every bout got one line from the list, never the same line twice running, in an order nobody with a pencil could find.

He read today's line once more.

*Fifth-day, wing three, the Shield. The second exchange goes to him, whatever it costs. Buys: one touch against, the figure down two. Spends: probably a rib.*

He put the book away.

Then he took his place inside the rate. That was how he thought of it now, since Norhold: a room inside the room. The house had a record of him, and the record held what it held. On sprung oak, six bursts free, which on these boards meant five, and never the fifth. Karis's spark to two contacts in a bout and no more. Reydan's give kept shut, so that a blow which landed on his forearm went on up the arm into the shoulder, as it would in any honest Iron. The push no more than a flat hand. And the rest of it, the lane and the weight behind it, not anywhere at all.

At Norhold the whole continent had seen him spend past every one of those lines, on a scored floor, with three scribes writing it down faster than any clerk could, and it was in the record now and would stay there for as long as there was a record. A rate you have shown is a rate you have shown. But a fighter did not fight every Fifth-day at the rate he had once spent for a title, and here, on the bluff, in front of three cards and a clerk, he fought at the house's rate and inside it.

Gault turned the glass.

"Exchange," said the desk clerk.

---

The Shield walked his guard forward.

He did it the way the good ones did, without hurry, the hard plane set a hand's breadth in front of his left forearm and the forearm held across his body like a gate across a lane. He did not swing, because he did not need to; he came forward in short level steps, and wherever Cael went, the plane was already there.

Cael gave him the read.

It went out of him the way it always did, under thought, and came back with the man's weight. The Shield carried himself on his back foot, which was right. When he turned, his weight went into his front heel half a beat before his shoulders came round, which was a habit. And the plane on his forearm turned with his shoulders, not with his heel.

Half a beat. That was all the read ever gave anybody, and it was enough.

The Shield's heel went. Cael went the other way, burst one, low and lateral off the left foot, and the boards came up under him eight feet to the Shield's outside, and the landing beat held him there. The Shield's shoulders were still coming round. Cael's right hand was on the back of his shoulder before they arrived.

"Touch," said Gault. "Provision."

The Shield went back to his chalk shaking his head, not in anger, the way a man shakes his head at a sum he has added wrongly.

One to nought.

"Exchange."

The second exchange was the one the book had already sold.

The Shield came forward again, and this time he came differently. He had learned, and he kept his weight off the front heel now and turned on the ball of the foot instead, so that his shoulders and his plane went round together; it cost him speed, and he paid it without any sign that he minded. He walked the guard forward a step at a time, and Cael gave him ground, and the window's light lay across the floor between them in bars.

Then Cael saw the opening.

The Shield had set his plane square in front of him to turn a straight blow, and he had his whole weight in it, the way a man leans into a door to hold it shut. It was a good guard against anything that came at it from the front. It had nothing at all to say about something that came from the side. A push at a fighting load, the thing Cael had given a Gold five times in one sitting, would have taken that braced plane on its edge and turned the whole man round on his own planted foot like a gate swinging on a hinge.

He did not give it. He gave a flat hand, at the edge of the plane, the way any careful Iron might test a guard, and the plane turned it off with a soft sound like a palm on a drum.

And the Shield, who had felt the hand and found nothing behind it, came through.

His forearm came across Cael's ribs on the right side, low, with his weight still in it. It was not quite a blow; it was more like a door shutting on him. The give was there, Reydan's give, waiting to take the whole of it into the forearm and keep it, and he did not let it. He took it the way any honest Iron would take it. It went in under the ribs and up, and all the breath he had went out of him with a sound he did not choose.

"Touch," said Gault. "Shield."

One to one.

He walked back to his chalk with his hand pressed to his side and the room going a little grey at the edges, and he made himself breathe slowly through it. It was not the rib that cost him. He had known the rib was coming; he had written it down the night before. What cost him was the gate swinging on its hinge. He had seen it, whole and clear, the way he saw a sentence finished before its speaker finished it, and he had shut his hand on it, and now the room had the Shield's touch on its cards and not his.

It was like writing your own name with the wrong hand, slowly, so that it would look like somebody else's.

"Exchange," said the clerk.

Neither of them touched in the third. The Shield came on with his new turn and his patient feet, and he had grown bolder since the second exchange, as men do when a thing works. Cael read him and gave him ground and spent a burst on getting out of a corner he had walked into. Once the Shield's plane turned a blow of his so hard that his whole arm rang to the shoulder, and he took that too, the honest way, and felt it settle in the joint like a stone into mud.

Then he spent the first of the two sparks.

He did it at the edge of the plane, where the Shield's own left hand showed past it, two fingers laid on the back of the knuckles for an instant and gone. The copper smell came up. The Shield's hand jumped as if a wasp had found it, and the plane wavered in the air for the space of a breath and came back. He had not been touched; there was nothing for the cards. But he had been told something, and he shook the hand once and set the plane a little further forward, to keep his knuckles behind it.

The glass ran out.

"Exchange," said the clerk. "Fourth."

The Shield had learned twice now, and he came forward in the fourth like a man who has decided that he understands the floor. He kept his weight off the heel and his knuckles behind the plane. He walked Cael back toward the south wall, steadily, and the light from the high windows lay on the boards behind him and in Cael's eyes.

Cael let him.

He let himself be walked back six strides, seven, until the south wall was a stride behind his own heels and the Shield had stopped walking and was setting himself for the last of it. A man setting himself puts his weight down, and a man who has just put his weight down cannot move it again for half a beat, however good he is. The read gave Cael the half-beat, and he spent the second spark in it, at the edge of the plane again, on the knuckles.

The hand jumped. The plane came back, as it had in the third, because the Shield had learned that it would.

But this time Cael was not where the plane came back to. He was gone left with his third burst of the bout, along the wall and out past the Shield's right elbow, low, and the boards came up eight feet on, and the landing beat held him a stride behind the man's back. The Shield was still facing the wall. Cael laid his palm between his shoulder blades.

"Touch," said Gault. "Provision. Two to one."

The Shield stood a moment with his face to the whitewash. Then he laughed, and turned round, and put out his hand again.

"The wasp," he said. "Twice. I learned the wasp and you used the lesson." He shook his fingers out. "Next time I'm going to learn something else."

"Next time I'll be fighting something else," said Cael, and it was the truest thing he had said all morning.

---

The three cards came down the trestle to Gault, and Gault added them in his head and then again on the back of his hand, and gave the figure to the clerk.

"Twenty-three and a third," he said. "Entered."

The clerk wrote it in the wing's book. The Ash instructor closed hers and held it against her chest again, and the Mire instructor took one pencil from behind her ear and put it behind the other ear, which Cael had never understood. Gault picked up the case by its handle and went back to his office with it, and the morning's business was done. The provision had been demonstrated, the hours signed, and a figure had gone into a book.

He went down to the east end of the room, still holding his side, and Ephram unfolded the soft sheet on the rail between them.

It was a chart, as Cael had known it would be; he had not known how much of one.

There were columns ruled down the page, and every line of every column was a bout. The first line was the first figure the house had put on him in the qualifying year, in the long room with five chairs and a rope. After it came every floor they had fought on, in every town, and the figures went down the page in Ephram's square, cramped hand, with the floor and the town and the opponent's Path beside each. Twenty-three, twenty, twenty-six, twenty-two, twenty-four, twenty-one, twenty-five, twenty-three. In the margins there were notes in smaller writing. *Soft boards. Loud gallery. He let the Blade have the first.* And once, against a figure of twenty, *bad fish at breakfast (mine, not his)*.

Halfway down the page there was a line with no figure on it at all. Just *Norhold*, in capitals, and a long dash, and then nothing.

Below it, three lines of this year. Below them, Ephram took a pencil from his ear and wrote a fourth while Cael watched. *Wing three. Shield R5. Twenty-three and a third.* In the margin he wrote *gave him the second*, and then he looked at what he had written, and drew a small question mark beside it, and left it.

"A year," said Ephram. "Every figure this house has put on you. I keep one on everybody, you know. It's what captains do. But yours is the only one that's three pages long."

"Why?"

"Because it's the only interesting one." Ephram ran his pencil down the column without touching the paper. "Look at it. It goes up and down like an honest man's. Better floors, better figures. A good gallery, a little higher. Bad fish, a little lower. If you showed this to a stranger he'd say, there's a strong Iron with good days and bad ones, and he'd be right." The pencil stopped. "Then look at the top of it."

Cael looked, though he knew what was there.

"Never past twenty-seven," said Ephram. "Not once. Not on oak, not in front of the Compact, not in the mill town with the whole hall stamping. It breathes. It never leaves the room." He tapped the long dash after *Norhold*. "And then once, at Norhold, it left the room, and nobody had a number for where it went."

He did not ask. Cael waited for him to ask, and he did not.

"I'm not asking you anything," said Ephram, as if he had heard the waiting. "Write that in your book if you like. A captain who asks his fighters questions gets told stories. I'd rather have the chart." He folded the sheet in four again, along its soft creases, and put it inside his shirt. "The chart doesn't tell stories. It just tells you where the edges are."

"And where are they?"

"That's the thing." Ephram leaned on the rail. "At Norhold I watched you do things I'd never seen you do on this hill. Not once, in two years. I watched you go past six in a ring. I watched you take a Gold's fire into your arm and keep it." He said it quite plainly, as he would have read off a bout card. "So I know the edges on this chart aren't yours. They're somebody's. Maybe the house's. Maybe the provision's. I don't know whose." He shrugged. "Doesn't matter. I'll keep charting. One day it's going to go off the top of the paper, here, on this hill, and when it does, I want to have been the one who drew the line it went past."

Cael said nothing. There was nothing he could say that was both true and his to say, and Ephram knew it, and had arranged things so that he did not have to.

He thought there was something else behind it, too. Ephram's eyes had gone to the empty floor while he talked, to the chalk marks at either end, and stayed there a moment longer than a man's eyes stay on a floor he has no business with. Whatever it was, it was not ready to be said. Cael knew that look. He wore it himself, in front of the Log, most nights.

"Your rib," said Ephram, in an entirely different voice.

"It's fine."

"It's a rib. Go and let somebody look at it." He pushed himself off the rail. "That was a good bout, by the way. Whatever it was. The wasp was very good."

He went out by the south door. Cael stood a while longer under the high windows with his hand against his side, where the bruise was beginning to come up like ink through blotting paper.

It would be yellow by Seventh-day and gone by the next Fifth-day. He had paid more for less. What stayed with him, as he walked back across the court in the hot bright morning, was the gate on its hinge. He had seen it and not used it, on purpose, in front of a captain who had a pencil. He had done that every week for a year and would do it every week for as long as the year asked.

He realized, crossing the court, that Ephram was right about one thing and did not know how right. The edges on the chart were not his. They were the price of a door he was keeping shut, and he paid it every Fifth-day, in front of three cards and a clerk, one rib at a time.

He wrote that down when he got to his room. Then, under it, the figure: twenty-three and a third. It belonged in the book too.

# Chapter 4 — The Honest Floor

Lira unlocked the east hall at a quarter to the eleventh bell on Sixth-day night, with Rooke's key on its string, and the three of them went in and shut the door behind them.

It was dark inside and smelled of new paint and old sweat. Brom had brought two lanterns and a jug of water, and he set the lanterns on the floor at either end of the painted lines and turned them up, and the white crosses and arrows came out of the dark across the boards like the bones of something. Lira went up the gallery stair two at a time and along the gallery, and Cael heard her drawing the shutters on the high windows one after another, all the way down the north wall, until there was no light in the hall but theirs.

She came back down wiping the dust off her hands.

"Karis says she's reading," she said. "She'll take the figures at breakfast. And if anybody breaks anything, she wants to know which thing, and she wants it in writing, in that order."

"Which thing first," said Brom, "or writing first?"

"Which thing. She was very clear." Lira sat on the bottom step of the gallery stair to tape her wrists. "She says writing first is how you end up with a very neat page about the wrong bone."

They had not done this since the road. On the road there had been no floor, and in the recess there had been the flag, and for eleven weeks nobody had wanted to see what the suite would do when it reached for one thing and got another. Lira had raised it at supper, on the evening of the fifth day, with her elbows on the table and her voice low. *It's settled. You wrote it settled. So show us.* Brom had said nothing, which from Brom was agreement, and Karis had said she would come if it were any other night of the week, and it was not.

So here they were, three people and two lanterns on a floor somebody had painted for another purpose entirely, at an hour when the rest of the house was asleep or pretending to be.

Brom came over before anything else and laid his hand on Cael's right side, low, where the Shield's forearm had shut on him the morning before. He pressed with two fingers, and Cael breathed in through his teeth.

"Shield?" said Brom.

"Shield."

"You let him."

"I let him."

Brom took his hand away and said nothing about it. He looked at Cael for the space of a breath, with the look he gave a floor before he stood on it, and then went to the far end of the painted lines and began unrolling the wraps for his forearms. It was all the comment Brom would ever make, and Cael felt it settle on him like a coat.

"Rules," said Lira from the stair. "Mine. I've been thinking about them all week, so you don't get to change them." She tore the tape with her teeth. "No band. No rotation. No rate. Whatever you've got, you use it, and whatever it costs, you tell us, and we write it down. Nobody's watching. Nobody's charting. If it goes off the top of a page, there isn't a page."

"And the quiet thing?"

"Stays shut." She did not look up from the tape. "That's not a rule. That's a promise, and it's yours, not mine."

He nodded. That one he had made himself, in a back room at Norhold, with the three of them watching, and it was not a rule of any floor; it was older than floors.

"Then the rest," said Lira, and stood, and rolled her shoulders, and the left one came round as easily as the right. "Me first. I want to see the one I haven't seen since the ring."

---

She ran the four counts at him.

She ran them the way the Stone had taught her, on the white crosses, from the first cross to the fourth, and she did not hold back. The entry. The second step with its long lie of weight on the front foot. The third count, in which nothing happened, which was the hardest of the four to watch because nothing happening was exactly what a fighter could not afford to believe.

On the fourth she burst, off the wrong foot, at the wrong angle.

In wing three, in front of the cards, he would have read her heel and gone with the read and been a fraction late, as an honest Iron would be against a Silver drill, and let the boards say so. Here there was no wing three. The read gave him her heel half a beat early, and he did not wait for anything. He burst into the angle she had lied about. Two bursts, one on the other, the second off the landing of the first, so that he was not where her burst ended. He was beside it.

She came out of her burst and he was there.

Lira stopped dead on the boards. Her face did something he had not seen it do in a long time: it went entirely still, and then it lit from the inside, like a lantern somebody has turned up.

"*There* you are," she said.

"Again?"

"Again."

They ran it nine times in all, and he lost count of his own bursts somewhere about the seventh, because he had not been keeping them; that was the rule. She kept hers. Three in each bout, Rooke's rule, never a fourth, even here. *I'm not breaking Rooke's rule for you,* she said, when he asked. *I'd have to explain it to him, and he'd write one word on my sheet, and it would be the wrong word.* So she had three bursts and he had as many as he wanted, and she still took two of the nine off him. Both times she did it on the third count, the empty one, by doing nothing at all for one beat longer than he could believe anybody would.

He felt the bill come in somewhere after the sixth burst. The thin line from the crest of the hip toward the knee, the one he could have drawn on himself with a pen. At the seventh it widened. At the eighth it went down into the long muscle at the front of the thigh, and stayed there, and began to sing. Five free on these boards. Everything past five was borrowed against the morning, and the morning would collect it.

He sat down on a white cross with his hand on his hip.

"Bill?" said Lira, squatting in front of him.

"Hip, crest to knee, the usual road. The eighth went into the thigh. The ninth, if there was a ninth, went somewhere I'm going to find out about at the first bell." He pressed the place with his thumb. "I went past six at Norhold, in front of the whole city. That cost me less than this did tonight."

"That was one bout." Lira wrote on the back of her hand with a stub of pencil. "This was nine. And you weren't fighting a Gold who wanted you to last, you were fighting me." She looked at what she had written. "I made you spend."

"You made me spend."

She sat back on her heels and looked at the painted floor as if she had just been given it. Then she said, more quietly, "He was right. The Stone. He said the drill was only worth anything against somebody who could see it coming. Everybody here can't see it coming. You can." She stood. "I'm going to want to do this again."

"So am I," said Cael, and found that he meant it.

---

Brom had finished wrapping his forearms. He stood at the far end of the painted lines with his feet a shoulder's width apart and his weight sunk into them, and he looked, in the lantern light, like something that had been built there and the hall built round it afterward.

"Give," he said.

Cael went down the floor to him and set his own feet and held up his left forearm across his body.

Brom's Path was Iron Skin, and he could go hard, not all at once and not for long, but enough. When he hit something, he hit it with a forearm that had stopped being entirely flesh, and the something knew. Cael had taken one of those forearms on an honest arm once, long ago at Ardenmere, before he had the give. He had not been able to lift a cup with that hand for three days.

Brom hit him.

It came at him like a beam swung on a rope. And it went nowhere. The give took it in the forearm, all of it, as a pocket takes a dropped coin, and kept it. Nothing went up the arm, and nothing went down through the hip into the floor. Brom's forearm stopped against his as if it had struck a sack of wool, and Brom looked at the place where it had stopped with his eyebrows up.

"Again," said Cael.

Brom hit him again, harder, and the give took that one too, and the hum came up in the bone, deep and low, and began to fade.

The third came before the hum had faded. The give took that too, but Cael felt it take it, the way you feel a cup take water when it is already more than half full. The hum did not fade this time; it stayed in the bone and grew a little, and his fingers began to tingle at the ends.

"Again," he said, and Brom looked at him, and hit him a fourth time.

The give took the fourth. It took it the way a full cup takes one more drop: it held, and the surface stood up over the brim, and trembled. The hum went up his arm as far as the elbow and stopped there, waiting. Cael lowered the arm and stood with his teeth together until the elbow let it go.

"That's the edge," he said, when he could. "Not the strength. The speed. It's a cup. I can pour it out again, but not as fast as you can fill it. Space them out and I could take you all night. Four in a row and the fifth goes somewhere."

"Where?"

"I don't know. I'm not going to find out on you."

Brom considered this, unwrapping and rewrapping the end of one forearm, which was what he did when he was thinking. "At Norhold she was filling it with fire," he said. "From across a ring. Faster than me."

"And it went somewhere."

"Mm." Brom set his feet again. "Push, then."

This was the one Cael had shut his hand on in wing three. He set his right palm flat against Brom's chest, below the collarbone, and Brom set himself to it, the way he set himself against anything, with his weight down in his heels and his knees soft and his whole body arranged to be the thing that did not move.

Cael gave him the push.

Not the flat hand. The real one, the one he had taken from the Bronze at Ardenmere and given to a Gold at Norhold, at fighting load. It went out of his shoulder and down his arm and through his palm into Brom as if he had put the whole of himself behind a cart on a hill.

Brom slid.

His planted feet went back along the boards a foot and a little more, with a long dry squeal of leather on pine, and stopped. He looked down at his feet, and then he looked up, and he was smiling, which Brom did perhaps three times in a year.

"Again," said Brom.

Cael gave him four more. At the fourth his right shoulder spoke, as it always spoke at the fourth. At the fifth it said something else, longer and lower, and he knew he would be counting it on the stair in the morning. Brom slid each time, less each time, because each time he learned a little more about where to put his weight, and at the fifth he slid no more than a hand's breadth.

"Five," said Brom. "Like her."

"Like her."

"Your shoulder."

"Counting it."

Lira, writing on her hand: "Shoulder, five, the fifth counted." She looked up. "Spark?"

He laid two fingers against the iron of Brom's forearm, where the skin had gone hard and dark under the wraps, and gave it the spark. There was a tick like a nail cooling, and the copper smell rose up between them.

Brom looked at his forearm. "Hm," he said, which was a review.

---

"Now the one we came for," said Lira.

She went to the north end of the painted lines and Brom went to the south, and they stood twenty feet apart with Cael between them in the middle of the floor, among the white crosses. The lanterns at either end threw their shadows long across the boards toward him, so that he stood where two shadows met.

"Both of us," said Lira. "Get past."

He knew the shape of it before they moved. Brom would come straight and slow and would not be where Cael wanted him to be. Lira would come fast and from the wrong side, with three bursts and the four counts and the empty third. Between them there was no room on the floor at all, only the place where their two reaches overlapped.

So he made room.

He asked the air to lean, along a line between them, from where he stood to the gap past Brom's left shoulder. Fifteen feet of it, a third deep, and the air tipped like a board stepped on at one end, and his ears closed and opened. Down at his feet the dust in the cracks of the boards woke up and ran, pale, along the lane toward Brom.

And the read went with it.

He felt it go. He had meant to keep the read on Brom's body, on the heels and the hips and the weight going down. Instead, the moment he laid the lane, the read went into the lane itself. It read the air: how hard it leaned, how far it ran, where it would let go. It was a beautiful read. It told him exactly where the end of the lane was. And it told him nothing at all about Brom.

He stepped into the lane and it carried him, fast, toward the gap past Brom's shoulder.

Brom was not there. Brom had moved half a beat before, and the read had not seen it go, because the read was looking at the air.

The forearm came across Cael's chest like a door shutting in a gale. He went down on the boards on his back, and the lane let go above him, and all the breath he owned went up into the dark of the gallery and stayed there.

Brom stood over him, looking down.

"You weren't looking at me," said Brom.

"No." Cael lay on the painted floor and looked at the shuttered windows. "I was looking at the road."

He had known it. He had written it in the year's ledger at Norhold, in the guesting-house, before the road home: *at Gold it will do one or the other and not both.* He had learned it in the ring, which was a bad place to learn things. Now he had learned it again on a floor where the worst that could happen was Brom, and he lay there and let the knowing go all the way down into him, past the place where he knew things to the place where he did them.

The read could take a body or it could take the air. It could not take both at once. Not at Gold. Not yet.

He sat up.

"Again," he said. "I'm going to lay it first, before I need it, and then give the read back to you. The lane doesn't need watching once it's down. You do."

Brom went back to the south end without a word, and Lira was already at the north.

The second time, he laid the lane while they were still walking to their marks. It ran from his feet to the gap past Brom's left shoulder, fifteen feet, a third deep, and the dust ran down it and gathered at the end. Then he took the read off it, deliberately, the way you take your hand off a dog you have told to stay, and sent the read to Brom.

Brom came. His weight went down into his right heel half a beat before he moved. The read had it.

Cael stepped into the lane.

It carried him down past the place where Brom's forearm was going to be and was not yet. Lira's burst came off the wrong foot at the wrong angle and met nothing; she had read him going one way and the lane had taken him a third faster than any feet could. He came out of the end of the lane past Brom's shoulder, behind both of them, and stopped.

Then the cost came in.

It was not in the hip and it was not in the shoulder. It was behind the eyes, a heaviness, as if somebody had laid a warm hand over the inside of his skull. And everything else he carried went slow with it. He reached for the Wind, out of curiosity, the way you test a stair, and it came, but it came late: a beat late, the way a dog comes when it has decided to come in its own time. He counted. For ten counts after the lane, everything answered him a beat behind his asking. At eleven it was gone, and the hand had lifted off the inside of his head, and the Wind came when he called it.

Lira had turned round, and Brom had turned round, and they were both looking at him.

"What did that cost?" said Lira.

He told her exactly: behind the eyes, ten counts of everything slow, gone at eleven. She wrote it on her hand under the shoulder and the hip, small, because she was running out of hand.

"Ten counts," said Brom, "in a real bout."

"Is a long time."

"Is a long time," Brom agreed.

Cael sat on a painted cross and thought about the notice.

He had it by heart. Every notice he had ever had he knew by heart, because he had read each of them in a corridor or on a stair with nothing else to look at, and the one from Norhold most of all. *Note: pressure-differential component; corridor-seeding component. Short range.* At the time it had read like a clerk's description of somebody else's coat. Tonight he thought he could put his hand on each line of it. The lane was the corridor; he seeded it, and it grew where he put it, and stayed while he left it alone. The tilt of the air, the ears, the dust running downhill, that was the pressure. Short range was fifteen feet. And the notice had said nothing at all about the read going into the air and leaving the body behind, or about ten counts of everything slow. Notices never told you what a thing cost. They only told you what it was. The cost you found out on a floor, with friends, if you were lucky, and in front of eight thousand people if you were not.

They sat down on the floor afterward among the white crosses, the three of them, with the jug of water going round and the lanterns burning low. Lira lay on her back with her taped hands on her stomach and her eyes shut. Brom sat with his legs out in front of him, unwinding his wraps slowly, a turn at a time, rolling each length up as he went.

And in the gap, it came.

He did not feel it begin. He was sitting on the boards with the jug in his hands, and then he was a little less than that, thinner, a place the lantern light slid over on its way to the wall.

"One," said Lira, without opening her eyes.

*Hold.*

He gave his attention to Brom. To Brom's hands, rolling up the wrap, turn after turn, the big careful fingers smoothing each length down before the next went over it. He watched the wrap grow into a neat tight roll the size of a fist. Watching Brom was not hiding, and the thinness went out of him like breath.

"Back," said Lira, still with her eyes shut.

"How did you know? You weren't looking."

Then he lowered it, and stood, and lifted it again.

Bracken was still at the window when Cael reached the landing. He did not turn round.

"Good morning," said Bracken. "Your hip is bad. I heard you on the stair."

From up here you could see the whole of the bluff road's last two switchbacks, and the bend below them, and a long stretch of the river road beyond the bridge, all of it pale and empty in the early sun. "You were at the lecture range's windows on First-day."

"I was."

"And this is the seventh day."

"It is." Bracken lifted the glass again, looked, and lowered it. He was quiet for a while. I've never yet seen an answer come down a road that I didn't see coming a minute before it reached the gate. I see no reason to begin now."

"How long do they usually take?"

"Longer than this, for a small question." Bracken put the glass in his coat. "This is not a small question."

He did not say anything else, and Cael did not ask him to. They stood together at the high window for a while longer, a narrow grey man and a boy with a bad hip, looking at an empty road in the sun. A cart came up out of the trees on the lowest switchback, loaded with hay, a farmer walking at the horse's head. Bracken took the glass out again, and looked, and put it away.

"Hay," he said.

That night Cael wrote it in Hesk's book under the honest floor's costs, which Lira had copied out for Karis three times in capitals and once more for him.

*Seventh-day. Bracken at his high window with a ship's glass. Seven days he has watched the road, and he says the question isn't small. I believe him. I have never known him to round a number.*

# Chapter 5 — Served

The eighth day of the year was a First-day, hot and still, and at the second bell Cael was on the covered walk with a book under his arm and nowhere in particular to be.

The house had found its feet by then. A week was long enough for that. The intake had stopped walking in groups of six and started walking in groups of two. The boards along the walk had filled up with the year's ordinary paper: the research standings, the program's floor hours, the refectory's rota, and a sheet in Ephram's square hand announcing that the captain would hear grievances on Third-day evenings in the common room and would not be held responsible for his answers. The fire-watch boy's slate hung on its nail under the north arch with last night's hours chalked down it in large careful figures, the last of them rubbed out once and made again.

Cael stopped at the slate, because he always did, and then he looked east, because the arch looked east, and saw them.

Three riders, on the last switchback below the gate.

They were coming up at a walk. Two rode a little ahead and to either side, in grey with a badge on the shoulder, and they rode the way men ride who have been told the pace and are keeping it whether it suits the horses or not. The third rode between them. He sat very upright, and across the front of his saddle, strapped tight against the horse's withers so that it could not shift, lay a case of dark leather the length of his forearm.

Cael knew what he was looking at before the knowing reached words. He had seen a rider carry a house's papers down that road once, at the start of last year, in a case strapped inside a saddlebag, at a working trot, and nobody had looked twice. A thing going down the road travelled like freight. This was a thing coming up the road, and it was travelling like a prisoner.

He looked up at the records hall. The high window at its north end, where Bracken had stood all week with his ship's glass, was empty.

Bracken had seen them a full minute before the gate did. Of course he had. He had been waiting eight days to.

At the gate, the lodge-man came out of his door and stood up straight, the way he stood for the Chancellor's carriage and for nothing else. The riders did not stop for him. They came through the gate at the same walk, without dismounting, and the lodge-man stepped back to let them pass and watched them go by with his hat in his hand. They crossed the outer court with their hooves loud on the stones in the heat, and every head on the covered walk turned to follow them, and they went on round the corner of the lecture range toward the records hall as if nobody on the hill had a right to stop them, which was true.

Cael put his book under his other arm and went after them.

He did not run. A boy running across a court after a registry escort would have been a story by noon. He walked fast, at the pace of somebody late for a lecture, and he came round the corner of the lecture range in time to see the third rider dismount at the records-hall steps and unstrap the case from his saddle. The two outriders stayed mounted. They sat their horses at the foot of the steps, one on each side, facing outward.

Cael went past them and up the steps and in.

A document that came up that road under that kind of escort was a document about him. He had been learning since his Kindling to be in the room when his own paper arrived, and he had never once been sorry he was.

---

The records hall was cool after the court, and dim, and very quiet.

Bracken was behind his counter. He had not come down from the window in a hurry; there was no sign on him that he had come down at all. He stood with both hands resting on the counter's edge, a narrow grey man in a grey coat, and when Cael came in he looked at him once and then back at the door, and said nothing.

The rider came in behind Cael with the case under his arm.

He was not a rider, Cael saw, now that he was on his feet. The men on the horses were riders. This man wore the plain grey of a registry office, cut well and kept clean, and at his collar he had a small seal of office in dull silver that Cael did not know. He was perhaps fifty, with thin hair combed close and the careful face of a man who has carried a great many things to a great many counters and has never once been the thing that was carried. He set the case on the counter and laid his hand on it and waited.

Withrow came in from the inner door thirty seconds later.

She was walking at exactly her ordinary pace. Cael had watched her cross courts and corridors and halls for two years and knew it as well as he knew Brom's step on a stair: unhurried, even, neither quick nor slow, the pace of a woman who has decided that nothing will ever be allowed to set it for her. He knew, watching her come down the length of the hall at that pace, with the whole hill already talking outside, that she had chosen it on the stair and was holding it on purpose. It was the most deliberate walk he had ever seen.

She stopped at the counter beside Bracken, and the officer bowed to her, to a depth that somebody had once measured, and took a card from his coat.

"An instrument of the registry, under seal," he read from the card, in a voice with nothing in it. "For joint service upon the Chancellor of Halcenvane Academy and upon the enrollee of record whom it names, the two together and in their own persons. Service to be acknowledged in both their hands. The seal to stay whole until it is." He lowered the card. "Are both parties present?"

"Both parties are present," said Withrow.

She did not look at Cael. She did not need to. The officer looked at him, once, the way a man checks a figure against the sum he was given, and looked away again.

He unlocked the case with a key from his watch-chain and took out a form first, a single printed sheet with two ruled lines at its foot. Cael had never seen its like. It was headed *Acknowledgment of Joint Service*, and under the heading there was a paragraph of small type, and under that, two lines.

Withrow signed on the first line. She signed her whole name, slowly, with the officer's pen.

Then she held the pen out to Cael without a word.

He signed beneath her. His hand was quite steady. He noticed that, and wrote it down in his head as a fact, because it surprised him a little.

The officer witnessed both signatures in his own hand, sanded them from a box in the case, folded the form along a crease that was already in it, and sealed it inside the case again. Then he took out the instrument.

It was thick. It lay on the counter in front of the three of them, a sheaf of heavy paper folded once along its length, and across the fold, holding it shut, sat a wafer of grey wax the size of a mark, pressed with a seal Cael had seen before only on the outside of other people's letters. The Compact's.

"Served," said the officer. He bowed to Withrow again, to the same depth, and to Bracken a little less, and to Cael not at all. Then he took up his case and went out. They heard him go down the steps, and a moment later they heard the three horses go away across the court at a walk, and then nothing.

Nobody touched the instrument.

Then Withrow put out her hand and broke the wax.

She read it on her feet. She did not sit, or pass the instrument to Bracken, whose office it was to read the house's papers, and she did not send Cael away from the counter. The paper named the two of them together. In two years Cael had never once seen Withrow open a letter that was not hers, or keep from anybody a letter that was theirs. She unfolded the sheaf and turned back its first leaf, and read it through once to herself, at her ordinary reading pace, while the hall stood quiet around her.

Then she read it aloud.

She read it in a voice Cael had never heard her use. He understood after a sentence or two that it was not a new voice at all. It was the oldest one she had. It was the flat, even, careful voice of the registry official she had been for twenty years before she was anybody's chancellor, the voice that had read instruments aloud on service so that the record could say they had been read, and had never once needed to put anything of itself into them. She was reading to the enrollee of record, on service, so that the house's book would show it had been done.

*To the Chancellor, Halcenvane Academy, and to the enrollee of record, Cael, unclassified, enrolled under the demonstration provision of the assay clause, jointly:*

*Be it known that the Guilds Compact, by its registry, having convened formal proceedings upon the question of the classification designated [SHATTERED] and its bearing upon evaluation integrity within chartered institutions, hereby files a formal challenge to enrollment basis against the said enrollment.*

*The challenge does not allege fraud in the enrollment's execution, nor contest the text of the provision under which it was executed. It asserts that an unclassifiable practitioner's presence within a chartered institution's evaluation architecture presents an evaluation-integrity question which the demonstration provision was not drafted to answer and cannot answer, and petitions the registry for compelled re-evaluation of the enrollee under standard Arbiter protocol, the enrollment to stand or fall upon that evaluation's result.*

*Proceedings to be convened at the registry seat at Ostrand upon a schedule of one academic year. The respondent institution and the enrollee of record are directed to answer within fourteen days.*

*Given under the Compact's hand.*

She lowered the leaf.

Nobody in the records hall said anything. Bracken had not moved his hands from the counter. Far off, through the open door, Cael could hear the escort's horses on the first switchback, and then he could not hear them.

"*Given under the Compact's hand,*" said Withrow at last. She said it in the same level voice, as if she were reading it again, though she was not looking at the page. "I have signed that line myself. Nine hundred times."

She turned the first leaf down again, edge to edge with the others underneath it. Her fingers did not shake. Cael saw that it had cost her something to make sure they would not.

"Registrar," she said. "Copies. One to counsel by the afternoon courier, under the house's seal. One to the enrollee. One to the reading room at the top of the library." She paused. "Tonight. All of them tonight."

"Tonight," said Bracken.

Then she turned to Cael, and her voice was still the registry voice, because it was the only instrument she trusted in that moment and she was not going to put it down to pick up another.

"They have found a new way to ask the old question," she said. "Go up and tell Karis. Tell her before she hears it from the stair. She'll want to be reading it before she's finished being angry, and she'll never forgive us if she spends one minute of the fourteen being angry without anything in front of her."

---

He took the library stair two at a time, all four flights, with the hip reminding him at every landing that it had been promised a quieter week.

The door at the top was shut. The card was in its brass holder. The new lock was locked.

He knocked, and the key turned on the inside, and Karis opened the door with a pen in her hand and ink on the side of her thumb and the look of somebody who has been interrupted in the middle of a sentence and means to go back to it.

Then she saw his face, and the look went.

"What," she said.

"A registry instrument. Under escort. Served jointly on Withrow and me in the records hall ten minutes ago." He made himself say it in order, the way she would want it. "A challenge to the enrollment basis. Fourteen days to answer. Withrow read the first page aloud. Bracken is making you a copy now."

Karis stood in the doorway and did not move.

He told her the first page. He had it nearly whole; he had been listening the way he listened to anything that would have to be written down later. *Does not allege fraud. Nor contest the text.* *Evaluation integrity.* *Compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol.* *Stand or fall.* She listened to all of it without interrupting, with her pen still in her hand, and when he had finished she looked past him, down the narrow stair, for a long moment.

"Standard Arbiter protocol," she said. "Those words? Exactly those?"

"Exactly those."

She was quiet. He knew what she was seeing, because he was seeing it too. Standard protocol meant a station: a frame in a stone room, a registry officer at its side, a practitioner standing in it while the frame read him. It was how every Path on the continent had been declared since before the registry kept books. The frame read the Path and named it, and set a tier against it, and a rank inside the tier, and the officer wrote down what the frame had said, and that was a person's classification for life. It was how Lira had been declared, and Brom, and Karis herself. It was how the frame at Denvash had been asked, on the morning of his Kindling, what he was, and had answered with one word in brackets and nothing else at all.

The demonstration provision had never asked a frame anything. That was the whole of why it held him.

"They read the hearing," she said. "Every page of it. Every page we won on." Her voice was quite level. "And then they went away and wrote a paper that doesn't touch a single one of them."

That was all she spent on it. He watched her spend it, and then he watched her put it away, somewhere he could not see, and turn round into the room.

"Sit down," she said. "No. Don't sit down. Go and find her."

"Your assistant?"

"She's in the library's lower reading room with the district digests. Tell her I want the registry code, all three volumes, the bound ones from the records hall and not the library's, because the library's second volume is missing a gathering and nobody has ever noticed but me. Tell her I want the Greyvane hearing's transcript, the certified copy, from my own shelf in the common room, the grey card cover with my initials on the spine." She was already clearing the long table, moving the founding-era compilations to the shelf in their three bindings, one after another, with great care. "And tell her to ask Bracken, very politely, whether the registrar would be so good as to lend me the four bound rulings in his locked press. He'll know which four. There are only four."

"Four what?"

"Four times anybody ever went to law against an enrollment made under that clause." The last of the three bindings went up beside the others. "I read their spines through the glass two years ago, and I've wanted to read their insides ever since, and I didn't want it badly enough to ask. I want it now." She turned. "And tea. Tell her to bring tea. And tell her not to expect to sleep tonight in her own bed, and to say so to whoever she needs to say it to."

He went back down the four flights to find a second-year with ink on her collar.

She was where Karis had said, in the lower reading room, at a table under the window with the district digests stacked in front of her, writing something in her small notebook. She looked up when he came in, and he saw her see his face and close the notebook.

He gave her Karis's list in order. She did not write any of it down. She said it back to him, once, all of it, including the missing gathering in the library's second volume, and stood up, and pushed her chair in.

"The tea first," she said. "She thinks better with it. The rest in the order she said."

"She said not to expect to sleep."

"She always says that," said the assistant, and went out past him at a fast walk, and he heard her break into a run on the walk outside, which he suspected was the first time she had run anywhere on the hill since the intake.

Nobody told it. Nobody needed to. Bracken's boy brought the enrollee's copy up to the residence at the seventh bell, in a grey folder under the house's seal, and Cael took it to his window and laid it on the sill and opened it at the first page.

Across the court and over the roof of the east range he could see the long row of the records hall's windows. In the last of them, at the north end, a lamp had been lit already, though the sun was not yet down.

He took out Hesk's book and Hesk's steel pen, and wrote the entry in one pass, without crossing anything out, because he had already decided every word of it on the library stair.

*They filed. After the hearing I won, and the inspection, and a tournament that ran out of numbers for me, they've gone back to the shelf for one more way to ask. Fine. Their text has lost to ours before. Karis has the code open already. Note the date. The season's open.*

He sanded it and gave it its minute by Hesk's clock, an hour fast, and closed the book.

Then he looked at what he had written, for a while, with the book shut.

He had meant every word. He found he meant them still. A year ago he had been a boy on a provision, alive because somebody kept writing it down, and a hearing, a panel and the continent's oldest contest had all tried their instruments on him and found they could not make him fit, and every time the text had held him. He had no reason to think this text would not.

But he noticed something, sitting at the window with the book closed on his knee, and he made himself look at it squarely, because noticing things was the one work he had never been able to stop doing.

It was the voice. Withrow's registry voice, reading the first page aloud in the records hall. He had thought, while she read, that she was using it because it was the right instrument for service. He thought now that she had been using it because it was the only one she had that would not shake.

He had never once heard Withrow need a voice that would not shake.

Down in the court a lamp-boy went past toward the records hall with his long pole over his shoulder. At the end of the east range, one after another, the records hall's windows were coming alight.

Cael put the book in his coat, and took the folder from the sill, and went down to join them.

# Chapter 6 — The Records Hall

The fire-watch kept an oil book. Every lamp lit on the hill after the tenth bell went into it, by building and by hour, so that the bursar could see at the year's end where the house had burned its money in the dark. Bracken signed it himself every morning, and had done for thirteen years, and in all that time the records hall had never once had a line in it.

At a little past the eighth bell he took the book down from its hook by the side door, opened it on the counter at the night's page, and wrote in his small upright hand.

*Records hall. Lamps late. On the registrar's authority, and at his charge.*

Then he hung it back on its hook and went to light the lamps.

There were six along the long table, on iron stands, and two on the counter, and one over the door to the locked press. He lit them all. When he had done it the hall looked like a room Cael had never seen before: warm at the middle and black at the edges, the tall windows gone dark and blind, the long table laid down the length of the floor like a road with lamps along it.

Four of them sat at it.

Bracken had the head of the table, nearest his counter, with the registry code in front of him in its three bound volumes, stacked in order with the spines toward him. Beside them lay the four bound rulings from the press. Karis had the near side, with her copy of the instrument open flat and her notebook open beside it and her pens laid out in a row along the edge of the table like a surgeon's knives. Her assistant had sat at her elbow until the tenth bell, writing whatever she was told to write, and had then been sent to bed and had gone protesting all the way to the door, in a whisper, so as not to disturb the room.

Cael had the foot of the table, with the observation notebook. He had nothing to do there and he knew it. But it was his anatomy that was being taken apart on that table, and he had been learning since his Kindling that a man should be present for his own anatomy.

The fourth chair was the counsel's.

She had come up the bluff road at the ninth bell, in a hired chaise from Ostrand, with one bag. Cael had never seen her before. She was not the counsel who had come up the hill in the inspection's carriages, the one who had talked, by every account, from the bridge to the gate, and had signed for her chests with a face like a cat in a bath. That counsel had been the Compact's, and this one was the house's. She had held the house's retainer for eleven years, and it had been a condition of the retainer from the first day that her name should appear on nothing she advised, only the academy's. So on the bluff she had no name. She was simply counsel, the way the river was simply the river.

She was a small grey woman with very straight shoulders and a short steel rule, the kind a joiner uses, which she laid under each line of the instrument as she read it, so that her eye could not slip to the next before it had finished with this one. She did not talk while she read. When she did talk, she spoke in short complete sentences with nothing left over at the end of them, as if each one had been costed in advance.

Withrow did not sit.

She came and went. She came down from her office to the table, and stood at its head behind Bracken's chair for a while, reading over his shoulder, and went back up. She came down again with the inspection year's finding in its folder, and laid it at Karis's elbow without a word, and went back up. She came down a third time with a tea tray, and nobody remarked that the Chancellor of Halcenvane was carrying a tea tray, and she set it at the table's foot by Cael and went back up.

The fourth time she came down, she was carrying a book.

It was a small book, in a brown cloth binding gone pale at the corners, and Cael knew at once from the size and the colour of it that it was a registry book. Every registrar on the continent had been issued one like it at the start of his service: the procedures volume of the code in a pocket copy, to carry on circuit. Bracken had one in his coat. This one was older than Bracken's and had been carried further.

Withrow opened it at a page near the middle that it opened at by itself, and laid it on the table beside Bracken's great third volume, open at the same place. Then she went and stood at the head of the table with her hands folded in front of her and did not say anything.

Cael leaned across and read the open page upside down.

It was chapter fourteen. The print was the same as the great volume's, only smaller. But the margins of Withrow's copy were full. There were notes in pencil and notes in ink, in a hand that was hers and was not, quite: the same letters, made by somebody younger and in more of a hurry. Most of the notes were dates and the names of districts.

He counted before he read, because counting came first.

One clause on the page had been underlined twice. The first line was in black ink, thin and very straight, made against a rule. The second, under it, was in a brown ink that had faded toward rust, and was not straight at all. Beside the second underlining there was a bracket in the margin, and nothing written in the bracket.

"You're counting my pencil marks," said Withrow from the head of the table, without turning round. "Go on. Ask about them."

"What did you underline?"

She came back down the table and laid a fingertip in the margin by the line. It did not tremble. Cael noted that, because he had expected it to.

"*At its discretion,*" she read. "Part two. *Suspension of practitioner status pending review, such review to be initiated by the registry at its discretion.*" She lifted her fingertip off the page. "The black line I drew in my second year at the registry, with a rule, very neatly. I was young. I thought *discretion* meant the registry would be allowed to be merciful, if it chose. I thought it was a kind word." She looked at the brown line. "I underlined it the second time nine years later, with no rule at all, sitting on a coach going home from a district I'd rather not name. By then I knew what it actually meant."

"What does it mean?"

"That nobody is obliged to begin." She said it quite simply. "The review can begin whenever the registry likes. Tomorrow, or in a year, or never. There is no date in it, and no officer named who must set one." She looked at the faded bracket. "There is a difference between a penalty and a condition, Cael. A penalty is a thing that is done to you, and then it is over. A condition is a place you are put, and nobody is obliged to come and let you out." She looked past him, up the table, to Bracken. "Registrar. Count me the dates in that chapter. Any date at all. Any limit of days."

Bracken went through it at the pace he went through everything, a line at a time with his finger running under the print, and it took him a while, and nobody at the table hurried him.

"None," he said.

"None," Withrow agreed. She closed her brown book and put it under her arm. "I'll be upstairs."

She went. They heard her on the stair, at her ordinary pace, all the way up.

Cael counted the instrument before anybody read a clause of it aloud.

There was nothing else for him to do at that table, and counting was the work he trusted. A paper had a floor plan like any building. You walked round it first. You learned its size and the number of its doors before you went in and started reading the writing on the walls.

Nineteen leaves, written on one side only, on a heavy cream paper with the registry's mark pressed into the corner of each. Thirty-seven numbered paragraphs. Three schedules annexed at the back, each with its own small heading. The paragraphs were short, most of them a few lines, and the longest was the ninth. The schedules were longer than any paragraph.

Then he counted words. He did it with a pencil, in the margin of the observation notebook, a stroke for each one, and he went through the whole instrument twice, because a count you have only done once is a guess.

*Discretion* appeared nine times. *Evidence* appeared three times, and two of those three were in the phrase *no evidence is required to be adduced*. The word *fraud* appeared once, in the second paragraph, in a sentence whose whole purpose was to say that there was none. The instrument cited eight provisions of the registry code by chapter and section, and the convening's own minute twice, by date. It cited nothing else at all. Not a ruling. Not a precedent. Not a witness.

Schedule two was a list of the file's standing entries. Cael read it with more care than the rest, because it was a list of himself. It was written in the registry's own grammar, the grammar of declarations, every entry with a heading for Path and a heading for tier and a heading for rank, as every practitioner's file on the continent was written. Under Path, on his, there was the one bracketed word from the frame at Denvash. Under tier there was a short ruled line with nothing above it. Under rank, the same. He had seen his file's front sheet once before, at Greyvane, and it had looked like this then: a form built to hold an answer, holding the shape of a question instead.

The entries ran down under it in order: his Kindling at Denvash, the Greyvane hearing and its finding, the provision with the stamp and its renewals, and the tournament's entry, every one of its eleven words. And between the hearing and the provision, given its full title as if it were the most ordinary thing on the page, the line he had known was on his file since the inspection year, and had never once seen written down in front of him.

*Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4.*

It was cited and not argued from; no paragraph leaned on it or so much as named it. It sat in its schedule like a stone in a field that the plough has been driven round.

He looked at it for a long moment, and then he went on, because it was not what this night was for, and he could feel Karis watching him from across the table, and he did not want her to have to decide whether to ask.

The last leaf had the attestation on it, above the seal's impression. It was in the same type as everything else and no larger. *Given under the Compact's hand.* Under it, smaller, a line he had not heard read aloud in the records hall that morning, because Withrow had stopped at the first leaf. *For the petitioner, the procurator of record.* And a name.

None of them had heard of it. Bracken said so, when Cael passed him the leaf. He said he knew the name of every procurator who had stood before a registry seat in this district for thirteen years, and of most in the next two districts as well, and this was not one of them.

"Which is information," he said, and handed it back.

Cael wrote the tallies down in a column, and drew a line under them, and looked at them.

What they said, before a single clause had been read aloud, was that somebody had written nineteen leaves without expecting to spend any time at all on proving anything. Nine discretions to three evidences. A man who expects to have to prove a thing writes the word *evidence* on every page, because he will be standing in front of a seat with his evidence in his hands. A man who writes *discretion* nine times expects to be standing in front of a seat that will simply choose.

He wrote that under the line. *They don't expect to prove anything. They expect to be agreed with.* Then he passed the column down the table to Karis, and she read it, and put it with her notes without a word, and he realized he had just done a piece of the night's real work.

The counsel read it backward.

Cael charted it from the foot of the table, because he had never seen anybody read a document that way. Most readers start where a paper starts, and she did not. She turned to the last numbered paragraph, the thirty-seventh, laid her steel rule under its first line and read that first, and then the thirty-sixth, and then the thirty-fifth. She went up through the instrument from its end to its beginning, a paragraph at a time, the rule moving up the page one line at a time like a man climbing a ladder.

At the tenth paragraph she stopped and made a note. At the fourth she stopped and made another. When she reached the address on the first leaf she did not read it at all. She closed the instrument and laid the rule across it.

"Why backward?" said Cael.

She looked at him down the length of the table as if he had asked her the price of something, and then decided that it was a fair question and he could have the answer for nothing.

"The end of a paper is what the writer wants," she said. "The middle is why he says he should have it. The front is the history he tells you so the why will sound reasonable." She tapped the rule. "If you read from the front you are reading his story, in his order, and by the time you reach the end you have already half agreed to it. If you read from the end you know what he wants before he has had a chance to tell you why. Then you can watch him build the why."

"And the history? At the front?"

"Recitals." She said the word as a joiner might say *green timber*. "That is where a nervous drafter puts his conscience. In twenty-six years I have won most of what I've won in the recitals. The other side recites some piece of history to make his case sound fair, and the piece of history gives me something he needs and did not notice he was giving." She turned the instrument over and opened it at the first leaf again, without looking at the address. "In this paper the recital runs to four sentences. On a paper of this weight I would expect forty. Whoever drafted it knew that somebody would read it backward, and he left me nothing in the front to read."

Bracken, at the head of the table, had laid the four bound rulings out in a row.

"You'll want these, then," he said.

They read all four before midnight.

They were not long. The provision's whole history at law would have fitted on a single shelf of a single press, and it did. Bracken read the headings aloud and Karis read the findings, and the counsel listened with her eyes shut and her rule across her knees.

The first was a river house's, from a long way south. It had enrolled a ferryman's daughter whose Path had come to her late, at a station that could not settle on what it was, and a richer house upriver had gone to law to say she had no business being taught. The finding was a dozen lines long and said, in effect, that the clause said what a house might do, and the house had done it, and so the girl would stay.

Karis read it out in her flat finding voice, and Cael found he had stopped writing.

He had not known there had been anybody else. He had known about the clause for two years, and about the four rulings in Bracken's press for nearly as long, and it had never once occurred to him that the rulings had people in them. A ferryman's daughter, a long time ago, standing in some hall in the south while two houses argued over whether she could be taught. A station that could not settle. He wondered whether she had kept a book. He wondered what had become of her after the finding, and whether anybody had ever written that down, and knew that nobody had, because findings did not have room for afterward.

"Her name's on it?" he said.

Karis looked at the heading. "Her initials. And the house's." She read them out. They meant nothing to anybody at the table. "It was a long time ago."

He wrote the initials in the notebook anyway, small, at the edge of the page.

The second ruling was a mason's guild that had put an apprentice into its school by demonstration, and a rival guild had challenged the form of the panel that watched him. It was sustained. The last two were much older, the paper gone brown and soft at the edges, the parties' names worn down to the guilds they had belonged to. Both had been sustained. Bracken closed each cover as Karis finished, and laid it back in its row, square to the table's edge.

"That is the clause's whole history at law," said the counsel, when the last brown cover had closed. "Four houses went to law against the text, and the text beat every one of them." She opened her eyes. "And Greyvane. Greyvane's hearing went at the text too, by another road, and lost the same way. Five."

"This one doesn't go at the text," said Karis.

"No." The counsel picked up her rule. "This one was written by somebody who read all five and decided not to write a sixth."

Karis took the first finding.

She did it standing, which she did when a thing was going to be minuted, and she did it with the instrument's second paragraph flat on the table under one hand and the Greyvane transcript open under the other.

"They allege no fraud," she said. "Read it." She read it. "*No fraud, irregularity or want of form is alleged in any act by which the enrollment was made.* That's the whole of it. Not in the filing. Not in the hearing. Not in the stamp or its renewals or the house's certificate or the tournament's entry. They don't touch one of them." She lifted her hand off the transcript. "Every story the broadsides printed last year was about a trick. The secret register. The hidden Path. The staged hearing. A clerk paid off. Every one of those stories was a road to a fraud, and this paper has walked past the end of every road without looking down it. Greyvane shut those doors on the record, and they've read the record, and they know the doors are shut." She sat down. "That's the first finding. They don't waste a word on anything they could lose."

The counsel took the second, and she did it in the voice of somebody reading out the weather.

"Paragraph six," she said. She laid the rule under it. "*The provision by which the enrollment was made is acknowledged to be valid, and to have survived in law the standardization directive. Neither is put in issue.*" She lifted the rule. "That is our case. That is the whole of what I would have spent this year proving to the seat. Whether the clause is still law. Whether the directive killed it. Whether a house can still enroll a practitioner by demonstration on a clause out of the founding text. They have given it to us. In the sixth paragraph. In writing. Under their own seal." She laid the rule down. "In twenty-six years nobody has ever handed me my best ground in the first paper they filed."

"Is that good?" said Cael.

The counsel looked at him.

"When a man gives you the field," she said, "look at what he has kept for himself."

Karis had already turned the page.

She read the seventh paragraph to herself. Cael watched her read it. He watched her finger move along the first line and the second, and stop.

Her finger stopped on the page, and her whole body went still around it.

He knew that stillness. He had seen it at Greyvane over a shelf, and in the reading room over the founding articles, and at Norhold over a charter, and every time it meant that she had found the thing on the page that the page had been built to hide. He counted it, because he could not help counting it. One, two, three. It went on past eight, past ten. At twelve she moved.

She did not read it. She turned the instrument round on the table so that it faced the rest of them, and put her finger on the paragraph, and took her finger away.

The counsel laid her rule under it and read it aloud.

*7. The petitioner notes, and adopts as its own, the advisory entered in the prior year by an evaluator of the Compact upon the fielding clause of the tournament charter, which reads in full: "The clause says what it says. The enrollment is lawful on its face and adjudicated at public record. If the Compact wishes practitioners of this kind not to compete, it should write different rules — not misread the ones it has."*

Nobody spoke.

"That's his," said Karis. Her voice was very level. "The Archmarshal's. Vastin's. Three sentences. You told us at Norhold there were three sentences of advisory, Cael, and none of us had ever seen them." She looked at the paragraph. "Now we have. They've quoted the whole of it. With approval."

"*The clause says what it says,*" said Cael slowly.

"I know." Karis did not look up. "I said that. On the bench under the thorn at the first bend, the morning the entry went down the hill. *It says what it says all the same.* I said it to you." She laid her palms on the wood, one either side of the instrument. "He said it to them. In writing. He told the Compact, in the plainest words any evaluator ever put on a form, that the text holds and that if they don't like what it holds they must write different rules."

"And they have," said the counsel.

"They have." Karis's face changed then.

Cael had been watching that face for three years. He had seen it angry and amused and bored and cold, and once or twice, very late, tired enough to be kind. He had seen it at Norhold on finals day in the moment the stoppage was called, and that had been the worst. He had never seen it look like this. It was not anger, and it was not cold. She was looking at a printed paragraph on a page of cream paper as if the page had moved.

"They haven't argued with the text," she said. "Not one word. They've agreed with it. They've taken the most honest thing anybody in the Compact ever wrote about you, from the most honest man they have, and they've done exactly what it told them to do. So here are the new rules, written, and the warrant they give for writing them is *him*." She took her hands off the table. "I want it minuted that this is the first thing in this paper that frightens me. Put in your minute that I said so at the seventh paragraph, before I'd seen the eighth."

"Minuted," said the counsel.

She had a page at her elbow now, Cael saw, that had not been there at the beginning of the night. At its head, in a small exact hand, she had written *Halcenvane: the Compact's petition. Working minute. Privileged.* She wrote Karis's sentence on it, word for word, and the time by the hall's clock, and blotted it.

Cael put it in the observation notebook as well, word for word, since word for word was the only way that notebook took anything. Then, underneath, he wrote the other thing. It had come to him while Karis was speaking, quite clearly, like a sum adding itself at the bottom of a column.

*Vastin, in the room full of clocks: the people who want this to stop are not going to use official channels much longer. This is the most official paper I have ever held. Nineteen leaves, a seal, an escort at a walk, and a form of joint service I had never seen in my life. So either he was wrong, or this is the last official thing.*

*And they've made his own honest sentence carry it. He said write different rules. They're telling the seat they're only doing what the honest man advised.*

He looked at it for a while.

He found he was not frightened, not yet. He was something closer to cold. It was the feeling he had had on the morning of his Kindling at Denvash, standing in front of a frame that had nothing at all to say about him and was perfectly calm about it. He knew from the feeling that he would be frightened later, when there was time, and that it would come at him in the dark at the window and not here at the table, where there was work.

He looked up, and Karis was looking at him across the lamps.

"Go on," he said. "There's more of it. You said so."

"There's a great deal more of it," said Karis, and turned the instrument back round to face herself, and found the ninth paragraph with her finger. "And none of it gets any better."

# Chapter 7 — Corridor

It was Bracken who knew the phrase first, and he knew it from the wrong side.

Karis had the ninth paragraph under her finger, the longest in the instrument, and had read it out once to the table, and the hall was quiet while the four of them turned it over. The paragraph said that an unclassifiable practitioner inside a chartered house's evaluations raised a question of *evaluation integrity*. It said that the demonstration provision had been drafted for practitioners whose classification was only pending, and not for one who had none to wait for, and so could neither foresee that question nor settle it. And it said that the question therefore fell to the registry itself, under its general charge over the soundness of its own evaluations.

"Evaluation integrity," said Karis. "I've never once seen that phrase in a hearing. Where does it live?"

"In the second volume," said Bracken, "in a part nobody reads unless something has gone wrong with a frame."

He did not reach for the volume. He sat with his hands folded on the table, looking at the lamp in front of him, and when he spoke again it was in the voice of a man remembering a particular day.

"I filed one once," he said. "My fourth year in the office. The district station in Ostrand had a frame that began reading every Stone who stood in it a rank higher than he was. Not by much. Enough. A month of Stones went out of that station with a rank on their papers they hadn't earned, and nobody noticed until a quarryman's boy came up the hill to us with a certificate that said Rank Four and couldn't lift what a Rank Two lifts." He turned his folded hands over. "I wrote it up under the integrity provisions. That's what they are for. They took the frame off its floor and tested it for a fortnight and found a seam in the casing that let the damp in, and they mended it, and they read every Stone of that month over again, and the frame went back on its floor and read true." He looked down the table at Cael. "That's what an integrity question is, in that volume. It's a frame that has drifted. You take it off the floor and you test it until it reads true again."

Nobody said anything.

By then the second volume lay open in front of the counsel. She had reached across Bracken for it while he talked, and found the place, and laid her steel rule under the heading, and now she read down the sections with the rule moving under each line.

"Twelve sections," she said. "Frames. Stations. Assessors suspected of being bought. Panels whose records disagree with themselves. All instruments, or the keepers of instruments." She lifted the rule. "In every reported use I can find tonight, not once has anybody turned these sections round to face the person standing in the frame."

"Until this paper," said Karis.

"Until this paper." The counsel closed the volume on her rule. "And see what it does when it turns them. On its first leaf this was a question about the enrollment: may a house lawfully take him in? They have given that question away, in the sixth paragraph, whole. In its place they've set a different question, one nobody has ever had to answer, because nobody ever could. Can the registry's instruments evaluate him?" She looked at Cael, briefly. "That answer has been in your file since your Kindling. It is no."

"So I'm not wrong," said Cael. "I'm the frame."

"You are the frame." The counsel's voice did not change at all. "And a frame that will not read true comes off the floor."

Karis had turned to the back of the instrument while the counsel was speaking. She found the thirty-fourth paragraph without hunting for it, because she had marked it with a slip of paper when the counsel first read the paper upward, and she read it aloud in a voice with nothing added.

*34. That the enrollee be compelled to re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol, and that the enrollment stand or fall upon that evaluation's result.*

She did not say anything about it. The paragraph had been written so that nothing needed to be said about it.

The counsel turned in her chair then, and looked down the length of the table at Cael for the first time that night as if he were the person the night was about, and not the paper.

"What are you writing?" she said.

He turned the observation notebook round and slid it up the table to her. She looked down the column: nineteen leaves, thirty-seven paragraphs and three schedules; nine discretions to three evidences and one fraud, in the negative; eight provisions cited, two minutes, no rulings and no witnesses.

She read it twice, which he had not seen her do with anything else.

"Before the registrar reads you the next part," she said, "you ought to hear this from me, because you are the one it's about, and nobody should hear a remedy read before he knows what he's facing." She slid the notebook back to him. "In front of the seat they will say that a floor and a panel and a written record only describe you. That they tell the registry what you did, on a given day, before given witnesses, and nothing whatever about what you are. I'll answer that a floor and a panel are an evaluation, and the oldest kind there is, older than any frame, and I'll answer it as well as anybody living can. And the seat will have the discretion to like their answer better than mine." She tapped the column where it said *nine*. "You counted. Nine times."

"I counted."

"Then go on counting. Bring me a column like that every week until this is over." She turned back to the table. "A count is evidence. Indignation never was."

The third volume lay at Bracken's elbow, shut, and for a while after the counsel had finished he did not touch it.

Then he drew it to him and opened it at a place he did not need to search for, and laid a strip of plain paper under the first line so that his eye would not wander, and put on his spectacles.

Bracken read it in a voice Cael had never heard him use, the flattest that had ever come out of him. Cael had heard Bracken read fee scales and certificates and the selection standards of a qualifying year, and all of them in the same dry level tone, the tone of a man who has read more pages aloud than he could count and has never once needed to put anything of himself into them. This was flatter still. It was the voice of a man reading a page he had certified a thousand times and had never in his life expected to hear, and who would not let himself listen to it now.

"Registry code. Evaluation procedures. Chapter fourteen." He did not look up. "*Where an evaluation conducted under standard Arbiter protocol returns no valid classification, the following shall apply in sequence.* One. *Nullification of any enrollment, sponsorship, or standing predicated upon the evaluand's practitioner status.* Two. *Suspension of practitioner status pending review, such review to be initiated by the registry at its discretion.* Three."

He stopped. The word that came next was one he had read many times and never once had any reason to say, and Cael watched him find that it was heavier to lift than to look at.

"*Asset-restriction.* Section fourteen, part nine. *The registry may restrict, freeze, or hold in abeyance any standing, record, sponsorship, endorsement, travel credential, or institutional relationship attaching to an unclassified person, pending resolution of practitioner status.*"

He took his spectacles off and held them by one arm.

"I have certified the page that's printed on," he said, "in every edition of the code that ever came through my office, and signed the flyleaf to say the copy was true. I never once read it as being about anybody. It was a page you turned past to get to the fees." He looked at the three short steps. "It wants an evaluation that comes back empty, you see. And evaluations don't come back empty. In thirteen years I've seen frames read low and frames read high and one that read every Stone a rank too generous. I have never seen one read nothing at all."

He looked up then, down the table, at Cael.

"And now there's one sitting at my table," he said. "Somebody, a long time ago, sat in a room like this and imagined you, and wrote down what we should do with you, in three steps, and filed it, and went home to his supper." He put the spectacles back on. "I've always thought of the code as a thing written by careful men. I'd never thought before tonight about what they were careful of."

Withrow had come back down while he was reading. Cael had not heard her on the stair. She was simply there, at the head of the table behind Bracken's chair, with the small brown book under her arm and one hand resting on the chair's back, as if she had been standing there all along.

"Let me tell you what it looks like," she said, "from the morning after."

She came round the end of the table into the lamplight, and spoke to Cael, and to nobody else.

"The morning after a restriction is entered, a practitioner goes down to the ferry, as he goes every week, and holds out his pass, and the ferryman has had a letter. Not a cruel letter. A short one, in the plainest form, telling him the pass is held in abeyance. The ferryman is sorry. The ferryman has a family and a licence." She did not raise her voice at all. "He goes to the guild hall where they've known him for years, and they've had a letter too, and it says that their association with him is under restriction, which in the registry's language means: decide this morning whether you would like to be next. He goes to the records office to see his own file and argue with it, and the file is sealed. Not destroyed. Nobody destroys a record. Sealed, so that he can't read it, and nobody he pays can read it, and nobody can stand up anywhere and say what's in it on his behalf." She paused. "And every house that ever put its name beside his gets a letter. This house would get a letter. And from that morning on, this house would answer every question about him to an office that had settled the answers before it asked."

She looked at him steadily.

"It's expulsion that follows you," said Withrow. "A town can put you out of its gate, and you walk to the next town. This shuts every door on the continent against you on one morning, by one post, and the paper never runs out, because nobody is ever obliged to begin the review." She took the brown book from under her arm and held it in both hands. "I administered two. In my registry years. The first was a man who had done worse than the registry ever knew, and I've never been sorry. The second I think about." She looked at the book, not at them. "He had a daughter. She came to my counter every week for a year to ask whether the review had begun. Every week I told her the truth, which was that nobody had begun it, and that nothing in the code obliged anybody to. And every week she thanked me for telling her, and went home." She was quiet a moment. "I have never forgotten her name. Put that down."

"Noted," said the counsel, and wrote it down, and did not write the name, because none had been given.

Withrow stood a moment longer. Then she laid her hand on Bracken's shoulder, briefly, which Cael had never in two years seen her do to anybody, and went back up the stair without another word.

Bracken took a card out of his coat.

It was the registry's pocket calendar for the year, printed small on stiff card, every day of the year in its own square. Every registrar carried one. Cael had seen Bracken take it out a hundred times at his counter to answer a question about a fee or a filing, and put it away again without looking at anybody. He laid it on the table now, under the lamp, and took up his pen, and Cael understood that when the weight on the table had grown too great for him, Bracken had reached for the one thing on any paper that weighed exactly what it weighed and no more, which was its dates.

"Fourteen days," said Bracken. "We'll be exact about them."

He put the pen's point on the first square.

"The instrument is dated the first day of the year. The morning of convocation." He did not look up. "Somebody at the seat pressed that wax while the Chancellor was on the platform telling this house to be what its papers say it is. I don't suppose they knew. I don't suppose it matters." He moved the pen. "Served this morning. The eighth day. Seven days from the seat to my counter. That's an escort at a walk, with three horses and a man on the middle one who had been told the pace. The post does it in three." He made a small mark in the eighth square. "It wasn't sent to get here. It was sent to be watched getting here. I watched it for a full minute from my window, and so did the gate, and so did the coats at the bottom of the hill."

"And the fourteen?" said Karis.

"Run from service, not from the date. The code is plain about that, for once." He counted fourteen squares along the card with the pen's point, touching each, and made a mark in the last. "The twenty-second day of the year. Our answer lies on the seat's table in Ostrand by the close of business on the twenty-second, or it doesn't lie there at all." He laid the pen down beside the card. "I've watched houses lose on a calendar who could never have been beaten on the law. If our answer reaches that table on the twenty-third, the seat's clerk writes three words in his docket, *respondent made default*, and there isn't one line in all three volumes that would let the seat take them out again. Not for rain. Not for a lame horse. Not for a registrar who was ill." He looked round the table. "I'm saying it aloud, at a table, in front of three people, so that when it matters it will have been said. Counsel, put it in your minute."

"It's in," said the counsel.

"Then that's that." Bracken put the card away in his coat beside the brass glass. "Half a registrar's trade is saying the date out loud. The other half is being there when it comes."

The hall's clock struck somewhere in the dark at the far end of the long room. Cael had stopped counting bells before midnight, and he did not know which this one was. The lamps along the table had burned down to their last third and gone orange, and the four of them sat in a small close island of light in the middle of a long black hall, with the tall windows gone blind on every side.

Bracken got up and went down the table with his trimmer, turning each wick up a little as he passed it, and came back, and sat.

---

Karis had been writing on small slips of paper.

Cael had watched her do it for some time without knowing what they were for. She had torn a sheet of her notebook into six strips while Bracken counted squares, and on each strip she had written a single word in her finding hand, and now she laid them out on the table in a row, one after another, from her own end toward Bracken's, like stepping stones across a stream.

*Executed.* *Valid.* *Integrity.* *Re-evaluate.* *Nothing.* *Fourteen.*

"For the minute," she said, and put her finger on the first. "They concede the enrollment was made properly. No fraud, no flaw. Every door Greyvane shut, they leave shut." She moved her finger to the second. "They concede the clause is good law, and they quote the most honest man in their own service to say so." The third. "They take provisions built for testing frames and point them at a person." The fourth. "They ask for him to stand in front of an Arbiter." The fifth, and her finger stayed on it a moment. "The Arbiter has nothing for him. That isn't our argument. It's the oldest fact in the whole file. A frame at Denvash that had nothing to say, and nobody in any office since his Kindling has ever once disputed it." The sixth. "And a frame that says nothing sends him into chapter fourteen. Three steps. Written down. In order."

She lifted her finger off the last slip and looked along the row of them.

"Now what it is. I'll choose the word with care, because care is the only thing we have on this table tonight." She looked up at the table. "It is not a trick. I want that minuted. A trick needs a trickster. Somebody bribed, a frame tampered with, a finding written false. There's none of that in this paper and I'd stake my standing there's none of it behind the paper either. Every clause is law, and every step is a procedure that honest officers will carry out in good faith, with clean hands. And it won't matter how honest they are. The end was fixed the morning they filed it."

She looked back at the six slips.

"It's not a question. It's a corridor. Every door in it is real law, and every door opens one way."

Nobody spoke.

The counsel wrote it down, slowly, every word of it. Bracken's spectacles stayed in his hand. Above them, through the ceiling, a board creaked once, the sound of somebody who has been standing still for a long time shifting her weight at last.

From the foot of the stair, where none of them had heard her come down again, Withrow said, "The house will answer."

"The house will answer," said Karis. "Every line of it. Because a respondent who doesn't answer is entered as having fled, and this house is a named respondent, and I won't have the word *fled* written anywhere near this house while I'm in it." She swept the six slips together into one hand and tucked them into the back of her notebook. "Four days. I'll have its shape in four days. And when the seat reads it, the seat will find nothing in it to correct. Not a date. Not a citation. Not a comma." She drew a clean sheet toward her. "If I'm right about the corridor, I want the record to show that we walked every yard of it properly."

"Minuted," said the counsel.

Karis had written a heading and drawn a line under it before she spoke again, and when she did, it came out of her sideways, with her pen still moving. Cael had heard her do that before. It was how her findings came when they were not findings yet.

"There's one more thing in it. I can't place it."

They waited, and the pen stopped.

"Every paper anybody ever filed against him went at the clause, one way or another," she said, "and lost to it. This one doesn't even try. It leaves the clause in our hands, sound and whole, and asks for one thing only, which is a station. A frame. An Arbiter." She looked at the heading she had written. "The clause keeps him on a floor in front of people, where the Arbiter never comes. They don't want the clause. They want the floor. The provision doesn't interest them at all. They're trying to move him back inside the instrument."

Nobody answered her. She did not seem to want anybody to.

"I don't know why that distinction feels important. Noting it."

She turned to the very front of her notebook, to the first leaves, where Cael had seen her keep, for two years, a small handful of notes that belonged to no case. The fielding clause was there, and a line about the squad rule, and a few words from the founding articles of a charter she had promised not to open until the cold term. She wrote the new line under them, small, and closed the cover, and was at her heading again before the ink was dry, as if she had never stopped.

Cael wrote it down in the observation notebook, in her words. Then he drew a question mark in the margin beside it. He drew one beside everything Karis began and did not finish. She always finished, in the end, and the notebook was only where he kept the place.

Karis sent him away a little after that.

*Go up,* she said, without lifting her pen. *There's nothing left tonight that you can count.* So he went out into the court with his copy of the instrument under his arm, and the fountain was the only sound on the hill, and the residence front was dark except for one window.

On his way across he looked, as he always looked, at the assessment wing.

At the far end of its long front, on the ground floor, behind the counter's window, a single lamp was burning. At this hour, in a wing where nobody had any business before the third bell. Cael stood in the court for a moment and looked at it. He did not need to stand at the counter to know what was behind that window. A copy of the instrument that nobody had handed to anybody in the wing. A sheet of the wing's paper and a pen. And, by morning, an index of all thirty-seven paragraphs and all three schedules, every reference run down to its chapter and part, better than anything the four of them would make. It would turn up on Karis's table some time tomorrow without a covering note, and nobody would ask where it came from.

He went on, and up the residence stair, toward the one lit window.

It was the common room, and it was not empty.

Brom was at the table. He had a pot of tea on the hob of the banked hearth, and from the look of the hearth he had been keeping it hot for hours, nursing the embers with a careful hand whenever they went low. There were cups on the table. Not three. Four, set out in a row with their handles all turned the same way, and the fourth was in front of the empty chair at the table's end, where Karis always sat because the lamp fell best there.

Lira was in the window seat with her knees drawn up and the red-covered drill-book open against them. She was not reading it. She had not turned a page, Cael thought, in some time.

They looked at him when he came in. Neither of them asked anything. He understood, standing in the doorway with the copy under his arm, that they had been sitting up for hours, and that it was not for him.

Karis came up the stair a little after. He heard her step behind him and moved out of the doorway, and she came to the threshold and stopped there, with ink on both hands to the second knuckle and her notebook against her chest.

He watched her count the room. She did it the way she counted anything, quickly and all at once: a lamp lit at an hour no lamp should be, a fire kept in that should have been let die, two people awake who had no work to do, a drill-book open and unread, four cups set out with their handles turned. He watched her come to the fourth cup, in front of her own chair, and stop.

She did not say anything about it. She went to the table and took up the cup and held it while Brom filled it from the pot, and drank half of it standing, and set it down.

"Four days," she said to the room. "That's all I've come up to say. I'll need the volumes carried from the hall and back, and I'll need my stair kept clear of the second-years, and I'll need you," she looked at Cael, "to read every draft before anybody else does. Not to approve it. Because it's about you." She picked up the cup again and finished it. "That's the arrangement. It always was."

"It always was," said Cael.

She went to the stair. On its first step she stopped, with one hand on the rail, and did not turn round.

"Thank you for the tea," she said to the stairwell, and went up, and they heard her door.

Lira let out a long breath and put the drill-book face down on the window seat.

"Well," she said. "Tell us."

He sat down at the table, in Karis's chair, because it was nearest, and Brom filled a cup and pushed it across to him, and he told them.

He did not tell them all of it. All of it had taken nine hours. He told them the shape, in the order the night had built it, because the order had turned out to be part of what it meant. The four sentences at the front. The four brown rulings out of the press, and the ferryman's daughter whose initials he had written down. The fraud nobody alleged. The clause handed over in the sixth paragraph. The three sentences in the seventh, the honest man's, quoted whole and turned into a warrant. The frame at the Ostrand station that read every Stone a rank too high, and what the integrity provisions were for. Chapter fourteen in Bracken's flattest voice. What Withrow had said happened on the morning after, and the daughter at the counter every week for a year. The twenty-second day. Six slips of paper laid out like stepping stones.

When he came to the corridor, he said it in Karis's words, every one.

Brom had been looking at his cup. He went on looking at it for a while after Cael stopped, the way he looked at a weight before he decided where to take hold of it. Then he said one word, quietly, to the cup.

"Corridor."

Cael told them the rest: the answer they would make anyway, and the four days. And last, the thing Karis had said sideways, with her pen still moving, that went into the front of her notebook with a question mark beside it in his: that the paper did not want the clause. It wanted him in front of a frame.

When he had finished, nobody said anything for a while. The embers ticked on the hearth.

"So what's the argument for?" said Brom at last. "If the doors only go one way."

"For the record. For the house." Cael turned the cup in his hands. "So that our names are on the right side of it, on paper, even if the wrong side has the seal. Karis says the answer will be perfect, and I believe her. Three years I've watched her get things right, and the right thing has held every single time."

"Every time the people failed," said Lira.

She had not moved from the window seat. She said it carefully, looking at the dark glass, not at him, the way she would set one foot on a floor she did not trust before she put her weight on it.

"That's your sentence," she said. "You've been saying it since Greyvane. A warden wanted you gone, and the text held. A provost was frightened, and the text held. A tournament didn't want you on its roster, and its own text put you there." She was quiet a moment. "It's always been the people who failed and the paper that didn't. That's what you've been leaning on." She turned from the glass and looked at him. "What if the people didn't fail this time?"

He opened his mouth, and found the answer was already there, whole, and certain.

"Then there's a place where they read it wrong," he said. "There always is. Karis will find it."

She did not argue. She looked at him for a while, in her slow way, three passes, the way she read an opponent's sheet the night before a bout, as if something on it might move if she waited long enough. Then she swung her feet down off the window seat.

"Bed," she said. "Four days of drafting, and she'll want us fed and quiet and out from under her feet." She went to the stair and up it, and did not look back.

Brom gathered the cups. He set them on the tray, all four, with their handles turned the same way, and carried the tray to the hob, and banked the fire properly at last. Then he went to the stair, and stopped at its turn, with his hand on the newel post, not looking at Cael.

"Corridor," he said, once more, to nobody, and went on up.

---

Cael did not light his lamp. There was light enough to write by, from across the court, where the records hall's long east front was still lit from end to end.

He sat on the sill with Hesk's book on his knee and Hesk's steel pen in his hand, and laid his copy open on the sill at his hip, at its first page. Then he wrote the entry straight through without stopping, as he wrote only the things he had finished deciding before he picked the pen up.

*I can work within the system. I have the receipts: a hearing, a provision, a stamp renewed four times, a tournament their own clause put me in. The system has text, and text has held me every time the people failed. This is more text. We'll read it better than they do. We always have.*

He read it back once and did not change a word of it. Every clause had been earned on a floor or in a hearing room or across a counter, with people he loved watching, and every one of them was true. He sanded it, and gave it its full minute by Hesk's clock, an hour fast.

It was the steadiest thing he had ever written in that book. He would learn, before the year was out, what it was standing on. Tonight it stood, in his own sure hand, and looked like the truth, because it was the truth as far as he could see.

Across the court, the records hall's lamps began to go out.

They went in pairs. First the two at the south end of the long front, together, and then a pause, and then the next two. Somebody was walking back up the long table with a trimmer, putting them out two at a time in the order the fire-watch's book laid down, and in no hurry about it. Cael knew the pace. He watched the pairs go dark one after another, all down the east front, until a single window was left at the north end, over the head of the table, where three volumes would be lying shut and a strip of plain paper marking the place in the third.

Then that one went too.

The court was dark, and the fountain went on in its basin below. Beside him on the sill the copy lay where he had left it, its first page face up to the night, and at the foot of the page, in type no larger than the rest, *Given under the Compact's hand.* He did not close it. He left it there, open, waiting for better light.

## Rhythm calibration (measured, 2026-10-01)

Book 1 Movement 1 — the first movement drafted from this brief — overshot the formula in
the short direction: sentence mean 8.8 words (target 14.6), 41% of sentences at five
words or fewer (target ~28%), 0.3% at forty-plus (target ~3.3%), Flesch-Kincaid grade 2.8
(target 6.8), about 106 dialogue tags per 10k words (target ~41). Paragraph median (15)
and scene spacing were close. So, when drafting:

- Let thought, action and description run in full, well-built sentences. Compound and
  complex sentences with a clear hierarchy are the house texture, not the exception.
- Join a run of three or four clipped statements into one sentence when they are one thought.
- Keep short sentences for beats that land — a recognition, a hit, a turn — not as default.
- Give each chapter a few deliberately long, readable sentences (forty words or more)
  where an action or a thought earns the length.
- Drop "he said" / "she said" when the paragraph already makes the speaker clear.
- Keep short paragraphs (median ~18 words) and scene breaks about every 950 words.

## No scripted prose surgery (2026-10-01)

Rhythm and length repairs are done by reading, sentence by sentence, never by a script that
splits sentences or paragraphs at clause or sentence boundaries. Two repairs did that and
each needed a recheck to find the splits that broke a thought. Where the formula and a
character's speech disagree, keep the speech: one-to-three-word dialogue lines and the short
landing beats of a fight are not drift to be repaired.

## Source-reuse gate tightened to 8 words (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

The 10-word gate let distinctive source phrasing through in 6–9-word fragments (the first
movement's opening line, for example, echoed the current edition's). Measured at 8 words, movements
written after authors began self-checking run 1–6 shared runs per 10k; the worst ran ~30. From now
on `ed.sh overlap` and every editorial review use runs of **8 or more** words. Every listed run that
BOOK_MAP does not protect is rewritten — re-composed, not a synonym swapped. Names, Path names,
ranks and plain facts of canon will sometimes share a short run; re-compose the sentence around them.
Movements closed under the 10-word gate get one book-level 8-word sweep at book completion.

## Draft from your own event list, not from the source page (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

Book 3 Movement 6's review found the retold scenes in ch42–44 to be close paraphrase: 23–32% of
sentences followed a source sentence word for word in order, with words varied enough to pass the
8-word gate. Everything the author invented was clean. The cause is drafting with the source open.
So, for every movement:

1. Read the source chapters the packet names, once, for what happens and who these people are.
2. Close them. Write a private event list in your own words — what happens, in what order, who
   wants what, what changes. Keep it in your working notes, not in the manuscript.
3. Draft from that list and the book map. Do not reopen the source chapters while drafting.
   Choose your own entry point into each scene and your own order of beats; the source's scene
   shape is not canon, only its events are.
4. Only protected wording (BOOK_MAP) is copied, exactly, from the map — not from the source page.

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

Canon status markers used throughout all planning documents.

---

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

## Using Status Markers

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

> **LOCKED:** The Path system uses seven tiers.

> **SECRET (reveals Book 9):** The Architect built the system to suppress Fractured Paths, not organize existing potential.

> **OPEN:** Whether Warden Coss survives the series.

> **RUMOR (source: Compact Registry):** [SHATTERED] classifications indicate dangerous instability.

When writing chapter architecture, check the relevant universe bible and series bible entries. If a scene requires disclosing a SECRET before its reveal book, flag it explicitly and return to the planning layer before drafting.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

### Archmarshal Vastin

**PROVISIONAL (character arc)**

The Compact's senior enforcement officer. Age 51 at Book 4. Silver-tier, Iron Wall Path — exceptional defense and institutional authority. Appears in Book 5 as a legitimate authority figure, becomes the Compact's direct antagonist in Book 6, and by Book 9 has switched sides when he understands what the Architect's will is actually doing.

His arc: the man who enforced a corrupt system for legitimate reasons, and what he does when he understands the system is far more corrupt than he knew.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

**LOCKED (existence); SECRET (true nature)**

**SECRET:** Arbiters are not spiritual entities. Flow architecture.

# Chapter 35 — Three Routes

The new thing asked nothing when he woke.

He lay on his back with the grey of the window on the ceiling and waited for it, the way a man waits for a dog in the next room to bark once it has heard him stir. The ribs told him where they were. The forearm under its dressing itched in a long line from the wrist to the elbow, which the healer said was the skin remembering how to be skin. The latch sat in its keeper and the door stood in its frame, and nothing in him leaned toward either of them.

He named the door anyway, under his breath, before he put his feet on the boards. *Door. It opens on the corridor.* It cost nothing to say. Lira had said on the stair that she would name them with him when she could, and she was not here, so he did it himself, the way you lock a door you know is already locked.

Then he got up, slowly, and went to the sill, and stood a moment with his hand on the satchel's flap.

Today was the shape.

He did not open the book, because there was nothing to put in it yet. He dressed one sleeve at a time, the right arm first because the ribs preferred it, and went down to find Lira.

The infirmary smelled of vinegar and clean linen and the stove. The house's healer was a spare grey woman who had strapped fighters for twenty years and did not hold with conversation while she did it. She had Lira on the high bench by the window with the right foot across her own knee, and a pair of long-nosed shears working up the strapping from the toes, one careful bite at a time.

Lira did not look round when he came in, because she was looking at her foot as if it belonged to somebody she had not yet decided to trust.

The last of the strapping came away. Under it the ankle was a little thinner than the other, and yellow-green round the bone, and wrinkled from six days in linen like a hand that has been too long in the wash.

"Stand on it," said the healer.

Lira stood on it.

She did it without help, with both hands on the edge of the bench behind her, and then she took the hands away. Cael watched her weight go over onto the right foot, a little at a time, the way you put weight on ice at the edge of a pond. Her face did nothing at all. That was how he knew it hurt.

"Walk to the door and back."

She walked to the door and back as if the floor might have been waxed in the night and nobody had thought to tell her. At the door she turned on the left foot, not the right, and the healer saw it, and said nothing, and wrote something on a card.

"No floor, no frames, and no running, not even for a bell." The healer put the card in Lira's hand. "Your coach knows, and he'll tell you when."

"He says when he sees me walk across the east hall without thinking."

"Then don't think about it where he can see you," said the healer, which was very nearly a joke, and went to wash the shears.

The crutch stood in the corner where she had leaned it on the first morning, and she looked at it for a moment and then left it there.

"Somebody'll need it," she said to Cael at the door. "Somebody always does." She stopped on the threshold and put her hand flat on the frame, and said, quite seriously, "*Infirmary door. People come in hurt and go out less hurt.*"

He said it after her. Nothing in him had been asking, and it was quiet anyway.

They went along the covered walk side by side, slowly, at her pace, which today was also his. The frost was still white on the flags wherever the sun had not yet reached, and their breath went up in front of them and hung there while they walked through it.

"What's your line?" she said.

"The fourth."

"I'm the third." She did not look at him. "I'll be slow on the stair, and don't help me."

"I wasn't going to."

"Yes, you were." She stepped round a puddle with a white skin of ice on it, carefully, with the left foot leading. "You've been going to since the road, and I'd rather be slow."

---

The floor schedule was on the refectory door, where it always was, in Seln's fair copying hand.

It ran down the whole of the door: four floors and their hours for the week, the Crown yard, the second floor, the old frames and the east hall, and against each hour a name or a house or a program, a hundred lines of it in a hand so even that nobody had ever once looked at it twice. On the Fourth-day, after supper, five lines sat a quarter of an hour apart against five different floors. One of them was his. *Hesk-ward. Old frames.* Under the Fifth-day it still said, in the same hand, *Hesk-ward: excused (ribs).*

Ephram was reading the board when he came in. Ephram read the board every morning, all of it, from the top, the way other people read a letter from home.

"Ribs?" he said, without turning round.

"Better."

"Good." The captain found his own name and moved his finger to the next. "The chart's got a hole in it, and I don't like holes." He considered the Fourth-day column for a moment, Cael's line among the others, a practice booking on a floor nobody wanted after supper. Then he went on down the door to the Fifth-day, as if he had read nothing at all, and Cael could not have said whether he had.

He went to the wing after breakfast because the excused slip wanted a countersign, and because he wanted to see the shelf.

The assessment wing's counter was its usual morning self. The desk clerk had the day book open and a queue of three in front of him: two first-years with floor sheets and a porter with a parcel. The clock ticked over the counter. Behind it, at the copying table under the long window, the teaching assistant sat with his pen in his column and his head bent, as he had sat every morning for two years.

Cael waited his turn. While he waited he looked past the clerk's shoulder at the shelf above the copying table.

It was empty.

Not all of it. The wing's other sealed things still stood there in their row, a box of old sigil cards and two bound runs of the wing's returns and a tin of sealing wafers. But at the end of the row, where the case had stood for two years, there was only a clean oblong of wood, a shade paler than the wood round it, with a soft grey edge of dust on three sides.

He looked at it for exactly as long as a man looks at anything on a shelf while he waits in a queue. Then he looked at the clock.

The clerk countersigned the slip and Cael said thank you. At the copying table Seln turned a leaf, and said, to the column and to nobody, "Mm."

That was all. Cael went out through the wing doors into the cold and stood a moment on the step.

Somewhere between the last time he had looked and this morning, Seln had taken it down. Nobody on the hill had seen him do it. Cael had been looking at that shelf every time he stood at the counter for a week, and he had not seen it either.

---

Nobody had planned the routes. That was the point of them.

Seln had said it once, in Cael's room, with the case on the sill and the rain on the glass. *Read your own line. The hour is when you come up the stair.* And then, as the boy was writing on the back of his hand: *Come the way you always come. A man who changes his road is a man who has a reason.*

So they did.

Karis came first, at the seventh bell, from the refectory, by the library's front door and the main stair. She had come that way every evening of the year with her supper still in her and her notebook under her arm. Brom came second. Brom never took the main stair if there was another one. He went in at the library's north end and up the iron stair through the stacks, three flights of open treads that rang under a heavy man, because he said the long way round was the only exercise the library offered. Lira came third, by the front, slowly. And Seln, Cael knew without being told, would come last, up the bindery stair from the yard behind, the way the wing's man always came when he carried returned volumes up to the readers' shelves.

Three ways onto the library's third floor. Then one stair from there to the top, the tight one, where two people could not pass. Everybody on the hill used one of the three. Nobody had ever thought about which.

Cael sat at his sill with his coat on and Hesk's clock in front of him and waited for the third quarter after the bell.

The clock lost time, as it always had. In tournament weeks he had set it an hour fast so that it would come right by accident, and on finals morning he had put it back himself. Tonight he had set it by the lecture range's bell at supper, and it would be a minute wrong by the eighth bell, perhaps two. He watched the minute hand anyway, because it was something to watch.

When it stood at the quarter he got up, and named the door, and went.

The court was dark and very cold.

The fountain went on in the middle of it, as it did all year, with a crust of ice round the rim of the basin and the water going up black and coming down white in the light from the residence windows. He went round it at a walk, past the dark bulk of the lecture range and the records hall with its one lamp burning in the side window, which was Bracken's lamp and burned there every night of the year whether Bracken was under it or not.

At the top of the library, four floors up, the reading room's window was lit.

It was lit the way it was lit every night of the year, a yellow square at the top of the dark building, with the lamp set close to the glass so that you could see it from the north wall and the gate lodge and, Seln had once said, from the ferry landing at the foot of the bluff. The whole valley knew that window. A coat at the foot of the road could have told you the hour by it: lit at dusk, out somewhere between the tenth bell and the eleventh, never once dark before the ninth.

Tonight it was lit exactly the same.

He went in at the library's front door. *Door*, he told it, as his hand went to the iron ring. *Heavy. It keeps the cold out.* The lower hall was warm and smelled of lamp oil and old paper and the stove at the far end. Under the green-shaded lamps at the long tables two readers sat with their heads down. One was a fourth-year from the Ash faculty with a code open and his chin on his fist, and the other a girl Cael did not know, asleep with her cheek on her own notes. The night porter sat in his box by the stair with a newspaper. He looked up, saw a student going up to the reading rooms, and looked down.

Three people. Not one of them would remember it tomorrow. He had caught the counting from Seln somewhere in the year without meaning to, and now he could not stop.

The main stair went up in long shallow flights. On the second landing the ribs said what they thought of it. He stopped, and stood with one hand on the rail, and breathed, and went on. On the third floor the stair ended, and the tight one began, the one Karis called the top stair and the porters called worse things. He took it a step at a time. At the turn he passed the place where Lira would have had to stop too. There was nothing there to show it. She had been slow, and nobody had helped her.

The top landing had one lamp on a bracket, and the annex door stood shut across it. The reading room's door had Bracken's card in its brass frame at eye height. *Reading room. Assigned: K. Dellenmoor.*

He knocked once, and Karis let him in.

---

The table was bare.

He had seen it covered for a year, and turned face down for one evening, and he had never once seen it bare. Every stack and every box and every volume of the code had gone. The long table stood under its old green cloth with nothing on it at all, and the cloth was faded in squares and oblongs where a year of paper had lain on it and kept the light off.

"The annex floor," said Karis, before he could ask. "All of it, in order, and I'll be a week putting it back."

She had kept the lamp in the window where it always was, turned up as high as every night, so that anybody looking up from the valley saw what they always saw. They were not sitting by it; a second lamp stood at the far end of the table, away from the glass, with a shade of brown paper pinned round it so that its light went down onto the cloth and nowhere else. The window seat was empty, and nobody would sit there tonight. A person in the window seat was a shape against a lit window, and Karis had thought of that before any of them.

Brom had the chair by the shelf, with the sling still across his chest for one more night, and his spirit kettle stood on the end of the shelf over its little blue flame, not boiling. He nodded to Cael. He did not say anything about the stair or the stacks or the ribs.

Lira had the bench, at the end of it, with her right leg out straight in front of her and her boot off, and the foot resting on its heel on the boards, and she saw him look at it.

"It's fine," she said. "It's not fine, but it's better than it was. I came up the top stair on my backside for the last six steps, if you want to know, and nobody saw, because I waited till nobody was on it."

"I didn't help you."

"No, you didn't." She almost smiled. "Sit down. You're making the room smaller."

He sat on the bench beside her. Karis sat across the table from them with her hands in her lap and the cord of her key round her neck and the key itself inside her collar. Nobody had brought anything. That was the strangest thing of all, in that room. Not a notebook, not a pen, not a cup. Karis had sent the assistant home at the sixth bell, she said, with the copying for the morning and a message for her mother, and the girl had gone down the stair singing.

They waited.

Nobody talked much. Brom said the iron stair had a new loose tread on the second flight, the ninth, and he would tell the porter tomorrow. Lira said she had passed the Ash fourth-year on the main stair and he had asked after her ankle, and she had told him it was a horse, and he had believed her. Karis said nothing, but sat with her hands folded and looked at the bare cloth, the way she looked at a page before she began to read it.

The kettle did not boil.

The lecture range's bell gave the eighth hour, far off across the court, muffled by the shutters. Before the last stroke had died there was a single knock at the door, not loud.

Karis got up and turned the key.

Seln stood on the landing with his coat buttoned to the throat and something square under it, and he did not come in. He stood in the doorway and looked at the room in the order Cael knew. The door, the lock, the card; the two windows, the lit one with its lamp, the narrow one at the end, dark, with the library's roof below it; the landing behind him, the annex door, the stair; the bare table and the shaded lamp; then the four of them, one at a time.

It took him longer than it had on the night of the birthday. Nobody hurried him.

Then he came in and Karis locked the door behind him and hung the key back inside her collar. Seln went to the middle of the table, on the far side, where he always sat. He did not sit. He unbuttoned his coat and took out the case and set it down on the bare cloth in the circle of the shaded lamp.

It was smaller than Cael remembered from the sill. Dark leather, scuffed at the corners, a brass lock and nothing else. No mark on the outside. There was a stamp inside the lid from the man who made it, Seln had said once, that nobody had ever seen but him.

He took the key from his waistcoat pocket, a small brass key on a loop of black tape. He put it in the lock and turned it, and the lock opened with a sound like a dry twig, and he lifted the lid.

He did it in four movements, the way a man sets out his tools on a bench before a job.

The first was the largest. He took out a deep stack of paper, held flat between his two hands, and began to lay it on the cloth in rows. Each piece was two sheets together with a brass clip at the top, and he laid them ten to a row, edge to edge, without hurrying, squaring each to the one before with the side of his finger. Four rows. Forty. When he had finished they covered half the table in front of the lamp like the squares of a field seen from a hill.

The second was a bundle tied in the wing's pink tape, about two fingers thick. He set it down at the end of the fourth row and did not untie it.

The third was printed. Cael saw that at once from the way the light lay on it, flat and even, the way it never lay on a page anybody had written by hand. Seln laid it in two stacks side by side beyond the bundle. The left stack was yellower than the right.

The fourth was a single leaf.

It was in a sleeve of folded paper, plain and heavy, made to fit it. Seln took it out of the lid's pocket last, with two fingers at its edges. He laid it at the very end of the table by itself, a hand's width from everything else, and took his fingers away.

Then he stepped back from the table and put both hands flat on the wood in front of him, and said nothing.

Nobody said anything.

Cael had no clock, because he had left Hesk's on the sill, and he did not count. He found afterward that he could not have told anybody how long it was, which had not happened to him since he was small. He knew only what he saw while it went on. The shaded lamp's flame stood up very straight and still in its glass. On the cloth the forty rows lay in its light, and the yellow stack, and the leaf in its sleeve at the end. Lira's hands were in her lap, one inside the other, and Brom did not reach for the kettle. Karis sat with her eyes going along the first row and back, along and back, the way they went along a shelf, and she did not reach for anything either.

Seln's hands lay flat on the wood, and Cael watched them, and they did not move at all.

At last Karis looked up.

"May I?"

"That is why they are on the table," said Seln.

---

She took the first one from the top of the first row. She drew the shaded lamp an inch nearer and held the two sheets under it, and Cael saw that they were both in the same hand, small and round and perfectly level, the copying hand of the wing that had filled the floor schedule on the refectory door every week for two years.

"The first sheet is a copy of a station's Kindling record," said Karis. She read it in her plain voice, the one she used for a citation, giving each word its weight and no more. "*River station, the fourth district. Entry of the twelfth. A girl of the town. Reading at the frame: Current, second rank. Keeper's note at the margin: reads into Mire. Strong.*" She laid it face up in front of her. "That's the keeper's hand copied. You can see where he pressed."

"The second?" said Brom.

She held the clipped sheet out to him across the table. He took it in his good right hand and held it to the lamp, and read it out in his slow plain way, as if it were a bill of lading.

"*Order of reclassification at evaluation. River station, the fourth district. Entry of the twelfth.*" He stopped, and found his place. "*Per corrective classification at Kindling evaluation: enter Current, Rank One. Margin reading not to be entered. Observe nine weeks following entry.*"

He turned it over. There was a slip pinned to the back, a narrow strip in the same round hand, and he read that too.

"*Observed nine weeks. Nothing to report.*"

Nobody said anything.

Karis took the second pair, and read the record, and Brom read the order. A station in the hill country, a boy. *Ash, first rank. Keeper's note: something of Stone in the frame; second family?* And the order: *Enter: Ash, Rank One. Margin reading not to be entered. Observe nine weeks.* And on the back, *Observed nine weeks. Nothing to report.*

The third was a coastal station, a girl again. *Current, second rank at the frame. Keeper's note at the margin: reads into Wind, faint.* Enter: Current, Rank One.

Cael listened, and watched the two of them pass the sheets across the cloth, and did not say what he saw.

Every keeper had written something at the margin: a second family, or a note that the frame had reached past the column it was meant to stay in. *Reads into. Something of. Faint.* And every order took the margin away and wrote down only the column. Twice now a rank had gone too, but the rank was not the thing; the margin was the thing. He could see it as plainly as he could see the clip, and he did not say it. It was Karis's to say, when she had it in order, and she would not thank anybody for saying it first.

Lira had been very quiet all this while. She leaned forward on the bench, carefully, with the right foot still out straight, and turned the third order over with one finger so that the back of it showed.

"Nine weeks of nothing," she said.

"That is my sentence," said Seln.

They all looked at him. He was still standing, with his hands flat on the table. He said it in the voice he used at the counter.

"*Nothing to report.* I have written it every week for fifteen years. On the bottom of every return, in that hand." He did not look at the slip. "It is a true sentence. When there is nothing, that is what you write. It is the commonest line in the service."

Cael looked at the narrow strip of paper on the back of the order.

He had been kept by that sentence. He knew it as surely as he knew the ribs. Two years of true, empty weeks had gone up out of the wing with his name on the outside fold, and every one of them had said that nothing had happened. That sentence had stood between him and the people who read returns. And here was the same sentence, word for word, clipped to the back of a girl from a river town. In her case it said that the correction had held. Nothing to report. She had gone home smaller and stayed there, and nine weeks of somebody's careful watching had found nothing in her worth writing down, because the thing worth writing down had been taken off her paper before she was old enough to know it was there.

The same words. One set had kept him, and the other had buried her.

He did not say that either. He put his hand on the edge of the table, and Lira, without looking at him, put her own hand over it for a moment and took it away.

They read eleven more pairs before anybody stopped.

Nobody had decided how many to read. Karis read records and Brom read orders, and after a while Lira took orders too, so that Brom could rest his hand. The voices went on across the bare cloth in the circle of the lamp, very low. *Reads into. Something of. Second family, faint. Enter. Margin reading not to be entered.* Station after station went by, a mill town and a ferry crossing and a district with a name Cael had once seen on a map, a girl and a boy and a girl, and on every back the narrow slip, the nine weeks, the nothing.

At the twenty-third pair Karis stopped.

There was something folded behind it, under the clip. A sheet folded small, in quarters, and then another sheet folded inside that.

"There's a letter," she said.

Lira put out her hand.

Karis looked at her a moment, and gave it to her. Lira unfolded the outer sheet on her knee and smoothed it flat with the side of her hand. It was Seln's copying hand again, but the copying hand had done something Cael had never seen it do: it had kept the shape of another man's writing inside its own. The lines ran uphill and the capitals were too large. The spelling was a careful man's spelling, a man who wrote seldom and had looked up the hard words.

Lira read it aloud.

"*To the clerk of the district office. Sir. I am the father of the girl entered at the river station on the ninth. I do not write to complain and I hope you will not take it so. At the frame the keeper told us she read strong and read into a second family besides, and he said he had not seen it often, and shook my hand. Now the paper has come and it says only the one, and the first rank. I expect there has been an error in the copying, which I know is easy done. I do not mind which it is. She is a good girl either way. Only she asks me, and I do not know which to tell her. If you would write and say which, I would be grateful. Your servant.*"

There was no name. Seln had copied everything but the name.

Lira laid the sheet on the table. Her hand stayed on it a moment.

Then she unfolded the second sheet, the one that had been inside the first. It was short. She looked at it, and did not read it, and held it out across the table to Cael.

He took it. Four lines, in Seln's hand again, and under them a district office's mark. He read them aloud because she wanted him to, and because he did not think he could have read them silently and kept his face.

"*Sir. The entry is the registry's and I am not able to alter it.*

"*I have looked at the station's sheet. The keeper's reading is on it, and it is not what the registry entered.*

"*I have asked my office why, and I have had no answer, and I do not expect one.*

"*I am sorry. I would tell her both.*"

He put the sheet down on top of the father's.

The lamp burned. Across the court, very faint through the shutters and four floors down, somebody in the residence was laughing at something, the long helpless laugh of a first-year who has been told a joke after the bell and cannot stop.

"He told the truth," said Brom at last.

"He did," said Karis. Her voice was quite steady. "He looked at the station's sheet himself and found the difference. He asked above him, and wrote down that he'd been given nothing. He told a man he'd never met the one honest thing he had." She touched the edge of the clerk's four lines with one finger, very lightly, the way she touched the founders' volumes. "And he sent it up with the letter, because that's what you do with a letter about an entry. You send it to the office that made the entry."

She looked at the case, open at the end of the table.

"And it came here."

Cael sat and looked at the four lines.

He had told himself all year, and the year before, that the room was the trouble and not the hill. He had found out on the Ostrand road that the room had never been the system at all. He had thought that was the bottom of it, and it was not. Here was a man who had sat in one of the rooms and done everything right. He had read the sheet and asked, and written down the answer he had not been given. He had told a father the truth in four lines, which was more than most people managed in forty. And all of it, his honesty and the father's careful spelling both, had gone up a stair into the drawer that held the corrections. It had been filed there beside them, clipped, in order, and kept.

The honest people were in the drawer too.

Seln took his hands off the table at last. He looked at the two sheets lying on top of each other, the father's and the clerk's, and then he looked at Lira.

"That is the only letter," he said. "In forty. I copied it because it was the only one. I have read it more often than anything else in the case."

Lira nodded. She folded the father's letter in quarters along its old folds and put the clerk's inside it, the way it had been, and slid them both back under the clip of the twenty-third pair, and squared the pair with the side of her finger to the one beside it.

# Chapter 8 — Keel

He slept past the first bell for the first time since the road, and woke with the copy still lying on the sill where it had spent the night.

The light had come for it in the end. It lay across the first leaf in a long pale bar, and the fountain was running in the court below, and somewhere along the residence front a door banged and a voice called for somebody's other boot. The ordinary morning of an ordinary house. He lay a while and listened to it, and counted what he could hear, and found that the counting did not help.

Fourteen days. Thirteen, now.

He got up and dressed and put the copy in its folder under his arm, because he did not want to leave it on the sill for the porter's boy to read, and went down.

Karis was not at breakfast. Her chair at the end of the table stood pushed in square, and her cup was gone from the rack, which meant she had been down before the bell and taken it up with her. Lira sat across from his place with her elbows either side of a bowl of porridge she was not eating. Brom ate for two, steadily, as if somebody had told him he would need it.

"She's been up there since the third bell," said Lira. "Brom heard her on the stair."

"I heard the key," said Brom.

"Did she sleep?"

"She says she slept." Lira pushed the bowl an inch away. "She also says the floor in her room has a slope to the west, and she's going to have it measured. So no."

He ate, because Brom pushed bread at him until he did, and then he went up.

Two porters were carrying a table across the top landing of the library when he reached it.

They had the door of the old lumber room propped open with a broken chair, and they were working the table through it on its side, sweating and grumbling at it in the low courteous voices porters used on the library stair. Behind them the room itself stood half empty. It had held, for as long as Cael had known it, the house's spare benches and a map case with no maps in it and two rolled carpets nobody would admit to owning. Now the carpets were out on the landing and the benches gone, and somebody had already swept the floor in long straight strokes and left the broom against the wall.

"Registrar's orders," said the older porter, when Cael held the door for them. "Table, two chairs, a shelf for big books. He says big books." He looked at the shelf, which was very big. "He says a girl's to sit in here and nobody's to go past her who hasn't been asked for."

"Which girl?"

"Second-year. Ink on her." The porter set his end of the table down. "She's already been and moved the chairs twice."

By supper the house would have a name for the two rooms together, the reading room and the cleared one facing it across the landing. Ephram would use it first, to a knot of curious first-years at the foot of the library stair, and it would stick: the annex.

Across the landing the reading-room door stood open for the first time Cael could remember. Karis was inside at the long table with her back to the window and her sleeves pushed above her elbows. She had the code's three volumes laid out on her left, open, one above another like steps. On her right lay something he had not seen before.

It was a sheaf of paper, bound along its edge with tape, thirty-odd leaves. It had no cover and no title and no note. He came round the table to see it the right way up. Every leaf was ruled into two columns. In the left column, one paragraph of the instrument at a time, in order, stood the paragraph's number and its first words. In the right, under each, ran every section of the code the paragraph leaned on, by volume and chapter and section, and under each section a line in very small print saying where else in the registry's history that section had been used, and by whom, and how it had come out.

The hand was a copying hand. It was so even that it might have been printed, and it made no letter larger than any other.

"It was under the door when I came up," said Karis without looking round. "At the third bell. Pushed through flat, so it wouldn't crease." She turned a page of the code. "The porter's boy was sweeping this stair at the second bell. He says the wing's teaching assistant came up it with something under his arm, and went down it again without, and wished him good morning on the way."

Cael looked down at the sheaf. Thirty-odd leaves. The paragraphs of a paper served at the second bell yesterday, mapped against a code of three volumes, with the history of every section underneath. It would have taken a clerk with a good lamp a week.

"Thank him," said Cael.

"He'd hate that." She turned another page. "I'll use it instead. He'll know I've used it, because he'll see the shape of it in what goes down the hill, and that's the kind of thanks he'd let me give." She looked up at him at last. Her eyes were red at the rims and perfectly clear in the middle. "Sit down. You're going to be told the rules once, and then you're going to read."

The rules took less time than breakfast.

Four days to the answer's bones, said Karis. After that, the flesh, and then Bracken and the house's counsel and the weighing, and then it went down the hill with time in hand. Every leaf of the code that left the records hall came back to it at night, because Bracken would not sleep with the press unlocked and she would not ask him to. Brom would carry. Brom had already been told and had already said yes, in one word, at the foot of the stair at the second bell.

"Twice a day," she said. "Up in the morning, down at night. The volumes weigh more than I do. He says that's no trouble. He says it in the voice he uses when something is going to be no trouble for a month."

"And Lira?"

"Lira is in charge of the door." Karis wrote a section number on a slip and tucked it into the second volume. "Not this door. That one has the girl. The records hall's. The intake found out this morning where we sat last night. There were nine of them on the records-hall steps at the second bell, looking at the windows as if the code were going to come out and wave."

He did not ask how Lira was going to keep a whole intake off a set of steps. He found out at noon.

She had gone to Ephram. Ephram, as captain, still held the meal rota the two second-years had drafted on the first night, the one with a column of names and a table chosen for watching how Cael held a spoon. Lira asked him for it. She took it to the common room, crossed out the heading, and wrote a new one above it in her square capitals. *RECORDS HALL STEPS. ONE PAIR AT A TIME. NOBODY SITS. NOBODY ASKS. ANYBODY ELSE IS SENT TO THE FRAMES.* Then she gave it back to Ephram to sign, and he signed it, and pinned it up on the covered walk beside the house's one-line answer from the day before.

By the first bell after noon there was a pair of second-years on the records-hall steps, standing very straight. One was the Mire boy who looked at ceilings. The other was the girl from the short table with her small notebook, and she was writing in it.

"She's on both rotas," said Lira, at the rail of the covered walk, very satisfied. "Karis's and mine. She won't sleep for a week."

"She's watching who comes."

"Of course she is. I told her to." Lira looked at the steps. "Somebody might as well."

---

He read the keel that afternoon, because Karis put it in front of him first.

It was the Greyvane ruling. She had laid it down before anything else, before the paper she was answering had a single paragraph answered, on the bare table in the middle of three cleared feet of wood. The ruling, the certified transcript under it, and Prynn's own copy of the provision on top of both, in that small exact archivist's hand Cael had first seen on a high desk in a room that smelled of cold paper.

"Keel," said Karis.

He had watched them build barges on the slip below Ostrand bridge. He had stood on the bank on the house's free afternoons for whole hours, because the work had an order in it and he liked orders. The shipwrights did not start with the sides you could see. They laid one long timber down the middle of the slip first, end to end, and squared it, and pinned it, and nothing else went on until it was true. Then the ribs went up from it on both sides, and everything that came after hung from the ribs. A barge could lose a plank and float. If the keel was wrong, nothing you nailed to it afterward would keep the river out.

"Everything else hangs from this," said Karis. "Every argument in the answer comes back to one fact: a seat has already heard this question and settled it on the public record, and the paper we're answering doesn't say otherwise. It doesn't even try. So the first thing on the table is the place where the question was settled." She put her palm on the transcript and left it there. "And then I don't move it."

She uncapped her pen and drew a sheet toward her, to write the heading.

Then she stopped, and Cael watched her stop.

It was a small thing. Her pen came down an inch over the paper and stayed there. She looked across at the instrument lying open on the table's far side, at its first leaf, and he knew what she was looking at, because he had looked at it himself all night. *To the Chancellor, Halcenvane Academy, and to the enrollee of record, Caelen Hesk-ward, unclassified.*

"You answer a paper in its own words," said Karis. "Always. If they call a thing a carriage, you don't call it a cart. You make them argue with the word they chose, not with yours." She did not look at him. "They've given you your registry name. So the answer goes back to the same address. Halcenvane Academy, respondent, with the enrollee of record, Caelen Hesk-ward, unclassified, answers jointly. Do you mind?"

Nobody had asked him that about the name before. Not at Denvash, where Pellin had stamped it on his card; not at Greyvane, where it had been read out over his head; not on any form since. Once, at Ardenmere, he had asked Vell to put it in her book the right way round, which was the nearest he had come. The registry gave him the name the way it gave a ledger a number. People said Cael.

"No," he said. "It's my name in that building. Write it the way they spell it."

She wrote it. He watched it go down in her finding hand, every letter, his own name on a heading he had not written, and found it did not feel like being named. It felt like being entered.

She ruled a line under it, and the keel was laid.

---

On the second day she put up the ribs.

There were three of them, and she explained them once, to the counsel, who had come up the hill again before noon with her steel rule in her coat pocket and sat at the table's end with her hands folded on it, listening.

The first was Karis's own finding from Greyvane, the long one, the one she had built from the standardization directive's repeal schedules a line at a time until she could say, flatly, in front of a seat, that nobody had ever repealed the clause. It had been left behind, like a child at a fair, when everything round it was swept up and carried off. The second was the house counsel's reconstruction, made the year after, by another road, through the registry's own index of surviving provisions, without reading a word of Karis's first. The third was Prynn's copy and the note on its cover, from the archive at Greyvane, which showed the clause standing in the founding text with nothing written against it in two hundred years of hands.

"Three roads to one town," said the counsel.

"Three roads to one town." Karis laid the three bundles side by side and pinned them together along their edges with brass fasteners, through the margin, three thicknesses of paper made into one. "Any one would do. I'm not going to let the seat find out which. If they knock one down they'll find the other two standing in exactly the same place."

"And if they don't knock any of them down?"

"Then they've conceded the clause twice." Karis capped her pen. "Once in their paragraph six. Once by silence in front of these."

The counsel looked at the fastened bundle for a long moment, the way Cael had seen Brom look at a lift before he took it.

"That's not a rib," she said. "That's a wall."

At noon Brom came up the library stair with the second volume of the code on his shoulder, the way a miller's man carries a sack, and set it down on the shelf in the lumber room without a sound. The girl at the outer table stood up when he came in. She sat down again when he went out. Then she got up and moved the volume an inch along the shelf so that its spine stood level with the others, and sat down once more and went on writing.

Cael read everything that day. That was the arrangement: Karis wrote, and he read. Not for approval, she said; because it was about him, and she did not put her name to documents about people who had not read them. He read at the window with the light behind him and the pages on his knee, and when he had read a leaf he laid it face down on the sill, and Karis took it back without asking what he thought, because if he had thought anything he would have said.

Near the end of the afternoon she did something he did not understand.

She was deep in the provenance, with the directive's schedules open in front of her and her finger on a line, and the finger stayed where it was. She reached behind her with her free hand to the pile of finished leaves and drew one out from near the bottom and held it over her shoulder toward him without turning round.

"Tonight," she said. "First."

He took it. It was the renewals table.

He looked at it, and then at her back, and waited for a reason. She turned a page of the directive and gave him none.

---

The renewals table was a single leaf, ruled in Karis's hand into a grid, and it had three rows.

He read it at his own window that night, with the fountain going in the court and the copy of the instrument shut in its folder on the bed behind him. Across the top of the grid ran the columns: the sitting, the panel, the day, the finding, the seal. Down the side ran the three times his place on the bluff had been written down by somebody obliged to write it. The grant at Greyvane, from the hearing. The first renewal, at Halcenvane, in the inspection year, before the Compact's delegation. The renewal at the mill town, last year, on the road.

Three rows. Every cell filled. Each day was entered in the form the registry used, and each finding was set down in the panel's own words.

He read it twice and found nothing. He had not expected to find anything; Karis did not make tables with things in them to find. He laid it on the sill and began to undress, and then stopped with one boot off and looked at it again.

Why first? Why tonight?

He sat down on the bed with the boot in his hand and thought about the inspection year.

He remembered the room by its east wall, because that was where the strangers had been. Four chairs set out by Bracken at a guest's distance from one another. The delegation's counsel in the nearest, untying a folder's ribbon with her teeth. Ilsev beside her, sharpening a pencil into a fold of paper on her knee. Havel with a board across his knees, writing the date. And in the fourth chair the Archmarshal, with nothing in his hands at all. Three hours of instruments on the oak under those four chairs: the frames, the board, the pendulum ticking in its case. Then Gault reading his note aloud at the trestle, and the Ash instructor signing, and the Mire instructor adding her four lines about the faulty frame, and the clerk bringing the stamp down on the house's copy. *Renewed.* That had been the day, and the house's copy had been stamped on it.

But the house's copy was not the only one. Bracken had told him so at the counter two days afterward, when the line went into the enrollment book. The delegation's counsel had asked, in writing, that the registry's own copy of the note go up the road under the delegation's countersignature before the registry sealed it into his file, and Bracken had objected on both sides of a sheet, and lost. So that copy had waited. And when it came back down, sealed and dated, so that the enrollee of record could initial its receipt before it went up again for good, Gault had called him into his office to do it.

He put the boot down and went to the drawer and took out the old volume of the Log.

It was full; there was no room in it for one word more. But every word that was in it was still there, and he knew roughly where in its thick back half the inspection year began. He turned the pages under the lamp, past fights and figures and the names of rooms, to the long entry for the evaluation day, the one that ended on *wondering*. Then he turned forward a page at a time, counting the days between entries by the bells and the weekday names, until he found two short lines he had nearly forgotten writing.

*Gault's office. Initialled the registry's copy of the renewal, back from the delegation under seal, dated today. Up the road again in the noon bag.*

Nine days.

He went back to the table on the sill. Its first renewal's row gave one day, the day of the trestle, which was the day the house's own enrollment book carried in Bracken's small upright hand, because that was the day the house had seen it happen. The registry's file, wherever it sat, would carry the other day, the day its own copy was sealed. The house had never kept a copy of that one. It had gone back up the road the same afternoon. The only place on the continent where both days stood side by side was a book he had filled himself and shut in a drawer.

He sat a long time with the two volumes open on his knees.

Then he looked at the table again, very closely, under the lamp. In the margin beside the first renewal's row, so faint that he had to tip the leaf to the light to be sure of it, sat a single pencil dot.

He took it up at the third bell, with the old volume of the Log under his arm.

Karis was already at the long table. She did not look round when he came in. She held out her hand behind her, palm up, the way she had held out the leaf, and he put the renewals table into it.

"Nine days," he said.

"Show me."

He opened the old Log on the table beside her elbow at the evaluation day, and laid his finger beside its last word without touching it, and then turned forward to the two short lines and did the same. She read both, the way she read anything with a date in it, once for the words and once for the numbers.

"Under the delegation's countersignature," she said. "Their counsel asked for that?"

"In writing. Bracken objected in writing. She won."

Karis said nothing to that. She laid the renewals table down square, took the whole leaf out of the answer, and drew a clean sheet toward her.

He watched her rule the grid again from nothing, column by column, and thought that a footnote would have cost her a minute. A small number in the cell, a line at the foot, and the nine days were explained. She was going to spend an hour instead. She had told the counsel the day before that she had no hours to spare that week.

"You're thinking a footnote," said Karis, without looking up.

"I was."

She went on ruling. Every row this time gave two days, the trestle and the seal, and the first renewal's row gave the reason for the gap in a single line. Under the grid she wrote where each day could be found: the house's enrollment book for the trestle; the registry's own file, to be produced on request, for the seals; and for the gap, *the enrollee's contemporaneous record, available to the seat*. Then she reached across the table for the instrument, opened it at schedule two, and laid it beside the old table she had just taken out.

"Read them together," she said. "The way he will."

He read them together. Schedule two listed his file's standing entries by the registry's own dates, and the registry dated a renewal by its seal. Her old table dated it by the trestle. Two papers on one desk, about one afternoon, nine days apart.

"Now you're the seat," said Karis. "One of these two was drawn up by people who are careful. Which?"

He did not answer. He did not have to.

"There is no such thing as a small error in a document about a person." She blotted the new leaf and laid it in its place in the pile. "Nobody would have called it a lie. They'd only have called us careless, once, in one line. And after that, every leaf of ours the seat turned over, it would be turning over looking for the next one."

He looked at the old leaf, lying out of the answer on its own. The pencil dot sat in its margin.

"You saw it the first morning."

"I saw that two papers disagreed. I couldn't see why." She did not pretend otherwise. "I could have spent a morning in the inspection's papers. Or I could hand the table to the one of us who was in Gault's office that afternoon, and not say what I was looking for." She did look at him then. "If I'd told you there was a mistake on it, you'd have found one. You'd have found one on a clean leaf. I wanted to see what you'd find if you were only reading. Gault is a signature now. The Archmarshal is a chair. You're the one witness who can't be replaced, and witnesses see more when they don't know what they're being asked."

She went back to the schedules.

He stood by the table with the old Log under his arm. She had not been testing him. She had used him, the way she used the code's third volume and Prynn's copy and the sheaf with no note on it: as the best source she had on one question. It was the most useful he had ever been to her, and he found he did not mind the method in the least.

And on the way down the stair he remembered the counter, and the four sentences Seln had spent on him in a queue on the third day of the year. *Standing papers on the provision come up for the ledger's half-year check. Keep the documentation current.* The renewals table was the provision's standing papers, set out in a grid. Whatever went down the hill this week would sit in the registry's file beside the registry's own dates when the half-year check came, and a table that disagreed with them by nine days would have been the first thing the check found. Karis had not heard Seln say it. She had kept the documentation current anyway, because that was what she did with documents.

---

On the fourth day, which was a Fifth-day, he went to wing three at the second bell, because the provision's hours did not stop for the registry.

It was a short bout and he fought it at the band. The Mire across from him was an Iron Rank Four who sank the boards in front of her a hand's depth at a time, so that wherever Cael meant to plant his foot the floor had turned to something nearer porridge. He read the boards before she softened them, and stepped where she had not yet been, and burst twice, and gave her the third exchange because the rotation's line for that day said the third. Two to one. The rib from the Shield had gone yellow and then gone, as he had told himself it would. Gault added the three cards on the back of his hand and gave the clerk twenty-four and two-thirds, and Ephram at the rail wrote it in his soft-folded chart and drew no question mark beside it.

Walking back across the court, he noticed what it had cost.

Not the burst, nor the exchange given away. Those were the band's ordinary prices, and he paid them every Fifth-day without looking at the bill. It was the hour. Up the library stair, Karis was spending a week of her life laying his name on a table one true day at a time, and he had spent this morning in front of three cards making sure the house's book held a slightly smaller version of him than the one she was writing down. The band had always been a daily expense. He had never before had to pay it in the same week as somebody else paid for the truth.

He had no column for that. He went in to supper without making one.

Karis came down to supper that night for the first time in four days.

She came in late, when the refectory was half empty and the porters had begun stacking the benches at the far end. She had washed her hands and the ink had not come off. She sat down at the end of the middle table in her own chair, and Brom, without being asked, pushed the dish of beans down the board to her, and Seln, at the middle of the bench, moved his cup out of its way by exactly the width of the dish.

She ate three mouthfuls. Then she put down her spoon.

"Bones," she said.

Nobody cheered. Lira put her elbows on the table and her chin on her fists and looked at her.

"And the rest?"

"Seven days." Karis picked up the spoon again. "Bracken inside them. And the scales."

"The scales?"

"Bracken weighs things," said Karis. "He's going to want the number." She ate another mouthful, slowly, as if she had just remembered that food was a thing a person did. "So am I."

Across the table, Seln cut his bread into exact squares, and buttered one, and did not look up. But when Karis said *bones*, his knife had stopped for a moment above the board, and Cael, who had been watching for it, had counted the moment, and it was a long one.

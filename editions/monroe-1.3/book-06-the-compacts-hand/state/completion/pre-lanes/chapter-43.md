# Chapter 43 — The Glide

He stood in the door, as he had promised, with his hands in his coat and nothing in them.

The east hall had its two lamps lit and its shutters down. Outside it was not yet light, and a wet wind was leaning on the north wall, so that the long room seemed smaller than it was, a lit box with six people in it and the dark pressing on the glass. The program's clerk had moved her trestle out from the wall to the edge of the painted floor and sat at it with the red book open and two pens laid ready. Rooke sat on her right with his board. On her left the Stone stood with a flag in each hand, a red and a blue, held down along his thighs like a man carrying two fish.

The Force leaned on the far rail with his arms folded; he had nothing to do this morning but watch, and he had dressed for it, in a clean shirt.

Lira was at her chalk at the south end with her back to the door. She had taped her own wrists but not the ankle, because the healer had said there was nothing left in it to tape, and she stood now with her weight even on both feet and her left arm tucked a little toward her ribs, from the old habit, and did not look round.

The Current girl came to the north chalk on her toes.

She was a third-year, Iron Rank Four, long in the leg and narrow everywhere, and Cael had stood in front of her himself once, in wing three, under four strangers' pencils. A Current's Path took the catch out of the floor under her. She did not run across boards; she went over them, level, the way water goes over a weir, and when she turned she did not turn so much as bend, leaning into a long smooth curve with her arms out for balance and her heels never once coming down. She had drilled on the cross beside Lira's since the first morning of the program. She knew the four-count as well as Lira knew it. She shook her hands loose at her sides, and looked at Lira down the length of the floor, and grinned, a little nervously, and then stopped grinning because Rooke had looked at her.

"Program trial," said Rooke. "My rule. You both know it, and I'll say it anyway, because it goes in the book." He did not get up. "Four exchanges. A flag to whoever takes each one, or to nobody. Most flags has it. Level after four, the table rates you both. Three bursts for the Wind; past three the card pays. The Current, what her Path allows." He nodded at the clerk. "Rated. In this book. Go when the Stone drops his hands."

The Stone raised both flags to his shoulders, held them there for the space of a breath, and dropped them.

Lira ran the count.

It was the thing she did best in the world, and for three beats of it Cael forgot that she had ever been away. She lied with the front foot, a weight laid on it and left a moment too long. She lied with the back. She lied with her shoulders, a half-turn toward the east wall that pulled the Current girl's whole long glide a yard that way, and the Current girl, who knew perfectly well that it was a lie and had been lied to by it two hundred times, went all the same, because her Path did not stop for knowing. She leaned into her bend.

On the fourth, Lira went.

Burst one, low and flat off the left foot, not across the glide but along the inside of its curve, the line she had found on this floor before the first sitting and beaten this girl's pattern with on more mornings than either of them could count. It was right, and Cael saw that it was right from the door. At the far end of that line there was a place on the boards where the Current girl's bend would bring her hip, and Lira would be standing in that place a quarter of a beat before the hip arrived, with her palm out.

The line ended on her right foot.

He saw her ask. It was in the air, and it took no time at all. Her right foot came down toward the boards and something in the set of her knee went soft and careful, and instead of landing she touched and stepped, one quick small extra step, and came down on the left.

The landing beat was late. It was not late by much, only by the length of a step, but the whole of Lira's fighting was built on that beat, the instant after a burst when the floor took her weight and gave it back and she could go again. She had it on the left foot, half a pace past where she had meant to be, facing a little the wrong way. The place where the hip was going to be was behind her.

The Current girl came round the rest of her bend and found nobody in her way. She looked surprised, and put out her hand as she went by, almost apologetically, and touched Lira on the back of the shoulder.

The Stone lifted the red flag.

"One," said the clerk, and wrote.

Lira walked back to her chalk, and did not look at Rooke or at the door. Cael watched the back of her neck and could not tell whether she knew what had happened or only that something had.

---

She did not run the count the second time.

The flags dropped, and she stood for one beat with her weight on both feet, and on the second she simply went. There was no lie in it at all. Burst two, flat and hard, straight across the Current girl's glide at the hip, the plain rude line that every drill in the red book told a Wind never to take against a Current, because you would arrive before she had bent and she would bend round you.

The Current girl had been waiting for the lie. She had her weight laid ready to be pulled, and she was pulled by nothing, and for half a beat she was a tall girl standing on a floor instead of a thing moving over one. Lira came down in front of her on the left foot, which was where that line happened to end, and the landing beat was clean, and her palm was on the Current girl's hip before the bend began.

Blue.

"One each," said the clerk.

At the far rail the Force unfolded his arms and folded them again the other way. Rooke wrote something on his board. Cael let out a breath he had not known he was holding, and Lira, walking back, opened and shut her taped hands twice, quickly, the way she did when she was pleased with herself and did not mean to show it.

She had one burst left, and two exchanges to spend it in.

In the third the Current girl changed.

Cael had fought her, and knew she would. She had come to wing three with two long glides and a bend, and when he had taken those away she had found a straight glide, and after that a glide broken by a short hard step, all inside one bout. She was not a clever fighter, but she was a quick one, and she learned on her feet. Now she came off her chalk in short pieces, a glide and a step and a glide, never more than six feet of anything, so that there was no long curve to get inside and no moment when her weight was laid out for a lie to pull.

Lira read it. He saw her read it, on the girl's front hip, in the first two pieces. She did not burst, but kept the last one in her pocket and walked the exchange instead, on her feet, cutting corners, trying to be where the next short glide would end.

Twice she nearly was. The second time she stepped across to shut the girl's line, and it was a good step, and it ended on her right foot with the Current girl's shoulder a hand's breadth from her fingers. All she had to do was plant and reach.

She shifted to the left to reach. It took as long as a blink. The Current girl glided out from under her hand, and the Stone, who was holding the glass as well as the flags, said, "Time."

No flag.

It was the Current girl who found it. Cael saw her find it. She stood at the north chalk before the fourth with her hands loose and her head a little on one side, looking not at Lira's face or her shoulders but at her feet, as a person looks at a sum on a slate that has come out the same three times. Then her eyes went, once, to a place on the boards to the left of where Lira was standing.

He could have called out. He kept his hands in his coat.

The flags dropped. Lira lied once, and lied twice, and went on the third, early, to be ahead of a girl who had learned the fourth; and it was her last burst, and it was fast, and it was on the old good line along the inside of the curve.

There was no curve. The Current girl had not bent at all. She had gone straight and level to the place she had looked at, a pace to the left of where Lira's line ended, and stood there on her toes, waiting.

Lira's right foot came down, and asked, and she stepped off it to the left, straight into the Current girl's hand.

Red.

"Two flags to one," said the clerk, and wrote it in the red book. "The Current."

Nobody clapped; there was nobody to clap but the Force, and he did not. The Current girl stood where she was with her hand still half out, and her face had gone red to the ears.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I saw you do it in the first, and I wasn't sure, and then in the third you did it again, and I thought, if she's going to come down there every time—" She stopped. "I'm sorry. I know it's the foot."

"Don't you dare," said Lira.

She was breathing hard, with both hands on her knees, and she straightened up and pushed her hair back with her wrist.

"Don't you dare be sorry. You saw where I'd land and you went and stood on it. That's the whole of what we're here to learn." She held out her hand, and the Current girl took it, looking as if she might cry. "It was good. It was better than anything you did at the confluence. If you say sorry to me again I'll tell Rooke you're going easy."

"She wasn't," said Rooke.

He had got up at last, and he came across the painted floor with his board, and stopped in front of Lira, and looked at her feet for some time.

"Three bursts," he said. "And a walk. I counted seven landings that mattered. Every one of them on the left." He turned the board round so that she could see it. He had ruled a line down the middle of it and made his marks on one side only. "The healer passed that foot a fortnight ago. I passed it on the First-day. It's sound. I'd put the Force on it." He turned the board back. "Your foot's mended. Your head hasn't heard."

Lira looked at the board. Then she looked at the floor, at the place where the Current girl had stood waiting.

"I didn't know I was doing it," she said.

"No. That's why you lost." He said it without any heat. "From tomorrow, every count ends on the right. Every one. I don't care where the line wants to go. You'll land on that foot until you've stopped asking it for leave, and then you'll land on it a hundred times more." He went back to the trestle and bent over the red book, and wrote one word under her name, and Cael, from the door, could not read it and knew what it was.

She came out past him into the covered walk with her wraps half off.

It was light now, a low grey light with rain in it, and she walked as far as the first pillar and stopped, and stood with her back to him, unwinding the tape from her left wrist in long strips.

"You saw it," she said. "Not today. Before. On the First-day, when I walked the count."

"Yes."

"And you didn't say."

"No."

She wound the tape round her fingers into a ball. He waited. He had thought, on the First-day, that it would keep, and that it was Rooke's to say, and that she had earned one morning of being pleased. All of that had been true. It did not seem like very much, standing here.

"Rooke didn't say either," said Lira. "But Rooke's my coach. It's his job to let me walk into a thing and then show me the hole. It's not yours." She turned round. She was angry, and she was being fair about it, which with Lira was the worst kind. "Next time, say. Even if I bite. I'd rather hear it from you on a First-day than from a red flag on a Sixth."

"Next time I'll say."

"Good." She put the ball of tape in her pocket. "That's four on my sheet. Three I never stood up for, and one I did." Her chin came up. "I'm taking the key tonight. Don't come."

---

The floor was colder at night.

Lira had forgotten that until she let herself into the east hall after the tenth bell on Rooke's key, alone, with one lantern, and stood inside the great door with her hand on its edge until her eyes had learned the dark. The shutters were down, and the painted crosses went away from her across the boards, white, then grey, then nothing. Somewhere at the far end the heavy frame ticked on its springs as the cold got into them.

"*Door*," she said aloud, to nobody. "*Oak. Rooke's.*"

Then she felt foolish, because he was not there to need it, and shut it behind her.

She hung the lantern on the hook by the rail and sat down on the bench under it to take off her boots. She did the left first and then the right, and when the right was off she held the foot in both hands for a moment and looked at it by the lantern. It was a foot. The swelling had gone out of it weeks ago, and the yellow after the swelling. She pressed her thumb into the hollow under the ankle bone, hard, where the healer had pressed, and it did not hurt at all, not even the ghost of a hurt.

That was the trouble. If it had hurt, she would have known what she was fighting.

She had turned it in a ditch on a chalk road, on the fourth burst, with six people coming through the dust who did not care whether she ever walked again. She remembered the sound of it more than the pain. The foot had not failed since. It was only that something in her had been standing a little way off from it ever since, watching it, the way you watch a dog that has bitten once.

She got up and went out onto the floor in her stockings and stood on the first cross.

"Right," she said. "On the right."

She ran the count. Front foot, back foot, shoulders, and on the fourth she went, not a burst, only a long hard stride, and aimed the end of it at her right foot, and came down.

On the left. With the little step.

She stood and swore at herself, in Fenmark's words, which were the worst she had and were not very bad.

It took her eleven tries to do it once. She counted them, because he had taught her to count things, years ago, at a table in Ardenmere, and she had never been able to stop. On the eleventh she came down on the right foot with her whole weight and held it, and nothing happened. The floor took her and gave her back. The beat was there, where it had always been. She stood on one leg in the middle of Rooke's painted floor with her arms out, and laughed, once, out loud, in the empty hall.

Then she did it again, and came down on the left.

By the thirtieth she had it four times in five. By the fortieth her thighs were shaking and her stockings had worn through at the ball of the right foot, and she went back to the bench and sat with her head against the rail and breathed.

It was not the foot. She had known that since Rooke turned his board round. It was the asking. Every time she came down, there was a moment, shorter than a blink, when she asked the foot whether it would hold, and waited for its answer, and the waiting was the step. The only cure for asking was to land so often that the answer got dull. Rooke had said a hundred more. She thought it would be nearer a thousand, and that she would do them, here, at night, where the Current girl did not have to watch her and be sorry.

She took the book out of her coat.

It was not much of a book. She had bought it from the chandler by the bridge on the day she carried the program's chalk, for less than the price of a meal: a thing the size of her hand, stitched, with an oilcloth cover, of the kind carters kept their loads in. She kept a stub of pencil pushed down its spine. There was nobody's name on it anywhere.

She had started it in the dairy week. Not that night; the night after, when she could not sleep for thinking about three carters and a road with four days of stamps on it and then none. She had thought then, lying on her back in the dark, about what she actually owned. Not the program. The program was Rooke's, and the east hall was the house's, and the red book belonged to a river academy and was only lent. The figure on the steward's sheet was hers, but it was hers the way a room is yours when somebody else holds the key; it meant something because a hall's seal was on it, and it would go on meaning something exactly as far as that seal was honoured.

Four days east, loaded, it would be a piece of paper with a number on it.

So she had started writing down what she knew. Not what she had won. What she knew, in her own words, in pencil, in a carter's book, so that it would still be hers out where no hall's seal went with it.

She turned the pages under the lantern. *FLOORS*, on the first leaf, in the capitals Karis hated. Sprung oak: six, and it gives you back more than you put in, so go early. Boards: five, and they're honest. Slate: four, and it takes the beat out of your knees by the third. New planks laid crossways: they slide, and nobody tells you. Chalk road in wind: four, and mind the ditch. She had underlined *ditch* twice and then, a week later, drawn a small rude face beside it.

*PEOPLE*, after that. A page for the Force: he builds on four, or on two when he's hurrying; you can see it in his knees. A page for the Silver at the confluence, with *go on your fifth* at the top in a box. A page for the Stone, which had almost nothing on it, because he never said anything, only a list of the places she had found him standing. Half a page for the Current girl. *Watch the front hip. Go along the bend.*

She took the pencil out of the spine and wrote under it.

*She learns in the bout. Third exchange, she broke the glide into pieces. Fourth, she stood where I'd land.*

And then, on the leaf at the back that she had headed *ME* and had not much liked writing on:

*Sixth-day, recess. Lost to the Current, two flags to one. Landed left every time and didn't know it. Cael saw on the First-day. Rooke saw. I didn't.*

*It isn't the foot. It's asking the foot.*

*Forty tonight. Four in five by the end. Tomorrow, right.*

She read it over. It was all true, and it did not make her feel any better, and she left it exactly as it was. That was the rule of the book. If she was going to carry it past the last station, there was no use in its being kind to her.

---

She did not get up at once.

The lantern hissed, and up in the dark of the gallery something small moved along the rail and was still. She sat with the book on her knee and her bare right foot flat on the cold boards, on purpose, and let herself think about the sheet.

Four losses. Rooke would send her program sheet down to the district seat with the meet record, when the time came, and a clerk at a counter would lay the two side by side. On one, a figure of twenty-seven against a Silver of eleven years' standing. On the other, *Not fought. Not fought. Not fought.* and a red flag from a girl three ranks up who was her friend. The clerk would not know about a ditch. There was no column on the sheet for ditches.

She found that she minded. That surprised her a little. She had thought, after the reading room, after the forty pairs and the father's letter that she had read aloud in her own voice, that she would never again be able to care what a clerk at a counter laid beside what. She knew what the counter was. She knew what sat behind every certificate in the wing's drawer, her own among them, three lines in a hand she had never seen.

She had wondered about that, once. Only once, on the second night, on the stair. What had the keeper read on her, before anybody wrote it down? She remembered a brass plate, and cold fingers turning her by the shoulder, and being told to stand still. She did not remember anybody saying a word she could use.

She had put it away on the same stair. There was no finding out. And she had spent four years being told what she was by people who had never watched her fight, and she was not going to start telling herself the same kind of story now, in the other direction, with no more to go on than they had.

Wind, first rank. That was what the paper said. What she was, she was writing down herself, in pencil, in a carter's book.

And still she wanted the ladder. She could say that, here, where nobody would write it. Rooke had rebuilt a hall for her. He had brought a man from the river to stand on crosses, and bought a red book, and put her name on a slate in his own hand, and nobody had ever done that for her in her life. At Fenmark they had told her that her form was correct and her room was wrong, and shown her the yard out the back. For a year after that she had stood at guild doors. This was the first door anybody had built to her measure and held open.

She knew now what it opened into. Karis had said it: both doors, the same room. If she climbed every rung Rooke had set for her and stood before the advancement sitting and was rated Silver, she would be a Silver in a registry that struck children's margins as a matter of form. She would be on the paper, and the paper was the thing in the case.

She wanted it anyway. She turned that over, sitting on the bench, and it did not come apart. She wanted to stand on a rated floor and be read right, once, by people whose whole work was reading. It was not noble. She did not think it was wrong either. It was only what she wanted, and she had spent too long wanting nothing to be ashamed of it.

What she did not know was whether she would get to. She was not going to work that out tonight. There would be a day for it, and it was not this one, and when it came she meant to be standing on two feet.

That was the part she could do something about.

She had said to Cael once, on a road, that the only thing she had ever asked of any room was to leave it on her own feet. Fenmark she had left by the yard, with her kit in her arms, because somebody pointed. If she ever left this one, by any door, the clean one or another, she was going to walk out of it at her own pace, with her weight where she chose to put it.

Both feet. She was not going out of here still asking one of them for leave.

She pulled her boots on, the left and then the right, and stood, and stamped the right one down hard on the boards, twice, to hear it. It made the same sound as the other.

She put the book in her coat and took the lantern off its hook. At the great door she stopped, with her hand on the oak, and looked back down the dark hall at the place where the Current girl had stood waiting for her.

"Tomorrow," she said to it.

Then she named the door once more, out of habit, and went out, and locked it behind her.

---

The hill filled up again on the First-day.

Cael watched it from the north wall, as he had watched it empty. The carts came up the switchbacks in the grey morning with the trunks swaying, and the ferry crossed full and went back empty, and at the foot of the road the coats stood against the chandler's wall and counted up what they had counted down. By the second bell the outer court was loud. By the third somebody was shouting for somebody from a window, and he could no longer hear the fountain.

He found that he was sorry about the fountain.

There was a floor sheet for him at the wing's counter, the first in fourteen days, and the desk clerk pushed it across without looking up. *Fourth-day, wing three. Supervised, per provision.* Under it, in the same hand, a line he had not seen on a sheet since before the recess: *Fifth-day: the seat. Excused all floors.*

He folded it and put it in his coat, and went up to his room, and took Hesk's book out of the satchel.

*First-day. The term after the recess. Four hundred back on the hill by noon. The coats counted them in.*

*Lira lost on the Sixth-day, two flags to one, on a foot that's mended. I saw why five days before and didn't tell her. That was wrong, and she said so, and I've said I won't again. She's had the east hall's key two nights running and hasn't asked for company.*

*Sixth sitting on the Fifth-day. Wing three the day before. The rotation's written. I know what both of them will say before I walk into either room.*

*It starts again.*

# Chapter 16 — The Column

The notice on the assessment wing's board was in Gault's hand this week, not the desk clerk's, and Cael read it twice before he understood why.

*Sitting week. The provision's supervised hours are moved to the Fourth-day, second bell, wing three. The seat sits on the Fifth-day and the enrollee attends it. Observers enter by the lodge book.*

The first two sentences were only sense: the seat sat on Fifth-days, and so did wing three, and the provision would have to give way to the registry and not the other way round. It was the third sentence he read twice. In the two years he had been reading that board, nobody had ever needed a line about observers. The people who watched the provision's bouts were the cohort, and the cohort came in at the south door and wrote its name in nothing.

The desk clerk saw him at the board and leaned over the counter.

"Four," he said, low, like a man passing on what he has been told to keep. "Four names in the lodge book since the First-day, asking to observe. Officers from other houses. The Magister's had the porters clear out the old gallery for them, over the west door." He straightened. "So the room won't surprise you."

Behind the counter, at the copying table, Seln's pen stopped for the length of one word, and went on.

"Thank you," said Cael.

"Mm," said Seln, to his page, and turned it over.

The real errand had been the board, but he had a docket number to ask Bracken about, so he went back by the records hall. The counter's daybook lay open on the brass rail where Bracken had left it to dry, and Cael read the day's line before he could stop himself. It was a page of figures and he had never in his life been able to leave a page of figures unread. Four narrow columns had been ruled across it in the last few weeks, in Bracken's small capitals: *HOUSES. SPONSORS. FACULTIES. OTHER.* Under *OTHER*, yesterday, the figure was one hundred and forty-one. The heaviest line on the page, the Fifth-day before the sitting, said one hundred and ninety-six.

Beside the daybook lay a bundle of fair copies tied with green tape, with a label on the tape: *Houses. Answered.*

Bracken came out of the post room with his sleeves rolled. He did not say anything about the daybook. He answered the docket question. Then he saw where Cael was looking, and untied the tape and turned the top copy round.

*Halcenvane's enrollment stands on a provision the registry has not set aside, and has been entered in full on the public record. Each chartered house must read that provision for itself.*

"Fourteen," said Bracken, and tied the tape again.

There was one letter on the counter that had not gone into any column. It was sealed in grey.

"The seat," said Bracken. "For the reading room. Those don't go in the baskets. I carry them up."

Cael crossed the fountain court with Gault's third sentence in his head. Holding himself inside the house's rate in front of three cards and a clerk had never been nothing, but it had always been private. Tomorrow four strangers with writing boards would sit over the west door of wing three, and lay what he did beside everything the broadsheets and the tournament books had printed, line for line.

---

Rooke went down the bluff road at a little before dusk with a satchel on his shoulder, and Cael watched him from the north wall because watching Rooke walk anywhere was unusual; he had business in the town on Third-day evenings, and nobody had ever asked what it was.

At the second switchback below the gate, Rooke stopped.

There was a hat on the milestone there. It took Cael a moment to see that there was a man under it, sitting with his collar turned up and a board on his knee, very still, the way people sit who have been sitting a long time. Lira, who had come up the wall stair with two apples and given him one without asking, said the porter's boy had counted: since the third bell. He had read the board on the gatepost, perhaps. It said in registry type that the house had answered the challenge in its filing, that the filing was public, and that the house had nothing to add to it. He had not come up to argue with the lodge-man. He had simply sat down where a man on the outside of the bend could see the gate and the wall and the whole road up to them, and waited for a boy to come down it.

They watched Rooke say something short. The man looked up the road toward the gate for a long moment. Then he got up, and tucked the board under his arm, and went down the hill in front of Rooke at a walk.

When Rooke came back up without the satchel, Lira leaned over the wall.

"Coach. What did you say to him?"

Rooke looked up. Cael expected nothing; nothing was Rooke's usual answer. But he gave one.

"That the boy trains before light and eats after dark, and neither of them happens on this road."

He went in at the gate. Lira laughed into her apple.

"Three of them came up to the lodge on the First-day," she said. "Did you hear? They argued with the lodge-man for a quarter of an hour about the board, and one of them tried to give him a coin, and he pretended not to see it, because he said afterward it was a very small coin." She turned the apple round to find the last good side. "I've got eighteen days to the road meet. Rooke says I'm not to think about it. I think about it every time I put my foot down." Then, in exactly the same voice, she said, "*How often does the unclassified enrollee come up to watch the Silver drills?*"

He looked at her.

"That's what one of them asked the clerk. On the east hall's gallery. Friendly, the way you'd ask about the weather." She threw her apple core out over the wall into the scrub, a long throw. "The clerk told Rooke. Rooke's had her keeping a second ledger since a little after the first sitting. Every name on the gallery, every morning, and their house, and how long they stay. He reads it every night when he locks up. He never said why." She held up four fingers, folded down one at a time. "Rhagen, Rhagen, the lake house, and a place up north she had to ask him to spell. Twenty-six names in a fortnight, and those four are the only ones with no business with us."

He saw them as she said it: the two travelling coats on the gallery yesterday morning, the one who wrote and the one who did not; the one who did not, turning his head four times to the north end of the rail, where Cael had stood being glad for her.

He thought of her coming away from Fenmark at fourteen with nothing, after an examiner had wiped his slate with his sleeve so the form would be clean. Now there was a red book on the east hall's trestle with her figures in it, and a card on the door with her name among the program's seniors, and a ladder somebody had built toward her for the first time in her life. And strangers were sitting at the top of it, watching the stair for him.

"Rooke hasn't stopped the drills," he said.

"Not yet." She pushed off the wall. "He writes it all down first. You'd know."

---

The schoolroom letter was already lying by itself on the board when Cael sat down after supper.

It was folded very small, on paper ruled for copying, set apart from the three piles at Lira's end of the long table and squared to the board's edge. Nobody was touching it. Lira read it to him, once, in a voice with nothing added.

*To the boy at Halcenvane. My teacher read us about you. I am nine and not Kindled yet. Does it hurt, not having a Path written down? I would like to know before it is my turn.*

She put it back where it had been, by itself.

Each of her three piles had a slip of paper tucked under its corner, and each slip had one word on it in her hand. Cael had seen them every night for a fortnight. He read them again now, left to right, because he liked how little she had wasted on them.

*Kind. Unkind. Unhinged.*

He reached across and took one letter off the top of each. The kind one was from a drover on the north road, who had stood on the Concourse's top step for the whole of the Norhold match and wanted it entered somewhere that his knees had held. The unkind one he got as far as the second line of before Lira took it out of his hand, folded it, and put it back, without a word. The unhinged one was a chart of the night sky in four colours of ink, with one star circled, and a note explaining with perfect confidence that this was Cael's Path, and that the registry would understand everything if only it would look up.

"Which star?" said Brom.

"The one with the circle," said Lira.

"It's a good circle," said Brom.

"None of these get answered," said Lira, to nobody in particular, squaring the kind pile. "They all go in my trunk. By pile, then by date. Somebody ought to have a record of this year that wasn't kept by a clerk."

"Good," said Karis, from the end of the table, without looking up from her volume.

Ephram came by on his way up with the captain's file under his arm and a folded sheet in his hand, which he laid on top of the unhinged pile with some ceremony.

"From my cousin at the river faculty," he said. "Their reading list for the second-years. Week nine." Cael unfolded it. Between a ruling about a ferry company and a ruling about a mill race, in a clerk's neat print, it said *The Halcenvane matter (pending)*. "You're on a syllabus," said Ephram. "A file sits in a drawer and nobody reads it. A syllabus gets read aloud by an old man with a pointer for a hundred years." He took the sheet back. "Try to be one of the dull weeks."

"I'll do my best."

"You never have yet," said Ephram, and went up.

The second post came up while she was tying the piles. The porter brought one letter to the table himself, because he had learned the seal: a square cream envelope, a dark red seal pressed with the bar and the ring.

He laid it by Brom's plate.

The last one had come on the thirty-first day, and Brom had kept it two nights on his table before he opened it. This one had come in fourteen. He looked at it. He picked it up and turned it over once in his big hands, and looked at the seal, whole, the bar and the ring. Then he put it, unopened, into the left inside pocket of his coat, where the summons was, and the docket, and the confirmation, and the others.

Nobody asked. That had been the arrangement since the recess, and it held. Lira tied her bundle with a knot she did not usually use. Karis opened her volume again at the page she had just closed. Cael watched Brom's hand come away from the pocket and settle on the board, and then, a moment later, go back to the pocket and rest flat on the cloth over it, and stay there, as if the letter might get out.

The arrangement held. It had started to cost the table something, and Brom more than any of them.

On the stair afterward Lira fell into step beside Cael and said, without looking at him, "I'd open it for him."

"I know."

"If he asked, I'd sit with him while he did. I'd read it to him if he wanted, and never say one word about it after." She climbed two more steps. "He won't ask. He's carrying it the way he carries everything, so nobody else has to. And the worst of it is that's exactly what we'd all want him to do, if it were any of us." She stopped at her own landing. "So we don't ask. And he carries it. And the letter sits in his coat getting heavier." She went in at her door and shut it quietly.

---

Karis had gone up before the piles were tied. When Cael followed her to the reading room at the ninth bell, she was writing, and he read what she was writing upside down before he knew what it answered.

*Research standing: enrolled, renewed in the recess by the council of the house in two sittings, entered as* Research, enrolled, standing. *Budget line: the house's accounts, the registrar's entry of the thirty-fifth day. Renewing officer: the Chancellor.*

It was an answer, then. Only afterward did he see the question, printed on the other side of the sheet she was writing on. It was a form from the district fee office at Ostrand, which put its seal on every request a chartered house sent to an archive outside its own district. Her request of the thirty-fifth day, to Greyvane, for the founders' signature leaves, was in order and its fee was paid; before the office would seal it on, it asked the requester to state her standing, its last renewal, and the officer who had renewed it. It cited the section that let it ask.

She finished, and blotted it, and did not say anything about it, and he did not ask.

The door from the landing opened, and the assistant stood there in her cloak with her face pale round the mouth and red at the cheekbones.

"I was boring," she said.

Karis put down her pen. "Shut the door. Come in. Start at the end."

The girl shut the door. "At the end she said *thank you* in a very small voice and went away. Before that she kept looking at the stair." She swallowed. "Before that, I'd told her about the ninety-one. And the paragraph numbers. And that the next sitting's on the Fifth-day, and that the petitioner's conceded the provision is valid and doesn't allege fraud, and that the Compact's own inspection looked at the provision and it stood." She was going backward through it as Karis had told her to, and her voice steadied as she went. "And at the start I told her he's enrolled under a lawful provision, adjudicated at Greyvane on the public record, under seal, with the grant and two renewals on paper. Because she asked me what the enrollee was *really* like. A Mire girl from the north stair, on the covered walk, after supper. I hardly know her." She stopped. "That was the beginning."

"Then you weren't boring," said Karis. "You told the truth quickly, to a question you weren't ready for, to somebody who came for gossip and got the code. That's the whole work; the rest is decoration. Do it like that for a year, every time anyone asks, and you'll be more use to this house than any clerk on the hill."

The girl stood very still. Then she said "Yes," very quietly, exactly as Karis said it, and went out, and they heard her sit down at the outer table, though there was nothing on it for her to do at that hour.

"Write it in yours," said Karis. "That she came back up the stair to tell me."

Then she lifted her head after all, and turned the fee office's form over, and pushed it across the table to the girl's empty place, as if the girl were still sitting in it. In the morning, when the assistant came up to the outer table, it was waiting there for her, with one line marked in pencil: the section the fee office had cited as its authority to ask. Beside it, in Karis's hand, two words. *Find it.*

She found it before the second bell. Cael was on the landing when she brought the volume in, open, and laid it in front of Karis with her finger under a sub-clause near the foot of the page.

"It doesn't say *shall*," the girl said. "Or *upon cause*. It says the office *may*, at its discretion. There isn't any condition at all."

"Yes," said Karis.

The girl waited, the way she had learned not to, for the rest. There was no rest. She went back to the outer table and wrote it in her own small notebook, and did not ask what it meant, and Cael did not hear anybody tell her.

---

The column was at the back of Hesk's book, where he had ruled it at the month's close with four names at its head, his own last. He wrote in it that night for the first time.

Under Lira: *Strangers on her gallery, two weeks. One asked how often I come up.*

Under Brom: *Velmere, a fortnight after the last. Into the pocket sealed. Hand on the pocket.*

Under Karis: *The fee office wants her standing before it will send her request on.* And under that, small, because the girl was not one of the four but there was nowhere else to put it: *Her assistant, on the walk. Answered with the code. Came back up the stair to say so.*

Under his own name there was nothing to write. He drew a short line at the foot of each of the four columns, the way Karis had told him to, so that each could be added up; and under the first three he wrote a figure, the number of lines above it. Under his own he wrote *0*, and looked at it for a long time, and did not write anything beside it.

Then he turned forward to a clean page, because tomorrow was the Fourth-day and there was a line still to write.

He had written one before every bout since the mill town: a single line out of the rotation, saying which exchange he would give away, how many bursts he would let the house see, where the spark would go, and what the figure ought to come to at the end. Seln had told him across a document table that his figures were too tidy. The rotation was the cure: scatter, built on purpose and paid for in advance, so that no one with a pencil and a season's worth of his numbers could find the hand that set them.

Tonight he sat with the pen over the page for a long time.

The trouble was the strangers. Three cards and a clerk who knew him were one kind of audience. Four officers from four houses, with the broadsheets and the tournament books in their satchels, were another. Rhagen wrote down every exchange of every bout it could see and bound the result and sold it; somebody in an inn that very night would be laying tomorrow's figure beside last season's, line for line. Whatever he did must look exactly like the record, which meant like a strong Iron with a few odd gifts on a good day. And it must not look like the last bout, or the one before, because a record that matches itself has been arranged, and men whose trade is arrangements can smell it.

He went back through the last eight figures in his head. Twenty-three and a third. Twenty-four and two-thirds. Twenty-two and a third. Before those, the season's. He had to land somewhere among them that none of them had landed.

He thought about whom he would draw. The wing's list had a Current third-year on it for the Fourth-day, Iron Rank Four, a long-legged girl from the movement floor who glided. He had watched her in the Crown yard. He had watched Lira beat her pattern two mornings ago, on the painted floor, by going along the bend of her glide instead of across it. He could do that. He could do it in the first exchange and be standing at the end of her curve with his palm out before she got there.

The book would not let him.

He wrote the line at last, and read it over, and did not like it, and left it, because not liking it was a sign that it was honest.

*Fourth-day, wing three, the Current. The first exchange to her. Two bursts in the bout, no more. The spark once, and late. Buys: a figure in the low twenties, nothing near the top. Spends: the forearm, probably.*

He put the book away and went down, late as it was, to wing three.

Nobody stopped him. The assessment wing was dark except for the lamp at the counter, and the door to wing three had never been locked in his time. He went in and stood on the old pine boards with the high east windows black above him, and then he walked the floor, slowly, from his chalk at the south end to the Current's at the north, counting strides. Fourteen. He walked it again along the line her bend would take. Then he went to the west end and looked up.

The gallery over the door was clean. The porters had swept it that afternoon; he could smell the damp of the swept boards. Somebody had set a row of four chairs along its rail, and a fifth stool, for whoever might not want a chair. From the floor, standing where he would stand, he could see all five of them without turning his head. From the gallery, he supposed, they would see the top of his head and his feet, and whatever his feet did.

He stood there a while longer in the dark, a fighter getting the measure of a room, as he had in every hall he had ever fought in. Only this time it was not the floor he was measuring. It was the people who would sit above it and decide, from his feet, what he was.

Then he went to bed.

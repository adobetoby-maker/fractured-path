# Chapter 20 — Lot Seventeen

Three nights before the third sitting, the honest floor was the only floor on the hill where anybody was allowed to see what Cael was.

They went in at the quarter to the eleventh bell, as they always did, on the key Rooke had lent Lira for the program's dawn drills and had told her he did not want to know about after dark. But the east hall was not what it had been. The shutters along the gallery had been closed since the Fourth-day by Rooke's order, and they did not need to be drawn. The chain still hung across the foot of the gallery stair with its padlock and its card, *CLOSED TO OBSERVERS*, so that Brom had to step over it to set the jug down on the bottom stair. The four lamps that the program now drilled under all day were standing at the corners of the painted crosses where the porters had left them, cold.

"Well," said Lira, looking round the shut-up hall with her wrists half taped. "The whole floor's honest now. Nobody's allowed to watch any of it."

Brom lit two of the lamps and left the other two dark, because two had always been the number. Karis sat on the gallery stair above the chain with her notebook open on her knee. Nobody had asked her to come. She had come anyway, every week since the first night, and counted.

"What do you want?" said Cael.

"The real one." Lira tore the last strip of tape with her teeth. "Everything you can't show in wing three. I've had six days of drilling under lamps, and I can't see my own heel coming any more, and I keep landing a quarter-beat late on things I could read in my sleep in daylight." She put her hands on her hips. "If I can see you coming at your true rate, in lamplight, I can see anybody coming at anything. So come."

He looked at Brom. Brom looked at the lamps and then at him, and nodded once, which meant *I'm holding the room*.

So Cael fought her at his true rate, for the first time since the first week of the year.

He let the read go all the way out and come all the way back, without stopping it anywhere short to look like timing. It brought him Lira's weight half a beat early, as it always did, and in lamplight half a beat was a long time. He used the Wind the way it wanted to be used and not the way the house's book had it. One burst, two, the second off the landing of the first; a third, low, along the wall; a fourth to take him round her lie on the four-count before her fourth arrived. On the boards in wing three he stopped at four, and never let a fifth show. Here he went to five without thinking about it, and then to six, and felt the old easy looseness come into his legs that he had not let himself feel on a floor in front of anybody since Norhold.

Lira lost the first two exchanges. She lost them cleanly and quickly and said "Again" both times before she had finished losing.

In the third she came at him with a straight palm, the hardest thing she had, from the end of a burst, and he did not move out of it. He let it land on his forearm. The give was there, Reydan's give, and for once he let it open. It drank her blow into the arm as a dry cup drinks water, and kept it, and her palm stopped against him as if it had hit a sack of wet sand. She stood there with her arm out, looking at it.

"I hate that," she said with great feeling.

"I know."

"I'd forgotten how much." She shook her hand out. "Again. And lay one."

He laid a lane.

He laid it across her line from the north wall, a road of leaning air fifteen feet long and no wider than his shoulders, tipping south. His ears shut and opened again. And on the gallery stair Karis began to count aloud, quietly, from the moment it was laid.

"One."

In the lamplight Lira could not read her heel. She had said so. But Cael, watching her come at the lane on the four-count, saw her find something else. The dust in the cracks between the painted boards had woken as the lane went down, the way it always did, and was running south along the lane in a thin pale stream. In daylight from the high windows you could hardly see it. Under two lamps set low on the floor, it threw a shadow. The whole lane showed on the boards as a faint moving band of dark, like the wake of a fish under still water.

Lira saw it on her second count. He watched her see it. Her lie went onto the back foot, outside the band, and on the third count, the empty one, she did not put a foot into the lane at all. On the fourth she burst along its edge, the length of it, not across, and came out at its end beside him with her palm already flat on his shoulder.

"Touch," said Brom, from the dark beyond the lamps.

"*Seven*," said Karis, on the stair.

Cael reached for the Wind. It came late. It came a beat behind his asking, the right thing answering slow, as it had answered every time since the first night on this floor. He stood in it. *Bill, not flag.* Lira, beside him, breathing hard, was looking down at the boards where the dust was settling out of the band and the band was fading.

"The dust," she said. "Under the lamps the dust makes a shadow. I can see the lane by its shadow."

"I saw you see it."

"*Ten*," said Karis. "*Eleven*."

The Wind came when he called it.

"Ten counts," said Karis, and wrote it down. "Same as the first night."

Lira had gone down on one knee by the end of the lane, and was looking along the boards at the last of the dust, with her face close to the floor and the lamp throwing her shadow long behind her.

"I lost my light," she said, to the boards. "Rooke shut it out on account of who was watching you. I've been angry about it for six days, all by myself, under these lamps, and I haven't told anybody, because it was the right thing to do and I knew it was." She stood up and dusted her knee. "And it turns out the lamps show me a thing the windows never did."

"That doesn't make it fair."

"No. It makes it mine." She went back to her cross. "Lay another. Not across this time. Lay it down the line, at me, and let me try to stand in it."

"We said we don't spend a new figure the night we find it."

"It's not a new figure. It's the same lane. I'm the new thing." She set her feet. "Lay it."

He laid it, once more, and she stood in it on the four-count and was pushed off her feet into the dust, and laughed out loud from the floor. Karis counted ten and wrote ten, and then Brom said that was enough for one night in a voice that nobody argued with.

They put out the lamps by the old order, the far one first, and stepped back over Rooke's chain, and Lira locked the door behind them.

On the stair Cael thought about it, the way he thought about everything on stairs.

In wing three, four strangers had written down a figure for him that was exactly right and entirely false: the band, the house's rate, twenty-two and two-thirds. Here, on a floor with the shutters closed and a chain across the stair, there had been nobody to write anything at all, and the figure had been true. Six bursts on boards. The give opened. A lane and its bill. Every true number about him this year had been made in a locked room by four people who would never be allowed to say it aloud. And every false one had gone into a book.

He wrote it in Hesk's book at the sill before he slept. *The only honest measurement anybody has taken of me since Norhold was taken tonight, by Karis, on a closed floor, under two lamps. It will never be entered anywhere. Lira found the lane by its shadow. That will be entered nowhere either, and it's the best thing anybody learned on this hill this week.*

---

On the evening before the third sitting the house's counsel came up the hill by arrangement for once, and spent three hours in the reading room with Karis, and Cael was allowed to sit at the window and listen as long as he did not speak.

They did not argue about what to say. That had been settled weeks ago, in eleven pounds four ounces. They argued about the order.

"Text first," said the counsel. She had her steel rule out on the long table and was turning it end over end in her fingers. "It's the strongest. The drafters' list. The seat can't get round a list."

"That's why it should be last." Karis had four slips of paper in front of her and was moving them about like a girl playing at shop. "If you open with the list, the seat spends two hours thinking it's a quarrel about paper. Jent will have given away all the paper before you stand up. He gives it away first; he always does. So start where he can't follow you." She put one slip at the head of the row. "Start with the people. Twenty-two of them, by name. Make the seat sit through every name. Then the preamble, so they hear what the sections were for. Then the shelf. And then the list, last, so the last thing they hear is the thing that isn't there."

The counsel stopped turning the rule. She looked at the four slips for a long moment.

"From the people to the paper," she said.

"From the people to the paper."

"I'll need to mark my place. Two hours is long for a gallery." The counsel laid the rule flat on the table, pointing at the window, and turned it a quarter of the way round, and then another quarter, as if she were trying the weight of the idea. "That will do. A turn for each. Anyone who loses the thread can look at the rule."

Karis wrote the four words on a card in her finding hand, one under the other, and put the card in her case for the morning. Then she sat back and rubbed her eyes, and Cael saw that her hands were not steady.

"It won't be enough," she said.

"No," said the counsel. "It will be the best argument that room has heard in a long time, and it won't be enough." She put the rule in her case. "Do it anyway. Somebody reads the minute afterward. They always do."

---

"That's her," said Lira, behind him, very low.

She was on the attendance bench, close enough to lean forward and say it into his collar, and she said it before Cael had looked toward the evaluation seat at all. He looked. The grey covers were gone from its five chairs, and four district evaluators he had never seen sat upright in four of them, two men and two women, with their files squared on small lap-desks. The fifth chair stood a little forward of the others. In it sat a broad-backed woman in the registry's grey. Her files were set at an angle he would have known anywhere, never quite square to the desk and never a hair different from the last time: a counter at Greyvane, the east wall of a room on the bluff, the first seat of the Compact's row over the Concourse.

Ilsev did not look up. She was reading.

"They've put the straightest person they own in the middle," Karis murmured on his other side, with her eyes on her own papers. "Nobody's going to look past her at what she's sitting next to."

Behind the evaluation seat, at the records table, the officer with the careful face was writing the time at the head of a leaf. Brom, on the bench behind, leaned forward to Cael's other ear.

"None," he said.

Cael knew what he meant. Brom had counted the man's looks toward the house's table at Greyvane, and at the first sitting here, and he was counting now. None.

The clerk was on his feet before the parties were called, reading a paper into the minute under the heading *statements entered*: the filer's name and house and Path and rank, the date, the line of receipt, and then the text, in the voice that adds nothing.

*I fought him. Your instruments failed him, not the other way around. Classify the instruments.*

The hood received it. The clerk laid the sheet on the pile of things received and reached for the next. Eleven seconds.

Cael did not write anything about it in words. On the back of his schedule, under the table, he wrote two numbers one above the other, *44* and *54*, the day the Archmarshal had been told the seat was full and the day the best practitioner alive had been laid on a pile. Karis saw them, and looked at him, and nodded once, and went back to her papers.

Everything else that morning he saw only between things. The side-door officer, his cold gone, not needing to ask the last name on his list. Somebody had already copied *Caelen Hesk-ward* into his door log from the last sitting, so that all he had to write was *the enrollee of record, unclassified*, which took him nine seconds now that his hand knew the way. *Seventeen* under Cael's left foot again, the third time, and this time chosen; he could not choose the room, but he could choose where he stood in it. And a slip of paper folded twice that Karis had handed Brom on the ferry, which was in Brom's right inside pocket now, with orders not to be opened until the coach went home. The left pocket was for Velmere, and it had a sealed letter in it.

---

Jent stopped in the middle of a sentence.

"A record kept under the demonstration provision," he had been saying, "is not a record that this seat could—"

And he stood at the petitioner's table with his empty hands at his sides, in front of forty benches and a hood, and Cael let the read go out across the floor to him while he stood there. It was his oldest habit, older than the band, and in that room it had never once brought him anything. It came back now with the man's weight, settled square on both heels, nothing leaning toward the thing he was deciding or away from it. No want under it at all.

"No," said Jent. "I withdraw that. It overreaches, and it is false. The provision makes a record, and the record can be read; the respondent spent fifty-one minutes at the last sitting reading it in." He turned his head a quarter-inch toward the clerk. "What the petitioner says is smaller. The record is of what the practitioner has done. It isn't, and doesn't pretend to be, a measure of what he is. The petition rests on that difference and on nothing wider. Clerk, take the wider words out of the minute, if you would."

The clerk drew his pen through a line.

Cael found he had lifted his own pencil off the paper and not put it down. Of everything he had seen in that room in three sittings, it was the first thing he had not seen coming.

What Jent had already given away, Cael could read without looking back at his own notes, because the house's counsel had been keeping score of it in the margin of her privileged minute, where he could see it. Three headings stood there in her small capitals, written before the sitting began. *EXECUTION. PROVISION. RECORD.* Through each of them now ran a single neat line. She had drawn the first in Jent's first minute, when he told the hood the petition had never said the enrollment was wrongly made. The second, a little after, when he called the clause law and took the house's history of it for his own, word for word. The third when he said again, for the minute, that he had never read a better-kept record of an enrollment, and the clerk looked up to ask if that was to go in as spoken, and Jent said, "Every word. I'd decline to soften it if I were asked," and something along the front benches shifted.

Under the three struck headings there was nothing. The counsel had not needed to write what was left, because there was only one thing, and everyone could hear it.

Cael's own notes had what Jent said it was. The registry's confidence in its own evaluations, which the integrity sections were there to protect; Jent had read their first line to show it. If no standard reading, no trust, *by that fact and by nothing else*, which he had said three times, like a man laying a plank across a ditch and then walking on it. The discretion, whole and undisputed, needing no fault found anywhere first. And then the thing he had just taken back, the overreach, the stop.

Cael sat with it and put it the way Lira would want it on the coach going home, in what happens to people and not in words from a code.

Everything they had spent six weeks fighting about was gone. Whether the clause was law; whether the house's record was honest; whether anybody had done anything wrong. Jent had handed all of it across the floor in his first ten minutes, and it could never be taken back now, because it was in the minute in his own voice. What was left was one question with no paper in it at all: may the registry stand him in a frame? And everyone in the room knew already what the frame would say.

Jent asked for a single word from the seat, *cognizable*: that this was a question the seat was allowed to pick up. Once it had picked it up, the code already said what came next. A frame. The standard protocol. And the enrollment would live or die by whatever the frame said back.

He gave a page number in the file from Norhold, once, and closed his mouth on it. Karis wrote the page number down and did not look it up; she knew what was on it.

Then he sat, and unscrewed his inkwell, and laid his hands flat on either side of his clerk's tabbed folder, which he had never once opened.

"I like him," Lira breathed behind Cael, unhappily.

"So do I," said Cael. "That's the trouble with him."

---

The house's counsel turned her steel rule four times, and each turn did something to the room before anybody in it understood what she had argued.

At the first turn the four district evaluators stopped looking at nothing and began to look at her. At the second, the front benches put their pencils down. At the third, the hood spoke, for the only time that day. At the fourth the rule lay pointing at the platform again, as it had when she rose, and the gallery made the long low sound galleries make when they may not clap.

Karis had slid a card along the table before the counsel stood. *NAMES. PREAMBLE. SHELF. TEXT.* "She's going from the people to the paper," she had breathed. "Tick them."

It was the names that had made the evaluators look up. Ilsev heard her own read out among them and wrote something small in her file without lifting her head. The counsel read twenty-two of them, one at a time, each with a grade and a house and a day, at the pace at which a person reads names in a room where names matter. The grant at Greyvane and its two renewals on paper, the season's officers under a regional registrar's seal, the tournament's, and three days of the Compact's own senior evaluator on the bluff. At the Archmarshal's name Cael thought the stillness under the hood altered, the way still water alters when something passes under it. It took four minutes. In the quiet he heard a cart's iron tyre grind over the cobbles in the street. More looking, the counsel said when she had finished, than most practitioners get in a working life; and all of it done by people who could see, sealed and minuted, across three years. She turned the rule. Tick.

It was Karis's count that made the pencils go down. Cael felt her go still beside him as the counsel gave it: seven times in the registry's whole record the integrity sections had been turned on a person, every one of the seven for something the person had done; eighty-four times on things; and not once on what a person was. Only after the count did the counsel lay her papers face down and read the room the preamble, ninety seconds of plain old words, so that the benches heard what the sections had been written for after they had heard what they had been used for. The testing of instruments. Frames, stations, records, protocols. So that the registry might know which of them it could trust.

"Only that nobody ever has," the counsel said then, without a pause, and Cael understood from her answer what the hood had asked: whether the sections could never lawfully reach a practitioner, or only had never been made to. "That's enough. In all the years these sections have stood, this seat is asked to go first, and turn them on a person for what he is. A seat that's asked to go first deserves to hear it in plain words. The petition never used them."

Jent rose to say the petitioner had never called the use an ordinary one, and was content for the minute to say it was new. The counsel said it after him, word for word, and this once, the only time all morning, she said it over her shoulder to the benches. Third turn. Tick.

Cael missed the shelf. Ilsev had turned a leaf in her file just then and looked, for the first time all morning, at the house's table, at him, for perhaps a breath; and by the time he had looked back to the counsel the rule had turned again and Karis had already ticked the line. Later, on the coach, he would have to ask Karis which rulings had been on it.

And last, because the whole case ended in something that was not there, the drafters' list. She took up the directive that had standardized the code and turned to the schedule at its back where its drafters had listed every older provision they meant to cancel, and walked the hood down it slowly, item by item. At the bottom she stopped. They had been careful men, she said. They had listed everything they meant to end. The assay clause was not on their list. Care only runs one way.

Twice Karis passed a card forward to her, and Cael, who could never help reading upside down, read both: a page number corrected, which the counsel used in her next sentence without a hitch; and *Leave the fifth point. Don't need it.* The counsel turned that one face down.

She finished with no flourish at all.

"The petition asks this seat to measure a man with the one instrument that has never once been able to see him, and to call that measuring. Twenty-two officers who could see him have told you what they saw. You have their names. Ask them."

She gave the rule its last quarter turn, and sat.

---

Ilsev was standing.

The hood had read the ruling and the room had not yet worked out what it had heard, and in that gap Ilsev rose from the middle chair and asked leave, and had it, and did not open her files.

Cael heard the hood's answer before he understood her question. *Its keeping to protocol. Not its result. Nor that.* Ilsev had asked what her seat was there to do, and whether that included deciding if the standard protocol could read this practitioner at all, and the seat had said no to the second. Now she was asking that its words be minuted exactly as spoken, so that nobody could stretch her chair afterward to cover the question. The clerk wrote. Ilsev sat, and wrote the same words into her own file in her own hand, and set the file back at its old angle.

"She's fencing her chair," Karis whispered. "So they can't say later she sat on it."

A chair whose only work was to watch how a re-evaluation was carried out. Then there was going to be one to watch.

Cael went back to the ruling and made himself hear it again in his head. The hood had read it from its single leaf, in the same unhurried voice it had used for its one question; only now he heard it the other way about, from what the house had kept to what it had lost.

*The house has observed its charter in every particular. Its record is entire, and stands untouched. The clause beneath the enrollment is in force; the standardizing directive did not reach it. The enrollment was made lawfully, and the seat so finds.*

The correspondent's hat had gone onto the floor of the second bench just there, when he half rose to be first to the doors and knocked it off his own knee; Lira had caught Cael's sleeve; and the hood had read straight on, its voice and speed unchanged.

*Argument on the petition to compel the enrollee's re-evaluation under the standard Arbiter protocol is set down for the sittings that remain, and the seat will rule on it before the academic year closes. The matter proceeds in full: how far the integrity sections reach a practitioner is for the registry to determine, and the seat determines that they reach this one far enough to be heard. On the question raised, the seat may take it up.*

Karis's hands had gone flat on the table, and stayed there.

Cael made himself say it the coach way, plainly, so that he would have it straight. What they had won, they had for good: no seat would ever again argue whether the clause was law or whether the house's paper was honest; that was settled under seal, in the minute, forever. What they had not won was the only thing that could still reach him. The sittings that were left would argue a single question, whether to stand him in a frame. And nobody in the room, not Jent, not Ilsev, not the four evaluators, not the hood, could pretend not to know what the frame would say.

Withrow bowed to the platform at the registry's depth when the hood rose. Jent did not smile or gather his papers or once look across the floor.

In the passage behind the selling floor, while the porters brought the cases down, Lira caught his sleeve again. She had lost the thread twice from the attendance bench.

"So we won?"

"Every point."

"And he's going in a frame."

"Probably."

She stood with that in the fleece smell of the old sample passage. "Then it's like a bout," she said slowly, "where the judges give you every exchange and write down you won every one, and then tell you the bout goes on anyway, under rules they'll pick later." She let go of his sleeve. "That's both. That's what she wrote on the slip, isn't it."

"I think so."

"I hate it," said Lira, with deep satisfaction at having understood it, and went to help Brom with the second case.

Withrow came through the passage a moment later, pulling on her gloves, on her way to her carriage. She did not stop. As she passed Cael she said, without turning her head, "Sit up straight in the coach. The town watches it," which was what she had said to him on the evening of the charter folder, and went on out to the steps.

Through the far doorway Cael could see the records table. The officer with the careful face had stayed at it. He was squaring the minute-book to the table's edge with both hands, and then, when it was square, squaring it again.

---

Brom unfolded the slip at the first milestone past the town and held it up so that they could all read it.

One word, in Karis's hand. *Both.*

Karis was looking out of the window at the river, and did not look round.

Then for a while everybody talked about the steps at once, and nobody agreed on what had happened first.

Brom said the first thing he remembered was the bruise: something had hit him hard on the left hip on the second step, and when he looked down there was a boy's satchel buckle going away from him round the corner by the corn chandler's, and a voice saying sorry over its shoulder. He had not seen the boy's face. He rubbed the hip now, thoughtfully, as if the bruise might tell him more.

Cael asked Karis what the shelf had established. "The clause survived five challenges," she said. "Greyvane was the newest."

Karis said Jent had stopped by her, not the counsel.

"He said, 'Advocate. The ninety-one. Did you count them yourself?'" She was still looking at the river. "I said every one. He said, 'I thought so,' and put on his hat, and walked off up the street as if he were going home to his dinner."

"He'll give more away next time," said the counsel from her corner, writing. "Giving things away is how that man walks forward." She wrote one line more, and Cael read it upside down: *Valid: held. To proceed: held. Both.* She closed the minute-book on it.

The river went by. Lira kept one hand on the closed drill-book and never lifted its cover. Brom put the slip back in his right inside pocket and left his hand over it.

At the ferry the coach waited its turn on the landing, and Cael looked out at the two grey coats who stood there as they always stood, changing on the bell. One of them was not looking at the coach's door or at the faces in its windows. He was looking at the luggage rail on the roof, where the house's two document cases were strapped, the way a clerk looks at a line he means to copy.

Nobody in the coach said anything about it. All of them had seen it.

The ferry took them across. On the far landing, while the coach was hauled up the ramp, Karis turned from the window at last.

"We won every point that can be won on paper."

Brom took the time he always took. "And?"

"And it's proceeding."

Cael set the night's entry in his head as the wheels found the road. It came out at one line, and he did not try to make it longer.

*Part of me is still keeping score. The rest of me has started counting doors.*

Seln had not opened his eyes since the town. He did not open them now. When he spoke, it was to the whole coach, and not loudly.

"Now you understand the file I've been reading my whole career."

# Chapter 6 — The Records Hall

The fire-watch kept an oil book. Every lamp lit on the hill after the tenth bell went into it, by building and by hour, so that the bursar could see at the year's end where the house had burned its money in the dark. Bracken signed it himself every morning, and had done for thirteen years, and in all that time the records hall had never once had a line in it.

At a little past the eighth bell he took the book down from its hook by the side door, opened it on the counter at the night's page, and wrote in his small upright hand.

*Records hall. Lamps late. On the registrar's authority, and at his charge.*

Then he hung it back on its hook and went to light the lamps.

There were six along the long table on iron stands, two on the counter, and one over the door of the locked press. When he had lit them all the hall was a room Cael had never seen: warm in the middle and black at the edges, the tall windows gone blind, the long table laid down the floor like a road with lamps along it.

Bracken took its head, nearest his counter, with the registry code's three bound volumes stacked at his elbow, spines toward him, and the four bound rulings from the press beside them. Karis took the near side, with her copy of the instrument open and her notebook open beside it and her pens in a row along the table's edge like a surgeon's knives. Her assistant sat at her elbow writing whatever she was told until the tenth bell. Just before she was sent to bed she put up her hand, as if the long table were a lecture hall, and asked the registrar a question.

"Why does a code need a word like *discretion* at all? Isn't the whole point of a code that nobody gets to choose?"

Bracken considered her over his spectacles for long enough that she went pink.

"Because the men who write codes can't see every case coming," he said, "and they know they can't. *Discretion* is the room they leave for the cases they couldn't see. Most nights it's the kindest word in the book." He looked down at the instrument. "It depends who's standing in the room."

Karis wrote something in the margin of her own copy. Then she sent the girl to bed, and the girl went protesting in a whisper all the way to the door.

Cael had the foot of the table and the observation notebook. Karis had asked him to stay. She wanted the person the paper described sitting in the room while it was read, she said, so that she could look up from a clause and check it against him.

The fourth chair was the counsel's.

She had come up the bluff road at the ninth bell in a hired chaise from Ostrand, with one bag, and Cael had never seen her before. The inspection had brought a counsel up the hill once, a woman who talked by every account from the bridge to the gate and signed for her chests with a face like a cat in a bath; she had been the Compact's. This was the house's own. In eleven years nothing she had advised the house had ever carried her name; that was her price, and the house had paid it gladly. People on the bluff called her counsel and nothing else.

She was small and grey, with very straight shoulders, and she read with a short steel rule of the kind a joiner uses, laid under each line so that her eye could not run ahead. She did not talk while she read. When she talked, it was in short sentences with nothing left over at their ends.

Withrow did not sit at all. She was in and out all evening on the stair from her office, and each time she came down she left something on the table, a pot of tea once, a folder once, and went back up without a word. Near the tenth bell she came down with a small book in brown cloth.

It was a registry book; Cael knew the size and the colour. Every registrar on the continent had been issued one at the start of his service, the procedures volume of the code in a pocket copy for carrying on circuit. Bracken had one in his coat. Withrow's had been carried further.

She opened it where it fell open, near the middle, and laid it beside Bracken's great third volume at the same page.

The page was chapter fourteen, and its margins were full: dates and the names of districts, in pencil and in ink, in a hand that was hers and not quite hers, younger and quicker. One clause had been underlined twice. Cael counted the lines before he read the words. The upper was black and thin and dead straight, drawn against a rule. The lower was brown ink faded to rust, and it wandered, and beside it in the margin stood a bracket with nothing written inside it.

"It was a kind word once," said Withrow, from the table's head. She had seen him counting. "Discretion. When I was new I took it for leave to be merciful."

She came down the table and laid a fingertip in the margin, beside the line. It did not tremble. Cael had expected it to.

"*At its discretion,*" she read. "Part two. *Suspension of practitioner status pending review, such review to be initiated by the registry at its discretion.*" She tapped the black line. "My second year at the registry. With a rule. Very neat." She moved the fingertip to the brown one. "Nine years later, in a coach on a bad road home from a district I'd rather not name, with no rule and not much light. By then I'd seen what it does."

"What does it do?" said Karis.

"Nothing. That's what it does." Withrow straightened. "It names nobody who must begin the review, and no day by which it must be begun. So nobody begins it." She looked at the faded bracket. "A penalty is a thing done to you, and then it is over. This is a place you are put, and the door has no one assigned to open it." She looked up the table. "Registrar. Is there a date anywhere in that chapter? A limit of days? Anything at all that runs out?"

Bracken went down the chapter a line at a time with his finger under the print, and nobody hurried him.

"Not one," he said.

Withrow closed the brown book without another word, put it under her arm, and went back up the stair at her ordinary pace.

Cael sat with it a while before he did anything else. For two years his whole place on the bluff had lived because somebody kept writing it down: a stamp, a renewal, a line in the enrollment book in Bracken's upright hand, every one of them a thing an officer was obliged to do on a day the code named. The provision was alive because it had dates in it. Part two had none. It was the same machine run backward, a thing that stayed done because nobody was ever obliged to undo it. He realized he had never once thought of a date as a kindness, and that it was the kindest thing on any form he had ever signed.

---

Cael had counted the instrument at the start of the night, while the others were still arranging their papers.

He began with the three schedules at the back, because they were the longest things in it. Every one ran past a page, and the paragraphs in front of them were short, a few lines each; only the ninth ran long. Then he went through every leaf with a pencil and a stroke in the notebook's margin for each word he was watching, and went through it twice, because a count done once is a guess.

*Discretion*, nine times. *Evidence*, three, and two of those three inside the phrase *no evidence is required to be adduced*. *Fraud*, once, in the second paragraph, in a sentence that existed only to say there was none. Eight citations of the code by chapter and section; the convening's own minute twice, by date; and nothing else. No ruling. No precedent. No witness.

Then he counted what it was made of. Nineteen leaves of heavy cream paper, written on one side, with the registry's mark pressed into each corner. Thirty-seven numbered paragraphs. On the first leaf it named him as the registry always did, *Caelen Hesk-ward*, a name nobody who knew him had ever used.

Schedule two was a list of himself, and he read it slowest. It was laid out in the grammar of declarations, as every practitioner's file on the continent was, with a heading for Path, a heading for tier and a heading for rank. Under Path stood the one bracketed word the frame at Denvash had given him. Under tier, a ruled line with nothing above it. Under rank, the same. It was a form built to hold an answer, holding the shape of a question instead.

The file's standing entries ran down beneath, in order: his Kindling at Denvash, the Greyvane hearing and its finding, the provision with the stamp and its renewals, the tournament's entry with all eleven of its words. And between the hearing and the provision, given its full title as if it were the most ordinary line on the page, sat the one he had known was on his file since the inspection year and had never once seen written down.

*Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4.*

No paragraph leaned on it or so much as named it. It sat in its schedule like a stone in a field that the plough has been driven round. He looked at it a long moment and went on, because it was not what the night was for, and Karis was watching him across the lamps, and he did not want her to have to decide whether to ask.

At the foot of the last leaf, above the seal's impression, in type no larger than the rest, stood the attestation. *Given under the Compact's hand.* Under it, smaller: *For the petitioner, the procurator of record*, and a name.

Bracken did not know it. He said so when Cael passed him the leaf. He knew every procurator who had stood before a registry seat in this district in thirteen years, and most in the two districts beside it.

"Which is information," he said, and handed it back.

Cael ruled a line under his column. A man building a case writes *evidence* on every page, because one day he will be standing in front of a seat with his evidence in his hands. This man had written *discretion*.

*They don't expect to prove anything,* he wrote under the line. *They expect to be agreed with.* Then he passed the column down to Karis. She read it, and laid it with her notes without a word, and he understood that he had done a piece of the night's real work.

The counsel began at the end.

She turned the instrument face down, turned it over again from the back, and laid her rule under the first line of the thirty-seventh paragraph. Then she climbed. Thirty-six, thirty-five, the rule moving up the page a line at a time like a man going up a ladder he does not trust, and twice she stopped and made a note. When she reached the first leaf she closed the paper and set the rule across it.

"Why backward?" said Cael.

She looked at him down the table as though he had asked her fee, and seemed to decide he could have this one free.

"The front of a paper is the story the writer tells you so you'll agree with him," she said. "The back is what he wants. If I read his story first, I've half agreed before I find out what for. So I find out what for." She tapped the rule. "Then I read the story and watch him build it."

Bracken had laid the four bound rulings in a row while she spoke.

They read them before midnight, Bracken reading the headings and Karis the findings, the counsel listening with her eyes shut and her rule across her knees.

The first was the longest. A river house far to the south had enrolled a ferryman's daughter whose station had taken a year to settle on what her Path was, and a richer house upriver had gone to law to say that a girl with no classification on her papers had no business being taught while she waited for one. The finding was a dozen lines. The clause said what a house might do; the house had done it; the girl stayed until the station read her, and after.

Cael found he had stopped writing.

He had known about the clause for two years and about the rulings in Bracken's press for nearly as long, and it had never once occurred to him that there were people in them. A girl in some hall in the south, a long time ago, waiting a year for a frame to make up its mind while two houses argued over whether she could be taught in the meantime. He wondered whether she had kept a book, and what had become of her, and knew nobody had written it down, because findings had no room for afterward.

"Her name's on it?" he said.

"Her initials, and the house's." Karis read them out. They meant nothing to anybody at the table. He wrote them at the edge of the page anyway, small.

The other three went quickly: a mason's guild that had put an apprentice into its school by demonstration and been challenged on the form of its panel, and two guild cases so old that the paper had gone brown and soft and the parties had worn down to the names of their trades. All four sustained. Bracken closed each cover as Karis finished and laid it back in its row.

"Every one of them went at the clause," said the counsel, opening her eyes, "and the clause threw every one of them out. Greyvane went at it too, by another road, and the same thing happened." She picked up her rule. "Whoever drafted tonight's paper has read that shelf."

"So they didn't go at it," said Karis.

"So they didn't go at it." The counsel turned the instrument to its first leaf. "And they told no story at the front. Four sentences of recital, on a paper this heavy. I'd expect forty. The front is where a drafter usually gives something away without noticing, and in twenty-six years I've won most of what I've won there." She laid the rule down. "This one knew somebody would read him from the back. He left me nothing to find."

---

Karis took the first finding standing, as she took anything that was going to be minuted, with the instrument's second paragraph under one hand and the Greyvane transcript open under the other.

"No fraud," she said. "Listen." She read it aloud, the same sentence Withrow had read standing that morning, the one that alleged no fraud at all. She lifted her hand from the transcript. "Last year the broadsides sold a story a week about a trick: a hidden Path, a bought clerk, a staged hearing. Every one of those stories ends at a door Greyvane locked on the record, and this paper hasn't so much as knocked on one. They've read the record. They know which doors are locked." She sat. "That's the first finding. They spend nothing on ground they could lose."

The counsel took the second, in the voice of somebody reading out tomorrow's weather.

"Paragraph six." The rule went under it. "*The provision by which the enrollment was made is acknowledged to be valid, and to have survived in law the standardization directive. Neither is put in issue.*" She lifted the rule. "I came up this hill expecting to spend the year proving exactly that. Whether the clause is still law. Whether the directive killed it. Whether a house may still enroll by demonstration on a clause out of the founding text. They've signed it for me, under their own seal, in the sixth paragraph of their first paper." She laid the rule flat on the leaf. "Nobody has ever done that to me before."

"Is that good?" said Cael.

The counsel looked at him.

"When a man gives you the field," she said, "look at what he has kept for himself."

Karis had already turned the page.

He watched her read the seventh paragraph. Her finger went along the first line and the second and stopped, and her whole body went still around it. He knew that stillness; he had seen it over a shelf at Greyvane, and over the founding articles upstairs, and over a charter at Norhold, and every time it had meant that she had found the thing a page was built to hide. This time he did not count it. He watched her finger, and waited for it to move.

---

She did not read it out. She turned the instrument round on the table so that it faced the rest of them, put her finger on the paragraph, and took the finger away.

The counsel laid her rule under it and read it aloud.

*7. The petitioner notes, and adopts as its own, the advisory entered in the prior year by an evaluator of the Compact upon the fielding clause of the tournament charter, which reads in full: "The clause says what it says. The enrollment is lawful on its face and adjudicated at public record. If the Compact wishes practitioners of this kind not to compete, it should write different rules — not misread the ones it has."*

Nobody spoke.

"*The clause says what it says,*" said Karis at last. "I said that. On the bench under the thorn at the first bend, the morning the entry went down the hill. *It says what it says all the same.* I said it to you, Cael." She laid her palms on the wood, one either side of the paper. "And somebody said it to them, in writing, a year ago. The Archmarshal. Vastin. You told us at Norhold there'd been three sentences of advisory, and none of us ever saw them. There they are."

"And they've taken his advice," said the counsel.

"They've taken his advice." Karis's face changed then.

Cael had watched that face for three years. He had seen it angry and amused and bored and cold, and once or twice, very late, tired enough to be kind, and at Norhold in the moment the stoppage was called. He had never seen it like this. She was looking at a paragraph on cream paper as if the paper had moved.

"He told them how," she said. "Write different rules, he said, don't misread the old ones. So here are the new ones, written, and when the seat asks them by what right, they'll point at him. The honest man's own sentence, for a warrant." She took her hands off the table. "Write it down. This is where I was frightened. The seventh paragraph. Not the fourteenth chapter, not whatever's coming. This."

"Written," said the counsel.

She had a page at her elbow now that had not been there when the night began, headed in a small exact hand: *Halcenvane: the Compact's petition. Working minute. Privileged.* She wrote Karis's sentence on it word for word, with the time by the hall's clock, and blotted it.

Cael put it in the observation notebook as well, since word for word was the only way that notebook took anything. Then he wrote the other thing under it. It had come while Karis was speaking, plainly, like a sum totting itself up at the foot of a column.

*Vastin, in the room full of clocks: the people who want this to stop are not going to use official channels much longer. I have never held a more official thing than this paper. Nineteen leaves, a seal, an escort at a walk, a form of joint service I had never seen. So either he was wrong about the channels, or this is the last thing they'll send through one.*

*And the warrant on it is his. He warned me in a room where nothing was written down. They answered in a room where everything is, and signed it with his name.*

He looked at the two lines for a while.

He found he was not frightened. Not yet. He was something nearer cold. It was the feeling he had had on the morning of his Kindling at Denvash, standing in a frame that had nothing to say about him and was perfectly calm about it. The fright would come later, he knew, at the window, in the dark, where there was time for it; not here, where there was work.

He looked up, and Karis was looking at him across the lamps.

"Go on," he said. "There's more of it. You said so."

"There's a great deal more of it," said Karis, and turned the instrument back round to face herself, and found the ninth paragraph with her finger. "And none of it gets any better."

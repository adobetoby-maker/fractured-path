# Chapter 12 — Admit

The east hall had never held a crowd before, and on the thirtieth morning of the year it held one.

Nobody had announced the trial beyond a line on Rooke's own board, chalked the evening before in his square capitals: *PROGRAM TRIAL. SECOND-DAY, SECOND BELL. LIRA / FORCE THIRD-YEAR. RATED.* That was all. But the house had read it, and the house knew what *rated* meant on that floor. It meant the first bout ever fought on the program's painted boards under the program's own seal, with a figure at the end of it that would go into the program's own book. By the half-bell the gallery was full along both sides. By the second bell the porters had opened the shutters on the high windows to let the heat out, and the light came down onto the white crosses in long bright bars, and there were people standing on the gallery stair.

He had found Lira an hour before it, sitting on the east hall's outer step in the grey light with her back against the doorpost and a roll of tape in her lap, doing her wrists. She did them slowly, as she never did on an ordinary morning, a turn at a time, pressing each turn flat with her thumb before the next went over it.

"Brom's way," she said, when she saw him look. "It takes longer. It gives you something to do with your hands that isn't shaking." She tore the tape with her teeth. "They're not shaking. I'm just telling you why it works."

He sat down on the step below her.

"You asked me once not to count your bursts," he said.

"I did. Ages ago." She started the other wrist. "I still don't want you to. I'll know how many. I'll be the one paying for them." She pressed a turn flat. "Count his."

"His what?"

"Anything. His stamps. His settles. How long he laughs. You'll see things from the rail I won't see from the floor, and I won't be able to ask you about them till it's over, and I want them all, in order, when I do." She finished the wrist and flexed the hand, and looked at it as if it belonged to somebody she was fond of. "Karis will count my bursts whether I like it or not. That's her business. Yours is him."

Down the walk the Force came round the corner of the range with two of his cohort, all three of them laughing at something, and saw her on the step, and lifted a hand. She lifted one back. Then she stood, and went in to the floor without another word, and Cael went up the gallery stair to find the rail.

Cael had the rail at the north end, where he had stood on the second morning of the year to watch the red book. Brom stood on his left with his arms folded. Karis stood on his right with her notebook shut and her pen in her hand. Ephram was three steps down the gallery stair with the captain's file under his arm, where he could see the whole floor and be seen by the whole cohort, which Cael supposed was the point. The girl from the reading room's outer table had found a place against the far wall of the gallery, with her small notebook open.

Below them on the floor stood the rating table: a trestle under the north windows with three chairs behind it. The clerk with the red drill-book sat at one end. Rooke sat in the middle with his board and his pencil. And at the other end, with a ruled card in front of him and his hands folded on the table like a man waiting outside a shop, sat the Stone from the river academy, who had asked to come and teach the girl who beat him and was now going to put a figure on her.

Rooke stood up at the second bell and said the rule, once, in a voice that carried to the back of the gallery without being raised.

"Program trial. My rule, not the region's." He held up his board as if the rule were written on it, which it was not. "Four exchanges. Each one, the table gives a flag to whoever took it, or to nobody if nobody did. Most flags takes the trial. Three bursts a bout for the program's entrant, as on every day she's ever stood on this floor. Past three, the table still gives the exchange to whoever takes it, and the card pays for every burst over. The other fighter, what his own Path allows. Level after four, the table rates them both, and the higher figure takes it." He sat down. "The figure goes in this program's book. Not the registry's. Not yet."

Lira stood at the south chalk with her wrists taped and her left arm carried a little close, out of habit. Across forty feet of painted boards stood the Force.

---

He was a big young man, broad in the back and thick through the thighs, and he stood at his chalk with his feet planted and his hands loose and a pleasant expression, the expression of somebody who has been looking forward to a thing and has decided to enjoy it. He had been drilling beside Lira every morning since the recess. He knew the four-count; he had run it himself, badly, for the practice of seeing it. He had laughed once on this floor when she lied to him on the second count and he went the wrong way. He was Iron Rank Seven, six ranks above her on the porter's wall, and he was the best thing the cohort had built in a straight line since Ephram.

Cael watched his knees.

From the rail there was nothing else to watch. A Force fighter's shove went on contact, and this one's contact was the floor. He stamped with his front foot, and a beat later the boards ahead of the stamp, out to a stride, jumped, as a table jumps when somebody slaps it from beneath, and went on jumping for a count or two before they lay down. Then the shove came back up into him along the line it had gone out by, and his knees gave and his hips dropped a finger's breadth while he took it. That was the settle. Cael could see it from the rail and hear the boards answer it. It was the bill for the last stamp, paid before the next.

"Exchange," said the clerk.

The Force did what Force fighters did. He did not rush. He stamped, and the boards in front of him jumped, and he settled, and stepped up onto them as they lay down again, and stamped once more a stride further on. Stamp, settle, step. He came forward behind a floor that would not hold still.

Lira ran straight at him.

It was the worst thing she could have done, and the whole gallery knew it. She did not run the four-count. She did not lie. She went at him in a plain hard line, burst one off the left hip, the old burst, the one she had brought out of a barn at fourteen and that every fighter on the hill had seen a hundred times. The burst carried her twenty feet and put her down inside his stride.

He stamped as she landed. The boards under her jumped a beat after her heel came down, and her landing went out from under her sideways. She caught herself. She had to; she was on a floor that was trying to put her on her back. And while she caught herself the Force laid his palm on her shoulder, square and plain, and the shove came after the touch, the way it always came after with a Force, and Lira went back a stride and a half and sat down on the painted boards.

The Stone lifted his flag. Red. The Force's.

The gallery groaned.

Rooke wrote nothing at all.

Lira got up. She dusted her palms on her thighs, and walked back to her chalk, and stood there with her hands at her sides and her eyes on the Force's knees. Her lips were moving very slightly.

"She gave it," said Brom quietly.

"Yes."

"On purpose."

"Yes." Cael had seen it from the first stride. Lira did not run straight at a Force fighter. She had not done it since the mill town. "She was counting him."

"Counting what?"

"His settle." He watched her lips. "She needed to watch him stamp and pay for it, start to finish, with nothing in his way. So she gave him nothing in his way."

Karis, on his other side, had opened her notebook. She wrote one line and showed it to him without a word. *First flag bought. Price: one flag on the record, in front of the table.*

"Exchange," said the clerk.

The Force stamped. Settled. Stepped. And Lira, at her chalk, began the four-count.

The first step was an ordinary step, any fighter's, and the Force watched it come and stamped again. The second step was the lie. She put her weight on her front foot and left it there one count too long, the long lie the Stone had taught her, so that anybody watching for the burst would see it coming and set himself to meet it. The Force saw it coming. He stamped exactly where her burst would land if it came off that front foot, a good hard stamp, and Cael heard the boards jump there a beat later and thought it was the best the man had done all morning.

The third count, nothing happened.

It was the hardest thing in the world to watch. Lira stood on the painted floor with one foot forward and one back and did nothing at all, for a whole count, while a Rank Seven Force stood twenty feet away with the boards still jumping in front of him and his knees bent, waiting. The gallery made a small sound. Somebody laughed, uncertainly. The Force laughed too, from surprise, the way he always did, a short bark.

Then his knees gave, and his hips dropped.

Cael saw it. Lira saw it a hair before he did. The settle was on the man's own fourth count, his own rhythm, the same rhythm he had paid to in the first exchange with nothing in his way. He had to take his own shove back, whether she had given him anything to shove at or not.

And her fourth count was the same beat as his.

She burst off the back foot. Not the front foot she had lied with, which stood on boards that had jumped a moment ago; the back one, which had never left wood he had touched. She burst at the angle the first three counts had sworn she would never take, low and to the right, past the place he had stamped, and came down on still boards two strides from his left hip with her landing beat holding her steady. The Force was still in his settle, knees bent, taking his own push back, with nothing to put it into.

She touched his shoulder. Lightly. Two fingers.

The Stone lifted his flag. Blue.

The noise in the gallery was not cheering, not at first. It was the sound of a great many people realizing at the same moment that the story they had been watching was a different story. Then it was cheering. Rooke, at the table's middle, put his pencil to his board and wrote one word, and Cael could not see what it was and did not need to.

"Two bursts," said Karis, writing. "Two exchanges. One flag each."

The Force stood a moment looking at the place on the boards where he had stamped for a girl who was not there. Then he shook his head, grinning, and went back to his chalk.

---

He had learned. That was what made him the best the cohort had built. In the third exchange he did not stamp to his own count any more.

He stamped faster, shorter, left and right and centre as he came, two counts apart instead of four, none of them as hard as before but each one coming before the last had lain down, so that the boards a stride ahead of him were jumping across the whole width of his reach. He came on behind them. Within a dozen heartbeats there was no still wood between Lira and him anywhere she could reach in a step.

Cael watched her look for it and not find it.

She tried the four-count once, and the lie on the second count put her front foot on boards that had been still a moment earlier and were jumping now, and her foot went sideways, and she went with it. She did not burst. She scrambled, two ugly running steps and a hop, out of the jumping boards on her own legs, and came up near the east wall beside the striking frames on their thick springs. He came after her. Not fast; Force fighters were never fast. Stamp, settle, step, stamp, a stride at a time, the floor ahead of him jumping like water coming up a beach.

She went back. A stride. Another. The frames were a pace behind her heel.

"She's out of floor," said Brom.

"She's not," said Cael.

She had done it on the honest floor three nights before, at the end of a lane he laid for her, with Brom standing at the end of the line. She had learned it standing in dust that ran. *Lie with the foot you don't care about. Go on the fourth.*

She stood with her back nearly against the frames and put her left foot forward onto boards that were jumping. She let them. She let them shove her front foot a hand's breadth sideways and did not fight them, the way a fighter lets a push go by. She kept her right foot behind her on the last strip of still wood on the east side of the hall. And she stood there for the empty third count, one foot moving and one foot still, while the Force came up for the stamp that would take the last of her still wood away.

He settled on the count. Even now, stamping fast, stamping in a fan, the man still had to take his push back, and a fighter cannot pay for one stamp and throw the next at the same moment. Two counts now, not four. But a count all the same.

She went on her fourth.

Burst three. Off the right foot, the still one, straight along the line of the frames, not toward him at all but past him, so close to the frames that the nearest post rang as her shoulder brushed it. She came down eight feet on, behind the end of his fan, on boards he had not stamped because every stamp of his had been in front of him. The Force was turning, and a man turning in his own settle turns slowly. He was a beat late.

She touched his back between the shoulders.

Blue.

"Three bursts," said Karis. "Her whole bout." Her pen kept moving. "One exchange to go."

Cael knew what the fourth exchange would cost before the clerk said the word.

The Force had learned twice now. He was not going to give her a settle to burst into. He was not going to chase her into a corner where she could go past him along a wall. When the clerk said *exchange* he did not come forward at all. He stamped where he stood, to the left, and to the right, and in front, and behind, a stride out on each side, and as each patch of boards lay down he stamped it awake again, turning slowly in the middle, until he stood in a ring of floor that never stopped jumping. It was like a well with the water always moving at its edges. He stood on the still wood in the middle of it and waited for her to come.

There was nowhere near him to land. To touch him she would have to come down on boards that were moving, and anything that landed there would slide.

Rooke's rule was three bursts. She had spent three.

Lira stood at her chalk and looked at the ring of jumping floor for a long moment. Her lips were not moving now. She was not counting him; there was no count left to find. Cael watched her decide, and saw her know exactly what it would cost.

She went.

Burst four, off the left hip, in one long low flight to the edge of the ring, the old burst, the barn's burst, the strongest thing she had ever owned. She came down on jumping boards. They moved. She let them. She let them slide her landing a hand's breadth and turned the slide into the start of the next thing, as she had learned in the dust on the honest floor, and burst again off the moving boards before they could take her any further.

Burst five.

It carried her over the last stride of the ring and onto the still wood at its middle, a stride from the Force, so close that he could not stamp between them, because there was no floor between them to stamp. He was turning to stamp behind him. He had built a well to stand in, and she had come over its edge.

She did not hit him. She put her taped palm flat on his breastbone and held it there.

Blue.

The gallery came apart.

---

"Three flags to one," said the clerk into the noise, and wrote it in the red book.

The Stone wrote on his card. He wrote for a long time, longer than a single figure needed. Then he slid the card along the trestle to Rooke, and Rooke read it, and stood up with it in his hand, and the gallery fell quiet to hear it, because nobody on the hill had ever heard a program figure read out on that floor before.

"Execution, ten. Effect, ten." Rooke did not look up. "Control, seven. Twenty-seven."

He put the card down on the trestle.

"Ten is a good ordinary day at the heart of Silver. She was there in two columns." His voice did not change at all. "The seven is the fourth burst and the fifth. Up to three, a fighter owns her bout. Past three she rents it, and the card collects the rent." He looked at the clerk. "Enter it."

Twenty-seven. Cael had her figures in his notebook since the wool town, every one: twenty-five, twenty-six, twenty-seven at her very best last year, the top of what the board called strong Iron. Twenty-eight was the floor of the Silver bracket. She had stood a point short of it before. She stood a point short of it now. But this time the point had a name and a place, and Rooke had just said both aloud in front of the whole house.

Lira walked to the middle of the floor and shook the Force's hand. Her collar was dark through and her chest was going like a bellows, and she was standing on her right leg more than her left.

Rooke came down from the table with his board under his arm. He did not call her over. He went to her where she stood at the edge of the painted lines, and went down on one knee on the boards in front of her, and put his thumb on the outside of her left thigh just below the hip, and pressed.

She hissed through her teeth.

He moved the thumb down a hand's breadth, and pressed again, and again, the way a farrier goes down a horse's leg, and she hissed at each one until he reached the knee, and there she did not.

"From the hip to above the knee, down the outside," said Rooke, to his board, and stood up. "That's four and five. Three never goes there." He wrote. "Two days at half work. No frames. No bursts at all until the leg is quiet, and you'll tell me when it's quiet, and I'll decide whether to believe you." He tucked the pencil behind his ear. "At the meet you don't fight one bout in a morning. You fight one, and eat, and fight again. A fifth burst before noon is a leg that won't carry you after it."

"Yes, Coach."

He went back to the table without another word, and sat, and began writing in the red book in the clerk's place, as if the trial had happened a long time ago.

Ephram came down the gallery stair against the stream of people going up it to leave, with the captain's file under his arm and a pencil behind his ear, and stopped beside Cael at the rail's end.

"I've started her a page," he said. He opened the file a little way, enough to show a sheet folded in four inside it, new, with only three lines on it so far. "I keep one on everybody. You know that. Hers was a line a month. It's going to be longer now."

"What's on it?"

"Today." Ephram closed the file. "Four exchanges, and which ones she bought, and which ones she paid for." He looked down at the floor, where the Force was rolling his wraps at the far chalk. "And his. I've started him a page too. He'll be worth watching after this. Men who get beaten like that either stop or start."

He went off toward the south door to tell the cohort where to stand, and Cael heard his voice go up behind him in the yard, patient and carrying, explaining to somebody that a captain did not have to know why a thing had happened in order to know that it had.

On his way to the south door the Force stopped at the foot of the gallery stair, kit on his shoulder, wraps hanging out of his coat pocket. Lira was on the bottom step by then, unwinding her tapes.

"Two counts was a mistake," he said. "I'll stamp to no count at all by the month's end. You'll see."

"Then I'll have to find something else to count."

He laughed, the short bark, surprised at himself for laughing. Then he shifted the kit on his shoulder.

"The first one. You gave me that."

"Yes."

He nodded, a short honest nod, the way a man nods to somebody who has told him where he left his hat, and went out of the south door. Cael watched him go and knew that by Rooke's next morning drill the man would be stamping to a rhythm nobody on the hill could hear, and that the whole cohort would be the better for having had him beaten.

Lira came up the stair to the rail.

She had her tapes balled in one fist and the other hand pressed flat against the outside of her thigh where Rooke's thumb had been, and her face had the look Cael had watched on it for two months of mornings and never had words for. She stood at the rail beside him and looked down at the painted floor. The trestle. The clerk back at his red book. The Stone, folding his card in half along a crease he made with his thumbnail, and putting it inside his coat.

"He came from the river to teach me a count," she said. "A man I beat. He wrote and asked." She turned the balled tape in her fingers. "There was a time I'd have thrown that back in his face and called it pity, and walked out to the yard to prove I didn't need it." She watched the Stone go out. "I'm better now. I can admit I want it."

Nobody said anything. Brom unfolded his arms. Karis, on Cael's other side, closed her notebook very quietly.

Lira looked at the floor a moment longer, and then went down the stair to find her water, walking carefully on the left leg, and did not look back to see how it had been received.

Halfway across the court in the heat, behind the crowd going out, Cael had the observation notebook open on his palm and was writing as he walked, in the working pages at the front where the day's figures went. He had got as far as Lira's name.

Karis reached across without breaking step, closed the notebook on his pencil, turned it over, and opened it again from the back.

"There," she said. "With the things that don't get crossed out. Her word was *admit*."

He stopped on the flags and wrote it there: the date, and the one word under Lira's name.

At supper Lira asked him, without looking up from her plate, where he had put it.

"The back."

She nodded once and went on eating, and that was all either of them ever said about it.

---

That same week two envelopes came for Brom.

The first came on the thirty-first day, to Bracken's counter, in the district seat's grey stock with the district's own small seal, and Bracken carried it across to the refectory himself at the noon meal. He did not lay it on the table this time. He stood at the end of the middle table and read it aloud, all of it, which he had never done with anybody's paper in that room.

"*Review for advancement of tier. The panel convened under this office's docket of the fourth day is confirmed: three assessors and a sigil frame, at the district seat. The practitioner's record is received and entered as it stands, the title included. Sitting: the last Fifth-day of the academic year.*" He turned the sheet to the light. "And at the foot. *Outcome anticipated: advancement, per title.*"

He read the last word again, by itself. "*Anticipated.*" Then he turned the sheet round and laid it in front of Karis, not Brom, with his finger beside the foot.

"You've read more dockets than anybody at this table but me," he said. "Have you ever seen that on one?"

Karis looked. "Never."

"Nor have I, and every docket that came up this hill in thirteen years has crossed my counter." He took the sheet back. "A docket gives the day and the room and the panel. Nobody lets it guess at the end. Some district clerk got tired of guessing quietly and wrote his guess where we could all see it." He looked at the word over his spectacles as if it might go away. "I'd like to shake his hand. I'd also like to have him moved. I'll be writing to the district." He held the sheet out across the table to Brom. "Well. They've as good as painted it on the door."

Brom wiped his hands on his napkin and took the sheet, and read it, and folded it twice along the creases the district clerk had put in it. Then he unbuttoned his coat and put it in the left inside pocket, with the first docket and the summons and the Velmere letters, and buttoned the coat again, and pressed the buttons down one after another with his palm.

"Thank you, Registrar."

Bracken's eyes rested on the buttons. He said nothing about where the paper had gone, and went back up the refectory between the long tables.

The second envelope came up with the evening post. Cael saw it on the post-room shelf when he went for his own letters, a square cream envelope with a dark red seal pressed with the bar and the ring, and he knew the seal. Brom took it from the shelf at the eighth bell. He did not open it in the post room or the court. He took it upstairs and shut his door.

Late that night, crossing the court from the north wall, Cael looked up at the residence front, because he always did. Brom's window was lit, and lit wrong. The lamp that had stood on the sill side of the table for two years was over on the far wall now, on the washstand, so that the light fell everywhere in the room except on the table.

Lira was sitting on the stair in the dark when he came in, with her bad leg out straight along the step.

"It's on the table," she said quietly. "Squared on top of the docket. Edges level. Not opened."

"You went in?"

"His door doesn't latch unless you lean on it." She looked at her own hands. "I didn't lean on it. I wanted to." She was quiet a moment. "When he opens it, nobody asks. Not what it said. Not whether he's all right. Tell Karis. I'll tell her too, so she hears it twice."

Karis heard it twice. She did not say anything about it to either of them. But from the next evening on, the annex door stood open every night until the last bell, with the lamp lit inside and Karis at the long table with her back to the stair, and anybody coming up could have walked in and sat down without knocking. Nobody remarked on it. Brom went past it twice the next evening on his way to the stair and did not go in.

On the third evening he opened the letter.

Nobody saw him do it. Nobody at the table learned what it said, because he did not tell them, then or afterward. What they saw was his face, when he came down to supper an hour later than usual and sat in his place and pulled the bread toward him.

It was the face of a man being offered his whole ledger back.

Cael looked at it once, as long as a man looks at a letter that is not addressed to him, and then he looked at his bread.

Nobody asked. Lira passed Brom the beans. Brom took some, and passed them on to Seln, who took some, and passed them on.

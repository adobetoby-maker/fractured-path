# Chapter 32 — Road Dust

The minute was exactly as long as Norhold's, and nothing like it.

At Norhold the wrong thing had answered. Here nothing answered at all. He knelt in the dust and called his own legs, out of habit, to get him up, and the calling went out of him and found nobody at home. The Wind did not lift its head. The read lay where he had left it. Everything he carried had turned, all together and very politely, to look at the new thing standing in the doorway, and none of them was listening to him.

It did not hurt. It was like being in a house where every clock has stopped at once.

Brom stood over him with his left arm hanging and his right hand open at his side, and did not say anything, and did not touch him. He stood the way he had stood in the channel. Anybody coming down the road would have had to come through him first. Nobody came. The wind went along the cut and the horses blew and stamped and the coachman talked to them. Somewhere down the bank the river roared through the narrows.

Then, one by one, the things he carried turned round again and looked at him.

The read came first, as it always did. Then the Wind, a stir low in the belly. Then the rest. He called his legs and they came. He got one foot under him, and then the other, and Brom put out his right hand and Cael took it and came up off the chalk onto his feet.

"That's the minute," said Brom.

"That's the minute."

Brom looked at him a moment longer to be sure. Then he let go and went to see to Lira.

He sat down on the lip of the south bank above the hollow, where she had come out of the shadow, because his legs had decided that for him. The chalk was cold through his clothes. Below him on the river side the hill fell away through hawthorn and willow to the brown water, and he could see a long way down the valley toward the city, the flat land shining with floodwater in long strips, and a smudge of smoke on the far edge of it that was Ostrand.

It had been waiting for him since he held still on his knees, as it always waited. It came to the place behind his eyes that belonged to nothing else, and stayed there, patient as a clerk, until he had a moment to look. He looked now.

He read it through twice. Then he counted, because he could not help it.

Eight.

*Silver*, it said. It was the first notice that had ever said Silver. The guess on the leaf had been right; he had priced it at the weight it came at. *Anchor-adjacent*, it said, and he thought of Seln at the long table with his eyes shut, saying the name, and Karis counting the words off on her fingers, keeping them straight. *Fixed-point binding.* That was the thing against his coat, and the thing he had let go of. *Lattice-perception.* That was the grey net he had seen laid down the whole cut, which he could not see now at all. *Contact-to-short range.* Close to her, cheap. Further off, at a price he had watched her pay, half a second at a time.

And at the top, where a name went, *unnamed*.

Every other notice he had ever read had come out of somebody he could have walked up to afterward. Lira, Brom, a Bronze at Ardenmere, Reydan, Karis, Seln on a stair, and Daeva on the stone, holding out her hand. He could have found every one of them, and some of them he had, and thanked them, or not thanked them, or simply known where they were. He had a line in the old volume for every one of those people.

This one had come out of a band of skin between a hood and a scarf, and a pair of grey eyes, and two loose hands, and it had gone up a rope over the top of the north bank and into the grass. He would never know her name. Nobody would ever write to him that she had been posted, or moved, or retired to a garden. Somebody somewhere had a posting roll with her on it, and that somebody had sent her to this road with no paper so that there would be nothing for anybody to find.

He had taken a piece of the thing they sent. That was the plain fact of it, and he sat with it on the chalk a while.

---

The bills came in round him while he sat there.

Brom was on his knees in the dust at the edge of the north ditch with Lira's foot in his lap. He had the kit bag open beside him, the one Lira had laughed at the night before, and he was strapping the ankle one-handed, with the linen held in his teeth. His left arm hung. Every time he needed to pull the strapping tight he did it with the right hand and his knee, and he did not once ask anybody to help him. Lira lay back on her elbows and watched him do it, white to the mouth.

"Four," she said.

Nobody had asked her. She said it to the sky.

"Four bursts. On chalk. That's the road's whole free budget and not one over it. I counted." She shut her eyes. "The ankle wasn't the bursts. The ankle was the ditch. I came down on the edge of the camber where it falls off, and the edge went." She opened them again. "Write it down that way. I want it right."

"I'll write it that way," said Cael, from the bank.

"Good." She looked at Brom's arm. "And that?"

"Shoulder," said Brom, round the strapping. "Left. It's in. It was out for a heartbeat and it went back in by itself when he came off me." He tied the knot with his teeth and his right hand. "A week. It's had worse." Then he sat back on his heels and, with the same right hand, without thinking about it, reached across under his coat and felt the left inside pocket, where the sealed letter was. Cael watched his face as he found it there. Then Brom took the hand away and put it on Lira's strapped foot, flat, and left it there.

Karis had gone down the gully to the culvert. She came back up it with her right hand wrapped in her own wet handkerchief and held up in front of her like something she had been asked to carry and must not drop. The fingers that stuck out of the end of the wrapping were red to the second knuckle and already shining. She sat down on the parapet beside the milestone, which said seven, and took her notebook out of her coat with her left hand, and opened it on her knee, and wrote something with the pencil in her left hand, slowly, the letters going wide and childish.

Cael could read it from the bank. It was one word.

*Fourth.*

She had kept a count of her hand's ignition margin since Greyvane. Three times before, she had pushed her heat past where the skin could take it, and three times it had healed, and she had logged every one in this notebook in her small exact hand. The fourth was in her right hand, and it was enormous, and it was crooked. She looked at it for a moment and did not try to make it neater.

The coachman had got down off his box. He was walking the near horse up and down the road between the coach and the cart, slowly, with his hand on its neck, talking to it. The horse's foreleg had been held by a point for most of the fight. It was trembling. The coachman walked it, and talked, and did not look at any of them. Once, passing the bank where Cael sat, he said without stopping, "Twenty-two years I've driven this coach," in exactly the tone he used for the horses, and walked on.

Cael sat on the bank and looked at all of them.

They had come for him. Every one of the six had been there for him, and no one else. The hobbles were for him and the hood was for him and the wagon in the gully, wherever it was, had a door that was meant to shut on him. And the four people he had come here with had paid for every yard of the road. Lira had paid in an ankle, Brom in a shoulder, Karis in a hand she would have to write with for a week. Even the coachman's horse had paid, standing there trembling with the coachman's hand on its neck.

He had paid in an arm and some ribs. He had also been given a thing that a Silver had spent her life on. He did not think that came out even.

He did not know yet what to do with that. He put it away, carefully, with the other things, where he would be able to find it.

Seln came up out of the culvert with an iron pin in his hand.

Nobody saw him come. One moment the gully beyond the culvert was empty, a cleft of wet stone with the stream running white down it, and the next Seln was standing on the parapet beside Karis with water to his knees and his coat dark with it. He had a long iron pin in his right hand, the length of a man's forearm and thicker than a thumb, with a ring through the head of it.

He looked round the cut once, at all of them. At Lira's foot in Brom's lap. At Brom's arm. At Karis's wrapped hand and the word in her notebook. At the coachman walking the horse. At Cael on the bank, with the left sleeve of his coat torn open from the wrist to the elbow and the arm under it raw and white with chalk.

"There's a wagon in the gully," he said. "Closed. Barred at the back, with a bench inside and two rings in the floor. They'd tied the horses a little way up the stream, out of sight of the road." He weighed the pin in his hand. "They took the horses when they went. They didn't take the wagon. It won't go anywhere without this."

"The linchpin," said Brom.

"The off-side one." Seln looked at it as if it were a thing he had found on a beach. "I was under the wagon when they came back down the gully. They walked past it. Nobody looked under it. Nobody looks under a wagon." He turned and dropped the pin into the ditch on the north side, where it went into the mud without a sound and did not come up. "I didn't see anything up here. The bank was in the way. I heard it."

He did not ask what he had heard. He did not ask how it had ended. He looked at Cael on the bank for perhaps one breath longer than he looked at anything else.

"Ribs?" he said.

"Right side."

"Then sit there. Don't lift anything."

He went down the road to help the coachman with the near horse.

Cael watched him go. All morning, from the ferry landing to now, Seln had not been in a single place where anybody on the road could see him. He had not been in a single place where the team could see him. He had not been in a single place where Cael could see him either, and Cael had looked, once, during the third exchange, down the bank toward the willows, and found nothing but willows. The six had come to this road expecting five people and a coachman. They had counted four. The fifth had been underneath their wagon with his hand on its pin.

---

He took Hesk's book out of his coat.

The leather was warm from his body. There was a small mark on the cover, over the place where the book lay against his ribs, a faint dull patch in the grain as if a thumb had pressed there for a long time. He looked at it and knew what had made it. He did not try to rub it out.

He found the pencil and a clean leaf near the back and wrote it on the bank, with the chalk dust drying white on his hand and the wind turning the pages under his wrist.

*Eight. Taken from someone the system sent namelessly, to hold me namelessly. No paper. No seal. No office to write to, not even a false one this time. She stood in the road and did the best thing she knew how to do, and I took it off her, and she'll go home tonight to wherever they keep her and nobody will ask her how her day was. A hand and no face. I don't know what I owe someone I can't find. I don't know if owing is the word. Write it down anyway.*

He read it back.

Then, under it, smaller:

*The bills: Lira, the right ankle (the ditch, not the bursts; she wants it written that way). Brom, the left shoulder, out and in. Karis, the right hand, the fourth. Me, the left forearm, the right ribs. The coachman's near horse. Seln, nothing anyone saw. The leaf said Brom for the minute. Brom was there for the minute.*

He shut the book and put it back inside his coat, against his ribs, where it had been.

The coachman had the horses back in the traces. Brom lifted Lira into the coach in his one good arm as if she weighed nothing, and set her on the seat with her foot up on the record cases, and got in after her. Karis climbed in holding her hand up. Seln shut the door behind them.

Cael got up off the bank, slowly, and walked down to the coach while the coachman and Seln saw to the cart.

It took them a quarter of an hour. The wheel had not broken. It had been unpinned and laid down, and it went back on in two minutes, and then the coachman put his shoulder to the cart and ran it back off the crown into the gully mouth beside the culvert, where it stood beside nothing. Cael did not look down the gully. He knew what was down there. There was a wagon with a bench in it and two rings in its floor, and no pin. Seln climbed up onto the box beside the coachman and sat there in his wet coat, looking down the road toward the city, and Cael got in.

They drove out of the east end of the cut, over the culvert, past the milestone. Behind them the white dust settled on the road where it had been.

---

The first one came a mile short of the city, at the toll-bar.

The bar was a long white pole on a pivot across the road, where the river road came in off the flats to the west gate. The coachman stopped for the keeper to raise it. The keeper came out of his box with his hand out for the toll, and the bar went up, and swung, and came to rest against its post. And as it touched the post, something in Cael said, very quietly and with great courtesy, *There?*

He did not know at first what it was asking.

Then he did. It was asking whether he would like the bar fixed there, against its post, where it had come to rest. It would be no trouble, and it was a good place. The bar was resting against a surface, and the new thing in him knew exactly how to set a point against a surface and tell it to stay, and it was offering, as politely as a clerk at the elbow, to do so.

*No*, he thought.

The offer went away at once, without argument, the way a clerk goes away when you shake your head. The keeper took his toll and the coach went under the bar and on.

He sat back against the seat and listened to himself, and found it was not gone. It was quiet, and waiting, and it had found out that he could hear it.

The west gate. The long street of warehouses by the river, every cellar door open and men with buckets. The market street, steep and wet. The four steps of the wool exchange and the side door in its flank. The coachman took them round into the exchange's yard by the stable arch, so that they would not have to get down in front of the steps, and drew up under the wall, and set the brake.

Over the yard's far roof the exchange's clock stood at five minutes before the hour. The sitting was at the hour.

Nobody moved to open the door.

"We tell them," said Lira.

She was sitting with her strapped foot on the record cases and her back in the corner and her face still white, and she said it before anybody else could say anything. "Six people with hobbles and a wagon with rings in the floor tried to take him off a road this morning, two hours from here. The seat's sitting in there. Somebody in that room should write it down."

"Who?" said Brom.

"The clerk. The hood. Anybody."

"And then what?" Brom did not say it unkindly. "What does anybody in that room do with it? They write it down. Then what?"

Lira opened her mouth and shut it again.

"The coachman will tell the stables tonight," said Seln, from the open trap, without turning round. "The stables will tell the town by the end of the week. That's a record. It's the only one that'll get written that anybody reads."

Karis had her wrapped hand in her lap and her eyes on the clock across the yard. She did not look away from it while she spoke.

"Think how it reads," she said. "Not how it happened. How it reads, on a minute, at a seat, in that room. The respondent arrives at a documentary sitting the seat asked for him in person, with chalk to his knees and his advocate's hand in a wet cloth. He says that on the way here, this morning, on the only road open, six people he cannot name, whose faces he did not see, with no paper and no seal and no office, tried to take him into custody, and failed, and left. His witnesses are the four people who came with him and a coachman in the house's pay." She stopped. "No faces. No names. No paper. No wagon, by tonight, I'd wager, if anybody went to look. On the morning of a sitting, six days after the house turned away an order at its gate and made the order look sinister." She did turn then, and looked at all of them. "It reads as manufactured theater. Jent wouldn't even have to say so. He'd be kind about it. Everybody in the room would be kind about it, and every weekly in four towns would print it by the Fourth-day under a woodcut of the gate."

Nobody said anything.

The clock across the yard moved its hand on.

"She's right," said Brom at last. He said it to his own knees, heavily. "I hate it. She's right."

Lira looked at Cael.

He had not said anything yet. He had been sitting with his arm across his knees and his ribs telling him about the eight feet of lane he had ridden into a point, and listening, and he found that he had known how it would come out before Karis had finished her second sentence. He did not like knowing it. He knew it anyway.

"She's right," he said. "We don't tell them."

Lira held his eyes a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and looked away out of the window, and he saw her jaw set the way it set when she was holding a count.

The clock stood at the hour less one minute. Four minutes, from *We tell them* to the nod. He had counted them without meaning to.

"Then we go in," said Karis, "and we do what they asked us to do, exactly, and nothing else."

---

The young officer at the side door had his door log open on its board, and he had already written the first name on today's page before they reached him, because he had written it at every sitting since the year began. He looked up to write the second and stopped with the pen lifted.

Then he wrote the second, and the third, and did not ask anything. Cael watched him decide not to. It was the most courteous thing anybody did all afternoon.

The house's counsel was waiting just inside, in her grey coat, with her bag and her steel rule. She had come up from her rooms by the wharf in the morning before the water reached the market steps. She looked at them as they came in out of the light, one at a time, the way she read a paper, from the end up: at Lira on Brom's arm with one foot strapped; at Brom's left arm hanging; at Karis's hand; at Cael's sleeve; at the chalk on every one of them to the knee, white as millers.

She did not ask what had happened. She asked one thing.

"Is there anybody I need to send for?"

"No," said Karis.

The counsel nodded, once. She turned her steel rule over in her fingers, a quarter turn, the way she did at the end of every part of an argument. Then she went ahead of them into the long room.

It was smaller today. That was what Cael noticed first. The benches were half empty; a documentary sitting drew no correspondents. Up on the platform the five chairs of the evaluation seat stood under their grey covers, as they had stood at the first sitting, before the seat was called. Lot seventeen was carved into the floor under his foot. The hood was in its place.

At the records table Havel sat in the junior chair with the times and the receipts, and wrote the minute they came in. He did not look up.

The seat's clerk read the designation. Then he read the contents leaf of the records the house had produced, bundle by bundle, in the house's boards, from the first card of the year to the last.

"The maker of the copies," he said.

Seln stood. He stood with his wet coat buttoned and his hands at his sides, and the chalk drying grey on his knees.

"Are these copies made by you, and true to their originals?"

"I made them," said Seln. "They are true." And then, because Karis had asked him to on the stair, "On the card for the Fourth-day of the seventh week the original carries a correction in the desk clerk's hand. I copied the corrected figure and noted both in the margin."

The clerk wrote it. The hood did not move.

"The enrollee of record."

Cael stood. His ribs told him about it.

"Are the figures recorded in these copies your own figures, earned on the house's floors?"

"They are mine," said Cael.

That was all it asked. It did not ask whether they were all of him. Nobody had ever asked that, and the form had no line for it.

"The advocate of record."

Karis went up to the clerk's table and signed the receipt leaf with her left hand. Cael watched the pen go. Her name came out wide and slanting and unlike itself. She looked at it on the page for a moment, and did not try to make it neater, and gave the pen back.

The seat initialled where the clerk's finger pointed. It rose.

Jent had sat at his table through all of it with his spectacles on their black cord and his travelling inkwell shut, and said nothing, because a documentary sitting gave the petitioner nothing to say. As the long room emptied he gathered his single folder and came along behind their table toward the door, and stopped by Cael's chair for a moment, and looked at the chalk on Cael's sleeve and on Lira's strapped boot and on all their knees.

He said it pleasantly, in the voice a man uses about the weather across a counter.

"Roads are poor this month."

Cael looked up at him and sent the read.

He did it out of habit, the way he had read Jent at the conference and at every sitting since, four times, and found nothing four times. He found nothing now. The man stood there in his brown coat with the mended cuffs, middle height and middle years, with a pleasant remark about the weather on his face, and there was nothing on him at all. There was no weight laid down anywhere, and no grain of hiding. It was not that Jent was hiding what he knew. It was that there was nobody in the coat for the read to find, only a procurator of record, doing the job he had been sent to do, courteously.

He might have known. He might have had no idea. There was no way, from the outside, to tell which. That was the whole point of him.

"They are," said Cael.

Jent nodded, as if that were the right answer to something, and went out.

He stayed in his chair a moment after the others had stood. Brom was helping Lira up. Karis was packing the wallet one-handed. Seln was already at the door. The long room was nearly empty, the clerk sanding his minute, Havel squaring his receipt book to the edge of the table.

Cael sat with the chalk on his knees and looked at the room.

He had believed in it. He saw that now, sitting in it, the way you see the shape of a thing only when you are about to put it down. All year he had believed in this room. Not the hood, and not Jent, but the room: the text on the clerk's table, and the code in its three volumes, and the provisions with their dates in them, and the minute that went up under the hood and came back initialled. He had written it into Hesk's book on the first night of the year in his best hand. *The system has text, and text has held me every time the people failed.* He had stood in a dairy with the slate wall behind him and defended it at full strength. He had brought receipts to this room all year, and when the room would not take them he had gone on bringing them, because the room was the system and the system had text.

This morning the system had sent six people and a wagon with rings in its floor down a chalk road two hours from here, and none of them had had a single word of text on them anywhere.

It had not stopped being the system when it did that. It had not stepped outside itself. It had simply used its other hand. The hand with the paper had been in this room all year, initialling where the clerk pointed, and the hand without the paper had been out on the road, and they had been the same body all along, and he had only ever been watching one of them.

The room had never been the system. It had been one room. The system had as many as it liked.

He did not feel anything break. He had expected, if it ever came, that it would break like a bone, with a noise. It went like the cage had gone on the road: one stitch, and then all the rest after it, quietly, in the order they had been laid. Every receipt he had ever counted was still true. Every one of them was still in the book. They simply did not hold anything up any more.

He got up out of the chair. Havel did not look at him. The clerk did not look at him. He went out of the long room after the others, with the chalk on his knees, into the afternoon.

---

The water was under the second ring by dusk, and the bridge-ward took the chain off.

They came home the short way, over Ostrand bridge and along the river street and up the bluff road, as Withrow had told them to, with the lamps lit and the river still loud and brown beside them but lower, much lower, with a long tide-line of sticks and froth on the stones where it had been. At the ferry landing the two coats stood in their places in the last of the light. One of them watched the coach go by. He was not looking at Cael. He was looking at Lira's strapped boot, which she had up on the seat by the window, and he went on looking at it until the coach had passed.

Withrow heard it in her office, standing, with her back to the fire.

She did not interrupt them. Karis told it, in order, and Cael put in the parts Karis had not seen, and Brom said nothing, and Lira said the thing about the ditch. When they were done Withrow stood a long moment with her hands behind her back. Then she went to her desk and took out a small book bound in black cloth that was not the house's book, and opened it, and wrote one line in it, standing, and blotted it, and shut it.

"That is mine," she said, "and nobody reads it. It will stay there until somebody gives me a better place to put it." She looked at Karis's hand, and at Lira's boot. "Go to the infirmary. All of you. Now. That is not a request."

They went.

The house's healer was a spare grey woman who had strapped every fighter on the hill for twenty years and had long ago stopped asking how anybody had come by anything. She looked at the five of them in her doorway, in their chalk, and sent Seln away at once, because there was nothing wrong with him but wet knees, and set the other four out along her benches in the order she meant to deal with them.

Lira's ankle first. She unwound Brom's strapping, looked at it, said that whoever had done it had done it properly and should have been a healer, and did it again exactly the same way, tighter. Six days, she said, and a crutch, and no floor until Rooke had seen her walk across one without thinking about it. Then Brom's shoulder, which she moved in her two hands in four directions while he looked at the wall, and said had been out and gone back in by itself, which it ought not to have been able to do, and put in a sling. Then Karis's hand, which she held under the lamp for a long time without saying anything, and dressed with something that smelled of honey, and wrapped, and told Karis not to write with for five days, at which Karis only held up her left.

Cael last. She washed the chalk out of the forearm with warm water, which hurt more than the road had, and dressed it and bound it from the wrist to the elbow. Then she put her cool fingers on his right side, low, and pressed in three places, and listened to him not say anything.

"Cracked," she said. "Two, I think. Not through. Eight days in strapping, and you'll know about every one of them." She began to wind the linen. "Chalk," she said, to herself, and that was the only question she asked.

That night, at the sill, with his arm dressed and bound and his ribs strapped and the lamp turned low, he did not write a second pole. He had thought he might. He sat with the pen over the page and found he had nothing to put on it yet, only the one thing, and he wrote that, by itself, under the eighth's line.

*The room was never the system.*

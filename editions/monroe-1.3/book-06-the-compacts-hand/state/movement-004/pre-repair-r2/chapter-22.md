# Chapter 22 — Both Roads

The kettle began to sing when everybody had finished talking.

It was Brom's little spirit kettle, which had ridden down in the seat of the good chair and sat on the slate shelf beside Lira's chalk all evening over a flame no bigger than a fingernail. Lira said it only ever boiled when nobody was looking at it, out of good manners. Nobody had been looking at it for an hour. Now it whistled, thin and rising, into a room where five people had said what they had come to say and nobody had decided anything, and Brom got up out of the good chair to see to it.

Nobody else moved.

Across the table Karis's notebook lay open at the evening's page, and Cael read it upside down while Brom found the cups. He read it from the bottom up. The house's counsel read every paper that way, from the end to the beginning, because the end was where a writer put what he wanted and the beginning was only how he hoped you would feel about it. Cael had caught the habit from her in the first week of the year and never quite put it down.

The last thing Karis had written was two words with a line under them.

*A floor.*

That had been Lira, a quarter of an hour ago, sitting on the table's corner with her chalk in her fist.

Cael had just told them that text was the only ground he had ever stood on, and meant it. She had let it go by. Then she had turned the chalk over twice and said, not to the table but to him, "I had ground once."

Fenmark. She did not tell it often, and when she did she told it short. A form with her name on it, every line of it right: the examiner had wiped his slate clean on his cuff before he copied the figure, so that nothing old would smudge it, and the clerk had blotted it twice and filed it in the drawer it belonged in. Not one word of it was wrong. It had simply put her in a room she did not fit, and the room had let her drop straight through.

"I'm not against paper. There's a supersession on my own file because somebody wrote a thing down right." She had put the chalk down. "I'm saying you've got the wrong word for it. It's not ground. It's a floor."

Karis had written it down then.

"Somebody lays boards over something," Lira had said. "You walk on the boards. You don't think about what's under them, because they hold. And then one day you notice you don't know who put them down, or on what." She had looked at Cael steadily. "Since the wool exchange I don't know what this one's on. That's all. I'm not saying walk out of the room. I'm saying somebody should get down on the floor and look under it." She had nodded at Brom's chair. "His three. Both roads."

"Both roads," Cael had said.

And it had cost him nothing at all. Everybody at the table had seen that it cost him nothing, because both roads meant he could keep standing on the one he was on. Brom had looked at the lamp. Karis's pencil had not moved. Nobody had made him pay for it, and he had noticed that, and he was noticing it again now, upside down, two words on a page.

Above *A floor*, a little higher, Karis had drawn a gate.

Four upright strokes and one across them, the way a carter scores a load. Beside it she had written *induction* in her finding hand, and after that, smaller, *a tally, not a guarantee*.

She had drawn the gate while he was still talking, he remembered, and turned the notebook so that he could see it when he had finished.

"That's what you've got," she had said. "Five cases. All one way. None the other. It's a good finding; I'd sign it." She had put the point of the pencil in the empty space beside the gate, where the next stroke would go. "But a gate of five looks finished, and it isn't. It's only where somebody stopped counting. Every rule anybody ever saw broken looked exactly like that the evening before. The word for it is induction. Yours is strong. What holds it up is the count, and counts run out."

He had not answered her. He found he did not want to. It had landed on him the way a clean touch lands, before he had worked out where it came from, and he had sat there with it while Lira picked up her chalk.

Above the gate there was a gap on Karis's page. Three ruled lines with nothing on them.

That was his. She had not written down a word of what he said. She had not needed to; she had heard every piece of it before, in reading rooms and on coaches, one piece at a time, for three years. Tonight was only the first time he had put the pieces in a row out loud.

He had started at the wrong end.

"Umber," he had said. "At Norhold. The man who kept the instrument. It couldn't score me, and he had a whole office behind him that would have liked a number, and he wrote *no number goes here* instead of a lie that would have cost him less."

"Start at the start," Lira had said.

So he had gone to the start. A woman at Denvash with a brass plate on her desk that sat dark for eleven seconds before it lit; a name stamped on a card; a fourteen-year-old put out of the back door with nothing written down anywhere that said she had been wrong. Three years to get one sentence on paper that undid her.

"And between?" Brom had said, from the chair, in the voice he used for a man who has left out the middle of a sum.

So he had filled in between, and it had come out in the wrong order, the way true things do when you have carried them a long time. Vell's ledger at Ardenmere, which rated him honestly when nothing else would. The Greyvane hearing, which read an old clause out of a compilation and found for him. The grant and two renewals on paper. An inspection with the Archmarshal in the fourth chair that had gone home without one word against him. Norhold's own charter putting him on the floor. Bracken's book, which would not tell a lie for anybody.

"People change," he had said. "Every one of those, a person failed first, and something written down was still there afterward, saying what it had always said. It's the only ground I've ever had under my feet." Then, because it had been Lira's question and she was owed the answer: "I'm not leaving the room while the text still works."

It had come out heavier than he meant. He had let it stay heavy.

That was when he had seen Seln's eyes open.

The man had sat through the whole evening at the bench's middle with his hands folded on the slate and his eyes shut, as he sat in the coach on the river road. He had opened them twice. The first time had been at Lira's chalk, at *stamps stop*, and he had looked at the column on the shelf for exactly as long as it took her to read it out, and closed them again. The second time was at that sentence. He had looked at Cael across the table for a long while, with no expression Cael had a word for, and gone on looking after Cael had stopped, until Lira spoke.

Cael had gone over the look since, the way he went back over a bout. Not disagreement; Seln never troubled to disagree, he simply went on being right in a quieter room. Not pity, or not only. If he had had to put a word in the book for it, the nearest he could have got was this: the look of a man listening to somebody describe a house he had once lived in, and get the number of rooms wrong.

At the very top of Karis's page, above his three empty lines, was Brom.

*Prepare both roads. Win the sittings. Keep a box.*

Three sentences. Lira had counted them on her fingers under the table while he said them, and held the three fingers up, and said "Three," and Brom had said "That's all of it," and Karis had written all three without asking to hear them again.

That had been the first thing said in the room, and it had sat at the top of the page all night, and Cael understood now, reading upward, that everything under it had been an argument with the last two words.

*Keep a box.* Nobody had said *box* before. Not on the coach, not on the stair from the wool exchange. They had all had careful words, corridor and default and the day after. Brom had walked past every one of them and put down a plain wooden thing that a person could pick up and carry out of a door.

"What goes in yours?" Lira had asked him.

He had thought about it properly, because he did not know any other way to think about a question. "Shirts. The strapping, all of it; nobody east of anywhere cuts it the way I like. The plaque."

"The plaque?"

"If I leave it, you'll go back for it."

"I would," Lira had said. "In the dark."

"So it goes in."

She had laughed. And then she had stopped laughing, because his right hand had come off the table and gone to the left side of his coat, to the inside pocket, and lay flat there over the cloth.

"And the rest?"

"That one packs itself," Brom had said. "It's the second box that's the problem."

---

He had his hand on that pocket now, with the kettle in the other.

Cael did not need to be told what was under the hand. A square cream envelope with a dark red seal on it, unbroken, which had come up the hill twenty-three days ago and had not been opened since. Under it, the summons, the docket, the district's confirmation with *outcome anticipated* on it in a clerk's round hand, and a sitting on the last Fifth-day of the year at which three assessors and a sigil frame would almost certainly find Brom an Iron. His family's ledger had been waiting for that line since before he could lift a practice weight. In the second box was a grandmother at the head of a long table, and a word he had never once said in the same breath as *east*.

Nobody asked. Brom poured.

The cups went round. Lira took hers with both hands and put her boots up on the bench end, and dug her thumbs into the side of her landing hip, which was Rooke's nightly order and which she obeyed with the face of somebody paying a fine. In three days she would be on a strange floor two days north. Karis turned her notebook back round to face herself and read her own page through from the top, the right way, and did not change a word on it.

"I'd like it on the record," she said, "that we decided nothing."

"Nothing," said Brom.

"I mean it. Memory improves things. In a month this will have been a vote, and somebody will remember how they voted." She looked round the table. "We didn't vote. We didn't decide."

Lira blew across her tea.

"It was the night we asked," she said.

Karis looked at her for a moment. Then she wrote it, exactly like that, at the very top of the page, above Brom's three sentences, so that the page now began with the last thing anybody said. She dated it and shut the book.

They went up together. Brom went first with the chair on his shoulder and the kettle hooked on one finger. Seln picked the lamp up off the table without being asked and stood at the foot of the dairy step and held it up while each of them climbed past him into the passage, Brom, Karis, Lira, Cael, one at a time. When they were all up he handed the lamp to Lira and said good night to nobody in particular and went out by the side door into the court.

Cael, at the passage window, watched him cross it. Not toward the staff stair. Toward the dark bulk of the assessment wing on the far side of the court, where nobody had business at that hour.

He wrote one line at the sill before he slept.

*We asked. Karis has it at the top of the page, so it's true. Seln wrote one figure all night and opened his eyes twice, and the second time was for me, and I don't know what I said that he'd already paid for.*

---

The return was on the copying table where he had left it at the sixth bell, sealed and addressed, ready for the morning bag.

Seln did not light the lamp. The fire-watch's lantern hung under the north arch at that hour and threw a long pale square across the court, in at the wing's tall window and onto the counter, and it was enough. He came round the end of the counter and sat down at the copying table, in his own chair, and picked the return up and held it.

He knew what was in it. He had written it that afternoon in the hand he used for the counter's forms.

*Subject attended. Subject was examined in wing three by the evaluator of record and two instructors, in the ordinary form. Subject's figure as entered by the house. Subject's associations unchanged. Nothing further.*

Every word was true. The boy had attended; he had been examined; the figure was the house's figure, twenty-two and two-thirds, written by a clerk who believed it. Nothing further. In the morning it would go down the hill in the bag, and a clerk in an office would open it and read it and file it, and it would lie in the registry's file on the boy beside the house's own copies from Bracken's enrollment book. Two honest hands, his and the registrar's, one on each side of the file, and nothing whatever between them.

He put it down on the table, square to the edge.

Across the court a lamp moved. It was Bracken, late, going from his rooms to the records hall with a ledger under his arm, as he did two or three nights in a week when a figure would not leave him alone. Seln watched the small light cross the stones and go in at the hall's side door and appear again, a moment later, in the high window over the counter. The registrar would be sitting down now to a column he had already added twice, to add it a third time, because a man who signs a book in his own name owes it that.

He had always liked Bracken. He had never said so. Saying so would have meant explaining how carefully he had watched the man, and for whom.

The boy's window, on the residence passage, had a lamp in it too. The boy would be at his sill with the old instrument-maker's book, writing his one line. Seln had never asked to read a word of it, and never would. It was the one record on the hill he had decided, a long time ago and without telling anybody, not to know.

Forty days. He turned that over next, because it was the only thing he had put into the room that evening and he was obliged to account for it. He had corrected the researcher because she was wrong, and a wrong figure written down in a room where he sat was, in some small part, his own wrong figure; that was the plain reason, and it had been his reason at the counter for fifteen years. But that had not been the whole of it. Forty days after the fourth sitting closed, a respondent who had not come back was entered as fled. Before the fortieth day, he was only absent. Between those two words there might be a place for a person to stand. Seln did not know yet whether there was. He had wanted the girl with the chalk to have the true figure in her head when she came to look.

He put two fingers into his waistcoat pocket and found the key.

It was a small brass key worn bright at the bow, and it had lived in that pocket for longer than he had lived in any room. He held it on his palm in the pale square of light. Above him, on the shelf over the copying table, in the shadow, was the thing it opened. He did not look up at the shelf. He looked at the key.

He thought instead about the step.

He had packed his own box nineteen times. That was the thing nobody at the table knew about him, and it had come into his head while the big one was naming his shirts. A posting ended on a morning like any other; a letter came; a man put what he owned into one box and his papers into another and went down to the coach. He knew to the ounce what a life weighed when it had to be carried. He knew, too, that the box you packed was never the problem. It was the other one, the one that would not go in a box at all, and he had watched the big one put his hand flat over it and say so, and nobody at the table had asked, and Seln had liked them all very much for that.

The dairy step, an hour ago, with the lamp in his hand, and the four of them coming up past him into the dark one at a time. The big one with a chair on his shoulder and a letter he would not open. The researcher, who had drawn the corridor they were all standing in more truly than anyone in the registry could have drawn it, and did not know who had built the walls. The girl with the chalk, who had walked a road east in three pieces so that nobody would see her add it up. The boy last, with his receipts. He had lit each of them up one step. He had not told one of them what the step was made of.

---

There was a cupboard under the copying table, and he opened it without looking, because his hand knew where the latch was.

Nineteen bundles stood in it on their ends, tied with the office's grey tape, each one his own duplicates from one posting. He had never kept a list of them. He had never needed to. The labels told him: a town's reference, a year, a count of subjects in his small square hand. One academy's label said four. A district seat's said eleven. The newest, on the end of the row, said one, and had the name of this hill on it. Fifteen years of his trade stood in that cupboard on their ends, sixty-three subjects in all, and every page in every bundle was as true and as empty as the return on the table.

He had been proud of that once. It was in a commendation, in a drawer, somewhere.

The newest bundle was the thickest. He did not take it out. He knew what was in it, and he knew, besides, what was not in it, which was more.

Not in it: a document table in the mill-town hall, a year ago, where he had told the boy that his figures were too tidy, and watched the boy go away and come back with a season of figures that never once settled into a line. Not in it: two slips of wing paper, a year apart, which the boy had read and put on the common-room coals without a word to anyone, which was exactly right. Not in it: Norhold, where everything the boy had kept back for a whole season went out of him in a single afternoon, in front of eight thousand people, on purpose. Seln had sat in that stand and watched it go and understood that the boy had chosen the day. He had never asked him why. He did not ask subjects why.

The boy believed the instrument had a fault in it, here and there, that a patient reader could find and read back into true. Tonight he had laid out his whole case for it, every piece, in order and then out of order, at full strength. It was the best argument Seln had heard from anybody in years. Every piece of it was true. And somewhere in the registry, in the same file as every one of those pieces, Seln's own returns lay too, true among true things, and the boy would have counted them as receipts.

He reached up, once, while the cupboard stood open, and put his hand on the edge of the shelf above the copying table, in the dark, where the other thing stood. He did not take it down. He left his hand on the shelf's edge for the space of a long breath, the way a man rests his hand on a door he has decided not to open tonight, and took it away.

He shut the cupboard.

The last thing he let himself think about, because it was the oldest, was a press.

His fourth posting, twelve years ago. A records room in an old building, which he had been in lawfully, looking for somebody's travel endorsements; and a press he had opened for that reason, and found to hold something else. *Maintenance records*, the label said, and that was what they looked like: a long run of upkeep, routine, dated, initialled, older than the building. He had copied a quantity of them before he went, because he copied things. It was not suspicion. It was the oldest habit he had. As a boy he had copied his mother's ledgers for the pleasure of seeing the two hands come out the same.

He had not understood them for most of a year. The understanding had come all at once in a corridor distempered green to the shoulder, between one ordinary appointment and the next, the way a column of figures will sometimes add itself while you are looking out of a window. He had stood still in that corridor long enough for a clerk to come out of a side door and ask if he was unwell. He had said no. He had kept his appointment, and kept it well.

And he had known from that day that every question his trade asked about a subject was the wrong question. The files asked whether a person fit. None of them, ever, asked whose rule the fitting was measured against, or how long it had been in use, or by whom. He had paper that showed whose question it had been all along. As far as he knew, nobody else alive had.

He had not left the service. A man in his trade could not leave it and keep what he kept. So he had stayed, and filed true, and kept.

The fire-watch climbed down off his bucket at the turn of the hour, and the square of light on the counter slid sideways and went out. Seln sat on in the dark with the key still on his palm. Before he put it away he said one sentence to himself, in the voice he kept for the audit, which was the only voice in himself he believed, so that it would be on a record somewhere even if the only record was in his head.

*For fifteen years I made the same decision every morning and never said it aloud, and it was quiet. The deciding has stopped being quiet.*

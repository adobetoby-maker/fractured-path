# Chapter 37 — Two Files

He had never told it from the beginning.

He understood that only as he opened his mouth to start, sitting on the bench in the circle of the shaded lamp with Seln across the bare cloth from him. Lira had learned it in a yard at Ardenmere with grit in her palms, one piece at a time, because she had been there when each piece happened. Brom had learned it from Lira and from his own three mornings in an alcove, and had asked the rest across a table, and been told. Karis had come to it at Greyvane with most of it already worked out on paper in her own hand, and had asked only for the parts she could not get by reading. Each of the three had it whole now. Not one of them had ever heard it in order, start to finish, in one telling.

Nor had he. He had written it down in pieces as it came, in two books, over years. He had never once said it straight through out loud.

"I'll tell it the way the book would," he said. "In order. With the prices."

Seln nodded once. He put his hands flat on the table and did not move them again.

So Cael began at Denvash.

He told the circle chalked on the worn boards of a district office, and the warden-adjunct three feet away watching the brass plate let into her desk, and the thing that woke behind his breastbone and came up to the edge of his sight. He told the eleven seconds he had counted while the plate did not light, and the one word that came at the end of them, very far away and very close, and the light at the edge of his sight going out like a pinched wick and never coming back when he looked. He told the letter that had made Denvash impossible for Hesk's sake, and the road east. He told Lira next, because Lira had come next: six weeks of bouts in a yard at Ardenmere, with her knocking him down on the boards between them more times than either of them had counted. Then one cold night on a cot under a window, with the Log on his knee, something had been written in the dark place behind his breastbone where the light had gone out.

"I didn't know what it was," he said. "I sat and read it twice. Then the first time I spent it on a floor, Lira saw it, and she knew what it was before I did, because it was hers."

"It was mine," said Lira, from the end of the bench. "I'd know my own burst in a dark room."

He told the others in their order. The Bronze from Ardenmere, who had driven him across a floor with flat hands until something of the push came into his right shoulder and stayed there. Brom and his three mornings, and the read that came out of them, the cheapest thing Cael owned and the one he trusted most. Reydan, on a scored floor, and the give that took a blow into the body and kept it from going further. Karis, at Greyvane, across a formal yard in the cold, with her little fires coming up out of the ground. Hers was the only one he had ever asked for first, and she had said yes in writing, and been asked again the night before he spent it, and said yes again.

He told the notices.

"They come after, every time," he said. "Words behind my eyes, as if somebody were holding up a card: *fragment acquired*, and the name of the Path with *adjacent* after it, because it isn't the Path, it's a piece of how the Path is done. Then how long it lasts and how far in it goes, and sometimes a tier, and then how it was taken and what kind of engagement it was." He looked at the cloth. "I don't know who writes them. Nobody's ever been able to tell me, and I've stopped asking out loud. I write them down word for word, every time, and I've never once found one wrong."

Seln did not move.

He told the rule: how the four of them had sat at a long table at Halcenvane, before the stair, and written it in a minute in Karis's hand, and numbered it, and signed it. Nothing made to happen. Only inside a fight somebody else begins. The cost on paper first. And after Norhold, a fourth line under the three, initialled by four: never again in the middle of a fight unless what follows has been priced first, at the weight it will come at.

"Because every one of them costs," he said. "The Wind's bill comes to the hip. The push comes out of the right shoulder, four blows a sitting and no more. The read is cheap, and the give is a cup that fills. Karis's spark burns me too, at the fingers, if I spend it past two contacts. The storm's the heaviest thing I own, and when I lay a lane the whole of me goes a beat slow afterward for ten counts, and if I lay three close together the third costs twelve." He said it in the log's voice, flat and exact. "Those are the prices, and they're all in the book. Nothing's free. That was the first thing I learned, on a cot at Ardenmere with the Log on my knee, and it's still true."

He told Daeva, on the stone at Norhold in front of eight thousand people, and the storm that came out of her into him, and the eleven weeks of it settling, and the morning on the second floor when it settled.

He told the woman on the Ostrand road.

"And under all of them," he said, "there's one I don't count. Session nine, at fifteen: a piece of something like Tide, from nobody. There was no Tide practitioner within a day's ride. There was nothing at stake and nobody against me, nothing the rule says it needs, and it came anyway." He moved his hand on the cloth. "I don't know what it is. I've kept it out of the count for three years, because the day I count it I've decided something I don't know. I've got three words for it. *Still open. Still real. Patience.*"

He stopped.

He had told it all but one, and he had told it in order, with the prices. He found that his hands were steady. He found, too, that he was tired in a way the ribs had nothing to do with. It was as if he had carried something heavy up four flights and set it down, and only now felt how heavy it had been.

Seln had not moved once. He had not turned his cup, because there was no cup. He had not looked away from Cael's face in all that time, and his own face had shown nothing at all, as it showed nothing at the counter. But Cael had watched him listen, and he listened the way Karis read, all of it, to the end, before he let himself think a single thing about any of it.

"Eight," said Seln. "And one you don't count."

"Eight." Cael looked at him. "I've told you seven."

"Yes," said Seln. "I counted."

---

Seln asked four questions.

He asked them one at a time, and he waited for each answer to finish before he asked the next, and none of them was the question Cael had expected. He did not ask what it felt like, or why, or how far it went, or what it meant. He did not ask about the woman or the storm or the notices. Every one of his questions was the same question asked from a different side, the only question his trade had ever taught him to ask about a thing that mattered: *who could find it?*

"Who knows?"

"Lira. Brom. Karis." Cael did not look at them. "Now you. Nobody else alive."

"Nobody you told when you were small? The man who raised you?"

"I didn't know what it was when I was small. By the time I knew, I'd learned not to say."

Seln took that, and set it somewhere, and went on.

"Where is it written?"

"Two books. The old volume's full, and it's in my trunk under the bed, locked. The current one's Hesk's book, and it's in the satchel." Cael touched the strap across his chest. "Everything since Ardenmere is in one or the other, every notice word for word and every price beside it."

"Under the bed," said Seln.

"Locked."

"Mm," said Seln, and Lira, at the end of the bench, made a small sound that might have been a laugh if she had let it.

"Can an instrument read it off you?"

That was the one Cael had thought about longest, over the years, at night, and he answered it carefully.

"No frame has ever read anything off me but what I put in front of it. The plates in the wing read what I give them, and I give them the band. The read at Brom's grade is a surface, and I can hold a surface. And my own Arbiter went out at Denvash after one word. It's never lit since. I've not stood in a station since."

"A light that goes out is a reading too," said Seln. "Somebody writes down that it went out."

Karis looked up from the cloth very sharply. Seln did not look at her; he waited.

"I know," said Cael. "They wrote down the word it said first. That's all they've ever had."

"And the last." Seln moved one finger on the wood, very slightly, the first time his hands had moved since he sat down. "Does it ever show where somebody counts? Not in a fight, but after, in a corridor or on a stair or at a table, where a man like me would see it."

"The bills show," said Cael. "The hip, the shoulder. People see a fighter with a bruise and a stiff walk, and they think bruise. Nobody's ever looked at a bruise and thought anything else." He thought of Ephram's three close-ruled pages. "The band shows, and Ephram's charted it for a year, but the band's the thing I chose to show. It breathes because we made it breathe."

"I know," said Seln. "I watched you make it."

He sat for a while after that with his hands flat and his eyes on the cloth, and nobody spoke. Brom's kettle had begun to tick on its shelf, the small dry sounds a pot makes before it thinks about boiling. Cael waited, and did not know for what.

At last Seln looked up.

"You may say it to me twice more in your life," he said.

Cael did not understand him. Seln saw it.

"What you have just told me, all of it, in order. Twice more, if there is ever a need, and you will know if there is, but not a third time." He said it as he would have said a rule of the wing to a first-year who had asked about floor hours. "Every telling is a copy, and a thing told three times is a thing on its way to being known. I have kept the case twelve years by never once describing it to anybody, and you will keep this by telling it as seldom as you can bear to." He paused. "I am not ungrateful. I am only telling you the price, because nobody told me mine until I had paid it."

"Twice more," said Cael.

"Twice more."

---

"There's one more," said Cael. "I've told you seven."

Seln waited.

"It's yours."

He watched that go into Seln's face and not come out. Nothing changed in it. Seln did not move or blink or draw a breath. That was how Cael knew it had gone in.

So he told the stair.

He told it the way he had written it, standing against a wall under a lamp in the covered walk with the notebook flat on the stone at the third hour. The archive at Halcenvane on a bad night two years ago, near enough, in the first year he was on the hill. The fire-watch gone the long way round. A man with a bag at the top of the upper flight, in the doorway, with his gloved hand already going into a press he had no business opening. And on the landing above him, in the light, the wing's assistant. He was reading the man a form, one plain line after another, about copying and paper and what a magistrate counted in years, until the man's hand came back out of the press with nothing in it.

"I was on the half-landing," said Cael. "Below you, in the corner behind Brom's coat, in the dark, where I'd sat three nights running."

"I know where you were," said Seln.

Cael stopped.

"I knew there was somebody in the corner on the second night, and I knew who on the third." Seln's voice had not changed at all. "I did not look down the flight. A man in a doorway with a bag looks where the officer looks, so I did not look."

Nobody in the room moved.

Cael went on, because there was nothing else to do. He told the rule counted on five fingers against Brom's coat: earnest, a man spending everything he had in the light and meaning every word; his, begun by him, before Cael knew there was anything to begin; close, one flight; his work, the wing's officer doing the wing's work; and awake, the little finger. He told how he had not done it the way he did a burst, all at once before thinking could catch up, but slowly, on purpose, on the cold stone, knowing exactly what he did and to whom. And the quiet coming down over him like a heavy door swung shut, the stair's sounds gone, the light gone flat, and three seconds or four of the night going on above him without him in it. And then the notice.

He said it word for word, because he had it by heart and had never once said it aloud.

Seln's hands lay flat on the table.

"Adversarial," he said, after a moment. "I was not against you."

"You were against him," said Cael. "In earnest, with everything you had. It was enough."

He told the rest quickly, because the rest was only time. Two years, near enough, since the stair. It had never been used anywhere, by anybody's count. He had never once asked it for anything in front of a living soul, save once on the first night, to get past a lantern, and then it had not come. Rent from waking every day, spent or not, the way a room costs rent whether you sleep in it or not. Drifts in the gaps, never in the work: a thinning at his edges on a stair or in a queue, when he was tired. Lira had counted them for him on the back of a chair with her thumbnail, and the count had fallen every week. Its two halves would not sit together in his hands. To hide moving he had to read the room, and to read the room he had to be the most awake thing in it, and being that awake was what put the hiding out.

"You do both at once," he said. "I've watched you. I still can't."

"A year of it," said Seln. "Before I was a man in a doorway, I glowed." He did not explain that, and Brom, in the chair by the shelf, looked at the floor as if he had heard it before somewhere.

Then Norhold.

"The night before the final," said Cael. "In a back room on the harbour, with the shutter open a crack. We'd costed everything I was going to spend the next day: the Wind past six, the push at fighting load, the give, the spark. Everything that had been held back for two years was going to go out in one afternoon, in front of the whole continent, and be on the record for good." He looked at Lira. "And I said the quiet thing stayed sealed, and Lira said to cost the seal, out loud, and say why."

"I did," said Lira. "I wanted it said."

"So I said why." Cael made himself look at Seln. "Because of who it came from. That fight was going to be written down three times over and gone through for years by people whose whole work is sorting. If I'd used it, somebody sorting would find one loose thread. Where did a fighter with no tier ever see that kind of work, done well, long enough to come away with any of it? It isn't a long thread, and if you pull it, it comes away in your hand at a counter in the wing, at a copying table, where a quiet man has spent his whole life making sure nobody ever looks at him twice."

Seln did not move.

"So it stayed sealed. It was sealed that night for a debt, and the next afternoon, in the last corner, against the barrier, with her lane coming and nowhere left to go, it offered. It always offers when I'm cornered, and it would have hidden me from every eye in the bowl." He let his breath go. "I didn't turn my head."

"We all held the coat," said Lira quietly. "All four of us, not just him."

Brom nodded once, slowly, at the floor.

"He isn't told," said Karis. "That's what we said in the back room. He's never told." She looked across the table. "We were wrong about the second half."

The kettle had stopped ticking. The lamp stood up straight in its glass.

Seln sat with his hands flat on the cloth and looked at Cael for a long time, and his face showed nothing, as it showed nothing at the counter. But Cael had sat across counters from him for two years, and had walked a circuit with him and read a lodge book at his elbow, and had watched him look at rooms. He knew that Seln was doing now what he did to a room, going back through it, two years of it: the counter, the bench and the cup, the slips on the common-room fire, the season's figures that never once settled into a line. The afternoon at Norhold where everything held back went out in one breath, and one thing did not. He was finding, one at a time, all the places where a boy had held a coat over him without saying he was holding it.

Then Seln spoke, very quietly.

"You kept my file too, then."

Nobody answered him, because it was not a question.

He sat a while longer. Then he took his hands off the table for the second time that night, and folded them in front of him on the cloth. Cael had never seen him fold his hands.

"Is it still sealed?"

"Yes."

"Tonight. Now. With me told."

"It's sealed till somebody unseals it," said Cael. "I don't know who that is now. It was a debt, and you've been told. I don't know what a debt is after it's been told."

Seln looked at the folded hands.

"I am not going to release it," he said, "and I am not going to take it back. I couldn't if I wanted to. It is yours. A notice says so, and I have never yet known one of those to be wrong either." He was quiet a moment. "But I would like to set the terms of the seal. If you will let me."

"Set them."

"While my name is on any roll of the service, it stays where it is." Seln said it slowly and exactly, the way he wrote a line in a return that he meant to be read by somebody careful. "Not for my file, which can look after itself now; it has had practice. For the trade. Somewhere, in a room I will never see, there is a man whose whole work is sorting, and while I am a name on his list he has a reason to look at anything that moves the way I move. When I am not on any list there is no thread to pull, and then it is yours. Use it or don't, as you judge." He unfolded his hands. "And you will not ask me first. That is the other term."

Cael took Hesk's book out of the satchel.

He opened it on the cloth to the leaf after the birthday inventory, and uncapped Hesk's steel pen, and wrote it there in front of all of them, in the smallest plain hand he had, with the shaded lamp drawn close.

*Shadow-adjacent, the quiet thing from the stair. Told tonight, whole, to the man it came from, in Karis's room, the four of us there. The debt is closed. The seal is kept. Grounds revised: no longer a debt kept from him. His terms: sealed while his name is on any roll of the service; after, mine, and I don't ask.*

He blotted it and turned the book round on the cloth so that it faced across the table.

Seln read it upside down without touching it, the way he read everything that came across the counter, twice, and then he nodded once.

"Mm," he said.

Brom got up out of the chair by the shelf, which took him the usual time, and went to the kettle. It had boiled, and none of them had heard it go. It stood over its blue flame with a thin plume coming out of the spout, as if it had been waiting a long while for somebody to notice. Brom said nothing about it, and made the tea one-handed, which he had got good at in a week, and put a cup in front of each of them on the bare cloth, Seln's last.

Seln looked at his cup.

Then he put out his hand and turned it a quarter turn on the table.

---

They went down at a little before the tenth bell, one at a time, a few minutes apart, because Seln said that five people coming down a stair together was a meeting, and five people coming down one at a time was the library closing.

Karis stayed, because she would turn the lamp in the window down at its usual hour and not a minute before. Brom went first, by the iron stair through the stacks, and Cael heard the ninth tread on the second flight say what Brom had said it would. Seln went second, by the bindery stair, and did not say goodnight. At the door he stopped and looked back at the shelf, at the dark leather case standing between two of Karis's grey boxes like one more box with no label, and then at Cael, and then he went.

Lira went third, and Cael went with her, because she had said not to help her and he was not going to, and that did not mean he could not be on the same stair.

She went down the top stair the way she had come up it, which turned out to be sitting, one step at a time, with her hands on the tread behind her and her right foot held clear. At the bottom she got up by the newel post and stood on the third-floor landing breathing hard, and looked at him.

"Not a word," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to." She put her hand on the newel post's cap, worn smooth by a hundred years of hands. "*Post*," she told it, seriously. "*It holds the rail up. People hold it going down.*"

He said it after her.

They went down the main stair together, slowly, at her pace. On the second landing she stopped, not for the ankle.

"Two years," she said. "He knew it was you in the corner. All the time we were holding a coat over him, he'd been holding one over you, on a stair, and he never once looked down it."

"Yes."

"I keep thinking about the first week." She started down again. "Walking me across the court to the east hall. Being pleasant." She did not say anything else until the street door, and at the street door she said, "*Door*," and he said it after her, and they went out into the cold.

---

He did not write the cache in the book.

He sat at the sill with the satchel on it and the book open and Hesk's pen in his hand, and the court below him dark, and the fountain going up black and coming down white, and he did not write it.

He had written everything for years. Every notice word for word, every price. The book was where a thing went to be true. And Seln had sat across the table and asked where it was written, and when he had answered, Seln had said *Under the bed*. In a voice that cost nothing, he had said, *A thing told three times is a thing on its way to being known.* The case had been kept twelve years by a man who had never once described it to anybody. Cael did not think he would last twelve hours if he started writing it down.

So he wrote what he could.

*Day 88. Five in Karis's room by three stairs across an hour. The case opened. What was in it does not go into a book. It is in a room with two keys, and the five of us have it, and that is where it will stay.*

*Lira's strapping off this morning, Brom's sling off tomorrow, and the ribs two more days by the healer's count.*

*Told the whole of it tonight, in order, to the one person at that table who had never heard it, the first time from the beginning. It's longer than I thought, and shorter. Twice more in my life, he says, and not a third.*

He looked at that for a while.

Then he turned back three leaves to the birthday inventory, to the line for the sixth, and drew one straight line through *sealed, rent from waking* so that it could still be read, the way Seln's mother had done in a grain factor's ledger on a river quay, and wrote the new figure beside it in the margin.

*Sealed, on his terms. Rent from waking.*

He initialled it.

Through the window, down in the court, a man in a dark coat came out of the library's shadow at the bindery end and crossed the flags toward the wing. He went at an even pace, neither slow nor quick. He did not take the covered walk. He went straight across the open court in the frost where anybody could have seen him, and nobody did, because nobody looks at a man going home at the tenth bell with nothing in his hands.

Cael watched him all the way to the wing door. At the door he stopped, and did not look back, and went in.

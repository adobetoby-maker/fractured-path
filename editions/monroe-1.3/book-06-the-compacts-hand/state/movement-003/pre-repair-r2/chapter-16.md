# Chapter 16 — The Column

The notice on the assessment wing's board was in Gault's hand this week, not the desk clerk's, and Cael read it twice before he understood why.

*Sitting week. The provision's supervised hours are moved to the Fourth-day, second bell, wing three. The seat sits on the Fifth-day and the enrollee attends it. Observers enter by the lodge book.*

The first two sentences were ordinary. The seat sat on Fifth-days, and so did wing three, and something had to give way; it was only sense that the provision should move and not the registry. It was the third sentence he read twice. In the two years he had been reading that board, nobody had ever needed a line about observers. The people who came to watch the provision's bouts were the cohort, and they came in by the south door and stood at the east rail and wrote their names in nothing at all.

The desk clerk saw him at the board and leaned over the counter.

"Four," he said, low, in the voice of a man passing on something he has been told to keep to himself. "Four names in the lodge book since the First-day, asking to observe. Officers from other houses. The Magister's had the porters clear out the old gallery for them, over the west door." He straightened up. "I'm only telling you so the room won't surprise you."

Behind the counter, at the copying table, Seln's pen stopped for the length of one word, and went on.

"Thank you," said Cael.

"Mm," said Seln, to his page, and turned it over.

Cael went back through the records hall rather than round by the covered walk, because the docket number he had come down to ask Bracken about was a real question even if it had not been the real errand. The outer room had a second table in it now, an old trestle from the lumber room set across the corner, with four boards along its edge chalked in Bracken's small capitals, *HOUSES. SPONSORS. FACULTIES. OTHER.*, and a basket under each. The basket under *OTHER* was heaped above its rim. The sacks came up the hill twice a day now, and Bracken had built the baskets on the second day of them rather than complain on the tenth.

He answered the docket question without stopping his dealing. Then he slid a fair copy across the counter, two lines in his own hand.

*Halcenvane's enrollment stands on a provision the registry has not set aside, and has been entered in full on the public record. Each chartered house must read that provision for itself.*

"Fourteen houses asked me in one week whether the clause was safe to use," said Bracken. "Fourteen got that, the same two lines, so none of them can say it was told more than another." He took the copy back. "Not one of the fourteen asked about you. Strip the slates off any chartered house and you'll find a clause holding up the roof. Houses write about clauses." He nodded at the heaped basket. "Everybody else writes to the boy. Your friend sorts that pile up at the residence. She says the table up there's better than this one, because it has people at it."

There was one letter on the counter that he had not dealt. It was sealed in grey.

"The seat," said Bracken, following his eyes. "For the reading room. I don't put those in baskets. I carry them up myself."

Cael crossed the fountain court with Gault's third sentence in his head, and with it the cost he had been paying every Fifth-day for a year without once looking at the bill. Holding himself inside the house's rate in front of three cards and a clerk had never been nothing, but it had always been private, a matter between him and his own record. Tomorrow four strangers with writing boards would sit over the west door and lay what he did beside everything the broadsheets and the tournament books had printed about him. He would have to look to them exactly like the record. Not a hair more, not a hair less, and not the same as last week either, because a record that repeats itself has been arranged.

---

The man on the milestone had been there since the third bell. The porter's boy had counted.

Cael saw him from the north wall at the sixth: a correspondent with his collar up and a board on his knee, sitting on the stone at the second switchback below the gate, where a man on the outside of the bend could see the gate and the wall and the whole road up to them. He had not come up to the lodge. He had read the board on the gatepost, perhaps, or heard about it. Withrow had given the lodge a standing line on the morning after the instrument was served, and Bracken had painted it in registry type and hung it where every visitor would read it before reaching the lodge-man. *The house has answered the challenge in its filing. The filing is public. The house has nothing to add to it.*

Lira came up the wall stair with two apples and gave him one without asking. She had her wraps still round her wrists, and her hair was dark with sweat from the Stone's afternoon hour, which she did not usually take; when she leaned on the wall her left calf stood out hard as a rope, and her burst hand kept flexing on its own, as if it were still counting. That morning Rooke had pinned the card for the program's first road meet on the east hall's door, with her name on it in his square capitals among the program's seniors. Ephram said she had read it four times on her way past without once breaking her stride.

"What's he waiting for?" said Cael.

"A boy of seventeen to come down a road." She bit into her apple. "Boys do, sooner or later."

The light went off the river, and the man on the milestone turned his collar higher and stayed where he was.

A little before dusk Rooke came down from the gate, alone, on foot, with a satchel on his shoulder. He had business in the town on Third-day evenings, and nobody had ever asked what it was. He came round the bend and found the man on the milestone, and stopped. From the wall they saw the man stand up with his board, and Rooke stay where he was, hands in his coat pockets, and say something short. The man looked up the road toward the gate for a long moment. Then he tucked the board under his arm and went down the hill in front of Rooke at a walk.

Half an hour later Rooke came back up without the satchel and passed under the wall.

"Coach," Lira called down. "What did you say to him?"

Rooke looked up. Cael thought he would not answer; not answering was Rooke's usual way. Then he did.

"That the boy trains before light and eats after dark, and neither of them happens on this road."

He went in at the gate. Lira laughed into her apple.

"Three of them came on the First-day," she said. "To the lodge. They argued with the lodge-man for a quarter of an hour, and one of them tried to give him a coin, and he pretended not to see it, because it was a very small coin." She finished the apple and threw the core out over the wall into the scrub, a long throw, and watched it go. Then, in exactly the same voice, she said, "Rooke's had the clerk logging the gallery."

He turned his head.

"Since a little after the first sitting. Every morning, every name on the east hall's gallery. Who, what house, how long they stay. He never said why. She keeps it in a second ledger and he reads it every night before he locks up." She held up four fingers. "Twenty-six names in a fortnight. Ours, mostly, and town people who like to watch. Four aren't. Two from Rhagen. One from the lake house. One from a house up north the clerk had to ask him to spell." She folded the fingers down. "Two of the four were there yesterday morning. You saw them."

He had. Travelling coats, good boots, a board each; one who wrote and one who did not.

"I thought they were scouting you," he said.

"So did I. I was pleased." She said it lightly. "Then one of them asked the clerk, friendly, the way you'd ask about the weather, how often the unclassified enrollee comes up to watch the Silver drills. She told Rooke. Rooke told her to keep writing."

Cael went back over the morning before as he went back over a bout: the man who did not write, who had watched the gallery rather than the floor, whose eyes had gone four times to the north end of the rail. He had stood at that rail, glad for her, while a man from Rhagen counted how often he came.

He thought, too late and with something like shame, of the morning on the gallery when he had been proud for her. She had come away from Fenmark at fourteen with nothing, after an examiner wiped his slate with his sleeve so the form would be clean. Now there was a red book on the east hall's trestle with her figures in it, and a card on the door with her name on it among the seniors, and a ladder that somebody had built toward her for the first time in her life. And strangers were sitting at the top of it, watching the stair for him.

"Rooke hasn't stopped the drills," he said.

"Not yet." Lira pushed off from the wall. "He writes it all down first. You'd know."

---

The schoolroom letter was already lying by itself on the board when Cael sat down after supper.

It was folded very small, on paper ruled for copying, and it lay apart from the three piles at Lira's end of the long table, squared to the board's edge. Nobody was touching it. Lira had read it before he came in, and Brom had refilled her cup since, and Karis had closed the volume she had been reading, and nobody had picked up anything else.

Lira saw him looking, and read it to him, once, in a voice with nothing added.

*To the boy at Halcenvane. My teacher read us about you. I am nine and not Kindled yet. Does it hurt, not having a Path written down? I would like to know before it is my turn.*

Then she put it on top of the tallest pile, and went back to sorting.

She had taken the pile on in the second week, without anybody asking her to, because somebody had to carry what nobody could set down, and that had always been a job she did without being told. Every evening Bracken's porter brought the *OTHER* basket up at the eighth bell and she tipped it out under the lamp and sorted it the way she read a bout sheet, fast and flat. A few strays went back down to Bracken. The rest went into three piles, which she had named aloud on the first night, once, and never renamed.

"Kind. Unkind. Unhinged."

Ephram, going past with the captain's file under his arm, stopped and looked down at them with the face of a man reviewing a cohort that has formed up wrong.

"Do you know what you are now?" he said to Cael. "You're a case."

"I've been a file since Denvash."

"A file sits in a drawer and nobody reads it." He picked a letter off the tallest pile, read the first line, and put it back. "A case gets read out every year to second-years by an old man with a pointer, for a hundred years, with diagrams. At Norhold you were a match, and a match ends. A case doesn't." He tucked the file higher. "Try to be one of the dull ones. Nobody remembers those."

"I'll do my best."

"You never have yet," said Ephram, and went up.

The kind pile was always the tallest and the strangest. Tonight it held a barge-master's widow from the lower Ost, who had watched the Norhold match from the top tier and wanted him to know that the whole river was on his side, though she could not answer for the fish; four close pages from a man who had washed out of a hill academy at Copper twenty years ago and had walked a corridor of his own, with a diagram of its doors; and a drover from the north road who had stood on the Concourse's top step through the whole match and wished it entered somewhere that his knees had held. The unkind pile was short, and Lira never read a word of it aloud. The unhinged pile was of middling size and good for morale. Tonight it had a chart of the night sky in four colours of ink with one star circled, and a note explaining, with perfect confidence, that this was Cael's Path, and the registry would understand everything if only it would look up.

"Which star?" said Brom.

"The one with the circle," said Lira.

"It's a good circle," said Brom.

The second post came up while she was tying the piles. The porter brought one letter to the table himself, because he had learned the seal: a square cream envelope, a dark red seal pressed with the bar and the ring.

He laid it by Brom's plate.

The last one had come on the thirty-first day, and Brom had kept it two nights on his table before he opened it. This one had come in fourteen. He looked at it. He picked it up and turned it over once in his big hands, and looked at the seal, whole, the bar and the ring. Then he put it, unopened, into the left inside pocket of his coat, where the summons was, and the docket, and the confirmation, and the others.

Nobody asked. That had been the arrangement since the recess, and it held. Lira tied her bundle with a knot she did not usually use. Karis opened her volume again at the page she had just closed. Cael watched Brom's hand come away from the pocket and settle on the board, and then, a moment later, go back to the pocket and rest flat on the cloth over it, and stay there, as if the letter might get out.

The arrangement held. It had started to cost the table something, and Brom more than any of them.

On the stair afterward Lira fell into step beside Cael and said, without looking at him, "I'd open it for him."

"I know."

"If he asked, I'd sit with him while he did. I'd read it to him if he wanted, and never say one word about it after." She climbed two more steps. "He won't ask. He's carrying it the way he carries everything, so nobody else has to. And the worst of it is that's exactly what we'd all want him to do, if it were any of us." She stopped at her own landing. "So we don't ask. And he carries it. And the letter sits in his coat getting heavier." She went in at her door and shut it quietly.

---

Karis had gone up before the piles were tied. When Cael followed her to the reading room at the ninth bell, she had the grey-sealed letter from Bracken's counter open on the long table and was already writing her answer to it.

It was a printed form from the district fee office at Ostrand, which put its seal on every request a chartered house sent to an archive outside its own district. This one concerned her request of the thirty-fifth day, to Greyvane, for certified copies of the founders' signature leaves. The fee was paid and the request in order, but before the fee office would seal it on, it asked the requester to confirm on the back of the form the research standing under which the request was made, the date that standing had last been renewed, and the name of the officer who renewed it. It cited the section that let it ask.

Karis was filling in the back. Research standing, renewed in the recess by the house's council, two sittings, *Research, enrolled, standing*; Bracken's line in the accounts; the budget. All of it, in full. She did not look up, and she did not say anything about it, and Cael did not ask.

The door from the landing opened. The assistant stood there in her cloak, with her face pale round the mouth and red at the cheekbones.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I know it's late. I did something, and I need to tell you in case it was wrong."

Karis put down her pen. "Come in and shut the door."

The girl came in and shut it and did not sit.

"A Mire girl from the north stair stopped me on the covered walk after supper. I hardly know her. She'd heard I work up here, and she wanted to know what the enrollee was really like. *Really*. She said it like that." The girl swallowed. "So I told her. That he's enrolled under a lawful provision, adjudicated at Greyvane on the public record, under seal, with the grant and two renewals on paper. That the Compact's own inspection looked at the provision and it stood. That the challenge doesn't allege any fraud, and the petitioner's conceded the provision is valid. That the next sitting is on the Fifth-day. I gave her the paragraph numbers. I gave her the ninety-one." She stopped for breath. "It went on about a quarter of an hour. She kept looking at the stair. At the end she said *thank you* in a very small voice and went away." Her face went redder. "I think I was boring. I think I was extremely boring. I'm sorry."

Karis looked at her for a long moment, and Cael saw her mouth do a thing it did not usually do, and then stop doing it.

"You weren't boring," she said.

"I watched her face."

"You told the truth quickly, to a question you weren't ready for, to somebody who came for gossip and got the code." Karis picked up her pen. "That's the whole work. The rest is decoration. Do it like that for a year, every time anyone asks, and you'll be more use to this house than any clerk on the hill."

The girl stood quite still. Then she said "Yes," very quietly, in exactly the way Karis said it, and went out. They heard her cross the landing to the outer table and sit down at it, though there was nothing on it for her to do at that hour.

"Write that down," said Karis, without looking up. "In yours. That she came back up the stair to tell me."

Then she lifted her head after all, and turned the fee office's form over, and pushed it across the table to the girl's empty place, as if the girl were still sitting in it. In the morning, when the assistant came up to the outer table, it was waiting there for her, with one line marked in pencil: the section the fee office had cited as its authority to ask. Beside it, in Karis's hand, two words. *Find it.*

She found it before the second bell. Cael was on the landing when she brought the volume in, open, and laid it in front of Karis with her finger under a sub-clause near the foot of the page.

"It doesn't say *shall*," the girl said. "Or *upon cause*. It says the office *may*, at its discretion. There isn't any condition at all."

"Yes," said Karis.

The girl waited, the way she had learned not to, for the rest. There was no rest. She went back to the outer table and wrote it in her own small notebook, and did not ask what it meant, and Cael did not hear anybody tell her.

---

He did it at the window that night, at the back of Hesk's book, where he had ruled the column at the month's close with four names at its head, his own last, and had hoped to leave it empty.

Under Lira: *Strangers on her gallery, logged two weeks. One asked how often I come up. Her drills are being watched for my sake.*

Under Brom: *Velmere, a fortnight after the last. Into the pocket sealed. Hand on the pocket.*

Under Karis: *The fee office wants her standing confirmed before it will send her request on.* And smaller, under that, because the girl was not one of the four but there was nowhere else to put it: *Her assistant, asked on the walk what I'm really like. Answered with the code, and came back up the stair to say so.*

Under his own name there was nothing to write. He had eaten a good supper and slept well the night before, and nobody had stopped him on any walk.

*At the month's close I wrote that this should cost me and not them. Look at the order the bills are coming in. Three lines, three people, each paid in a coin I never saw leave their hands. Under my own name, not one.*

*I had the sentence right and the order wrong. I'm keeping the column.*

Then he turned forward to a clean page, because tomorrow was the Fourth-day and there was a line still to write.

He had written one before every bout since the mill town: a single line out of the rotation, saying which exchange he would give away, how many bursts he would let the house see, where the spark would go, and what the figure ought to come to at the end. Seln had told him across a document table that his figures were too tidy, and the rotation was the cure: scatter, built on purpose and paid for in advance, so that no one with a pencil and a season's worth of his numbers could find the hand that set them.

Tonight he sat with the pen over the page for a long time.

The trouble was the strangers. Three cards and a clerk who knew him were one kind of audience. Four officers from four houses, with the broadsheets and the tournament books in their satchels, were another. Rhagen wrote down every exchange of every bout it could see and bound the result and sold it; somebody in an inn that very night would be laying tomorrow's figure beside last season's, line for line. Whatever he did must look exactly like the record, which meant like a strong Iron with a few odd gifts on a good day. And it must not look like the last bout, or the one before, because a record that matches itself has been arranged, and men whose trade is arrangements can smell it.

He went back through the last eight figures in his head. Twenty-three and a third. Twenty-four and two-thirds. Twenty-two and a third. Before those, the season's. He had to land somewhere among them that none of them had landed.

He thought about whom he would draw. The wing's list had a Current third-year on it for the Fourth-day, Iron Rank Four, a long-legged girl from the movement floor who glided. He had watched her in the Crown yard. He had watched Lira beat her pattern two mornings ago, on the painted floor, by going along the bend of her glide instead of across it. He could do that. He could do it in the first exchange and be standing at the end of her curve with his palm out before she got there.

The book would not let him.

He wrote the line at last, and read it over, and did not like it, and left it, because not liking it was a sign that it was honest.

*Fourth-day, wing three, the Current. The first exchange to her. Two bursts in the bout, no more. The spark once, and late. Buys: a figure in the low twenties, nothing near the top. Spends: the forearm, probably.*

# Chapter 14 — Standing Distribution

The seal had gone in crooked.

Vastin held the petition up to the window and tilted it until the light lay along the wax. The device was all there, the Archmarshal's mark and the ring of small letters round it, but the left edge was shallow, almost a ghost, where the press had bitten at a slant. It was the press's fault. He had screwed it to the corner of the desk himself, nineteen years ago, on his first morning in this room, in a hurry and out of true, and he had never once straightened it. In nineteen years it had never mattered.

He found that he was asking himself whether a seal with a faint edge could be challenged.

It could not. He knew it could not; he had sat on a hundred inquiries into a hundred seals and never once seen a petition refused because a press was old. But the question had come up in him by itself, from somewhere under the morning, and an evaluator who lets a question go unanswered because it is foolish has only taught himself to stop hearing his own instruments. So he sat back with the petition in his hand and asked the better question underneath it. Not *will this seal hold?* but *why am I afraid that it won't?*

Down in the paved court the porter was folding his paper bag along its old creases. The pigeons had been fed late this morning, and they were still walking about on the stones in the sun, looking for anything they might have missed.

The answer was on the blotter, under his left hand, where he had put it at the second bell.

It was a narrow printed slip, pinned to the corner of a bundle in grey tape. *For the evaluator's file, per standing distribution.* For three years every paper about the enrollee at Halcenvane had come up the too-steep stair to this desk wanting something from him: his advisory, his assessment, his view. He had given each one and signed each with his name entire. This bundle wanted nothing. It had been sent to him the way weather returns were sent, to every office on a list, so that no office could afterward say it had not been told.

And it had been told late. The last paper in the bundle was the minute of a sitting at Ostrand. The seat had sat yesterday. It had opened its proceedings and confirmed its schedule, and noted that its evaluation seat was *to be called*, and risen. Nobody had called him.

He had read the whole instrument by then, slowly, the way he read everything, and in its seventh paragraph he had found three sentences he knew because he had written them. *The clause says what it says. The enrollment is lawful on its face and adjudicated at public record. If the Compact wishes practitioners of this kind not to compete, it should write different rules — not misread the ones it has.* The petitioner had set them off from the paragraph with a little white space above and below, as a clerk does with a passage he is pleased to have found. And above them it had put one word for what it was doing with them. *Adopts.*

His left hand had begun to ache on the blotter. Some years ago it had decided that it disliked mornings. Lately it had added evenings, and it disliked this noon as well. He opened it and closed it, twice, slowly.

He had never had his own words used as a warrant before.

That was not quite true, and because it was not quite true he made himself correct it. Once, a long time ago, a girl had used his words for something. She had written to him from a coast town in a careful hand that had plainly been practised on other paper first, and she had asked a single question, and he had answered it in his own hand with one word, because anything longer would only have given her a second sentence to wait on. *No.* Her father's name would not come off the list.

Her father had been a district officer who took money to enter a rank in a station book that no frame had ever read. Before him there had been a keeper at a hill station who let a cracked housing read every Ash a rank high for six years and wrote false returns to cover it. Two findings in forty years, both of them sound, both of them written at this desk and sent down the stair; and somewhere below, in an office whose door he had never opened, the rest of chapter fourteen had run to its end for each of them. He knew the chapter's three steps without opening the code. He had started them twice.

And now a paper had come up the stair, with his own sentence inside it, asking the registry to start them for a boy.

He set the petition down on the blotter, face up, with its faint-edged seal toward the window.

The seal was his. The press was crooked and the seal was his, and it would carry what he had written under it as well as any seal in the building. He had asked himself the foolish question and answered it, and found the fear underneath, and named the fear, and a fear with a name was a thing he could work beside.

He rang for the clerk.

---

The clerk wrote the date in the dispatch ledger, and the petition's number, and then his pen stopped.

Vastin had come to the inner door to watch the petition go into the wallet, and he saw the stop from there. The pen stood a finger's breadth above the routing column. It did not shake. It simply did not come down.

He knew why. He had kept this clerk nine years, and in nine years the outer office had sent paper out of this room by only one road. A finding went *back* to whatever office had asked for it. That was the shape of the work. A desk like this one did not reach out toward a proceeding that had not sent for it, any more than a clock reaches out to a man who has not looked at it. The man's pen had come to the edge of a road it had never taken and stopped, the way a good horse checks at a gate it does not know.

So Vastin had a choice to make, and he made it quickly, because the clerk would feel any pause as a judgment on himself.

He could say why. He could say *the Compact's own proceedings have adopted my advisory and not asked for my finding, and I mean to offer it to them, and the old code says they must receive it*. It would all be true. And the clerk would carry it down the stair in his head with the wallet, and somewhere between the stair's foot and the dispatch room it would stop being the Archmarshal's petition and become the Archmarshal's quarrel.

"Ostrand," he said. "The registry seat there. Into the proceedings, under the institutional heading."

"Sir."

The pen came down and wrote it, in the tidy upright hand that never asked anything. The clerk blotted the line, closed the ledger, tucked the petition into the noon wallet, and went down the stair at his ordinary pace, two treads at a time to the turn. Vastin listened to him go.

He came back up a quarter of an hour later with the dispatch slip, and laid it on the corner of the blotter by the crooked press, where slips always went.

"Thank you," said Vastin.

The clerk did not go at once. He stood with his hands at his sides and looked at the slip, and then at the Archmarshal's left hand, which was lying open on the blotter as it often did at that hour, and then at nothing. Vastin waited, and nothing came, and after a moment the man nodded and went out to his own desk. But he left the inner door open a hand's breadth wider than he usually left it, and twice before the noon bell Vastin heard his chair creak as he turned in it.

So the outer office had begun to watch him. He found he did not mind. A clerk who watches is a clerk who has noticed there is something to see.

Then he went back to the desk and tried to steady himself by counting.

Four times before this, in forty years, he had sent a finding to a proceeding that had not called him. A district that had challenged a house's enrollment, a dozen years back: his finding had gone in against the district's own petition, and the seat had weighed it and the district had lost, and the district had written to him afterward in perfectly good temper to say so. A hill station's frame that read high: the frame had come out of its housing on his evidence. A guild hall. A sponsorship quarrel so old he could barely recall the guild. Four times offered. Four times taken in.

It was a small number. He laid it out anyway, on the blotter, in his head, the way he laid out any cases before he let himself guess at an outcome.

It did not steady him. A sample of four was a sample of four, and every one of the four had been a proceeding that wanted to know something. He did not yet know what this one wanted.

What steadied him was the drawer.

He got up and went to the long cabinet of shallow drawers that ran down the wall at his left, and took out the boy's file, because he had never in his life let himself think about a subject from memory. The file had grown since the bluff. Most of what had swelled it was other people's: a season's figures under a regional registrar's seal, the tournament's papers, a bundle of broadsheets some junior had clipped under *public record*, with a woodcut on top of a boy with a jaw like a spade.

His own part of it was nine leaves.

He laid them out across the blotter in their order. The observation schedule. His notes from three days on the bluff, in his own small hand, instruments set up and failing and set up again, and one frame that had stuttered and gone dark with the boy standing in it, quite still. And the clerk's ruled sheet from the small room on the east side, twelve feet by fourteen, where the whole interview went onto a single page: minutes counted down the margin; down the middle, eleven questions, numbered; and near the foot one question written the other way, the boy's to him. *What is the Compact evaluating? Me, or the provision?*

He read the eleven. They were good questions, properly ordered, each opening the door to the next.

The twelfth was nowhere on the page.

It would have been the obvious next one. The boy kept records, privately, in a book; he had said so in that room, and said what they were for: *so I can't lie to myself later.* Any officer in the building would have asked what the records said. Vastin had looked at the question across four feet of table for less than a second and left it lying where it was, because a private book was not in the record, and an officer of his height who reaches for whatever is within his reach has stopped evaluating and started to collect. He had once spent ten years helping to take a collector apart. He had promised himself, in a corridor with rain on the window at the end of it, that he would not turn into one.

He had been right. He sat with the nine leaves in front of him and turned the thought over looking for the flaw, and there was no flaw.

But there was something lying beside it that he had not seen before, small and cold. Eleven honest questions made a finding that could be answered. The twelfth would have made one that could never have been written down. And because he had been careful, the only thing of his anywhere in this matter that the petitioner could pick up and use was three sentences of plain advice about a clause. They had picked it up. It was in their seventh paragraph.

He squared the nine leaves, edges knocked true on the desk, and laid them back in their folder, and did not put the folder away.

---

On the third evening the copy was still in the *held* tray, and he had moved it twice.

It was not his. It belonged to a junior from the northern district, a young evaluator perhaps twenty-five years old, who had climbed the too-steep stair that afternoon with a file under his arm and the look of a man who had argued with himself the whole way up. Vastin had given him the chair, and made him lay the file out on the desk himself, because a young officer learns more from putting his papers in order in front of a senior man than from anything the senior man says to him.

It had been a Kindling file. A station's entry, four years old, for a girl: her Path, her first rank, written in a station clerk's ordinary hand. Beside it, the junior's own floor notes from three sessions this season, with the girl in front of a frame. The two did not quite meet. What she did on the floor had run on ahead of what the station had written of her, by a margin the junior had measured four separate ways and could not make go away.

"It's inside tolerance on every measure, sir," the junior had said. "I wrote that myself. But I don't like it, and I can't say why I don't, and I thought a thing like that ought to come up the stair and not stay in my drawer."

"You were right to bring it."

They had spent an hour on it. Vastin had made him walk the station's line, and then his own notes, and then the tolerances for a girl of that age on that Path. Everything had been where it ought to be. The station's figures were sound and so were the junior's; a girl had simply grown faster than a station had guessed she would, which girls sometimes did. The gap between a guess and a girl was nobody's fault.

"Within tolerance," Vastin had said. "Sound record, sound notes. The margin is growth. Write it so."

"And the recommendation, sir?"

"Continue observation."

Vastin had watched the relief come into the young man's face and had remembered, without wanting to, a stair of his own, a long time ago, in a district house with a smoking stove. He had carried a file up it to a senior evaluator whose name was now on a plaque in a corridor he no longer walked down. The man had heard him out, and gone through the file with him line by line, and at the end had told him the thing every evaluator is told once and has to learn for himself afterward: *Write what the instrument says. What you feel about it is information about you.* Vastin had gone down that stair lighter too. He had written exactly what the instrument said for forty years since. He had just said the same thing, in other words, to a young man who would very likely say it to somebody else in forty years' time.

He did not doubt that it was right. He only noticed that it had been easier to say than to hear.

The junior had written it and blotted it, and gone down the stair a lighter man. He had left the copy behind on the blotter, as juniors do.

Vastin had picked it up to put it in *done*, and read the station's line once more instead, and put it in *held*. Before the lamp he had taken it out of *held* and read the line again: the Path, the rank, the clerk's hand, the date, the station's seal. An ordinary line on an ordinary day. He had spent an hour proving that there was nothing in it.

He put it back in *held* now, a third time, and left it there.

He did not write anything about it. There was nothing to write. It was not yet a thing with edges, only a line read more often than it had earned, and an evaluator who wrote down every unease he could not name would end with a file full of himself and nothing else.

He turned down the lamp.

---

On the fourth morning he opened the boy's folder to check a date in his floor notes, and found the denial already lying on top of the nine leaves.

For a moment he could not remember having put it there.

That had never happened to him. He stood at the desk with the folder open and the sheet in front of him, and made himself go back, a step at a time, as he would have made a witness go back. It was registry-seat stock, heavy and white. The seal had been pressed into the paper and not inked onto it; a seat that inked its seal was writing to a colleague, and a seat that pressed one was writing for the file, and he had noted that grade before he read a word. Before that, the clerk at the door with the sheet held out in both hands as if it were hot, saying *seat courier, sir, not the post*. And before that, from the stairwell, a tread he had not known for his clerk's: single steps, quick and even, the whole flight from the bottom, with no pause at the top while the man decided whether the Archmarshal was reading.

He had heard that tread and arranged his face. He remembered that now. He remembered minding, briefly, that his face had needed it.

Then he had read the two sentences. And then, it seemed, he had put them on the nine leaves and shut the folder, and gone to the window, and stood there a long time while the porter fed the pigeons, and none of that had gone into his memory at all.

He read them again now.

*The proceedings' evaluation seat is fully constituted. Supplementary findings are not contemplated by the schedule.*

Then he went to the shelf behind the desk and took down his letter-book.

It held fair copies of everything that had left this room in nineteen years. He knew roughly where to look. In his eleventh year here a field assessor had asked leave to add a page of his own to a review that had not asked him for one, and Vastin had answered him in a single line. He found it. *The assessor's additional view is not contemplated by the review's terms.* He laid the open letter-book on the blotter, and set the seat's sheet beside it.

They might have been cut on the same bench by the same hand.

He tried to remember what had become of that assessor, and found that he could, which surprised him. The man had left the service two years later and taken a post with a guild on the coast. Vastin had never learned whether the extra page would have been right. He had not needed to learn it; that had been the whole convenience of the line. It closed the question so smoothly that nobody was left holding the other end of it, not even the man who had closed it. He had taught that line to juniors as a courtesy, because it spared everybody an argument, and he had been a little proud of how well they learned it.

Somebody at Ostrand had learned it very well indeed.

He went to the first sitting's minute, which was still in the grey-taped bundle, and laid it beside the denial. Five days ago, at the wool exchange, the five chairs of the evaluation seat had sat under grey covers, and the minute said *to be called*. This morning the same seat was *fully constituted*. Somewhere in the four days between that sitting and this refusal, then, somebody had filled five chairs. He knew every senior evaluator in that district by name and grade. Not one of them had written to him for the boy's file, which any of them would have needed before they sat; and none of the five chairs had been offered to the one officer who had already sat across a table from the boy with an instrument.

That told him more than the sentences did. A schedule drawn to find something out would have wanted him in it. This one had been drawn by somebody who already knew what it would find, and had left no chair for anyone who might find something else.

And it cited nothing. His petition had stood on two provisions, the old and the new. The answer stood on none, and he understood that this was not carelessness but the point: a ruling has legs, and can be walked up to a higher office and knocked over there. This had no legs. It was a shut door with nobody's name on it, and it had cost whoever shut it a sheet of good paper and the time it takes to sand two lines.

He put the letter-book back on its shelf.

Then he did what he had always done when a paper had shaken him: he went on with the tray. The *in* tray held a station's request to reopen a finding of its own on a frame, which had come up the week before and which he had already decided to refuse. He drew a sheet toward him and began, and his pen wrote *The station's request is not contemplated by*, and stopped.

He sat looking at the half-line for some time.

Then he struck it through, corner to corner, so that it could still be read underneath, and on a clean sheet he wrote the refusal again, longer, giving the station the section it had failed to meet and the one officer it might apply to instead. It took him a quarter of an hour where the old line would have taken a quarter of a minute. He signed it with his name entire and put it in *done*.

Last, he read the denial one more time, as he had been taught to read any finding before signing it: slowly, for what it actually said, and not for what he had braced himself to hear. It said what it said. He closed the folder over it.

---

In the morning the clerk wrote one candle into the consumables.

He did it first thing, before the wallet came up, in his tidy upright figures, against the Archmarshal's name and not the floor's, as he had been told; and while he blotted it, he let himself remember the evening, which was not a thing he often did with evenings.

He had come up at the sixth bell for the day's wallet and found the Archmarshal writing. That was rare. The Archmarshal wrote in the mornings, on the office's forms. Last night he had been writing on plain sheets, in the hand he used for things that were his own, and he had said "Nothing of mine tonight" before he was asked. The house put the lamps out on that floor at the half-bell after six. The clerk had gone down and lit a candle in the outer office and come back up with it in a tin dish, and set it on the corner of the desk by the crooked press, and the Archmarshal had said, "Put it on the consumables. Under my name. Not the floor's."

And then the clerk had done a thing he had not done in nine years. He had not gone. He had stood in the doorway with the wallet against his chest and looked, not at the desk, but past it at the window, where the evening had gone close and grey, and he had said the only sentence he had ever said in that room that had nothing to do with paper.

"Will you be getting home tonight, sir? It's set in to rain."

The Archmarshal had looked up at him for what felt like a long while. Then he had said, "Later than usual. But yes," and gone back to his sheet, and the clerk had gone down the stair with the oddest feeling of having been answered.

It had rained. He hoped the Archmarshal had got home.

Above him, in the high room, Vastin opened the lowest drawer at the far end of the long cabinet, where he kept the few things in the room that were his and nobody else's, to make sure the folder was there. It was. It had a tab, as every folder did, and the tab had a heading in his own hand, the only heading of its kind he had ever written in forty years, because every finding he had ever made had come with somewhere to be sent.

*Pending a proceeding worth sending it to.*

He did not take the folder out. He knew what was in it, and he went through it in his head the wrong way round, from the end, because the end was where he had put the most weight.

Nine pages. The last of its claims said that the proceedings, if they meant to evaluate at all and not merely to proceed, had in their hands already the one honest instrument there was: three years of the demonstration record, kept under seal by officers with eyes. Before that it said that a re-evaluation whose answer was known in advance was a procedure in an evaluation's coat. Before that, that when the standard protocol went silent in front of this boy the silence belonged to the protocol and told nobody anything about the boy. And at the start, in one line, that the standard Arbiter protocol could not evaluate him.

Somewhere in the middle of the nine pages was a sentence he had been saying about frames since before the clerk was born: that no frame was to blame, that the protocol had done exactly what it was built to do, that an instrument which tells you plainly where its reach ends has told you the most honest thing an instrument can. He had said it at inquiries, to men who wanted a housing blamed for a bad rank. Last night he had found himself writing it about a person. He had sat a long while with the pen lifted, looking at it, before he let it stand.

And at the end, the paragraph that no finding has any business carrying, put down a word at a time. *Offered to the proceedings at Ostrand under the code's two provisions; declined without citation as outside their schedule. The schedule itself is not disputed. One thing only is set down. A schedule that leaves no room for the one personal assessment of the subject the Compact holds was drawn for a purpose the evaluator was never told and cannot name from his desk. Where the outcome of an evaluation is settled before the evaluator arrives, it is not the subject who is being measured.*

It was as clean a piece of work as he had done since he came to this room. It had nowhere to go.

He shut the drawer. At the head of the first page of it, under the subject and the basis and the date, on the line the sheet kept for the name of whoever the finding was meant for, there was nothing written at all.

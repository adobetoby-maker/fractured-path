# Chapter 19 — The Shape

When the plates had been cleared, Seln put four dried beans on the board in a row and said, "Four offices. Ask."

They were the speckled brown beans the kitchen soaked for the Second-day stew. He had taken them out of his coat pocket without any hurry and laid them a hand's breadth apart, in front of his own place at the middle of the bench, and now he sat back from them with his hands folded and looked at none of the four people across the table.

Nobody asked anything for a moment. The refectory went on round them, loud at the far end where the first-years were.

"A drawing can be found," said Seln, to the beans. "These can be eaten. Ask."

Brom asked first.

Cael had not expected that. Brom did not ask questions at table; he answered them, when he had to, in as few words as the question would bear. But Brom leaned forward on his forearms now and put one thick finger on the board beside the second bean from the left, not touching it.

"That one."

"A provincial registry," said Seln. "The one that keeps the Velmere estate's file. Nine days ago. A query on the standing of the household, the tiers of the people in it, and the estate's registered relationships."

Brom did not move his finger.

"Does the query show at the estate's end?"

"Nothing at a provincial counter is hidden," said Seln. "Every question asked of a file goes into a book that lies open beside it. Whoever has a name on the estate's own file may turn the pages and see who came asking, and when, and why." For once he lifted his eyes and looked straight across the table. "The head of your house has a name on it."

Brom took his finger off the board. He nodded once. Then he sat back and folded his arms, and Cael, who had watched Brom carry everything from code volumes to a hurt friend's kit without once looking as if it weighed anything, saw him look for a moment like a man carrying something he could not put down and could not shift to the other shoulder.

Lira asked second. She did not point. She said, "Which one's me?"

Seln touched the third bean. "The district seat. Twelve days ago. A query on the certification history of a Wind practitioner of this house, Iron Rank One, *for completeness of the respondent institution's file*."

"Which file?"

"There is only one."

"Then they've read the bottom of it." Lira said it lightly, and the lightness did not hold. "At Fenmark the examiner wiped his slate with his sleeve so the form would be clean. He kept the form. Everything anybody's written about me since, the Greyvane line, the Iron line at the Ostrand station, all of it went down on top of that form, and none of it rubbed the form out, because the registry never rubs anything out. It just writes on top." She pressed her thumb on the board. "Four years, near enough, of good people writing over it. And somebody with a reason has gone to the bottom of the pile on an ordinary afternoon to see what was there first."

"There's nothing in it anybody can use," said Karis.

"I know." Lira looked across at Cael. "Put it in your column. Under my name. That they read it."

"Tonight."

Karis did not ask about hers. She took a folded letter out of her sleeve instead and laid it on the board beside the first bean on the right, the one nobody had pointed to yet.

"That's mine," she said. "I know which one it is, because the keeper of the archive at Ternhall wrote to tell me. Privately, this morning, four lines. A district clerk came to her counter six days ago with a sealed request for my transfer file. My old standing. My privileges. The training log from the paired floor." She kept two fingers on the letter, as if it might blow away. "On the Third-day a fee office wanted to know who I was before it would send a request of mine to Greyvane. Now somebody wants to know who I used to be." She was quiet a moment. "That's twice."

That left the last bean, the one at the far left of the row, and Cael asked about it because nobody else was going to.

"This house," said Seln. "Our own counter, three weeks ago. A request from a district office for the house's sponsorship papers, *for the proceedings' record*. Ordinary. Its fourth item asked for a schedule of every tie this house has to the enrollee: the sponsorship, the floor hours, the standing that hangs on his evaluation, his travel endorsements." He touched the bean very lightly. "The registrar answered it in full, and said in his covering letter that it was in full, twice. That is the registrar."

Then, at last, he said the rest of it.

"You've seen the road for yourself," he said, to Cael, without looking at him. "The two at the foot are holding it and doing nothing else. Whatever was watching you has gone on to these. You'll notice your own name isn't on any of them. Each one is on somebody who sits near you, and each asks the same thing in its own office's language: whom does his name touch, and by what road?" He drew his little finger along the row and straightened it, so that the four beans lay on one line. "One query is a clerk. Two is a careful office. Four, in four offices, in three weeks, each reaching for a different person at one table, is somebody's plan. Every one of them is lawful. Two of them are the sort of thing a junior sends off on a slow afternoon to look keen. The office has no view of any single bean. It was only ever trained to see a row."

Karis had her notebook out. She wrote the four offices down a page in her finding hand, each with its date. Under the fourth she laid the edge of her steel rule on the paper and drew one more line, straight, and left it bare, and turned the page.

On the stair afterward Lira caught her up and asked what the bare line was for.

"For whoever's next," said Karis, climbing. "So far they've touched people with a seat at that table. A careful clerk doesn't stop at the table. A careful clerk asks what else the man would mind about, and goes looking." She reached the landing. "I've left them a line."

Brom had come up behind them, slower. At the turn of the stair he stopped, and Cael, a step below, saw his right hand go to the left side of his coat, where the inside pocket was, and rest there flat over the cloth. There was a square cream envelope in that pocket with its dark red seal still whole. It had come up with the post four days ago, and he had turned it over once at the table and put it away unopened, and nobody had asked. Now an office in his own province kept an open book with a question in it about his family, and his grandmother could turn its pages whenever she liked.

Brom took the hand away from the pocket and went on up the stair. Nobody asked him then, either.

---

Cael ran.

He had not run on the covered walk since his first year, and he ran the whole length of it now, past two second-years who flattened themselves against the pillars, with an envelope in his hand that he had opened and read once, walking, and not read again. He came in at the residence door and through the hall and into the refectory, where the four of them were still at the middle table because none of them had wanted to go up, and he stopped at the end of the table with the envelope held out in front of him like something hot.

Seln was already looking at the envelope. He had been doing the morning sort for the post porter all week. He knew that hand, and he knew which town's mark sat on its corner.

Before anybody had said a word, Seln put his hand in his pocket and took out one more bean, and laid it on the board: not in the row with the other four, but apart from them, beyond the left-hand end, by itself, in the place where a fifth would go.

Cael saw it. Lira saw it. Karis opened her notebook to the bare line and held her pen over it without writing.

He went to the window, because in that house letters were read at windows, and turned his back to the glass, and read the third paragraph aloud to them.

*Two polite men from the registry came through Denvash asking after my trade licenses and the shop's title. Wanted to know if you had any interest recorded in either. I told them my grandson's interest in this shop is that he grew up in it, and they wrote that down. Whatever they're building, it has a shape. Mind the shape. — H.*

Nobody said anything.

So he read them the rest of the letter, too, the first part, which he had smiled at on the walk before he reached the third paragraph. He did not quite know why he did it. Perhaps he wanted them to hear Hesk as Hesk was, a man with a bench and a stove and opinions, and not as a line in somebody's inventory.

*The brass came up from the coast at last, and it is a third dearer than last year and not as good, and the founder says it is nobody's fault, which I believe, because it never is. I have a surveyor's level on the bench that came back to me after thirty years. I made it for the man's father. The bubble has wandered a hair, and the son brought it in to be put right; and then, when I had it in pieces, he asked me to set it back the way it was, because his father laid out every boundary in the valley with it a hair wrong and the whole valley is built to agree with it now. I spent four days deciding. In the end I did as he asked.*

*The stove draws well. The shop is the shop. The widow across the lane has a new dog that barks at me in particular, and I have decided to take it as a mark of my character.*

It did not work. He heard it not work as he read it. The dog and the stove and the level went out into the room and fell on the table among the beans, and none of them could get Hesk back out of the third paragraph. He stopped reading and folded the letter.

*Grew up in it.* He had. At five years old he had slept under the long bench in a nest of clean shavings, because the stove ticked there and it was warmer than upstairs while Hesk worked late. He had learned his letters off the brass makers' plates on instruments that came in to be mended. The shop's title was one sheet of the district's paper folded in four, in a tin box under that bench with the receipts, and it had Hesk's name on it and no other. Two polite men had asked whether his was in the tin box too. And on a form somewhere, in a column under a printed heading, a pencil had set down *grew up in it*.

"Denvash," said Lira.

Only the one word, in the voice she kept for a fact she had just walked all the way round and found was bigger than it looked from the door.

Karis wrote on the bare line. *Denvash. Provincial registry. Trade licences and the title to the shop.* She dated it, and drew a stroke under all five lines with her rule, from one edge of the page to the other.

---

It was Karis who said it first, and she said it as a guess, which she almost never did.

"If I wanted to shut a man out of everything at once," she said slowly, "every door at once, so that he couldn't move a deed or carry a letter or warn a friend before the next one shut, I would want to know where all his doors were. Beforehand." She looked at the five beans. "Is that what this is?"

"Nearly," said Seln.

"Which part's wrong?"

"*Beforehand*." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "You've put it before the shutting. It's before the ruling."

Karis's pen lay on the board where she had let go of it. Cael had never seen her let go of a pen with a question still open.

"Go on," she said.

"Shut every door on one morning, yes. That's what it's for. A restriction that runs one office at a time leaks: somebody hears, somebody moves a deed, somebody's cousin carries a letter over a district line before the freeze arrives. So nobody runs one like that." He laid a fingertip on the board beside each bean in turn, not touching any of them. "Every office finds its own door first, and writes it down, and puts what it found in a drawer. Five drawers, here. Each file lawful. Each as dull as a laundry list. All five indexed under one name. And then they wait."

"For the ruling."

"For a ruling." Seln's voice did not change. "Think what five offices' clerks cost for nine weeks. An office spends that only when it already believes it knows the answer. So if the walking has begun, then somebody well above the seat has settled in his mind how the seat will rule, and has started paying for the morning after. The walking is a finding of its own. It's made by people who don't sit in the seat, and made first."

"And if the seat rules the other way? Our way?"

"Take the bad way first, it's shorter. One clerk, one afternoon, five files carried to one desk; every door shut on the same morning; and the enrollee learns what all this was for on a day when there's nothing left to do about it." He turned the cup back. "Your way, the five drawers are simply never opened together. Each office weeds its old papers in due course, as offices do, and nobody anywhere has broken a single rule."

"What do you call it?" said Brom.

"In the office we called it walking the fences." Seln looked at the five beans. "On paper, it's a pre-restriction inventory."

"How long does it take?"

"For a smith with a forge and a wife and a guild, six weeks. Ten, if the wife's people are spread about." He looked at the four in their row and the one apart. "For a boy with friends at three provincial registries, and a house that took him in, and a grandfather with a shop at the far end of the country—" He laid one finger flat on the board beside the fifth bean. "Nine weeks. They are in the sixth."

Nobody said anything.

Then Karis picked up her pen again and held it level in both hands, and when she spoke it was in the voice she kept for a finding she had tested two ways and disliked more each time.

"You close an outline round a man by finding out what he'd mind," she said. "Not what he owns. What he'd mind." She tapped the pen once, gently, beside the fifth bean. "Chapter fourteen's schedule has nine headings, and the ninth is *other relationships*, and that's the one they've used. An old man in a shop has no standing anybody could freeze; he's never been inside an academy or before a seat. He's in their drawer for one reason. They know what it would do to the person sitting by that window." She set the pen down square to the notebook's edge. "Four beans were us. The fifth is somebody none of us would let them near if we could help it. That's not restriction any more; that's the restriction's own machinery lent out for another errand. I want it said aloud, by all of us, that we saw it."

"Said," said Brom.

He was looking at the folded letter in Cael's hand: the square level address, the old fold. He had never met Hesk. He had heard about him at a hundred tables, the stove and the spectacles pushed up on the forehead and the barometers waiting on the bench to come right, and he looked at the letter now as if it were something of his own that a stranger had taken out of his coat.

Lira reached across and picked up the bean at the right-hand end of the row, the one that was Karis's, and turned it in her fingers.

"There are men in Denvash asking an old man about a tin box," she said, evenly, the way she read the true sum off a bout sheet after a loss. "And we're down here being good at sittings. We are good at them. Karis wrote the best thing anybody's ever written on this hill, and the house's counsel wins whatever there is to win, and the seat writes it all down." She put the bean back exactly where it had been. "They'll let us be good at sittings all year. It costs them nothing." Her chin came up. "The question is what we do the day after."

Nobody answered her.

Cael looked round the table, because looking was what he did when there was nothing to do. Karis with her palm on the notebook's cover. Brom bent forward with his elbows on his knees and his eyes on the board. Lira with the question still lying open where she had put it, and her chin up over it, waiting for one of them to pick it up. And at the middle of the bench Seln, who had walked fences like these himself for years and knew from the inside what the morning after one looked like, sitting with his hands quite still on either side of the five beans, as if the table might tip if he moved them.

The others went up in ones and twos, Brom last, with his hand on his coat. Cael stayed. After a while Seln began to put the beans back into his pocket, one at a time, the fifth last.

"You'll want to write to Denvash tonight," he said, without looking up.

"Yes."

"Write it." The fourth bean went into the pocket. "Give it to the registrar for his pouch, not to the town post. The pouch is locked in Ostrand and opened here, and I open it." He put the fifth bean away. "Letters from a house under inventory are read. Not by anyone wicked. By a clerk, for completeness, who will copy a line of it into a file. Don't give them a line." He stood, and turned his empty cup a quarter-turn on the board, and left it there. "Tell him the stove sounds well. Tell him nothing else he wouldn't want read aloud in a provincial counting-room. He'll understand. He's been minding shapes longer than either of us."

He went out at his even pace, and Cael sat a while longer alone at the long table, where the beans had been.

---

Withrow read the letter at her window, by the lamp, and smiled at the dog, and then stopped.

The first two paragraphs went by at the pace she read everything. Partway down the third she did not move or change colour; she simply stopped, with the page in her hand, long enough for the lamp's flame to dip and stand up straight again twice. He had known her two years and had never seen a paper stop her. Then she read to the end and folded the letter along its crease and gave it back.

She did not talk about the letter. She talked about a girl.

"There was a girl who came to my counter every week for a year," she said. "You know that. I told you on the night the instrument came. What I didn't tell you was what she was carrying the first time." She went to her desk and sat, and pointed him with two fingers to the visitor's chair, and he sat. Neither was a thing she did. "She wasn't carrying anything. Her father was. He came on the fourth morning, before she ever did, with a flour sack, and tipped it out on my counter, and it was letters."

She took the brown pocket code off the corner of her desk and held it closed in both hands.

"Nine of them. I read his week off my counter in the order they lay. The bank's was on top: the note on his house named his standing as its surety, and a standing under restriction is no surety, so the note was stopped. Under it, the transit office. His endorsement had run out, which meant the law no longer let him cross out of his own district to argue about anything. Then the guild, which may not keep a contract with an unclassified man and so had suspended his. Then the yard, which had already given his run to somebody else." She turned the closed book over in her hands. "And the school. A child's place rests on the parent's standing, so the girl's place was *under review*. He'd put that one at the bottom of the sack. I think on purpose. The other four were the same letter in different hands, and I won't give you those."

"Who was he?"

"Force Path. Bronze, Rank Four. A haulier, thirty-one, on a guild contract in a river district, with a wife and a son and the girl and a house he had fourteen more years to pay for." She said it like a line read off a form, which was perhaps how she had to say it. "His fault, if you want to call it one, was a slip a station clerk made twenty years before, when they wrote his Kindling down, and it could never be put right, because the only officer who could have sworn to the true reading was dead. So the registry stood him in a frame again. Twice. And twice the frame had nothing to say."

"And the nine letters—"

"Were mine." She opened the code then, near the back, without looking for the page, and turned it round on the blotter so that he could read the short list at the top. "Nine headings. Employment. Membership and standing in a guild. Credentials and endorsements. Property, his own or shared. What he owed and what was owed him. Enrollments, his and his family's. Travel and transit. Any office or contract. Other relationships." Her finger stopped at the bottom of the list. "The district was short of clerks that year, and I was good at it, so I walked those fences myself. Seven weeks. It was careful work, and I was proud of the care. I've had a long while since to think about that."

"What did you tell him? At the counter."

"That he didn't have nine troubles. He had one, which had come to his door nine times in nine different coats, and there was no counter anywhere he could stand at to face it, because a restriction isn't a thing anybody does to you. It's a condition you're left standing in." She shut the book. "Nobody did anything to him. Nobody had to. That's how it was built."

"What became of him?"

"In the end he crossed into the east, beyond the last station. Men with families usually go, sooner or later; this side of the line has no room for a man with children and no classification. It took him eighteen months, which is what it costs to get round a lapsed endorsement and stay inside the law. After he crossed, I don't know. A man past that line stops making records, and records were the only eyes I ever had." She was quiet. "The girl stayed with her mother's people. And came to my counter."

Withrow closed both hands round the little brown book.

"Your grandfather," she said. "*Grew up in it* is what he told them, so it's what they wrote, and it's true. It goes under the ninth heading, a true line among true lines, and it waits there for the morning somebody wants the ninth heading." She did not raise her voice. "On that morning nobody will say one word against him. They won't need to. Any licence held by a man the registry has entered as tied to a restricted person can be called in for review. Nobody refuses it. They review it."

"And the review?"

"Takes exactly as long as somebody would like it to," said Withrow. "That's the whole design. Not a hand on the door. A hand on the clock."

She opened the brown code again at the nine headings, and took up her pen, and in the margin beside the ninth, small, in the hand she had used to underline that book in her second year, she wrote one word. She turned the book so that he could read it. *Choosing.*

"It had no price on it when I said it to you over the charter folder," she said. "It has one tonight. I've read the price. I've written it there so that I'll see it every time I open the book." She shut the code. "Go to bed."

---

He wrote it past midnight with the letter flat on the sill beside the column, and the fifth line of Karis's list in his head.

*I win the sittings. Karis wins the filings. The counsel wins every point there is to win, and the seat writes down that she did.*

*And two polite men have been to Denvash to ask an old man whether my name is in a tin box under his bench.*

*For three years I've stood on one line. On one side, the text, which holds. On the other, the people, who fail me, and who can be read, and beaten. I've never once had to look for that line. Tonight I went looking for it in Withrow's office, with a flour sack on her counter and nine letters in it, and I couldn't find where it ran. A hand on a clock isn't a person. It isn't text either. Where in all that is the system doing what it's for? I don't know. I've never needed to know.*

Then, because Seln had told him to and because he could not have slept otherwise, he wrote the other letter.

It was short. He wrote it three times. The first was angry, and he tore it across. The second was frightened, which was worse, and he tore that too, and sat a while with the pieces in his hands before he put them in the stove. The third he wrote slowly, reading every line as a clerk with a pencil would read it in a provincial counting-room, for completeness, and taking out anything he would not have wanted copied into a file.

*You were right to set the level back the way the son wanted it. A valley that agrees with itself is worth keeping agreed. You'll have known that before you spent the four days; you spent them to be sure.*

*The stove sounds well. Tell the widow's dog it has good judgment.*

*I'm well. The house is good to me. I read your third paragraph at a window, to the people I'd most want to hear it, and they heard it. I'll mind it.*

He signed it with the one letter Hesk signed his with, and sealed it, and in the morning he gave it to Bracken for the pouch, and Bracken took it without a word and put it in the pouch himself.

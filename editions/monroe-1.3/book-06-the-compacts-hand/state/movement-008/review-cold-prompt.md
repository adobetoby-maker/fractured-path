# Chapter 48 — Two Hundred and Six

The long room was full, and he counted it.

It was the only use he could find for the quarter hour before the seat came in. The coach had gone down the bluff road at the usual pace behind Withrow's carriage, and the two coats at the road's foot had looked at it as they looked at it every Fifth-day, and the young officer at the side door had written the same two lines in his log. But the exchange's four steps had been crowded to the plinth this morning, and the benches behind Cael were filling with a noise he had not heard in that room since the preliminary. A closing, the house's counsel had said, was the one day besides the first when a proceeding sold.

So he sat with lot seventeen under his left boot and his hands flat on the house's table, and counted the people, as he had once counted the words in an instrument.

He took the benches first. There were forty of them, and for eleven sittings he had been able to count the heads on them at a glance. Today he had to go bench by bench, turning a little in his chair, and Bracken, beside him, watched him do it and said nothing, having very probably done it himself on the way in. Twenty-three had folding boards on their knees. There was a whole bench of district clerks in their grey, who had come because it was their seat and their Fifth-day. There were wool-factors, who owned the room and seemed to feel that it had been borrowed for long enough. An instructor in a blue-grey coat sat near the back with her arms folded, and he thought she was the lake house's. On the fourth bench sat Lira, Brom, Gault and the assistant with her inky collar, and at its far end, by the pillar, where a man could see the whole platform and both tables without turning his head, Seln sat with his coat buttoned.

A hundred and eighty-seven on the benches.

Then the people who were there by office. The seat's clerk at his small table. Ilsev in the middle chair below the platform, with her file at its angle, and her four district evaluators. The senior chair and the junior at the records table. At the petitioner's table Jent, and for the first time a clerk of his own, a thin young man with a satchel who looked as if he had been told not to touch anything. Three officers at the doors. At the house's table Withrow, the counsel, Karis, Bracken with the seal on his knees, and himself.

And the hood, when it came.

Nineteen. He added them twice, which was once more than the sum needed.

Two hundred and six.

He did not stop there, though he might have. He went down them again, more slowly, and this time he was not counting heads. He was doing what he had done on the night the instrument was served, when he had gone through nineteen leaves looking for the place where it lied. He had looked for that place in every sitting since. He had looked for it in Jent's weight in a chair, and in a seal that Havel had found on no register, and under a grey hood that had never once shown him a face.

The clerks in their grey had come to see their own seat do its work. The correspondents would write down what was said, and get some of it wrong, and mean none of the errors. The four evaluators were working officers who had read every exhibit they were handed. Ilsev had asked the seat to minute the edge of her own chair, so that nobody could push her past it. At the records table sat a man who had receipted a hundred and seventy-nine leaves without losing one. And Jent, with his travelling inkwell and his mended cuffs, had given away every point that was not true as soon as he was shown it.

There was nobody. He had known it for weeks, in the way he knew a figure he had not yet written down, and this morning, with the room full, he wrote it down.

Two hundred and six people, and not one of them was dishonest.

He did not know what he had expected a thing like this to look like. He had thought, when he was younger, that it would have a face somewhere, and that the face would look away. He looked along the benches at a hall of people who would all go home tonight and tell the truth about their day.

"The seat," said the clerk, and two hundred and four of them stood up, and Cael stood with them.

---

"In the matter of Caelen Hesk-ward," said the seat's clerk. "Upon the close. The twelfth sitting. The petitioner."

Jent rose.

He had no folder today. He had left even the one folder on the table, shut, with his spectacles lying on it, and he stood in front of the platform with his hands at his sides, as a man stands who has come to say a thing he could say in his sleep and does not wish to seem to be reading it.

"The seat allowed each party an hour," he said. "The petitioner will use ten minutes, and I should like to say why, so that it is not taken for discourtesy. There is very little left between the parties. I have spent a year making sure of that."

He turned his head a little toward the house's table.

"The enrollment was lawfully made. The provision under which it was made is law, and has not been repealed, and the seat has held so. The renewals are good upon their days; the respondent's table of them is the petitioner's own. The evaluator of record is competent, and his figures are honest, and his notes were produced entire. The tournament office's record is accepted. And the house has kept within its charter in every particular I have been able to examine, and I examined a great many." He paused. "I want one of those in the minute in my own voice, and not only in a schedule. The Chancellor has done nothing wrong. She answered under escort. She read every order at her own gate. She produced what was asked before it fell due. If every respondent I met were the Chancellor, I should have very little work, and I should think the better of the registry for it."

Withrow did not move. Cael, behind her chair, saw the line of her grey collar, and it was as straight as it had been at the first sitting.

"That is what is agreed," said Jent. "Here is what is asked, and it will take me four sentences. A practitioner is enrolled in a chartered house. The standard protocol has not classified him. The registry asks that he be evaluated under that protocol, as every practitioner in every chartered house is evaluated. If the protocol reads him, his enrollment stands, and I shall be the first man in this room to say so."

He let that lie a moment.

"If it does not, the code says what follows. It said so before anybody here was born. The petitioner did not write the fourteenth chapter and does not ask the seat to add a word to it. He asks only whether the registry may put the question. That is a matter of discretion, and I have said so since the fourth sitting, in answer to the best question I was asked all year."

He inclined his head an inch toward Karis, and she inclined hers the same inch back. They might have been two people who had met on a narrow stair every week for a year.

"It may," said Jent. "The seat held at the preliminary that the question was cognizable. Nothing the respondent has filed since has touched that holding, and nothing was meant to; her filings have been upon the facts, and the facts were hers before she filed. So I have nothing to answer. I have used ten minutes because the seat was generous with the hour, and I did not like to seem ungrateful."

He sat down, and screwed the lid of his inkwell a quarter turn tighter, though it had not been open.

Cael had sent the read after that man five times in the year and found nobody in the coat. He did not send it now, and he found that he no longer wanted to. He had spent months wishing Jent would turn out to be the arm, because an arm could be found out. But Jent was a hand, and a clean one. He had said exactly what he was asking for, in four sentences, before two hundred people, and every one of the four was true.

That was the edge on him. Cael saw it plainly for the first time, sitting behind Withrow's chair. A liar would have had to keep his story straight for fourteen sittings, and somewhere in a hundred and seventy-nine exhibits Karis would have caught him. An honest man had only to stand up and ask.

---

The house's counsel took twelve minutes, and she spent them all on one thing.

She did not go back over the year. She laid a single sheet on the edge of the table, with her steel rule across it, and told the seat what the house had found when it went looking for a like case, and how far back it had gone, and in whose digests.

"The registry has ordered frames tested," she said. "It has ordered stations inspected, and records opened, and officers examined upon their conduct. It has ordered practitioners evaluated, a great many of them, where a provision had lapsed, or was wanting, or had been got by a false paper. The house has read every such order it could find." She turned the rule a quarter turn. "It has not found one in which a seat ordered a practitioner into a frame over a provision that stood. Not one. The petitioner was asked whether he had, at the ninth sitting. He answered that he had not, and that he did not need one."

She was a very small woman, and the room had gone quiet for her as it always did.

"He is right that he does not need one. A discretion needs no precedent. But the seat may like to know, before it uses a tool, that nobody has ever picked it up by that end." The rule ticked again. "That is the house upon the remedy. Upon the merits, the house's close is the work of its advocate of record, who drew every submission this seat has received from it. The house renews its motion under the second paragraph of the hearing provision, and asks that she be heard."

She sat.

Cael did not look at Karis, but he felt her beside him as he would have felt a lamp. She had her two hands laid flat on a shut wallet, pale fingers and all, and she had not written a word since the clerk spoke. She had thirty slips in that wallet. She had written every one of them out in full without knowing whether anybody would let her stand up.

Under the hood there was the small stillness that came before it spoke.

"The motion was laid before the seat at the eleventh sitting," said the hood. "It has had it under consideration since. The provision stands. Leave is given."

Somebody on the benches let out a breath.

"The advocate of record for the respondent will be heard at the next sitting. She will be heard once, and within the hour. The petitioner may reply, if he is so advised, and not otherwise. The seat will then reserve." The level voice went on with no more weight on it than before. "So ordered."

"So ordered," said the clerk, and wrote.

Karis did not move at all. It was Bracken who moved; he took one hand off the seal's box, which he had not done in that room all year, and laid it for a moment on the table beside her wallet, not touching it, and then he put it back.

On the steps, in a thin bright wind, the counsel stopped with her bag in her hand and looked up at Karis, who was a head taller and looked, just then, about twelve years old.

"You have a week, and a room," she said. "I could not promise you the room, and now you have it. Don't spend the week being grateful. A seat can smell gratitude, and it takes it for doubt." She tucked the rule into her sleeve. "And eat something. You have the look of a person who has decided that food is for afterward."

"Within the hour," said Karis. It was the first thing she had said since they sat down. "I've sixty-four minutes."

"Then you have four too many, and you'll find them faster than you think. They are always in the part you like best."

She went down the market street toward the wharf. Karis stood on the bottom step with the wallet against her chest, looking after her, and her lips were moving. She had begun to cut.

---

Ephram had the Crown yard to himself on the First-day at the second bell, as he had had it on every First-day for three years.

Cael came along the rim on his way to the wing with a floor sheet he no longer needed to lodge, out of a habit that had outlived its use, and stopped at the rail. The oak lay four feet below him in the hole somebody had dug for it, sixty feet by forty, pale and dry, with the stone tiers going up empty on three sides. On the fourth side the porter's four black boards stood on their wall above the faculty stand, chalked with the year's names. Ephram's was at the head of the Iron column, where it had stood since before Cael came up the road.

He was running the six steps.

It was the Blade drill he had been running on the day he first called Cael down to the oak, slow, against nobody, with the lath low in his right hand. Heel. Hip. Edge. He did it three times in a row without a pause between, each a different way in, and each began before the last had ended, so that the three made one thing. Then he walked back to his chalk and did it again.

On the fourth time through he did something to the last of the three that Cael had not seen him do before.

It was shorter. Cael could not have said how, from the rim; the heel went down, and then the lath was simply there, a beat sooner than the first two had taught the eye to expect it. Ephram stopped and looked at the place where it had finished, and shook his head, and walked back to his chalk.

"You're early," he said, without turning round. "Floor sheets go in at the third bell."

"I haven't one to lodge. The hours are complete."

"I know they are. I ruled the line." Ephram turned then, and came to the edge of the oak under the rail, and stood looking up with the lath across his shoulders and the sweat drying on him. He had a plain open face, and it had never been much use to him for keeping things in. "I'm keeping something," he said. "I'd like you to know I'm keeping it, so that you don't think I've forgotten."

"You've never forgotten anything."

"No." He did not smile. "It's a thing to ask you. I've had it ready since the tenth sitting's week, and I'm holding it until after the last Fifth-day. I'll not ask a man for anything while a seat has him. It wouldn't be a fair ask. You couldn't say no to me properly, and I'd never know whether you'd said yes."

Cael looked down at him.

He found that he was not afraid of it. A month ago he would have been. He had stood at a rail in wing three and watched Ephram write on the back of a sheet, and had gone cold, because for two years the worst thing anybody on the hill could do to him was to ask. Now there was a date he could count to, and after it there would be either no band or a door, and in neither case anything left to guard from a friend.

"After the last Fifth-day," he said.

"After." Ephram nodded, once, the captain's nod, and went back to his chalk. "Tell your advocate the whole cohort's coming down to hear her. I've done the rota. Nobody's to cheer. I've told them a seat fines for it, which I made up."

He set his feet, and the six steps began again, slow, and the third one came short.

---

Karis read the slips to the window for three days, and on the fourth night she asked for the key.

"Not for the floor," she said. She had come to Lira's door with the wallet under her arm and Hesk's clock in her hand, which she had taken off Cael's shelf without asking. "For the room. I've been saying it to a pane of glass, and a pane of glass doesn't sit there in a hood deciding not to like me. I want to say it standing up, to people, with somebody timing who isn't me." She held out the clock. "It loses. Add two."

So they went down after the tenth bell, the five of them, to the east hall, by the ways they always went.

It was the honest floor's last night, though nobody called it that. Lira lit the two lanterns and drew the gallery shutters. The painted crosses and arrows of the program ran away into the dark at the far end, and the frames stood on their heavy springs along the wall, and the hall smelled of resin and lamp oil and cold.

"Where's the seat?" said Karis.

There was a short silence, and then Brom went to the corner where the stable had left a pile of things for the wagon, and came back with a grey horse blanket. He carried Rooke's chair out from the table to the middle of the north end, and sat down in it, and put the blanket over his head.

It came to his knees. He looked like a haystack that somebody had left out in the rain.

"The seat will consider the motion at the close," said the blanket, in Brom's voice, very slowly.

Lira made a sound like a kettle and sat down on the floor.

"No," said Karis. "No, that's — you mustn't. I have to be able to do it without —" She stopped, because the corners of her own mouth had gone, and she pressed the back of her wrist against them. "It's very like," she said, when she could. "Stay there. Don't say anything else. It never says anything else."

"So ordered," said the blanket.

"I'll be the other one." Lira got up and went to the opposite wall and stood with her hands at her sides and her chin a little raised, and Cael saw, with some alarm, how well she had watched. "I shan't need a folder. I never do. I'm going to agree with everything you say in a pleasant voice, and you're going to lose anyway. It's the whole of my method."

"Sit down, Advocate," said Karis, and Lira sat, exactly as Jent sat, and screwed down the lid of an inkwell that was not there.

Seln had not come onto the floor. He stood at the great door with one hand on its edge, holding it, as Brom had held it on the night they measured ten feet; he had said on the stair that he would take the door tonight, since the seat could not. He watched the blanket for a moment with no expression at all.

"I have sat under four seats," he said. "That is the first I have liked."

Cael set Hesk's clock on the bench where he could see it under the lantern, and Karis opened the wallet.

She did not read them. He had thought she would. She laid the thirty slips face up along the edge of Rooke's table in three rows, in their order, and looked at them once, and then she turned her back on them and spoke to a horse blanket.

It was a strange thing to hear in that hall. He knew pieces of it; he had fetched the volumes for it, and held a cord while she tied it up. He had not heard it whole. She began with a list, and went from the list to a question, and from the question to a scale, and her voice, which was a small dry voice that had never carried across a refectory, filled the east hall to its rafters. Somewhere near the twelfth slip Lira forgot to be the petitioner and sat with her arms round her knees. At the nineteenth Karis stopped, and looked at nothing, and went on to the twentieth. The blanket did not stir once in all that time, and Cael did not know how a man of Brom's size kept so still for so long under wool.

"That is the whole of the respondent's close," said Karis.

He looked at the clock, and added two.

"Sixty-four."

"I know. I could feel it." She had turned back to the table and was already moving slips, and her face was pink. "Everything from the sixth to the eleventh is the renewals and the nine days. He conceded the renewals. He adopted my table; he said so again this morning. I'm proving a thing to a seat that the other side has signed." She took five slips out of the first row and laid them face down. "I liked those. She said they'd be in the part I liked best."

"That's five," said Lira. "You need to lose four minutes."

"Those five are nine minutes. I'm putting some of it back, further on. There's a place where I go too fast because I'm frightened of it."

She did not say which place, and nobody asked. Cael thought of the nineteenth slip, and of the breath she had taken before the twentieth.

Brom had come out from under the blanket with his hair on end. He folded it in four, squarely, as he folded everything, and laid it over the back of Rooke's chair.

"It was good," he said. "I'd not have known what to do with it, sitting there. I'd have wanted to say yes after the first quarter hour, and I'd have gone on wanting to, and I couldn't have told you which clause to do it under." He looked at the chair. "Maybe that's what it's like under the real one."

Nobody answered that. It was too near.

They put the hall to rights between them. Lira went down the painted floor to the far lantern, and on the way she did what she did on any floor now, without thinking about it: three lies and the truth, a small low burst, and a landing. "Left," said Brom, a beat after she was in the air, in the blanket's voice, and she came down on the left and laughed.

Karis had the slips back in the wallet, the twenty-five and the five, in two bundles. She stood by the bench with Hesk's clock in her hand, and did not give it back.

"Say it," she said to Cael. "You said it in the reading room in the recess. I want to hear it again before I stand up in front of that hood, so that I know exactly what I'm doing."

He knew what she meant.

"It isn't ground," he said. "None of it. It's the best thing I've ever heard anybody say, and it's true from the first slip to the last, and it won't move that seat an inch."

"Good." She let her breath out. "Now I can say it as if it were. I couldn't, if I were still hoping. Hoping makes you hurry." She put the clock into his hand at last, and her fingers were cold. "Thank you for timing me. I'll want you where I can see you, on the day. Not to look at. Only there."

"I sit behind the Chancellor."

"I know where you sit."

He stood by the great door a moment after the others had gone through it, with the clock ticking in his hand and losing its minutes. Seln still held the door, and the lanterns were out behind them. He had laughed tonight, at a haystack in a chair, until his ribs remembered the road. He could not think when he had last laughed like that on the hill.

He knew, then, what he would be sorry to lose. It was not the enrollment. He had been defending that for a year, in a coach, with his back straight, and at some time in the year it had become a word in a column. What he would be sorry to lose was a painted floor at night, with a man under a blanket and a girl being a procurator and an archivist who had asked to be told the truth so that she could do her work.

None of that was in the seat's gift. The hood could not revoke it, and no chapter of the code could restrict it.

"*Door*," he said, under his breath, because the new thing had lifted its head at the threshold, as it still did. "*It's held.*"

"It is," said Seln, and let it go behind them.

# Chapter 49 — Fifty-One Minutes

Karis ate nothing on the morning of the second closing, and Brom carried her breakfast down the hill in his coat.

Cael saw him do it. He took two apples from the bowl at the refectory door, as he had taken them for a year, and then he looked at Karis's place at the table, where the porridge stood untouched and going cold, and went back and took two more. He put them in the right-hand pocket, the one with the slip in it, and buttoned the flap.

In the coach she sat with the wallet on her knees and did not open it. She had the twenty-five slips by heart; he had heard her say them through twice more since the east hall, once to the reading room's window and once, at midnight, to the landing, when she thought the stair was empty. She looked out at the switchbacks going by, and her fingers lay on the leather, quite still, and paler than the rest of her hand, as they would be for the rest of her life.

"Fifty-nine," she said, at the bridge. "With the five out and the piece put back. I timed it against your clock and added two."

"You've a minute in hand," said Bracken.

"I've a minute I mean to spend being slow." She did not turn her head. "I go too fast when I'm frightened. I've marked the places."

The market street was full. People stood on the four steps of the wool exchange and down the plinth and out across the cobbles as far as the trough, so that the coachman had to walk his horses through them to the stable arch with the brake half on. Faces turned to the glass as they went by, and nobody shouted anything. It was a wool town, and it knew what a closing was; you came, and stood, and heard which way the price had gone.

At the side door the young officer wrote the first two lines without looking up, and then looked up to write the third.

*Advocate of record for the respondent, under the hearing provision.*

He had written it at every sitting since the fourth. This morning he wrote it more slowly, and when he had finished he turned the board round on his arm, which he had never done before, so that she could read it the right way up.

"Thank you," said Karis, and went in.

---

The long room was fuller than it had been a week ago, and Cael did not count it.

He had counted it once, and the sum had told him what he needed to know. He sat down behind Withrow's chair with the lot number under his boot and looked instead at the things that were different. The whole of Ephram's cohort was on the ninth and tenth benches, in their greys, sitting like people who had been threatened. The lake house's instructor had brought her Blade, who had his lath in its canvas sleeve between his knees. And Rooke was there, who had never come down for a sitting; he stood against the back wall by the doors with his arms folded and his board under one of them, as he stood at the east rail on a meet day.

On the fourth bench Lira had her hands under her, as Karis had sat on hers in the Crown yard, and Brom sat beside her with his coat buttoned over four apples. Gault had his case very straight on his knees. The assistant was holding something flat against her chest, and Cael saw the edge of it and knew it for a clean sheet of the house's good paper, which she had brought to write on and would not put a word to. At the end of the bench, by the pillar, Seln sat as he had sat last week, with his hands in his lap, looking at nothing.

Ilsev was in the middle chair, reading, and her four evaluators had their pens out. At the records table the senior chair had the minute and Havel had the times.

Across the aisle Jent took his spectacles off their black cord, and put them on.

Cael watched him do it. In thirteen sittings the man had worn them to look at the sky and to read a leaf, and had taken them off to speak and to listen alike. He put them on now, and folded his hands on his shut folder, and turned his whole body a little in his chair toward the house's table, as Brom turned toward a frame.

"The seat," said the clerk.

It came in by its own door and sat, grey and still.

"In the matter of Caelen Hesk-ward. Upon the close. The thirteenth sitting. The advocate of record for the respondent, by the seat's leave."

Karis stood.

She was nineteen, and thin, and she had put her hair back so tightly that it pulled at the corners of her eyes. She had left the wallet shut on the table. She came round the end of it with nothing in her hands and stood on the worn boards between the two tables, where the house's counsel had stood, and where no enrollee of any house had stood in that district in living memory, and she looked up at the hood.

She waited. He had not known she would do that. She let the room finish settling, and went on waiting until the last bench had stopped creaking, and for three breaths more.

"The house does not ask the seat to find anything the petitioner disputes," she said. "It could not. There is nothing left that he disputes."

Her voice was the one she had found in the east hall. It was not loud, but it was placed, like a thing set down on a table, and it went to the back wall.

"I should like to begin by reading the seat a list. It is the list of what is agreed between the parties, and it is the longest such list I have found in any contested proceeding in the digests. I looked, because I wanted to be able to say so truthfully." She did not look down, for there was nothing to look down at. "That the enrollment was lawfully executed. That the provision it was executed under is law. That the renewals are good upon their days. That the evaluator of record is competent in every particular. That every figure he has signed was honestly come by. That the tournament office's record is accurate. That the house has kept within its charter. Seven things. The seat held the first two at the preliminary, and the petitioner gave me the other five himself, across three sittings, without being asked twice."

She turned her head toward Jent, the inch she always gave him.

"I thanked him for the first of them. I did not thank him for the rest, because my counsel told me not to, and she was right. I thank him now. It is a rare thing to be opposed by somebody who will not keep a point he does not own."

Jent bowed where he sat. The counsel's steel rule lay on the table by Cael's elbow with her hand beside it, and neither moved.

"So I shall not prove anything this morning," said Karis. "There is nothing to prove. I have an hour, and I am going to spend it on one sentence, which is not mine."

---

"At the fourth sitting I was allowed one question," she said. "I asked the petitioner what evidence, if any, would defeat the petition. His answer is in the minute, and I have read it most days since."

She said it as it had been said. She did not imitate him; she only gave it its weight, a word at a time.

"*None, Advocate. The petition doesn't turn on evidence. It turns on discretion.*"

Nobody on the benches moved.

"That was an honest answer, and it is the whole of this case. Every leaf the house has filed went to the facts, and the petitioner has agreed the facts. What is left is a discretion. The registry may ask this question, the seat has held, if it chooses to, and I do not come here to say that it may not; I am bound by the seat's holding and I respect it." She paused. "I come to ask what the discretion is for."

She had been frightened of this part, he thought, and she took it slowly.

"A discretion is not a blank leaf. Nobody who drafts a code hands an officer a blank leaf. A discretion is a tool, and it is handed over with its purpose written on the handle, and where a code is well made the purpose is written down. In this code it is. The sections under which the petition is brought have a preamble, and the house's counsel read it to this seat at the preliminary. It took her ninety seconds. I shall take less."

She gave it to them plainly, as a thing that belonged to the room: that the provisions existed for the testing of instruments, of frames and stations and records and protocols, and that they had been written so that the registry might know which of its own instruments it could trust.

"Instruments," said Karis. "That is the handle. I went and read how the tool has been used, to see whether the hands that held it could read, and they could. There are ninety-one recorded invocations of these sections in the registry's whole record. Eighty-four concern things: a frame that read a rank high, a station seam that let in the damp, a plate, a ledger, a seal. The sixty-third was filed by the registrar who is sitting at this table, in his fourth year, upon a station frame in this town, and the registry thanked him for it." Bracken looked at the seal's box. "Seven concern persons. I have read all seven, and every one is about something the person did. A false return. A rank that was bought. A seal that was lent. In all that record these sections have never once been turned upon a person for what he is."

She let that stand.

"I am not telling the seat anything the petitioner denies. He was asked at the preliminary whether the use was new, and he said that it was, and struck out a sentence of his own that had suggested otherwise, and the seat minuted the word. *Novel.* It is in the minute of the third sitting."

In the row of chairs below the platform one of the four evaluators had stopped writing and was simply listening, with his pen held a little off the page, and Ilsev had not turned a leaf since Karis stood.

"So," said Karis. "A tool for the testing of instruments, used ninety-one times as it was made to be used, and now to be used a ninety-second time in a new way. I should like to look at the new way, and I shall be exact about it. Being exact is my discipline, and I have no other."

She came half a step nearer the platform, and Cael did not think she knew that she had done it.

"The petition asks that the enrollee be stood in a frame, under the standard protocol, and it calls that an evaluation. I want to say what an evaluation is. It is a question whose answer is not known beforehand; that is all it is, and it is the only thing that makes it one. When a wool-factor in this room lays a fleece on his scale, he does not know what it weighs. If he knew, he would not weigh it. He would write the figure down and go to his dinner." Somebody at the back laughed, shortly, and was hushed. "A weighing whose result is known before the fleece goes on the pan is not a weighing. It is a ceremony."

She held up her left hand, pale fingers spread, and turned one down.

"Is the answer known? The registry's own tournament office has entered it, on the record of the continental meet, under a regional registrar's seal. *Unscorable under standard.* That is the registry writing down, in its own book, what its own protocol does with this practitioner."

A second finger.

"The Compact's senior evaluator advised upon the question in three sentences, and the petition quotes them, with approval, at its seventh paragraph. I did not put them there. They say that the enrollment is lawful upon its face, and that if the Compact wishes practitioners of this kind not to compete it should write different rules. *Practitioners of this kind.* Nobody writes that of a man he expects a frame to read."

A third.

"At the third sitting the evaluation seat asked this seat whether its scope reached the question of whether the standard protocol can read this practitioner at all, and the seat answered that it did not. I make no complaint of the answer. I say only that the question was thought worth asking, by the most careful officer in this room, and that it is minuted."

Ilsev looked up. It was only for a moment, over the top of her file, at the thin girl on the boards; then she looked down again, and her hand moved to the foot of a leaf and wrote something small.

"And the fourth," said Karis, and her little finger went down. "I asked. I asked the petitioner what evidence would defeat his petition, and a reading in the frame is evidence, and he told me there was none. He did not say, *let us see what the frame returns.* He is too honest a man to say so, and in thirteen sittings he never has. Nobody has. No officer, no clerk, no witness has stood in this room and told the seat that the frame will return a classification, because everybody here knows what it will return. It will return nothing. It was asked once before, at a district station, when he was fourteen, and it took eleven seconds to answer, and its answer is the word on his file."

She lowered her hand.

Cael sat very still behind Withrow's chair. He knew what the eleven seconds had been, and so did she. She had read them to him out of a locked case, with a glass, on a night when she wore gloves; and she had just laid them before a seat as a plain fact from a district's record, with nothing behind them, because that was all she was allowed to lay.

---

"So this is the remedy," said Karis. "Stand a man on a scale that every officer present knows cannot weigh him. Write down that the scale returned nothing. And then apply to him the chapter of the code that opens by supposing an evaluation under the standard protocol which *returns no valid classification.*" She gave the four words their full length. "I have read the fourteenth chapter a great many times this year. It is a careful piece of drafting. It was written for the case in which the registry tried, in good faith, to find out what a person was, and could not, and it gives the registry something to do in that case, so that the person is not left in no column at all. It is harsh, and I think it was meant as a kindness."

Withrow's collar did not move.

"It was not written to be reached on purpose. No drafter writes a remedy for *where the registry has made sure of failing*. And that is what is asked. The seat is asked to order a test in order to obtain a failure, so that the failure may open the chapter."

She stopped, and breathed. He saw her shoulders go down a little, and knew she had come to one of the marked places and was spending her minute there.

"Now I come back to the handle. These sections are for the testing of instruments, so that the registry may know which of them to trust, and there are two instruments before the seat. One is a frame, which was asked about this practitioner once and had no answer. The other is twenty-two officers of the registry. The house's counsel read their names to the seat at the preliminary, each with a grade and a house and a day: a provost's panel, a regional registrar's assessors, the tournament's, an evaluator of record who has sat across a trestle from him every week for two years. They watched, and wrote down what they saw, and signed it. Their notes agree with one another, and the petitioner has accepted every one."

Her voice had not risen. It had only grown very clear.

"Twenty-two officers can see him, and one instrument cannot. The preamble tells the registry what to do when its instruments disagree: it is to find out which of them is to be trusted. It does not say that the registry is to prefer the one that failed, and fine the man for the failure."

At the back wall Rooke had unfolded his arms.

She took one breath, and Cael saw her right hand move at her side.

It was a small movement, and he thought nobody else in the long room saw it, not even Lira. Her thumb crossed her palm and pressed, once, hard, against the place where her fingers began to go pale, as if she were turning something over.

There was no slip in her hand; the slips were in the wallet on the table, twenty-five in one bundle and five in the other. But he had heard her say the close three times through, and three times she had stopped after this part, and looked at nothing, and gone on. He had counted the pause in the east hall, and it was four seconds long. She stopped now, and looked at the grey shape above her for four seconds.

He knew what lay in that pause as well as he knew his own Log. It was the true argument. She had just told a seat, with perfect accuracy, that its instrument had failed to read him, and she knew why it had failed, and what was appended to the reading, and how long it had been so, and whose hands had signed for it. She could have dated the thing to within a renewal of Greyvane's charter. It would have taken her nine minutes, and she had the nine minutes.

And it could not be said, in this room or in any room that stood on it. She had worked that out herself, over five nights, and had been the one to tell them. So she turned it face down. She did it where she stood, in front of two hundred people, with her thumb, and nobody saw.

He had been sorry all year that she would have to argue as if the room were ground. He had not understood until now that she would also have to stand in it and leave out the best thing she knew, and that nothing in her face would say that it weighed anything. It was like watching Brom hold a bar.

---

"I have nearly done," said Karis.

She told them what the house had made. It was not a boast, and she did not dress it; it was two years of a practitioner's work set down by the people whose office it was to set it down. This year alone there were seventeen panel notes, produced entire, and the leaf nobody had asked for was among them. There were a hundred and seventy-nine exhibits, and not one had been found out of its order; the records table had counted them, she was told, and she was content to rest on the records table's count. At that table the junior chair's pen stopped for the length of a word and went on.

"The petitioner called it the best-documented enrollment he had reviewed in thirty years, and I believe him. If the remedy is granted, the registry will strike that record. In its place it will enter three words: *no valid classification.* It will exchange the fullest true entry in its books for an empty one, and it will do that under sections whose only purpose is that entries be true."

She looked along the five chairs, and then up.

"I do not say that would be unlawful. I say it is the one use of this discretion that defeats what the discretion is for."

Then she spoke of the people who would come after, and she did it quickly, for she had cut it twice. A ruling stood in the registry's books, she said, for every house that held a charter in the same words. The seat had before it four old challenges to the clause, and in one of them a ferryman's daughter in a southern river house had waited a year for a station to settle upon what she was; the house had waited with her, and at the end of the year the station had read her, and she had stayed. Under the ruling now asked for, nobody need have waited. Somebody need only have filed.

"The next person these sections reach will not have a chancellor who answers under escort," said Karis. "She will not have a registrar who reads every leaf, or an evaluator who hands up the note nobody wanted. She will not have an hour. I have had all of those, and I know what they are worth, and I know what it cost the people who gave them."

She stood quite still.

"The house asks for one thing. It does not ask the seat to hold the petition unlawful. It asks the seat to use the discretion it has for the purpose that is written on it. The entry is true. Twenty-two of the registry's own officers made it. Leave it standing." Her voice did not break, though he had been afraid that it would. "That is the whole of the respondent's close."

She went back round the end of the table and sat down.

Cael looked up at the exchange's clock over the doors, because he had promised to.

Fifty-one minutes. She had dropped nine more slips on her feet, in front of the hood, without a mark on her face to show where, and when he went back over it he found them: every place where she would have told the seat a thing the other side had already signed. *I dislike sitting through an argument whose answer I already hold*, Jent had said on a step, and she had heard him. She had given a registry seat fifty-one minutes with nothing in them that anybody in the room already owned.

Nobody made a sound. Ephram had told his cohort that a seat fined for cheering, and the ninth and tenth benches sat like two rows of posts.

"The petitioner," said the hood. "If he is so advised."

Jent stood. He took off his spectacles, and let them fall on their cord, and stood for a moment with his hands at his sides.

"The registry does not dispute a syllable of it," he said.

He said it to the platform, and then he turned and said the rest to her.

"I closed a week ago, and I will not close twice. I rise only so that the advocate is not left to wonder whether she was heard. She was. Every proposition she has put to the seat is true. The petitioner adopts her account of what is asked, in her own words, the scale included; I could not have stated my own petition so exactly." He paused. "And it stands where it stood. She has shown the seat that the discretion would be unwisely used, and I think she has shown it beyond answer. But a discretion is not defeated by being shown to be unwise. It is only exercised, or it is not, and that is the seat's and never was mine."

He sat down.

It was the best that could have been said, and it was the worst, for he had not left her so much as a quarrel to go home with. Cael looked at the side of Karis's face, and it was perfectly composed, and her hands lay flat on the shut wallet.

"The seat has heard the parties," said the hood. "Before it reserves, the clerk will read the standing entries."

The clerk rose with the minute book.

It was the dullest reading of the year, and it was meant to be: the count of a proceeding, set down so that a ruling could stand on it. Thirteen sittings held, upon their days. Exhibits of the petitioner, nine, received. Exhibits of the respondent, a hundred and seventy-nine, received, with the supplemental submissions and one correction slip. Orders of production, two, complied with. Statements entered under the provision for such statements, one, noted and set aside. The evaluation seat constituted at the third sitting, its scope as minuted.

The four district evaluators were writing it down. They would have returns of their own to make, and they copied each line as it came, four pens moving together along the row.

The clerk turned a leaf, and there was a form pasted to the next one. Cael could see the edge of it from where he sat, a single sheet of registry stock under the seal with the river and the scale. He knew the hand that had written on its ruled line, though he had never seen the hand. He had seen the man; he had sat across a cold stove from him, four streets back from this room, and heard him say what he would write.

The clerk read the entry as he had read the others, in the code's own old words, as the form's column was headed.

"*Evaluation-protocol certification: declined by the evaluator of record; proceeding on the presiding seat's authority.*"

Four pens stopped.

It was a small thing to see, and he did not think the benches saw it. The four district evaluators had been writing in a row, and at that line they stopped, one after another, like men on a road who see the one ahead halt. They were working officers. Every one of them had stood practitioners in frames for years, under a protocol that carried the Archmarshal's certificate as a matter of course, as a loaf carries a baker's mark; that office had been the protocol's evaluator of record since before any of them was born. It had been asked twice for its name and had not given it, and the form had no line for why.

The evaluator nearest the records table looked at Ilsev. Ilsev did not look at him. She was looking at the clerk, with her file shut on her knee, and her face told Cael nothing whatever.

On the fourth bench Gault had not moved. He was the house's own evaluator of record, the only man in that room who was called so every day, and he had declined nothing in his life. He sat with his case straight on his knees and his eyes on the pasted form, and then he took out his small book and wrote a line in it.

"So entered," said the hood, and the pens began again.

That was the whole of it. Five words had gone out from a desk by a noon dispatch, after a man had weighed them for four days on a post road and found them worth sending though they would stop nothing. They had stopped nothing. They were in the minute as an absence, between the count of the exhibits and the count of the orders, and the clerk was already reading the next line.

Cael found that it was enough, and it surprised him. He had wanted a wall, a month ago, and had been given a blank on a form. But he had watched Gault hand up a leaf because it was there, and he had heard Karis spend fifty-one minutes on a room that could not be moved, and he thought he was beginning to learn how the honest ones did it. They did not do it to win. They did the thing that was theirs to do, to its full weight, and let it be entered.

"The seat reserves," said the hood. "It will give its ruling upon the last Fifth-day of the academic year, at the hour after noon. From the First-day of that week the parties will keep within the bell, at the seat's call and the evaluation seat's. So ordered."

"So ordered."

The seat rose, and went out by its own door.

---

The house's counsel caught Karis on the second step, by the sleeve, which Cael had never seen her do to anybody.

The crowd was coming out past them on both sides, quietly, like people leaving a place of worship. Rooke had gone down through it already with his board under his arm, and had touched two fingers to the board's edge as he passed Karis, which from Rooke was a speech.

"Twenty-six years," said the counsel. She was looking up, with her small grey eyes very bright, and she still had hold of the sleeve. "Twenty-six years in front of seats, and I have heard perhaps four closings I should have been glad to sign. That is the best argument I have ever heard made in one of those rooms, and I include every one of mine. I want you to have that from somebody who is paid to know." She let go. "It will not have moved him. You knew that. You did it as though you didn't, and that is the whole of the trade."

"He agreed with it," said Karis. Her voice had gone back to its usual size. "All of it. He stood up and agreed."

"He did. I have never been so thoroughly beaten by a man agreeing with me, and neither have you." The counsel picked up her bag. "Go home. Eat."

In the coach Brom unbuttoned his right-hand pocket and held out an apple, and Karis took it and ate it in about nine bites, core and all but the stalk, and then he held out the second and she ate that. Then she said, to nobody, "I left out the tournament charter's provisions. I meant to. I had a note for the cold term," and she put her head against the leather beside the window and was asleep before the bridge.

She did not cry, though he had wondered whether she would. She slept with her mouth a little open and the wallet sliding off her knees, and Brom put out one hand and held it there, flat, all the way up the switchbacks.

Lira sat opposite, looking at her.

"She was better than him," she said, very low. "She was better than everybody. And it's going to go against us anyway, isn't it?"

"Yes," said Cael.

"Then what was it for?"

He thought about it while the coach climbed. He had asked himself the same question in a reading room in the recess, in harder words than hers, and Karis had answered that she would write it as if it were ground and pay what that cost. He looked for the anger he had felt that day, and it was not there.

"It wasn't for the seat," he said. "I thought it was, all year. It's in the minute now. Every word she said is there under her own name, in the registry's own presses, for as long as there's a registry, and somebody will read it. Some girl in a reading room with ink on her collar, looking for something else." He watched Karis's pale fingers, open on her knee. "And it was for her. She wanted to do the thing properly, once, with everybody looking. She's done it."

Lira said nothing for a while.

"I'd have cried," she said at last. "I nearly did, and I wasn't even the one saying it."

"I know. I saw you sit on your hands."

Bracken, in the corner, had the seal on his knees and his eyes shut.

"Fourteen," he said. "One to come."

# Chapter 50 — Within the Bell

The rule was older than the wool exchange, and the house's counsel explained it on the First-day morning in the coach, with her bag on her knees and her steel rule lying across the bag.

"Call days," she said. "Four of them, before any ruling. In those four days a seat may recall a party to put a question it has thought of late, and so may its evaluation seat, and the code gives them an hour's notice to do it in. An hour, on foot, from wherever the party is lodged." She looked out at the switchbacks. "That was a fair rule when every respondent lived over his own shop in the town. It was not written with a bluff in mind. Your hill is an hour and a quarter from that room by a good horse, and so for four days the house must live where it can hear the exchange's bell. The code calls it keeping within the bell. I have always thought it rather a handsome phrase for a nuisance."

"Has a seat ever used it?" said Karis. "The recall."

"In my time? Twice. Once to ask a guild master his mother's maiden name, which had been left off a form." The counsel's mouth moved at one corner. "Seats do not think of things late. They are very thorough about thinking of them early. But the rule stands, and we keep it, and in four days nobody will be able to say that the house was not where it was told to be."

So they came down to Ostrand with trunks. Withrow rode in her own carriage and Bracken beside her with the seal. The coach carried Karis and Cael, as it always did, and with them Brom, whose panel sat in the same town on the same Fifth-day, and Lira, who had no business there at all and had got Rooke's leave in four words. *She's drilled in worse*, the card on the east hall door had said, and under it, smaller, *Bring her back sound*.

Seln stayed on the hill. He had a schedule to post and a return to write, and he had stood at the wing's door that morning with his hands in his pockets and watched the coach go, as he might have watched any coach.

The house's lodging was the wool-factors' guesting-house, in the lane behind the exchange. It was a tall narrow building of old brick with a gallery along its back, and it smelled of lanolin, as everything did in that quarter of the town. The factors kept it for buyers who came upriver at the sales. Between sales it stood three parts empty, and the woman who kept it had given the house its whole upper floor for four nights at a price that made Bracken take off his second pair of glasses and look at her.

From the window of the room he was to share with Brom, Cael could see the green cupola over the roofs, and hear the bell under it. He could see, too, the upper window of the inn across the lane, where a man in a travelling coat sat with a cup and did not pretend to be doing anything but sitting there.

"They've moved in," said Brom, behind him. He was unpacking, which for Brom meant taking four things out of a bag and squaring each of them on the press. "I saw the other one at the lane's end with the horses. They didn't want the walk down from the road foot every morning either."

"They're keeping within the bell."

"Everybody is." Brom laid his whetstone in its sock beside his razor. He had not brought the envelope; it was on his table on the hill, square to the edge, with its seal up. He had brought the summons and the two dockets and the schedule, and they were in the left pocket, and his hand went there now and came away. "Four days. I never thought I'd want a Fifth-day to hurry."

---

He saw Withrow that evening, through glass, and did not know what he was seeing.

He had come down to the ground floor for a candle. The guesting-house had a parlour at the back, a square room with a long table where the factors settled their accounts, and its door had two panes set in the upper half. The lamp was lit inside, and he would have gone past if a small bright thing on the table had not moved, and ticked.

It was the steel rule.

The counsel sat at the table's end with the code open in front of her, the fat volume with the schedules, and a single sheet beside it that was not the house's paper. Withrow sat across from her with both hands on the wood. Bracken stood by the hearth with his back to the room. His spectacles were pushed up on his forehead and he held a slip of paper in his hand, and he looked like a man who had read something off a wall and wished he had not been the one to walk past it.

The counsel was speaking. Cael could not hear a word. He saw her lay one finger on a line in the code and then on the sheet, and then on the code again, lower down, as a person does who is adding days. He saw the rule turn its quarter turn at the end of it.

Withrow said something short, and the counsel answered, and then for a long moment nobody in that room spoke at all.

Then Withrow reached across the table and shut the code herself, with one hand, quite gently. She drew the single sheet toward her and folded it once and put it inside her coat. She looked up as she did it, and saw him through the pane.

Her face did nothing. It was the registry face, the one she had worn at her own gate under four riders, and it rested on him for the space of a breath and gave him no more than a shut door would have. Then she turned her head and said something to Bracken, and Bracken pulled his spectacles down.

Cael took his candle and went up.

He told himself it was the ruling they were preparing for, some form to be ready, some order of who should stand where. It was very likely that. But he lay awake a while with Brom breathing across the room like a slow bellows, and he kept seeing her hand come across and close the book. It was the way a person shuts a ledger after reading the sum at the foot of it.

---

There was a wool press in the yard behind the guesting-house, and by the second morning Brom and Lira had found a use for it.

Cael came out onto the gallery with the satchel on his shoulder and looked down. The yard was flagged, with a pump in one corner and a stack of empty woolsacks under a pentice roof. In the middle stood the press, a thing as old as the building: two oak posts as thick as a man, a beam across their tops, and a great wooden screw coming down through the beam to a platen of black timber six feet square. A capstan with four long bars turned the screw. In sale weeks, the woman of the house had said, it took four men to bale a sack with it. It was not a sale week.

Brom stood under the platen.

He had his coat off and his feet set and his forearms crossed above his head, the right over the left, and the platen rested on them. Lira was at the capstan with both hands on a bar and her heels dug in, walking it round a quarter turn at a time. With every quarter the black timber came down a finger's breadth, and Brom took it.

"More," said Brom.

"That's all of me. I weigh what I weigh."

"It's not enough. Ephram put more on than that in the old frames, and the clerk said they'd set it a little over." He did not sound out of breath, only patient. "It's steady, as well. A frame isn't steady. It comes down on its springs all at once."

Lira let go of the bar and stood back with her hands on her hips and looked at the press as she looked at a floor.

"All at once," she said.

She walked away from it, ten paces across the flags, and turned.

Cael saw what she was going to do a beat before she did it. He had no time to say anything, and Brom, under the platen, could not see her.

She ran the count, quite small, on wet flags, front foot and back foot and shoulders. Then she went. It was one low burst, flat across the yard, and the end of it was the capstan bar, a round of ash no thicker than her wrist and three feet off the ground. She came down on it with her right foot.

The bar dropped under her and the screw turned, and the platen came down on Brom's crossed forearms like the lid of a chest.

His knees gave an inch, and held.

Cael had let the old read go out across the flags without deciding to. It came back to him as it always did, in weight and where the weight went. The platen's whole mass, and a girl's, and the speed she had brought with her, ran down through two forearms into the shoulders, both alike now, and down the long back and through the heels into the stone. The skin had gone dull at the wrists. Brom stood under a wool press like a third post.

Lira was standing on the capstan bar on one foot with her arms out, perfectly balanced, grinning.

"*That's* a little over," she said.

"Again," said Brom.

They did it nine times. She had four free bursts on stone in a day, and she spent them, and after that she ran at the bar and jumped, which was not the same and was near enough. She took it on the right foot and then on the left, and once, for nobody's benefit that Cael could see, on the right with the left already swinging through for the step off. The bar was round and moving and wet, and she landed on it every time as she would have landed on a chalk mark. He thought of a girl in the east hall in the recess, touching one foot down and stepping to the other. He did not think she remembered it herself.

On the ninth Brom said, "Enough," and she walked the capstan back, and the platen rose off him, and the skin went back to being skin.

He sat down on the pump trough with his forearms on his knees. There were two red bars across them.

"Three days," said Lira. She had hitched herself up onto a capstan bar and was swinging her feet. "And then three people in a draughty hall write *Iron* on you, and you're a rank nobody can take off."

"They'll find it."

"You say that as if it were bad weather."

"I say it like I say anything." Brom turned his forearms over and looked at the marks. "I'll stand in it, and I'll hold what they set, and the plate will say what's so. I want that. I've wanted it since before I had a Path." He was quiet a moment. "It's the paper after that I've stopped being able to see properly. Somebody hands you a sheet. It says *found*, and there's a line at the bottom for your name."

Lira stopped swinging her feet.

"I went and looked at the district hall yesterday," she said. "From outside. It's three streets over. It's got a blue door, and a board beside it with the sittings on, and the first one after the year's close has six names. Mine's the fourth. Somebody's written *Wind* after it, and then *Halcenvane*." She picked at the tape on her wrist. "I stood there a good while. A carter asked me if I was lost."

"Were you?"

"Ask me after the Fifth-day."

Neither of them said anything more. Brom pumped water over his forearms, and Lira jumped down and went to look at the screw's thread, and after a while she asked him how you would grease a thing like that, and he told her.

On the gallery Cael stood with his hand on the satchel's new strap.

They had each finished something, and he could see it from up here as he could see a floor from a tier. Brom's left shoulder took its whole share. Lira landed where she chose. Whatever a panel or a blue door might add to either of them was a line on a sheet. They had done the work in a harness room and a painted hall and a wool yard, with nobody writing.

And there were two boxes roped under a bed, and a carter's book in a coat.

He had told himself all year that whatever this cost him, it should not cost them. Karis had read that over his shoulder and said it was not how any of them were built. Looking down at the two of them at the pump, he understood for the first time what she had meant by it. It was not his to decide what they paid. It never had been. He could only see to it that when the day came, each of them was asked, and not carried.

---

Karis filed her last paper that afternoon, and he went with her to carry the wallet.

It was three leaves. She had written it at the parlour's long table in the morning, in pencil first, from habit, and then in ink, and the counsel had read it standing and signed the foot without altering a word.

"It's upon the entry they read out," said Karis, as they crossed the lane. "The certificate. The code lets a seat go on by its own authority where the certificate's withheld; I looked at the section the night we got home. So I went back through the digests to see how often a seat ever had." She held up four fingers. "Four times. I've set them out, with their years. A guild hall's frame, where the assessor wouldn't sign. Two station frames in the hill districts. A plate. Four times in the whole record a seat has proceeded without that office's name, and every time it was upon a thing. Never once upon a person."

"It won't—"

"I know it won't. Don't say it; you said it on the floor, and I only needed it once." She was not cross. "It's inert. It goes in under my name and it sits there. But the count's true, and nobody else was going to make it, and I didn't like the seat to rule without having been told."

The seat's office was a hatch in the exchange's side passage, with a brass grille and a shelf. The junior chair sat behind it. Cael had never seen the man so close. He was a man with a careful face and a clerk's stoop, and he took the three leaves from Karis without lifting his eyes above her hands. He counted them and looked at the signature and entered them in a book against the hour. He wrote a receipt in a plain square hand, and tore it at the rule, and slid it under the grille.

"Received," he said. "It will be before the seat."

"Thank you," said Karis.

His right hand was opening and closing a little, under the shelf, as if it ached. He did not look up at either of them, and after a moment he drew the next thing in his tray toward him.

In the lane Karis folded the receipt into the wallet's back pocket, with all the others.

"That's the last one in this proceeding," she said, and shut the wallet. "I thought I'd feel more."

---

On the third day he walked north.

He was within his rights. The counsel had drawn the bound for him on a scrap of paper at breakfast: the bridge, the wharf, the east gate, and on the north road the toll-bar, beyond which a man could no longer be said to hear the bell. Inside that line he might go where he liked. He said where he was going to Brom, who nodded, and to Bracken, who wrote it down.

The coat came with him. It was the one from the upper window, the older of the pair, a grey patient man with a walking stick he did not need. He followed at forty yards, in plain sight, down the middle of the street, as Seln had taught four people in an orchard to walk when they wished to be written down as what they were. Cael found that he did not mind it. They had been walking the same roads for a long time.

The north road left the town between tanyards and came out among garden plots and low walls. A little short of the toll-bar, on the left, stood the district station.

He had known it was there. He had not come to see it. He saw it, and stopped at the horse trough on the far side of the road, and did not go on.

It was a low stone house with a slate roof, and behind it a second building, windowless, with a wide door, which was the frame-house. Over the front door a sigil was cut in the lintel. There was a bench under the wall, in the sun, and a board beside the door with the day's hours chalked on it in a round hand. It was the most ordinary building he had ever seen. There was one like it at the edge of every town on the continent that had a registry, and he had not stood in front of one since Denvash.

A girl sat on the bench with her mother.

She was about fourteen. She had on a clean brown dress that had been let down at the hem, and good boots, and she sat with her hands in her lap and her knees together and her back very straight, as she had plainly been told to sit. And somebody had done her hair. It was plaited in two braids and the braids were pinned up round her head in a crown, tight and even, with not a strand loose, and a narrow green ribbon was worked through the plait from one side to the other. It was not a style for an ordinary day. It had taken somebody the best part of an hour, early, by a window, with a mouth full of pins.

The mother sat beside her with a basket on her knees. Every little while her hand came up, without her seeming to know it, and touched the braid above the girl's ear, to be sure of a pin.

Cael stood at the trough.

He knew what was in that building. He knew it as he knew the inside of Hesk's shop. There was a room with a brass plate in it. The girl would be told to stand still, and to put her hand where she was shown, and something in her that had been asleep for fourteen years would wake, and the plate would light. In the frame-house a keeper would read what the frame gave him, and write it in his station book in two figures, a reading and a margin.

And then the thing that was appended to the reading, and was not the reading, would advise what was to be entered. It would be entered as advised.

He did not know that it would be different from what the plate had shown. Most were not. There had been forty pairs in a locked case, from a few districts over a few years, and Karis would not guess past them and Seln had stopped estimating. This girl might walk out of that door with the truth on a slip of paper. He would never know. That was the whole of the horror of it, standing in the sun by a trough. Neither would she. If the margin had run past her column, nobody would enter it, and somebody would be told to observe her for nine weeks, and would see a girl with a green ribbon going about her life, and would write *nothing to report*.

The door opened.

The district officer who came out was a stout, fair, unhurried man in registry grey with his sleeves turned back, and he had a board in his hand. He looked at the bench and smiled. It was a real smile. He said a name that Cael was too far off to catch, and then something else, and the girl laughed in spite of herself and stood up.

The mother stood too, and was told, kindly, by a hand held out flat, that she was to wait. She sat down again. The girl went in under the sigil with her crown of braids, and the officer held the door for her as he might have held it for his own daughter.

The new thing in Cael lifted its head. It always did, at a threshold, and this was more of a threshold than most. *There?*

*Door*, he told it. *A station's. It's theirs. Leave it.*

It left it.

He waited. He did not count, for once. The mother sat with the basket and looked at the door. The coat, forty yards back, had stopped by a garden wall and was looking at a row of beans. He had seen a thousand stations, and this one had nothing in it for his column.

It did not take long. It had taken eleven seconds once, in another town, and a woman behind a desk had said a word, and he had walked out into a different life. This took perhaps as long as it takes to boil an egg.

The girl came out with a slip of paper in her hand.

She was reading it as she came, and she had gone pink to the ears. She said something to her mother. Cael saw her lips make the word, and then, because once was not enough, make it again. The mother put the basket down on the bench and took the girl's face in both hands, carefully, with her thumbs clear of the braids, and kissed her on the forehead.

In the doorway the officer stood and watched them. He was pleased. Anybody could have seen it. He had done a thing he had done ten thousand times, and done it properly, and a child was happy, and he liked his work. He looked at his board, and turned it over, and went in to see who was next.

Cael would have staked the notebook on that man. He would have staked Hesk's book. There was not a lie anywhere in him.

*It isn't broken*, he had said in a reading room. He had said it of a case of paper. He had not yet seen it on a north road in the middle of an ordinary morning, with a mother's hand going up to check a pin.

They went away toward the town, the two of them, the girl holding the slip and reading it over as she walked. They passed within six feet of him. She had a scatter of freckles and one front tooth a little crooked, and she did not see him at all.

He stood at the trough until they were out of sight.

He had been arguing about himself for a year. He saw that now. It had been his enrollment and his file, the nine days of his renewal, his frame. Every leaf of a hundred and eighty had his name at its head. And the whole while, on every north road in the Compact, on every morning the stations opened, this had been going on behind a door that a decent man held for you.

The seat would rule in two days. He had thought of the ruling, all year, as the thing that would decide. It would decide what the registry did. He understood, with the sun on the back of his neck, that it would leave him with a decision of his own all the same, and that it was not the one he had been preparing for. It had nothing to do with whether he could win. It was whether he would stand in a frame, knowing what was appended to it, and let it enter him as advised.

He walked back. The coat fell in forty yards behind.

That night, at the window, with the cupola dark against the sky and the bell quiet under it, he opened Hesk's book and wrote the day's entry.

*Third-day. The district station, the north road. A girl of fourteen. Her mother had done her hair.*

He looked at it, and then blotted it and shut the book. He had not written a shorter entry all term, and he did not add to it.

---

The call came on the fourth morning, at the second bell.

It came by the seat's own messenger, a boy in grey with a strap of papers, who would not give his leaf to the woman of the house and stood in the passage until Bracken came down. Bracken read it on the stair with his first pair of glasses and then with his second.

"The evaluation seat will make its return," he said. "In the long room, at the third bell. The parties are called." He lowered the leaf. "It is a return, and not a sitting. I should like that understood by everybody before we cross the lane. I said fourteen. I am holding to fourteen."

"What does an evaluation seat return?" said Lira.

"Whatever it found within its scope and is obliged to lay before the seat. Generally nothing. Generally one line, to say there is nothing." Bracken folded the leaf in three. "She has asked for the room, and for the clerk, and for the records table. That is a great deal of furniture for one line."

The long room was empty.

Cael had not known how large it was. He had seen it with thirty people in it and with two hundred, and never as it stood now, with the forty benches running back bare to the doors and the lot numbers showing in the floor between them. The high east windows laid four long bars of light across the boards. There was nobody on the steps outside, for nobody had been told. A return was posted in the porch after it was made.

Eleven people. He counted them, because it took no time. Jent and his thin clerk. Withrow, the counsel, Karis, Bracken and himself. The seat's clerk. The two at the records table.

On the platform the presiding chair stood behind its long table with a grey cover drawn over it.

He looked at that for longer than he looked at anything else. For thirteen sittings there had been a hood in that place, and he had tried each time to learn something from how it sat. There was a cloth there now, hanging in folds, as there had been cloths on the five chairs below it before the third sitting. It told him exactly as much.

Four of the five chairs were empty too. Ilsev had come alone.

She stood up from the middle one. She was a spare woman of sixty or so with her grey hair cut short, in a plain coat, and she had her file open on her left forearm at the angle at which he had seen it for a year. She did not come forward. She stood where she had sat, and looked at the seat's clerk.

"The evaluation seat makes its return," she said. "I shall read it at the pace of dictation. I ask that it be taken down as read, and not from my leaf afterward."

"As read," said the clerk, and dipped his pen. At the records table the junior chair wrote the time.

Beside Cael, Karis had opened the wallet. She had no table for ink in a wool yard, and had not needed one for a week. She took out the square of planed deal and a pencil, and set the pencil's point on a clean sheet.

"The scope of this seat was minuted at the third sitting, in the presiding seat's words," said Ilsev. She spoke slowly, with a small pause at the end of every clause, and three pens went with her. "It has kept within them. Within that scope the evaluation seat is obliged to lay before the proceedings any matter upon the subject's file that would govern the conduct of an evaluation. There is one such matter. The file carries a designation. The petitioner has set it out in the second schedule to his petition, at the fourteenth line. A designation of that kind governs who must be present at an evaluation, and to what office its result is sent."

She turned a leaf in her file.

"This officer has twice referred that designation, in the proper form, to learn the office that entered it. She has received two returns. She will read both into the record, entire, as they came to her."

Nothing in the empty room moved.

"The first. *Return, from the chain of review, to the senior evaluation seat, on referral of the matter at Greyvane. Designation: Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4.*" The pause. "*Determination: no originating authority of record. Designation predates file creation. No action available at this clearance.*"

Karis's pencil did not stop. Cael watched it go across the deal in her small upright hand, a word behind the voice.

"The second. *Referral closed at registry level; no further inquiry authorized.*"

Ilsev turned the leaf back.

"And the note which this officer has written beside that designation upon every form she has filed concerning the subject, and which she now enters here. *Authorizing office: not traced.*"

She lifted her eyes from the file. She did not look at the house's table, or at Jent, but at the grey cover on the presiding chair.

"The evaluation seat notes the contradiction. The registry relies upon a designation to govern an evaluation, and the registry's own returns say that no office of record entered it. The evaluation seat makes no finding upon that. It is not within the scope minuted for this seat. This officer has been told that no further inquiry is authorized, and she makes none. She reads what she was sent." A last pause, no longer than the others. "That is the return."

She sat down, and set the file at its angle.

Cael looked up at the clock over the doors. Six minutes.

He sat with his hands flat on the wood, and they were quite steady, and he was glad of that.

*Designation predates file creation.*

He had been fourteen. A room at Denvash, a desk, eleven seconds. Karis had laid it out for him on a night of frost, with her gloves on: something that was not the reading had looked for a column to put him in, and had found none, and had done the safe thing. It had written a word where an older name had been. And it had set a watch on him, at that same instant, before a clerk had so much as ruled a sheet with his name at the top.

There had been no file yet. The registry had just said so. It had said so twice, under seal, in a clerk's round hand, and a careful woman with short grey hair had read it out at the pace of dictation in an empty wool exchange so that it would be taken down right.

She did not know what it meant. He was as sure of that as he had been of the man at the station door. She had found a hole in the record and been told not to look into it, and she had done the one thing that was left inside her clearance, which was to say aloud, in public, that it was there.

"Received from the evaluation seat," said the clerk, "and entered in the minute. A fair copy will stand in the public record before the ruling. Has either party an observation?"

Jent rose. He looked, for the first time in a year, a little tired.

"The petitioner set that designation out in his schedule because it is upon the file," he said. "He was not told its office either. He has no observation."

"The respondent?"

The counsel stood. "None," she said, and sat.

That was all. The clerk shut his book.

On the steps, in the empty street, Karis stopped with the deal board against her chest. She had the whole of it, in pencil, in seventeen lines, and she was looking at the eleventh.

"That's the truest sentence anybody has entered about you in a year of sittings," she said, "and the woman who read it out has no idea what it says."

She put the board into the wallet, and did up the strap.

# Chapter 51 — The Hand

Havel had written the time when the senior evaluator stood, and the time when she sat, and the difference between them was six minutes.

He looked at the two figures after the room had emptied. He had written a great many pairs of times in that book since the fortieth day, and he could not remember looking at a pair before, for it was not his place to notice how long a thing had taken. It was his place to see that anybody who wanted to know could find out. He blotted them.

"Junior," said the senior chair. He had the minute open in front of him and was running his thumb down the fresh page. "This wants to be in the porch before the ruling. That's the code, for a return. I've a tooth that won't let me climb, and the clerk's gone to his dinner. Take it up to the loft and stand over them while they copy it. Fair, one sheet, and you compare it yourself."

"Yes," said Havel.

He took the minute book in both hands. It was heavier than it looked, as a book is that has had a year written into it.

The copyists' loft was over the long room, up a ladder-stair behind the platform. It had been the factors' sample loft, and there were still hooks in the beams where fleeces had hung to be judged by the light, though four slanted desks stood under the north windows now. In a sitting week the seat hired four copyists from the counting-houses along the wharf; on a call day it had kept only one, a young woman with a quick neat hand and a smudge of ink on her chin.

He laid the book open on her desk, and showed her the place, and where it ended.

"As it stands," he said. "Every stop."

She began. He stood at her shoulder, as the manual said an officer was to stand when a minute left the records table, and read each line as she made it against the line it came from.

He had not read it yet himself; he had only heard it, and he had always known that those were different things. He had sat forty feet from the middle chair with his pen moving, and had written a time, and had kept his eyes on his own page while a voice he had worked under for years said a thing slowly into an empty room. Now he read it.

The second of the two returns he had by heart. He had read it once before, upside down, on a desk at Norhold, in perhaps three seconds, and had written it that night at the head of a sheet of his own, which lay in his kit at the inn, the fifth of six. He watched the copyist make the words, and they were the same words, and he felt nothing about them that he had not felt already.

The first he had never seen. He had carried the file it answered; he had laid that file at her elbow in a hall on a bluff, square to the edge, and gone back to the foot of the table. A return had gone into it then, and he had not read it, because a records officer did not read what was addressed to the senior seat.

*Determination: no originating authority of record.*

The copyist's pen went across it without a check, and his eyes stayed on the line when her pen had gone on.

His oldest sheet said that there was a mark in a boy's file with no office behind it. He had written that after he asked, in the proper way, and was told behind a shut door that it was not his clearance, and he had taken that to mean that somebody above him knew whose mark it was. In all these years he had wanted nothing from that mark but the name of an office. He would not have needed to be told the name. It would have been enough to know that one was written down somewhere, in a register he could not open, against a date.

The registers had been asked properly, by a seat senior enough to ask, and they had answered. They knew of no office either.

He stood very still at the copyist's shoulder. It was an answer, and he made himself be exact about that, as he was exact about a count. He had kept six sheets of things the registers could not answer, and here, in a minute, under a seal, was the registry's own hand saying of the first of them that it could not. He had not been wrong, on a pump step, when he was a younger man. He had only been alone with it.

"Sir," said the copyist.

She had stopped, with her pen lifted over the next line.

"This can't be right. *Designation predates file creation.* A mark can't be older than the file it's written in. That's like a stamp being older than its letter." She looked up at him, quite earnestly, with the ink on her chin. "I think somebody's dropped a word. Shall I query it?"

Havel looked at the line in the minute. It was in the senior chair's hand, taken down at dictation, and the senior chair did not drop words.

"It is as it was read," he said.

"But it doesn't make sense."

"It is not required to. A fair copy says what the minute says. If the minute is wrong, that is for the seat, and it will be wrong in the copy too, so that the seat can find it." He heard his own voice, level and dry, saying what the manual said. "Copy it as it stands."

She copied it as it stood. She was a good copyist, and she made the capital on *Designation* exactly the height of the one in the book.

He did not let himself go on to the thing she had seen. He was aware of it, as a man is aware of a stair he has decided not to climb. A girl from a counting-house had read that sentence once and known at a glance that it could not be so; the senior evaluator had read it out in a wool exchange, and called it a contradiction, and made no finding. She had done what her clearance allowed, to its edge, and stopped there. He knew what it was to stop there. He had been doing it for years with a kit bag.

He compared the copy, line by line, with a dry pen, and it agreed. He wrote *compared* at its foot, and the hour, and his name.

---

He pinned it in the porch himself.

The exchange's porch had a board for the seat's public record, behind a brass grille that opened with the junior chair's key. He put the sheet up with four pins, and squared it by the grille's bars, and locked it.

There was nobody on the steps. A boy came across from the trough, one of the lads the correspondents paid to watch the board, and read the heading with his lips moving: *Return of the evaluation seat.* He looked for a ruling under it and did not find one, and went back to the trough.

That was the public record. Havel stood a moment with the key in his hand and looked at his own pins. A thing he had carried between two manuals since before that boy could read was up on a wall in a market street, in a fair hand, for anybody; and the street went by. A carter passed, and two women with a basket between them, and on the far side a man in a travelling coat with a walking stick, who glanced at the board as he glanced at everything and wrote nothing down.

He had thought, when he was younger, that if it were ever said aloud something would happen.

He went back in to the records table and entered the posting against the hour.

The senior evaluator was still in the long room, which he had not expected. She sat in the middle chair below the covered platform with her file shut on her knee, quite alone, looking at nothing, like any woman at the end of a day's work. As he came down the side of the benches her right hand went to the breast of her coat and rested there flat for a moment, over the cloth, and came away.

He knew what was under it. He had seen her fold it twice, on a desk at Norhold, a sheet that was not office stock, and put it where she had never carried anything that was the Compact's; and he had gone back to an inn that night and written his fifth. There were two of them in one town again, each with paper the office had not asked for. He had thought so once before and had taken a kind of cold comfort in it.

But it was not the same now, and he made himself see why. She had not taken out the sheet in her coat, which was hers and would stay hers. She had taken out the returns, which were the registry's, and stood up in the registry's own room, and read them into the registry's own minute at a pace a clerk could follow. She had found the one door her clearance left open and had gone through it in the plainest way there was.

He had kept sheets. He did not think less of them for that, since a man did what his chair allowed. But he sat down at the records table and squared the receipt book, and he knew, as he knew when a count had come out, that they were finished.

---

At the inn by the bridge he ate his supper downstairs among the carters and went up while it was still light.

The river ran low and plain under the middle pier. He set the kit on the table by the window and unbuckled it, and took the six sheets from between the second manual and the third, and laid them out in their order, left to right, as he had laid them on the night of the seventh sitting. A mark with no office. Three between, each in its year. A sentence that shut a door. A seal on no register.

He read them through, slowly, as an officer reads anything he means to rely on. Then he did something he had not done to them before. He took the pencil from the kit's loop and wrote a small figure in the top corner of each, from one to six. They had never been numbered, for to number a thing was to say how many of it there were to be.

He did not take a clean sheet. He looked at the place where the clean sheets lay, and thought about what a seventh would say. It would say that on the fourth call day the senior seat read two returns into the minute; and that was in the minute, and pinned up in a porch. The six held what the registers could not answer, or would not, and this the registers had answered themselves, aloud. A thing that stood in the public record did not want a private sheet. That was what a public record was for.

He tied the six with a length of the red tape he used for receipts, once round and once across, and made the knot flat.

Six entries. Done.

It was a strange word to think, and he sat with it, because nothing whatever was done. The mark stood on the file as it had always stood, and the seal he had hunted through three ledgers still belonged to no office, and tomorrow at the hour after noon a ruling would be read and he would write the time. He did not understand it tonight any better than he had on the pump step. He had six facts and no leave to add them up, and he had never once tried.

But his part of it was done. He had kept them until somebody whose office it was said one of them aloud. He could not have said why that was enough, and yet it was.

He put the bundle back between the manuals, the same way round, and took out the other paper.

It was a form. He had drawn it from the district seat's stationery press some weeks ago, on the day after the eleventh sitting, and had carried it blank ever since: *Request for relief from rotation*, a single leaf with five ruled heads. He had never filled one in. In all his service he had gone where he was sent and stayed until he was fetched.

*Officer.* He wrote his name and grade.

*Present duty.* He wrote it as his order had it: *Records, junior chair, institutional proceedings at Ostrand; the seat's Fifth-days, until relieved.*

*Relief sought upon.* He considered that for some time, and wrote *The conclusion of the proceedings by ruling.* It was tomorrow, and a man might fairly ask to be relieved of a duty on the day the duty ended.

*Posting sought.* The district seat's records room was a man short. He had seen it for himself when he went there with a rubbing of a seal: an impression book four months behind, and a ledger of dies that nobody had balanced against the cutters' copies since the last officer left. He wrote *District records, Ostrand, or any records room that is short.* He did not write *out of monitoring*, for the form had no line for what a man wished to leave.

*Grounds.*

The pen stayed over it.

He knew the grounds. He had sat forty feet from a table for a year of Fifth-days and had not looked at it. He had looked at that boy before, on a pump step and at Greyvane and on a sprung floor at the bluff; he remembered every one of the looks, and had sworn off another, and had kept to it for a year by looking at his own hands. He was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hours, and he wished to work in a room where the leaves were only leaves.

None of that was grounds. He had never kept a sheet for what he felt, and a form was not the place to begin.

*The proceedings conclude. The district records room stands a man short, to the officer's own knowledge, and its impression book is in arrear.*

It was true, every word. He read it over once and signed it and dated it for the morning, and laid it on top of the kit, where he would see it when he woke, to hand in at the district seat on his way to the exchange.

The bridge-ward had lit his lantern. Havel sat at the window with his aching hand open on his knee and watched it, and thought, a little, about the young man at the far table, who had sat up straight for a year. He did not know what a ruling would do to him. He had never known what any of it would do; that had not been his to know. He found, sitting there, that he hoped the registers would be kind to him. He knew of no register that entered kindness, and he hoped it all the same.

Then the lantern went out, and he went to bed.

"I can see both doors," said Bracken, "exactly as I said."

They were at the head of the market street, the two of them, at the second bell on the last Fifth-day, where the cobbles began to fall toward the river. Below on the left were the four steps of the wool exchange, with people gathering on them already for a ruling that was hours off. Along a side street to the right, three corners down, a blue door stood in a plain stone front with a board beside it, and Brom and Lira were walking toward it.

Cael watched them go, because he could not go with them. He was a respondent on a call day, and though the district hall was well within the bell, a respondent did not walk into another proceeding's room on the morning of his own ruling; the house's counsel had said so, and then had said that she was sorry. So he stood with the registrar and saw a broad back in a good coat and a narrow one beside it go down a street. Brom walked as he walked to a frame, and Lira had her hands in her pockets and was, for once, not talking.

"She said nobody goes in alone," said Cael.

"She said it to me at breakfast, as though I had proposed the opposite." Bracken had the calendar card in his hand, the stiff one, and was turning it over and over without reading it. "Eight," he said. "That will be the eighth advancement docket I have seen go down from the hill. Seven have been found for the tier. I have no reason to think the district has changed its habits."

The blue door opened, and the two backs went in, and it shut.

It was the longest hour of the morning. He spent it at the trough by the exchange's corner, counting the people on the steps as they came, and stopped at ninety. Bracken went in to see the seat's clerk about the house's copy and came out again, and the older coat stood across the street under an awning with his stick.

Then Lira came round the corner at something that was nearly a run, and stopped herself, and walked, and Brom came after her at his own pace. He looked just as he had looked going in, with his coat buttoned, except that there were two red bars across his forearms where the cuffs had ridden up, darker than the wool press had left them.

"They loaded it," he said.

"And?"

"I held it." He thought about how to say the rest. "It was more than Ephram ever put on, a good deal over. They let it down on springs, and then they let it down again while I had it, which he never did, and I heard the middle one say *forty* and go on counting." He looked at his forearms. "The plate lit. I felt it under my boots. It's warm. I didn't know they were warm."

"What did it say?" said Cael.

"They don't tell you. They write. It goes to the registrar by the bag." Brom glanced at Bracken, and Bracken inclined his head. "Three of them at a table, and a clerk, and a frame the size of a hay wagon. Nobody said a word to me but *stand there* and *thank you*."

"It said Iron," said Lira. She had got her breath. "I was on the bench by the door and I watched the middle assessor's face when the plate came up. He looked like a man who'd put his hand in his pocket for a coin and found the coin he expected." She shoved her hands deeper into her own pockets. "He was right about himself, in front of people whose job it was to check. I saw it happen. I wanted somebody to have seen it who knew what it was."

Brom did not say anything to that. He stood in the market street with his forearms marked and his face quite calm, and Cael saw that it had been enough. Whatever a clerk wrote on whatever paper, a plate had come up warm under his boots and said what was so.

"The hour after noon," said Bracken, and put the card away. "I suggest that everybody eats."

The long room held more than it had ever held, and nobody in it made a sound.

They were standing along the walls, and the doors had been left open so that the steps could hear, and the steps were full, and past them the street. Everybody who had come down for the closing had come again, and Ephram's cohort had the ninth and tenth benches, and this time nobody had needed to threaten them.

Cael sat behind Withrow's chair with lot seventeen under his boot, and he sat up straight. It was, as the counsel had told him once, nearly the last time he would have to.

The five chairs were filled. At the records table the junior chair had his pen ready, and Jent sat with his hands folded on a shut folder and his spectacles hanging.

"The seat," said the clerk.

It came in and sat, and it had a single leaf in front of it.

"In the matter of Caelen Hesk-ward. For ruling. The fourteenth sitting."

The hood read. It read in the voice it had used all year, level and unhurried, with no more weight on one word than on another, and it paused at the end of each head so that the clerk might have it. Cael fixed his eyes on the exchange's clock over the doors.

"*The seat, having heard the parties upon the merits and at the close, rules as follows.*

"*It records that fourteen sittings were held; that the exhibits of the petitioner and of the respondent were received entire and without objection; that the statement entered under the provision for such statements was noted; and that, the certificate of the protocol being withheld, the seat proceeds upon its own authority, as the code allows.*

"*First. The enrollment of the said Caelen Hesk-ward at the chartered academy at Halcenvane was lawfully executed under the demonstration provision of the assay clause, which provision stands. The seat so held at the preliminary, and so holds.*

"*Second. The respondent institution has been compliant with its charter and with every order of this seat. No finding is made against it, and none is to be entered.*

"*Third. The integrity question raised by the petition being within the registry's discretion, and the seat being satisfied that the question cannot be answered upon the record alone, the enrollment basis is revoked. The revocation shall take effect at the close of the present academic year.*

"*Fourth. The said Caelen Hesk-ward shall be re-evaluated under standard Arbiter protocol, at the district seat at Ostrand, within five weeks of this day, upon a date to be notified. The evaluation seat constituted for these proceedings shall see to its conduct.*

"*Fifth. Upon the result of that evaluation, the provisions of the fourteenth chapter shall apply as they are written.*

"*Given under the Compact's hand.*"

Two minutes and forty seconds.

"So ruled," said the hood.

"So ruled," said the clerk, and wrote, and at the records table the junior chair wrote the time.

Cael had thought, for a year, that when it came he would feel the floor go, and he had braced for that as he braced for a landing. He sat with his hands flat on the house's table and waited for it, and the lot number stayed under his boot, and the boards stayed where they were.

Lawful. Compliant. Revoked.

He had heard those three words coming since the preliminary, when the seat had said *sound* and *whole* and *notwithstanding* in one breath. There was no trick in them. A year of Fifth-days had been walking toward exactly this leaf, and every door on the way had been real law and had opened one way, as Karis had told them on the first night. He had read it better than they did, and so had she, and it had made no difference, because it had never turned on the reading.

What he felt was not the floor going. It was a corridor ending. He had been walking down it so long that he had stopped feeling the walls, and now there was a last door, with a year's close chalked on it, and behind him nothing whatever that he needed to go back for.

The seat rose. It gathered its one leaf, and turned, and went to its own door, and for a moment the grey back was all there was of it. He had tried at fourteen sittings to learn something from how that shape sat, and had wanted a face under it, because a face could be read, and hated, and found out.

*Presiding seat per seal.* He had copied the words off a notice in the year's fourth week, and today he believed them. There was somebody under the cloth, and whoever it was would go home, and hang the hood on a peg, and tell the truth about the day. But it was not that person who had ruled. A seal had ruled. That was what the hood was for, and it was not a cruelty or even a disguise; it only showed, as plainly as cloth could, what he had been arguing with.

The door shut behind it, and two hundred people breathed out.

Jent came across the aisle before the benches had finished rising.

He came to Withrow first. He stood in front of her, a man of middle height in a coat with mended cuffs, and bowed, not to the registry's depth but a little past it.

"Chancellor," he said. "Your house is clear in every line of that leaf. I asked that it should be, in terms, and I wanted you to hear so from the petitioner and not from a clerk. If an inspector ever opens your folder at this year, send him to the second head."

"I heard it read," said Withrow. Her voice was perfectly even. "I shall know where to send him."

He turned to Karis, and for once he seemed to have nothing ready. He looked at her for a moment over the shut wallet.

"Advocate," he said, and that was all. He said it as he had said it into the minute at the fourth sitting, when it had been a courtesy; and it was not one now.

Then he looked at Cael, and Cael looked back and did not send the read. He had sat across from this man at a conference table in the year's fourth week and found nobody in the coat, and he knew why now. There had been nobody to find. Jent had been sent with an honest question, and had asked it honestly, and had been given the answer the code gave.

"Hesk-ward," said Jent. He nodded, the same nod he had given on a step in the rain. He seemed about to say something more, and to think better of it, and he picked up his one folder and his inkwell and went out by the side door with his thin clerk behind him. He had another docket somewhere, and he did not look back at the room.

In the stable yard Withrow stood by her carriage with one gloved hand on its door, and waited until Karis and Cael had come up, and Bracken with the seal.

"I want one thing said before the hill gets hold of it," she said. "I read losing filings for twenty years at the registry. It was my work to say why they had lost. They lost because a date was wrong, or a section was misread, or somebody had hoped a seat would not notice a thing it was paid to notice." She looked at Karis, and then, longer, at Cael. "You did not lose badly. I should like that to be the first thing either of you remembers about today, and I shall say it to anybody who asks me. There is not a line in that ruling that answers a line of yours. It could not answer them. It went round."

"It still went against us," said Karis.

"It did. You lost to a discretion, and nobody has ever beaten one of those from inside it. I knew that on the eighth day." Withrow opened the carriage door herself. "I chose to stand there anyway, and I'd choose it again, and now I can show anybody who asks me exactly how well it was done. That is not nothing. It is a good deal of what I have."

She got in. For a moment, before the door shut, he saw her face with nobody looking at it, and it was the face of a woman who has known a thing for four days and is not going to say it in a stable yard. He did not understand that then, and put it away to look at later.

Bracken changed the slate before he took his coat off.

He rubbed out the three lines with the heel of his hand, the two closings and the ruling, and left the fourth, because that was Brom's and not the house's. He wrote in their place, square and plain:

*Ruling given. No finding against the house. The enrollment basis revoked, from the year's close.*

He read it over, and dusted his fingers, and went behind his counter, where the lamps were lit in pairs down the length of the records hall and both his clerks were already bent over the middle table.

"Copy-sets," he said, to Cael, who had not asked. "The Chancellor's order, from the carriage. Thirty-four houses wrote to ask what this one would do if the seat went against it. They are to have the ruling in the house's own fair copy by tomorrow's first bag, with the standing entries, and no gloss. Three leaves to a set, and five sets more for the faculties." He pulled down his second glasses. "Thirty-nine. I have told the kitchen to send bread."

Seln was at the counter's far end. He had come down in the coach's corner without a word, and he stood now under the counter lamp with the enrollee's copy of the ruling laid in front of him on the wood. It was the seat's own engrossment, on registry stock, with the wax of the river and the scale at its foot; Cael had carried it up the hill in the satchel and had not been able to think what to do with it.

Seln did not read it, for he had heard it read. He held it up to the lamp by two corners and looked through it, for some time. Then he laid it flat and took Bracken's boxwood rule from its nail, and measured something at the sheet's edge, and measured it again from the other side. He turned the leaf over and ran his thumbnail very lightly round the rim of the seal, as Bracken had once done under an arch, and bent close to the last line to look at the hand.

"Well?" said Cael.

Seln laid the rule down.

"I have read paper all year for what stood behind it," he said. "A mark a sixteenth low. A lot written above the ruled line, with a mark for a word. A seal that is on no schedule. I have been reading paper that way for a good while longer than a year." He touched the leaf's corner. "This is registry stock from the first mould, and the mark sits where it should. The die has its number in the rim, and the number will be on its line in the cutters' ledger; I should be surprised if it were not. The hand is the seat's own clerk's. I have watched him write it since the fortieth day."

"So it's honest."

"It is exactly what it says it is. There is nothing behind it at all." He said it in his mild voice, as he might have reported a floor schedule. "In my trade a man is taught four grades of pressure, in their order. The record. The instrument. The knock, which is paper nobody will own. And last the hand, which is a road with no paper at all. I was taught that the hand was the worst of them, and I believed it for fifteen years." He shook his head, very slightly. "I had the order wrong. What stood in the road was a glove." He looked down at the last line of the leaf. "*Given under the Compact's hand.* That is the only true sentence of its kind I have been shown this year. This is the hand. It does not have another."

He squared the leaf to the counter's edge, and stepped back from it.

"I am sorry," he said. "I should have liked to find something. It would have been easier for you."

Cael looked at the engrossment under the lamp.

*I keep looking for the person and finding the hand.* That was his own line, from the year's sixth week, and he had gone on looking. He had looked at Jent and at a hood and down a road with chalk on it, and had wanted an arm, somewhere, that the hand belonged to.

There was no arm. There were two hundred and six honest people, and a leaf two hundred years old in a locked case, and a clerk with a good hand. He stopped looking, and it was like setting down a thing he had carried so long that he had forgotten it had weight.

At the middle table the copy-sets were coming off the clerks' pens, and Brom was squaring them.

Nobody had asked him to. He had come in behind them and stood about for a little with his coat on, a man who had held a frame that morning and had nothing to do with his arms. Then he had gone to the table's end, where the finished leaves were being laid in threes, and had taken up each three as it came, and knocked its edges true on the wood, long side and short side, and laid it on the pile with its corners exactly over the corners beneath.

Then he began to weigh them. He did it on his palm; he lifted each set a little, and let it settle, and put it down. Cael watched him do nine, and on the tenth his hand stayed up.

"This one's light," said Brom.

The senior clerk looked round. "It's three leaves."

"It's light."

The clerk took it, and counted, and counted again more slowly with a wet thumb. There were two; the standing entries had been left out of it. He went red and reached for a clean sheet, and Brom took up the next.

He had said, in a harness room, that he could not read a registry sheet anymore; that since the case he looked at one and saw only what it was a form of. He was not reading these. He was doing the one honest thing he could still do with such paper, which was to say what it weighed.

Lira was on the stair. It was the narrow stair behind the counter, the one that went up to Bracken's rooms, and she sat on its fourth step with her knees drawn up and her chin on them, in the shadow above the lamps. She had not said a word since the coach, and she was watching Brom's hands.

She did not look like somebody who had lost. He knew that face; she had worn it on the north wall when she first asked why they were still there, and on the night after a dairy. She was adding something up.

He left the engrossment where Seln had squared it, and went to find Karis. It was nearly the tenth bell.

# Chapter 52 — What Leaving Costs

The reading room had a bare table, and there was nothing left on it to turn over.

He noticed that before anything else, coming in fourth, as he had come in fourth on the night the case was opened. On that night every sheet in the room had been squared and weighted and ready, and on his birthday every sheet had been turned on its face, because the captain was coming. Tonight the cloth lay bare from end to end. The shelves behind it had gaps in them the width of grey boxes, and the only paper in the whole room was three slips in front of Karis and the book he had brought himself.

The lamp in the window was turned up as high as it had been every night that year, so that the valley would see what it always saw.

Karis had the table's end. Lira sat on the bench with her back to the wall and one boot up, and Brom was in the good chair from the dairy, which had somehow followed them up four flights months ago and never gone down. Seln came last, on the tenth bell, and shut the door behind him without a sound. He had his post satchel over his shoulder, and when he sat he did not hang it on the chair but set it on the floor between his feet, upright, and Cael heard the weight of what was in it meet the boards.

So the case would leave the room tonight on Seln, and nobody remarked on it.

"The ruling," said Karis. "Everybody heard it, and I've the engrossment by heart, so I'll give it once and short. Lawful. Compliant. Revoked from the year's close. A frame within five weeks, on a date they'll send, and the fourteenth chapter on whatever the frame says." She laid her pale fingers on the three slips. "That leaves three roads, and I've written one on each. I haven't put them in order of anything, because I didn't want to cheat."

She turned them face up, one at a time, and they said *Frame*, and *Fight*, and *Leave*.

"Before anybody says which," said Cael, "I want them priced."

They looked at him.

He had Hesk's book open on the table at the leaf he had ruled on the night of the first sitting: a column, and four names at its head. There were lines under three of them now, in his cramped hand, a year of what the case had cost each of them, entered as it fell due. Under the fourth name, which was his own, the column was empty.

"All three roads," he said. "Aloud, at full price, by the person who pays it. I've kept this column for a year by watching you and writing down what I saw, and it's the worst-kept leaf in the book, because I was only guessing. Tonight you tell me, and I write what you say." He turned the book so that they could see the ruled lines. "I said once that whatever this cost me, it shouldn't cost you, and Karis told me that wasn't how any of us were built. I've had a year to find out she was right. So I'm not going to pay anybody's share for them tonight, and I'm not going to decide what it is. I'm going to ask."

Brom leaned forward in the good chair with his forearms on his knees.

"Then start with the one nobody wants," he said. "The frame."

"That one's mine," said Cael. "I'll price it."

He had done it on a north road two days ago, by a horse trough, and he gave it to them as he had found it there.

"I stand in it. Within five weeks, at the district seat, with Ilsev seeing to the conduct of it, and she'll see to it properly. I put my hand where I'm shown. It was asked once before, and it took eleven seconds." He did not look at Seln, and he went no nearer to it than that. "We know what it says. Karis told a seat so for fifty-one minutes and nobody contradicted her: *no valid classification.* Then the chapter opens, in its order. One: every enrollment or standing that rests on my being a practitioner is void, and that's this hill. Two: I'm suspended as a practitioner pending a review, and the review is to be begun by the registry at its discretion."

"With no date," said Karis.

"With no date. Withrow underlined that twice in her own code, nine years apart. And three is part nine." He had it by heart, having heard Bracken read it, flat, on the night of the procedure. "They may restrict, freeze or hold anything that attaches to me: a record, a sponsorship, a travel paper, *any institutional relationship.* They've walked those fences already. "He told them I grew up in it, and they wrote that down." He let that go. "So that's the price in paper. It's the haulier's price, and Withrow told us what it did to him: nine letters from four offices in a flour sack, and nobody ever laid a hand on him."

"And there's what it is. Karis found it, the fourth of her findings. The frame is how they get me back inside. I'd have a line on a file that says *suspended, pending review*, and somebody told to observe, and I'd be entered, and whatever I was after that would be what was written." He looked at the slip. "I saw a girl go into a station on the north road on the Third-day, and she came out happy. I'll not stand in that building. I can lose everything else that's on this table tonight, but I'll not be entered as advised."

Karis took the first slip between two fingers and laid it face down.

But a thing ought to be argued once at its full strength by somebody who wants it to win, before you leave it behind, or you never know afterward whether you only flinched." She squared her shoulders, as she had on the worn boards of the long room. "So I'm its advocate. Somebody stop me if I cheat."

It took her a quarter of an hour, and she did not cheat.

"We have the thing that ends it," said Karis. "It's between Seln's feet. I spent fifty-one minutes last week telling a seat that its instrument had failed to read him, and I couldn't say why. I know why. I can date it to within a renewal of a charter. That isn't an argument from discretion; it's the newest matter anybody has put before the registry in two hundred years, and the code gives an appeal from a seat's ruling upon new matter. Brom did not answer, for he was looking at the second paper. It was a printed slip, a third the size of the sheet, with a narrow margin and a rule at its head, and Cael, from the bench, could read its heading upside down: *Guidance to the practitioner upon advancement.*

"It is issued with every finding," said Bracken. His voice had changed; it had gone careful. "By rule. A district clerk takes one from a pigeonhole and puts it in the packet. I issued it myself when I was a clerk at that seat, eleven times, and I thought it a kindness, since it tells a young person what the new standing will ask of him. Mostly it is about fees." He took the glasses off his nose and held them. "There is a paragraph near the foot, however, which the clerk is to mark in the margin where a practitioner has associations of record. I see that he has marked it."

Brom read it, and this time he did not read it aloud. He read it twice, by the movement of his eyes, and then he held it out across the table, not to Cael but to Karis, because she was the one who read such things, and she read it to the room in her small dry voice.

"*The practitioner is advised that associations of record with any person subject to an order under the fourteenth chapter may be noted upon the practitioner's file, at the registry's discretion, and may be considered upon any later review of standing or sponsorship. The practitioner may wish to regularize such associations before signature.*"

She laid it on the bare cloth.

"Regularize," said Lira.

"It means put them in order," said Bracken. "It was drafted, I believe, with debts in mind: a man advancing to Iron who had stood surety for a suspended partner." He was holding his glasses very tightly. "Nobody has lied in it. The clerk who marked that margin did so because a file told him to. I have marked such margins. I should like that said, in this room, by me."

Brom stood with the finding in one hand. He was a large man, and he looked for a moment as he had looked under a wool press, with a load come down on him all at once that was a little over.

It was all there on two pieces of paper. There was the number he had wanted since before he had a Path, found honestly that morning, in a frame, in front of people whose work it was to check; there was his family's ledger, which closed if he signed; and there was a polite printed paragraph telling him that it would all sit easier on his file if he were first to put a certain association in order.

He folded the finding along its folds and laid it on the table. He did not sign it, and he did not tear it, and he did not say that he refused it. He put it down as a man sets down a thing he has decided not to carry.

Then he turned to Karis.

"How many boxes is your research?"

She looked up at him, with her pale fingers flat on the cloth.

"Three."

"Then I'm carrying three boxes."

He sat down in the good chair.

Nobody said anything. Lira had both hands over her mouth. Cael found that he could not see the ruled column very well, and bent his head over it, and wrote under Brom's name what Brom had said, word for word, because he had promised to write what they told him and not what he guessed.

On the landing, Bracken had not moved.

"*Or to her order*," he said, and his voice was not quite steady. "I wrote that in a deposit book on the first day of the month. I have been thirteen years at that counter and I knew what I was writing." He put his lantern down on the top stair, carefully, and took the last pair of glasses off his forehead, and then there was nothing between his eyes and the lamp. They were wet, and he let them be wet. He stood on the landing with three pairs of spectacles in his hands and wept, briefly and without any noise, as a man does who has kept true records all his life and has just watched one do what this one had done.

"I beg your pardon," he said, after a little. "I have never done that at my counter, and I find I prefer a stair." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist, once, and put one pair of glasses on. "I ask when, and never why. So. When?"

Cael looked round the table, and each of them looked back.

"Before the year closes," he said.

"Then it is a matter of forms, and forms are my trade." Bracken picked up his lantern. "The registry prints one for an enrollee's withdrawal, and I hold a quire of it. In thirteen years I have not had occasion to square more than one at a time on that counter." He looked at the four of them who were enrolled, one after another, and did not look at Seln, who was staff and outside any form of his. "I shall have them ready. I say nothing about how many."

He went down the stair, and they listened to him go, every step of four flights, and to the door at the bottom.

"I'll go next," said Lira, "before I lose my nerve, and because after that I can't follow it anyway."

She took the carter's book out of her coat. He had seen it perhaps three times in the term: stitched, in oilcloth, with a pencil in the spine. She opened it near the back and laid it on her knee.

"I've got it written. I did it on the stair in the records hall, watching him square copies, and it's under *ME*." She read it as she would have read a bout sheet, quickly, with her finger under the line. "The program. That's a hall painted for me, and a red book from a river academy, and a Stone on loan who writes two lines to my coach every week. A year of it. The trial rule, and twenty-seven on a card, and then twenty-seven under a hall's seal at the confluence, against a Silver who told me afterward to go on my fifth. Twenty-eight on the oak against a Blade. Four losses on my sheet, and I know what every one bought." She turned the leaf. "A sitting. The first after the year's close, behind a blue door, with my name fourth on the board. Rooke chalked it over the week's drills and ruled a line under it so nobody could wipe it off. He's been building toward that door since the recess before this year, and he never once asked me to promise I'd walk through it."

She stopped, and her finger had stopped too.

"And Rooke," she said. "I haven't a line for that. He found my name at the foot of the Iron column and built a ladder up from it with his own hands, and he ate in an open seat on the day of the riders. I'm going to have to tell him."

"Is that the whole of it?" said Cael.

"No. That's the bill. Here's the other side, because you asked for full price and that means both columns." She shut the book. "Nobody ever built anything to my height before. Fenmark had the form right and the wrong room. I walked out of there on my own feet and I've been proud of it ever since, and I thought that was all I'd ever want from a room: to be let leave it standing up. Then somebody built me a ladder, and I found out I wanted to climb. I said so, and you wrote it down. *Admit.*"

"I've still got it."

"I've wanted it all year. I thought the thing I wanted lived on that floor, and I've looked for it there every dawn since the first week." She put the book away inside her coat. "Turns out it doesn't live here. It came up the road with me. I landed on a capstan bar in a wool yard two days ago on whichever foot I liked, with nobody holding a card, and it was all there. It's in my feet. They can't keep it in a hall, and they can't revoke it." She looked at the last slip. "But the ladder lives here, and I'm leaving the ladder. I want that written at full price, and I don't want anybody telling me it's cheap because I can still jump."

He wrote it. *The program, the sitting, Rooke. Full price. It doesn't live here.*

"Iron Rank One stays mine," said Lira. "That's a certificate, and it's in my coat, and it was true before any of this."

"Mine's short," said Karis, "because I've put it in order."

She had a single sheet, ruled and written in ink, which she had taken from inside her notebook's front cover, where she kept the things that mattered.

"I didn't know how else to do it," she said. "So I've done it as a bibliography, by head-word." And she read it, quite steadily, as she would have read the contents leaf of a bundle.

"*Annex, the: Halcenvane, the library's top landing; the reading room and the room across; assigned in the recess before this year; a brass lock. Forfeit.*

"*Archive privileges: the house's own, and by its seal those of Ternhall and Greyvane. Forfeit.*

"*Assistant, the: an enrollee of the second year. Not mine to forfeit, and so a letter, which I have written, to the research council. It says that the line they voted is for research, enrolled, standing, and that she is all three, and can find a section from a struck heading; and it asks that the room and the line pass to her.*" She paused. "*A second letter, to the assistant herself: not written. I don't know how to mark it.*

"*Budget: the first the house ever voted an enrollee; spent once. Forfeit.*

"*Greyvane, the archive at: a second request, sealed by the fee office in the week the first was paid, for the founders' correspondence; answer expected in about eight weeks. I can't forfeit an answer that hasn't come, and so I have written to redirect it.*" She looked up from the sheet, at Cael, and for the first time that night she seemed unsure of herself. "*Care of an instrument-maker, at Denvash.* I ought to have asked you. There wasn't anybody else whose address will still be an address."

"He'll charge you for the string," said Cael. It was all he could manage.

"I should hope so." She looked down again. "*Research standing, enrolled: forfeit. Restricted collection, right of entry to: forfeit.*" That one took her half a second longer than the others. "*Tier, registry certification of: Iron, Rank Three. Retained.*"

She laid the sheet on the bare cloth, beside Brom's finding.

"Eight heads," she said. "I wanted a room with a lock on it my whole life, and I had one for a year, and it was the first room I ever wanted to stay in. I said so to you on its second day." She put her hand on the table's edge, the pale fingers pressed to the wood. "I'm not going to say it doesn't cost. It costs the whole of what I asked for. But I found out this term that I can find a leaf in the dark on my knees in under thirty seconds, with cold hands, and I didn't build that habit for nothing."

Cael wrote *eight heads; see her sheet*, and under it, because it was true and she would never say it, *the room*.

Then all of them looked at Seln, and none of them had meant to.

He had not been in the column. Cael had ruled it on a night when there were four of them, and a man at a copying table whom nobody had yet thought to count.

Seln turned his cup a quarter turn on the cloth, slowly, and watched his own hand do it. Then he looked up.

"I resigned fifteen years ago. I just never filed it."

He did not add to it. Cael ruled a fifth line on the leaf, to the right of the four, and left its head blank, because that was a name that did not go into books, and under it he wrote the sentence.

"And you," said Brom. "You've a line with three words on it."

"I know." He looked at his own column, at *Wanted it true*, and set the pen under it. "The hill. Two years, near enough three, of the only place that ever let me in by its own rule. Gault's trestle. Hesk's shop has no interest of mine recorded in it, but they've asked, and they'll ask again, and I can't be in Denvash to see what they do. A rematch on a floor I can't name yet. He blotted the line.

"That's leaving, priced, by everybody who pays," he said. "I'm not going to ask for hands yet. There's somebody who ought to be told before it's a thing that's been decided behind his back."

"Ephram," said Brom, and got up.

Brom was gone a quarter of an hour, and the bell for midnight went over the court while he was on the stair.

In that time Seln lifted the satchel from between his feet onto his knees, under the table's edge, and folded his hands on top of it. Karis put her bibliography back inside the notebook's cover, and left the last slip where it lay.

"He gets the departure," she said. "Nothing about what's in that bag. I said it of another man in this room, and it's the same rule."

"He won't ask," said Lira. "He's never asked."

"That's why he's owed being told the part we can."

They heard two men on the stair, one heavy and one quick, and the door opened.

Ephram came in as he had come in on a birthday, a little out of breath, with the captain's file under his arm in its green tape, and he had plainly not been asleep. He stopped inside the door, and his eyes went along the table from end to end as they went along a floor.

"There's nothing face down," he said.

"There's nothing left on it," said Karis.

He took that in. He looked at the gaps in the shelves, and at the one slip on the cloth, which he could read from where he stood, and Cael saw him read it. His plain open face did what it had done at a rail in wing three, and went still.

"Say it anyway," said Ephram. "I'd rather have it said."

"We're going," said Cael. "All five of us. Before the year closes, by a door that leaves the house clean. East."

"Past the stations?"

"Past the last of them."

"Past the Line, then." Ephram stood with his hand on the file. "Then nobody will ever write a figure for you again. Not Gault, nor a panel, nor me." He was quiet for the space of two breaths. "I thought it might be that. I've thought so since the boxes went onto Bracken's shelf; I've eyes, and I walk the records hall on Third-days. I didn't know it was all of you." He looked at Brom, who had come in behind him, and Brom looked back, and whatever passed between the two of them had been settled in a harness room over a rack of iron plates and did not need a word.

"I'm not coming," said Ephram. "You haven't asked, and I'd like it said before you feel you ought to. This is my hill. I've a cohort, and a column, and next year's first-years who'll want somebody to tell them the rota's a joke. I'm made for a place with a board in it." He said it without any apology. "I think you aren't. I think I've known that for longer than you have."

He untied the green tape. He took out the three sheets, close-ruled, soft at the folds, and unfolded them on the bare cloth where Karis's slips had been: ninety-eight entries in a square hand, with a dot beside each, and the line of dots breathing down three pages and never once touching the mark ruled across the top in red.

Then he turned the third sheet over.

There were two lines on the back, in pencil, which he had written at a rail, facing a wall, on the day the hours were complete. He turned the sheet round so that Cael could read them.

*He has been under the line on purpose. All ninety-eight.*

And below, after a space:

*Ask for one. After.*

"I told you on that oak, at the end of your first year, that I'd learn what you were the only honest way there is," said Ephram. "With a floor, and somebody at the rail. I've had ninety-eight lessons since in what you'll let a table see. I've a chart of them, and it's the best-kept record of a thing not happening that anybody ever made." His voice was quite level. "So here's what I've been keeping. I want one bout. The Crown yard, tomorrow, under Rooke's rule, in front of whoever comes. And I want all of it. Not the ceiling. I ruled that ceiling, and I know to the half point what's under it. I want whatever's over it." He put two fingers on the red line. "If you give me the band I shall put my lath down and stand there till the glass runs out, in front of the whole house, and you can explain it to them."

Nobody moved.

"I've been glad for three years that you came up our road," said Ephram. "I said some hard things on a tier in your first months here, and I've been glad every day since that I was wrong. I'd like to find out whether I'm still glad when you stop being careful with me. I think I will be. I'd like to know."

Cael looked at the two pencilled lines.

Two years ago this was the worst thing that could have happened to him on the bluff. He had built a band and a rotation and a variance so that nobody would ever stand where Ephram was standing and say what Ephram had just said. He waited for the old cold to come up through his stomach, and it did not come. There was a ruling in his satchel, and a slip on the table with one word on it. The band had been rent paid on a room, and he was leaving the room.

And Ephram had asked. He had asked of his own accord, in earnest, for a fight. Lira's head had come up at that, for she knew what such an asking meant under a rule with four sets of initials, and so did Brom, and Karis had gone very still.

"It isn't only mine to give," said Cael.

"I thought it mightn't be." Ephram gathered the three sheets, and folded them, and did not tie the tape. "I'll be on the landing. I'll have my back to the door." He went out, and they heard him cross the boards and stop.

Cael turned to the table.

"One question, then, in two parts," he said, and kept his voice low. "That we go, by the clean door, inside the year. And that before we go I give him what he's asked for, in the Crown yard, with the lid off, in front of the school and the town and whichever coats come up to write it down."

"They'll write it," said Seln. "All of it. It will go west by the next bag."

"I know. The band was for the corridor, and the corridor's finished."

"The rule," said Lira. She said only that, and she did not look at Seln.

"Tonight," said Cael. "On paper, before I sleep. You'll all three have it to read."

She nodded.

Brom's hand went up first, as it had in this room once before, the right, flat, like a man taking an oath. Lira's followed, and then Karis's, pale to the second knuckle, and Cael put up his own.

Seln sat with both hands folded on the satchel on his knees. He looked at the four hands, and then at the lamp in the window, turned up as high as it would go. He had been in a great many rooms, and had sat at the edge of every one of them for fifteen years, and he had counted five beans onto a gardener's table and called it arithmetic.

His right hand came up from the satchel, slowly, and it was the last.

"Five," said Brom. He counted them again, though the first count had been right.

Lira went to the door and opened it. Ephram was standing at the head of the stair with his back to her, looking down four flights into the dark, with the green file under his arm.

"The fourth bell," she said. "Go and wake Rooke. He'll want to chalk it himself."

# Chapter 53 — The Sunward Mark

There was a light under the chancellor's door, and he had known there would be.

He stood in the passage a moment with the satchel on his shoulder. It was past midnight, and the house was dark from the lodge to the library, and he had come down four flights and across the court to tell a woman a thing before a board could tell her. He had not thought about what he would say. He knocked on the frame, as he knocked on Brom's.

"Come," said Withrow.

She was at her desk with the lamp turned low and her hands on the blotter, a hand's width apart, and she was not writing. The two piles of letters were gone. The charter hung in its frame behind her, and under it the locked case stood open, and the folder from the case lay on the desk in front of her, shut.

"Sit," she said. "You've come to tell me you are going, and you would rather I heard it from you than from Bracken's face in the morning. I have already seen Bracken's face. He came down your stair an hour ago and asked me for the key to the stationery press, and he is not a man who can ask for a key without my knowing what it is for."

Cael sat down in the chair across the desk.

"All of us," he said. "Before the year closes. By a door that's in the code, and in an old enrollment book. Karis found it in the term before the recess, and it leaves nothing in that folder."

"*Resigned in good standing.*" She said it without surprise. "I know the door. I have known it for four days."

He looked at her. He had seen her through two panes of glass, on the first evening in Ostrand, reach across a table and shut a book, and he had seen her face before a carriage door closed. He had put both away to look at later, and it was later now.

"Then you should have the whole of it," said Withrow, "since you have brought me yours." She took a folded sheet from inside her coat and opened it on the blotter and turned it toward him. It was not the house's paper. There were four lines on it in the small capitals of the house's counsel, and each had a day written against it.

*Ruling: the Fifth-day.*

*Notice of date, to the Chancellor, by a district officer: upon the ruling, or the day after.*

*Evaluation: the Fourth-day following. The last day of the academic year.*

*Revocation takes effect: at that day's close.*

"The ruling says five weeks," she said. "That is the outside of it. The inside is in a section upon notices, which gives a district five days, and in the habits of a district clerk who has never in my time notified a date one day later than the code allowed him. Bracken went to the district hall on our first morning in the town, to look at the room where Brom would stand, and there is a board beside its door. He read on it that the frame-house was reserved for the registry's use on the last day of the year." Her voice was quite even. "It was written up before any ruling was given, and I want you to understand that there was nothing crooked in that. A prudent clerk books a frame when he sees that one may be wanted, exactly as Bracken books a coach, and he would have struck it off again without a thought if the seat had gone the other way."

"So it isn't five weeks."

"It is five days. An officer will ride up to my gate with the notice tomorrow, or the day after, and it will be addressed as the last one was: to the chancellor, to produce the enrollee. You will still be mine on that day, for the revocation does not bite until its close." She looked down at the four lines. "I read an order aloud at that gate once and declined it, because nobody could tell me whose it was. This one would be a seat's, and I could not decline it. I should have to walk you into that frame-house myself, under the house's seal, or else refuse a seat in writing and let them enter the refusal in my folder."

Five weeks had become five days. He heard the sentence in his head in the log voice, flat, and knew that he would write it down.

"The counsel showed you this on the First-day," he said.

"She did. She showed me the arithmetic, and then she showed me the section Karis found, and she recommended that I put it to you." Withrow took her hands off the blotter and folded them. "She was right to recommend it, and it was good advice. If the enrollee withdraws before the day, the order has nobody to stand in the frame and no house to produce him, and my folder shows a challenge answered and a house cleared. I have known since the First-day evening that the best thing that could happen to this academy was for you to walk out of it."

"And you didn't say so."

"No."

The lamp ticked. He waited, because she was not a woman who left a thing half said.

"Four chancellors advised me this term to let you go," said Withrow. "I answered every one of them in my own hand, and told them that the harm was done the day a house handed over an enrollee to spare itself. I believed it when I wrote it." She looked at him directly. "And then a small woman with a steel rule showed me that I need hand nobody over. I need only mention a door: a quiet word, in a guesting-house parlour, from a chancellor to an enrollee who has spent a year trying not to cost her anything. You'd have gone that night. Don't tell me you wouldn't. You'd have gone for my folder, and I should have done with one sentence what I refused to do under four riders."

He did not tell her that he wouldn't, for it was true.

"So I kept it," she said. "For four days, through a return and a ruling. I let you hear the hood say *revoked* without knowing how short the time was. I should like to say that it was easy and that I never wavered, and I can't; I sat in a carriage this afternoon and very nearly said it through the window." Her mouth moved a little. "I wanted it to be yours. If you went, I wanted you to go because you had priced it with your own people, at a table, with my need left out of the sum. And if you stayed, I meant to walk beside you into that frame-house and take what they wrote."

"It wasn't left out," said Cael. "I've counted it since the first week."

"I know. I watched you count it. But you put it there yourself, and I did not. That is the whole of the difference, and it is the only thing I have had to give you this year that was worth anything."

He found that he could not answer. He had been keeping the largest thing he knew from her for half a year, and it had sat between them like a lie in every room; she had kept a thing from him for four days, and he saw now which of the two silences had been the kinder.

---

She opened the folder.

She turned it on the desk so that it faced him, as she had on the night she told him to write down a word, and it was open at the same leaf. On the left was the entry for the eighth day, in Bracken's hand: the house a respondent before the seat at Ostrand, the subject of the proceedings, *Status: pending*. On the right the column was ruled, and still empty.

"I showed you my column once," she said. "Four papers out of a drawer. Now the other one."

She laid one finger on the empty ruling.

"In the week after the close a clerk will come up our road with a stamp, and he will write here what the second head of the ruling says. *Respondent institution compliant; no finding.* I shall give him tea. I told you a year ago that the best day this folder could have was a clerk writing *sustained*, and I was wrong, for this is better. It is the cleanest remedy column I have seen against a contested entry in twenty years at the registry and all my years in this chair." She took the finger away. "And in Bracken's enrollment book, under your name, there will be five words in his hand. Under three other names as well, I gather, though I have not asked how many and I shall not."

She shut the folder, quite gently, with one hand.

"That is what a year of this comes to on paper. An enrollee was challenged. The house answered and was cleared. The enrollee left. Every inspector who opens that case for the next thirty years will start at the grant and turn forward, and that is what he will read, and he will draw the natural conclusion from it. He will think the boy saw which way it was going, and ran, and that the house was well rid of him."

"It's what the form says."

"It is. Forms say what happened and never why, and I have spent my life filling them in." Withrow stood up. She was a tall woman, and she stood very straight, and for the first time since the recess she did not look tired. "The record will say you left. I'll spend the rest of my tenure making the record say why."

He looked up at her.

"There is no form for taking a leaf out of that folder," she said. "I told you so. There is also no rule against putting one in. I have a closing in the registry's presses under an enrollee's name, which I can cite, and a magister's note for the seventy-sixth day. I have thirty-four chancellors who wrote to ask me what I would do, and who now owe me the courtesy of reading what I did. And I have a right-hand column in a great many places besides this one." She came round the end of the desk. "I do not know what took you up that stair tonight with four others. I am not a fool, and I can see that it was more than a ruling, and I am not going to ask. Bracken asks when. I find I do not want even that."

"Chancellor—"

"No. I said in the term that I'd be annoyed if you thanked me, and I should be." She had her hand on the door. "The captain came to me at supper to ask for the Crown yard tomorrow at the fourth bell. I told him the yard was Rooke's to give and the bout was yours, and I see by your face that he has since asked you. I shall be in the stand. I have sat under a rug in other people's halls for two years to watch my own fighters, and I should like to see one thing on my own oak before the year closes."

She opened the door herself.

He had never known her do it for anybody. A chancellor rang, or said *that will be all*, and one found one's own way out. She stood with the door held wide and the dark passage beyond it, as a district officer had stood at a station on the north road, and it was not the same thing at all.

"Go to bed," said Withrow. "You have a floor tomorrow, and for once nobody has told you to look ordinary on it."

He did not go to bed. There was a thing to write first, and the rule said it was to be written before, at its full weight, by a lamp, with nobody hurrying him.

He sat at the sill with Hesk's clock losing its minutes on the shelf behind him and turned to a clean leaf. He had priced the eighth on a leaf like this, in the week of the rain, and four people had put their initials to it, and he had not thought then that he would ever price another. He wrote it slowly.

*The ninth, if it comes. It may not. I'm not to make it come; that's the first rule, and it's kept. He asked. Nobody put the asking in his mouth. He wrote it on the back of a chart in wing three and kept it till the seat had done with me.*

*In earnest: yes. He'll put his lath down if I give him less. Awake, and on purpose: yes. I'm writing this the night before, with a pen, and I know exactly what I'm about.*

*The weight. The storm was Gold and the road was Silver. He's Iron, the sixth rank of it. Karis's curve says it comes quicker each time. I'll price it as if it didn't.*

*Buys: an edge, I think, and a way of knowing where a line will be crossed. I've watched it for three years from a tier and I couldn't tell you how it's done.*

*Spends: the left forearm. He will have to cut at me, and I'll have to take it where I can read it, on the guard, more than once. A Blade's edge isn't the wood's, and I don't believe the give will hold it. Breath. The hip and the shoulder for everything else, because he's asked for all of it and he's to have it. Ten counts for a lane, and ten again. Then whatever the new one does to me afterward. The last asked* There? *forty times an hour for two days.*

He stopped there, with the pen lifted, because the rest was not a bill of that kind.

*And this. He isn't to be told. Not tomorrow on the oak, nor in a letter from wherever we are by then. He's the one person on this hill who wanted the true measurement and nothing else, and he's waited three years for it, and I'm going to take something from him in the middle of it and never say. Lira's known since Ardenmere. Brom, Karis. The one I took the quiet thing from knows now. He can't. It would put him on a file beside mine, and he's staying.*

*That's the cost that doesn't heal in a week. Entered at full weight. I've read it over, and I'm still going to do it, if it comes.*

He blotted it and put his initials at the foot.

Brom's lamp was lit. He was sitting on the edge of his bed in his shirt with his forearms on his knees, and on the table under the window lay two papers. On the left was the district's finding, folded along its folds, with the printed slip tucked into it; on the right, a hand's breadth off, lay the envelope with the red seal, as he had squared it four weeks ago, and its seal had not so much as a crack across it.

Karis was still in the reading room, on the floor, with a lantern. She read the leaf twice, and at *I'll price it as if it didn't* she made a small sound through her nose and wrote *K.* under Brom's mark.

Lira's door was shut and there was no light under it. He knocked, and nobody answered, and after a moment he tore a corner from the back of a schedule and wrote on it against the wall: *Priced. It's in the book, on my sill. Come and read it.* He pushed it under the door.

At the sill again he turned back a leaf and wrote the day's line under the ruling's three words, because it would not wait for morning.

*Five weeks became five days.*

He meant to sit up until she came, and he put his head down on his arm to rest his eyes.

Lira had the east hall to herself, and no lantern.

She had not lit one. She knew the floor well enough by now to walk it blind, every cross and arrow of it, and the gallery shutters let in a little grey from the court. She stood at the south chalk with the key in her fist and her boots off.

Somebody had painted this floor for her.

She had never quite let herself say it in so many words. She had said *the program*, and *Rooke's floor*, and once, to Cael on a stair, *heavy*. But the plain fact was that a man had taken a hall, and had it planed, and sent to a river academy for a red book, and had a painter put four hundred marks on the boards, each one where a Wind fighter of exactly her reach would want it. Nobody had built anything to her height before. Fenmark had built a room for a girl it had imagined, and when she had turned out not to be that girl it had put her outside the room with her form correctly filled in.

She ran the count, front foot and back foot and shoulders, and went on the fourth, a small low burst across the grey, and came down on the right. The boards took her and gave her back. She went again and came down on the left, and she had not needed to choose. That was the thing she had learned in this hall, on eleven tries one night and a thousand since: that the foot was not a question you asked.

She stood on a painted cross and breathed.

All her life she had wanted one thing from a room, which was to be let walk out of it standing up. She had been proud of that. She had said it to Cael on a road somewhere, as if it were a thing to be proud of, to want so little. And then a man with a board under his arm had built her a ladder, and she had found out in about a week that she wanted to climb it more than she had ever wanted anything, and had been ashamed, and then had stopped being ashamed.

She was going to walk out of this room standing up, and nobody was putting her out. The door was open, and there was a sitting with her name fourth on a board, and a coach who had ruled a line under it so that it could not be wiped off. She could stay. She had turned that over on a stair in the records hall, honestly, watching Brom weigh paper: she could let four people go east, and be at a blue door in a fortnight in clean wraps.

She had put her hand up second.

It was the first room she had ever left that she would miss, and she thought that was probably what it was to have been given something.

The red book lay on Rooke's table, where it always lay. There was a word in it under her name; he had written it on the day of the Blade, and then a second, and she had never seen either. She stood over the book in the dark with her hand flat on its cover, and did not open it. It was his, and whatever he had written, he had written of the program's entrant. She would tell him tomorrow, after the bout, in one sentence, to his face, for she owed him that much and he would want no more. If he chose to show her the page then, he would.

She laid the key on the red book, and then took it up again. Not yet. There were five days, and she meant to drill on every one of them.

There was a scrap of paper under her door when she came up, and his door, when she got to it, was not latched. He was asleep at the window with his head on his arm and the lamp burning down, and that foolish clock ticking on the shelf, behind the world as it always was. The book lay open under his elbow.

She eased it out, and he did not stir. He slept like a person who had been sat up straight for a year.

She read the leaf standing, by what was left of the lamp. She had read one of these before, in a week of rain, and had written across the bottom of it in capitals that nobody was to go in after him alone. That one had been about a woman on a road who meant to put him in a wagon; it had frightened her, and it had been simple. This one was not simple. She read down it, through the forearm and the lanes, and came to the part where the pen had plainly stopped and started again.

*He isn't to be told.*

Lira stood with the book in her hands.

She had known what he could do since Ardenmere, when she was fifteen. She had never once thought of it as a thing taken from her; it had come out of him, and it was hers too, and she had been glad. But she had known. Nobody had ever kept it from her.

The captain was going to stand on the oak tomorrow and be given the truest fight of his life, and one true thing would be kept back from him for as long as he lived. He had asked for the measurement, and he would get a great deal of it. She found that she minded for him, more than she had expected to; he had turned his back on a table of papers for her friend's sake on a birthday, and gone down a stair whistling.

And she saw that Cael minded more than she did. He had written it down as a cost, at full weight, in ink, before he could be tempted to call it something else. That was the rule working, and it was what the rule was for.

She took the pen from where it had rolled against the clock. Under Brom's barred letter and Karis's neat one she wrote *L.* Then she turned the pen in her fingers, and wrote one line more, across the foot, in the capitals she kept for such leaves.

*HE ASKED. DON'T BE KIND.*

She slid the book back under his elbow and turned the lamp down to save the oil.

At the end of the corridor a window looked north over the roofs, and from it you could see the Crown yard, a black pit with a pale floor, and she stopped there because there was a light in it. A lantern stood on the faculty stand. By it she could make out the porter's ladder against the first of the four boards, and a short square man on the third rung with his sleeve rolled, writing with a lump of chalk.

He wrote for a long time, for a man who used one word.

He had not waited for the porter, or for morning. Somebody had come to his door at midnight and told him, and he had put on his coat and gone down to the yard in the dark and chalked it himself, before any officer of the house could wake up and wonder whether it was wise. She watched until he climbed down and held the lantern up to read it over, and then rubbed out one word with his cuff and wrote it again.

She went to bed in her wraps. She thought she would not sleep, and she slept at once.

Cael read the board at first light, with a stiff neck and three sets of initials under his own.

He had come down by the rim, alone, before the first bell. The oak lay below him in its pit, pale and dry, and on the wall above the faculty stand the first of the four black boards had been wiped of the year's afternoons, every one, and four lines stood on it in Rooke's square capitals.

*THE CROWN YARD. THE FOURTH BELL.*

*BLADE, IRON SIX. THE ENROLLEE.*

*ROOKE'S RULE: FOUR EXCHANGES, A TOUCH OR NONE IN EACH. TWO ENDS IT.*

*THE HILL AND THE TOWN.*

He read the last line twice. Rooke had closed a floor to observers in the year's seventh week, with a chain and a card, and had kept a ledger with *none* in it ever since; and now he had chalked an invitation to a valley.

By the second bell the valley was coming.

Cael watched it from the north wall, because there was nothing else he could usefully do. The ferry came across low in the water and went back empty and came again. The bluff road had people on every switchback: wool-factors in their good coats, and carters, and a file of district clerks who had found that they had business on the hill. The man who had bought a second press was there, and so was the woman who kept the guesting-house, with a basket. The lake house's blue-grey wagon had not gone home after the ruling, and it came up at a walk with a lath in a canvas sleeve sticking out of the back.

The lodge-man had his book out on a trestle under the arch and was making every one of them sign it. *Callers, sign and wait*, said his board, and he had kept that gate since before Bracken, and saw no reason why a ruling should alter it.

Two men in travelling coats came up among the rest, and waited their turn at the trestle like anybody, and took the pen, and wrote. Cael was too far off to see what they put. They did not look up at the wall, and one of them had a walking stick he did not need.

He had no line to read at his chalk today, and he found that strange. For a year and more he had read one before every bout: what to take late, and what to give, and the figure it was meant to buy. He had the rotation's book in his coat from habit, and at the north wall he took it out and opened it at the day.

*Sixth-day. The Crown yard. Ephram. All of it.*

There was nothing under that. What it bought and what it spent were on another leaf, in another book, with capitals across the foot. He shut the rotation and did not open it again.

At the fourth bell he went down the steps onto the oak, and counted the yard one last time.

It was sixty feet by forty, laid so close that a fingernail would not go between the boards, and sunk four feet below the ground, with three short flights of steps, of which he had come down the east one. The stone tiers went up from the rim on three sides, with eleven hundred seats in them and a little over; he had counted them at dawn in his first week on the hill, when they were empty, and had felt them on the back of his neck.

They were not empty now. He counted by tens and by rows, as he had counted benches in a wool exchange, and gave it up at twelve hundred, where the stone ran out and the people did not. They stood along the rim behind the top tier, and sat on the steps, and a row of first-years had got onto the wall of the lecture range and were dangling their legs.

On the fourth side was the faculty stand. Withrow sat in the registrar's chair at its middle, upright, with her hands in her lap, and Bracken stood behind her. Gault was at the stand's end with his instrument case set very straight beside him, though there was nothing in a bout for it to measure. At the long table Rooke stood with his glass and his board, and his clerk sat at his elbow with two books open, and the house's healer had the bench below with her bag.

He found his own people without looking for them. They were on the east tier, low down: Brom, with his arms folded, and Karis beside him with her hands under her, and Lira between the Current girl and the Stone. The assistant sat behind them, and behind her were two benches of Ephram's cohort, who had been told nothing about cheering today. At the end of the stand, by the porter's boards, where the year's names stood in their columns, a man in a dark coat stood with his hands in his pockets, and nobody would have looked at him twice.

High on the west tier, at the very top, sat a grey man with a walking stick across his knees and a pencil.

*Let him write*, Cael thought, and it surprised him how little it weighed.

Ephram was on the oak already. He stood in the middle of it in his greys with the lath in his right hand, point down, and when Cael came off the steps he turned and walked to a chalk. He did not go to the south one. It was the fourth bell, with the sun come round over the lecture range, and from the south chalk a man had it at his back. Ephram walked the whole length of the floor to the north mark and turned there, and stood with the light full in his face.

It was the worse mark, and any first-year knew it. A captain who had headed the Iron column for three years could have taken either, and nobody would have said a word.

He was leaving himself no excuse. Cael understood it as he walked to the south chalk with the sun warm between his shoulders: whatever Ephram learned in the next four exchanges, he meant to have learned it against every disadvantage he could arrange, so that there would be nothing afterward to set against it.

Rooke came to the edge of the stand.

"Rooke's rule," he said. He did not raise his voice, and twelve hundred people heard him. "Four exchanges. A touch or none. Two ends it. I call a guard a guard." He looked along the oak, from one chalk to the other. "Exchange."

His hand came down.

---

Ephram came off the north mark at a walk.

Cael knew the walk. He had stood in front of it for a season on this floor, calling the weight aloud: every angle, every pace, the lath low and never thrown. The old read went out through his boots into sprung oak and came back with Ephram in it, early, as it always did. The weight was forward and a little left, the shoulders had not dropped, and the back heel was down. It was the front foot only, then, and the thing to do was wait.

The heel came up. Cael slipped right and outside, as he had slipped a hundred times, and the lath went by his hip.

It did not stop, and that was new. The first cut had not finished before Ephram's front heel was down again, a pace on, and a second cut was already beginning out of the end of the first, a different way in, flat, at the ribs. Heel, hip, edge. He could not take the first one back and had not tried to; he had only built the second on top of it, as a mason lays a course.

Cael gave ground with one burst, small and low, spent short so that from a tier it would look like a quick step.

And the third was coming out of the second. He had watched this from the rim on a First-day, slow, against nobody: three in a row, each a different way in, each begun before the last had ended. He read the heel go down for the third, and the weight, and where the cut would finish, and he knew, a half beat early, exactly where he needed to be.

He waited the half beat.

He did not choose to. That was the shame of it, and he felt it even as it happened. A year of Fifth-days in wing three had put the waiting into his body as a drill puts a landing into a foot. Twelve hundred people were looking down, and a grey man with a pencil, and the coat was on him before he had so much as thought of it. He stood in his own bout and waited, so that it would look as if he had found the answer when a good Iron would have found it.

The hip never came.

Heel, and then the edge. Ephram had cut the middle out of it, and the lath was simply there, a beat sooner than three years of watching had taught Cael's eyes to expect, and it lay across his left forearm from the wrist to the elbow like a bar of cold iron. Then it was hot.

"Touch," said Rooke. "Blade."

The tiers made a noise, though not much of one. They had seen a good fighter take a touch from a better, as they had seen it ninety-eight times.

Ephram did not lift the lath in salute. He looked at Cael along it for a moment, with the sun in his eyes and no pleasure at all in his plain face, and then he turned and walked back to the north chalk.

Cael walked to the south. His forearm had begun to throb in a line. A Blade's edge was not the wood's; he had written that last night as a guess, and it was a guess no longer.

*Take the first late*, the rotation had said for a year.

"Exchange," said Rooke.

Ephram did not move.

He stood on the north chalk with his feet together. He turned the lath over once in his hand, and set its tip on the oak, and folded both hands on the grip, as a man leans on a spade. The yard waited. Rooke's hand had come down, and the glass was turned, and the sand was running.

"That's ninety-nine," said Ephram.

He did not shout it. He said it across forty feet of oak in his captain's voice, and it carried to the rim.

"I've ninety-eight of those on three sheets. I don't want another. I said what I'd do." He nodded at the lath under his hands. "It's down. I'll stand here till the sand's out, and the next glass, and the one after that. You can touch me twice whenever you like, and they'll put it on the board that you won."

A murmur went round the tiers and died. On the stand Rooke had not stirred, and he had not called it either.

Cael stood on the south chalk with the sun on his back, and he could feel every one of them above him. He had felt eleven hundred empty seats on his neck at dawn in his first week, and it was nothing to this. Withrow sat in the registrar's chair, and Gault had signed two years of his figures, and a coat held a pencil at the top of the west tier. And there were four hundred people he had eaten beside since he came up the road, who knew him as a quiet enrollee with a provision and a decent read.

*With a table and a yard full of people*, Lira had said, sitting on a painted floor. *I know what their face is going to do.*

Five hands had gone up for this, and he had priced it. He looked down the length of the oak at a man leaning on a lath with the sun in his eyes, who had ruled a red line in the first month he knew him and had waited three years to be shown the far side of it.

He did not take the coat off. He let it fall.

It was easier than it had been in the east hall, and he had not thought that possible. Everything he owned came awake at once, under an open sky, in front of everybody, and for the first time since Norhold there was no part of him standing guard over any other part.

He did not wait.

The Wind first, because it was the oldest: low and flat off the left foot, straight down the middle of the floor. The oak took the landing and threw him on, as it was built to, and he was in the air again before the beat had finished. Three. Ephram's hands had come off the grip, and he had the lath up, and his heel was going down, and the read had all of it, deep, to the hips, a full beat before it happened.

Cael put the push into the oak under that heel.

It was not a flat hand, which was all he had ever shown on the bluff. This was the push at fighting load, the whole shove of it, through his palm and down, and the air over a square yard of floor went solid and then gave. Ephram's front heel came down on a board that had jumped, and the first beat of his cut landed crooked. A Blade could not take it back. The hip followed the crooked heel, and the edge followed the hip, wide, a yard from anybody.

The tiers had gone silent.

Four. He went past the wide cut on the outside, and Ephram turned on the miss faster than a man of his size ought to turn. A line was already there. He had not cut it; he had turned his wrist, and the lath lay level across the place where Cael's next landing would come down, because he read lines, and knew what would cross one a breath before it crossed.

Cael saw the lath waiting and took a fifth burst out of the air above it, sideways, off nothing but the landing beat of the fourth, and came down ten feet clear with his hip beginning to write. Five on the oak, and the day's free six were nearly gone.

Ephram was grinning. Cael saw it, across the floor, with the sun full on it, and he had never seen that face grin on the oak in three years.

Then he asked the air to lean.

He had done it on a stone floor at Norhold with eight thousand people watching, and on a chalk road, and in a painted hall at night, and he had never done it here. He set his feet and asked, and a lane went down across the Crown yard from his chalk to the space behind Ephram's left shoulder, fifteen feet of it, shoulder-wide. His ears closed and opened. The dust in the joints of the oak woke and ran, pale, along the line.

Somebody on the east tier screamed, with delight, and he thought it was the Current girl.

The read had gone out into the lane, as it must. He took it back off the air and gave it to the man, as he had learned to at a price, and then he stepped into the downhill and it carried him a third faster than any feet.

The lath was lying across the lane's end.

He saw it with a beat to spare, and understood it in the same instant. A lane was a line. It was the longest, plainest line anybody had ever laid on that oak, with dust running down it to show the way, and Ephram had not needed to know what it was. He had read where it went, and had put his edge at the end of it and stood there, waiting, with his heel down.

Cael came out of the lane into a cut that was already committed, and burst out of it. Six. Straight up and back, off the lane's own speed, with the edge passing under his boots near enough to feel, and he landed badly, with the hip shouting.

And then the bill came in.

It was the warm hand over the inside of his skull, and everything went slow. He began the count, because it was the only thing that came when he called. *One.*

Ephram did not know about the ten counts; nobody on the bluff did but three people on a tier. But he was the best Iron on the hill and he could see a man who had gone a beat late, and he came in.

*Two. Three.* Three cuts in a chain, heel and hip and edge. Cael asked for the Wind and it came after he had asked, and he got out from under the first by the width of a sleeve. *Four.* The second he took on his raised left forearm, with the give open, and it opened late, and the edge went through it. *Five.* He had been right about that too. The give was a cup, and a cup holds a blow, and this was not a blow; it was a line of cold drawn across the bruise that was already there.

"Guard," said Rooke.

*Six. Seven.* The third was the short one, with no hip in it. He saw the heel go down, and he knew, and he could do nothing with the knowing for a whole slow beat. He spent a burst he could not afford. Seven. It was one past what the oak gave free, and it took him out sideways along the rim wall with the lath's wind on his neck.

*Eight.*

Ephram turned and came on, breathing through his mouth. He had the sun behind him now and Cael had it in his eyes; they had changed ends without either of them choosing it.

*Nine. Ten.*

At eleven the hand lifted off the inside of his head, and everything answered when he called.

He had not felt the pull. He noticed that, with the sweat running into his eyes: no wrong thing had answered in all of that, on a full floor, at this weight. The right things had come late and then they had come. It was the bill, and not the flag.

He set his feet, and Ephram set his. The lath came up, and the heel began to go down.

"Time," said Rooke. "No touch."

The sand had run out.

For a breath there was no sound in the Crown yard at all. Twelve hundred people had watched a quiet enrollee with a provision go down the middle of the oak on a river of dust, and they had not yet agreed with one another about what they had seen.

Ephram lowered the lath.

He was soaked, and his chest was going like a bellows, and there was a look on his face that Cael had never seen on it, on the oak or off. It was the face of a man who has been adding a long column for three years and has reached the foot of it and found that the sum was larger than the paper.

He looked up at the tiers for a moment, as if the three sheets were pinned there, with the red line he had ruled across them and the ninety-eight dots that had never touched it. Then he looked back, and laughed, once, out of what breath he had.

"There it is. Three years. Again."

# Chapter 54 — Good Room

"Exchange," said Rooke, and his hand came down for the third.

Ephram did not walk this time. He came across the oak like a man who has been shown at last what he is fighting, and means to have as much of it as there is, with the sun behind him now and the lath a dark bar against it.

Cael did not go to meet him. He stood on the north chalk, with his hip aching from the crest to the knee and seven bursts gone out of a day that gave six, and he went down the rule as he had gone down it on his knees in a chalk road.

*The conditions are not to be made.* He had not made them. A man had written four words on the back of a chart and kept them until a seat was done.

*Only inside a fight the other begins, in earnest, awake, and on purpose.* Ephram had begun it in a reading room at midnight, with five people listening, and he had grounded his lath in front of twelve hundred rather than be given less than all of it. Nobody had ever been more in earnest who did not also wish him harm.

*The whole cost on paper first.* It was on a leaf in Hesk's book, in the satchel on the east tier at Brom's feet, with capitals across the foot of it.

He did it with his eyes open.

The first cut came, heel and hip and edge, high, from the sun. He did not slip it. He put up his left forearm, as a man puts up a guard, and opened the give, and let the edge land where he could read it.

"Guard," said Rooke.

It went through the give as it had gone through before, across the two bruises that were there, and for an instant the yard went white at its edges. He had been ready this time, and he held himself open in front of it, all of him, as he had held still in a cage. It was a line; that was the first thing. A Blade did not strike with a piece of ash. He said where an edge was, a hand's breadth out beyond the wood, and it was there because he had said so, and it was as thin as a thought.

"You're letting me," said Ephram, between his teeth. He had checked, and stood a pace off with the lath raised and the heel not yet down, breathing hard, and he looked angrier than he had looked all afternoon. "I said I wanted all of it."

"I'm not letting you." It was the truth, and it was as much of the truth as there was to give him. "I'm looking at it. I've watched that edge from a tier for three years and I've never once been under it. Let me look."

Ephram stared at him. Then something went out of his shoulders, and what came into his face instead was not softer, but it was pleased.

"Then look," he said, and put the heel down.

He cut in earnest. Cael saw the whole engine of it, close, in the moment before it reached him: the heel that committed the body, and the hip that carried it, and the edge that could not then be called back by the man who had sent it. And he saw the other thing, which he had never understood from the rim. The line did not stop at the edge. It ran on, out past the lath's tip, straight as a rule, and Ephram *knew* along it; whatever was going to cross that line, he had word of it a breath before it came. That was how a lath had been lying at the end of a lane.

Cael took it on the forearm, and kept everything he had turned toward it.

"Guard," said Rooke, a long way off.

They came together.

It was quiet. The storm had come down on him like a roof, and the road had gone in like a key into a lock that had been cut for it, and this was smaller than either. It was like watching Hesk lay a steel rule along a line he had already scribed and find that it lay true.

And for the length of one breath he could see edges.

He could see every edge in the Crown yard. The coping of the rim wall stood out all round him like a drawn wire, and the joints of the oak, sixty feet of them, each a line with two sides. The risers of the tiers went up the sky in ruled steps, and the black boards had their corners. In front of him was the lath, with its true edge standing clear of the wood, and from its tip a thin bright line ran out through the air and ended on the third button of his own shirt. He knew where that line would be crossed, and by what, and it was the plainest thing he had ever known.

He did not pick it up.

It was there, new and whole, like a tool laid on the wrong side of the bench, and he left it lying, because it was not for this floor. A man had asked to be shown what he was fighting, and he was owed the fight with what Cael had carried down the steps, and with nothing that had been his own a breath ago. Cael shut it like a drawer, and the edges went out of the yard one by one, the coping last.

He had only the old read then, and it was enough, because he had stopped waiting.

Ephram's heel went down for another cut out of the last, and it was the short one, with no hip in it. Cael had it a half beat early, as he had had it in the first exchange, and this time he went.

Eight. It was low, and inside, under the lath, along the line of Ephram's own arm, with the hip shrieking and the edge passing over his back. He came up off the landing beat with his right hand out, and laid two fingers on the inside of Ephram's wrist, on the tape, and gave it the spark.

It went out of him like a word, and the flint smell came up between them. Ephram yelped, and the lath jumped in his hand, and then he was laughing again, helplessly, shaking his wrist.

"Touch," said Rooke. "The enrollee."

One each.

The tiers were on their feet for the fourth.

He did not know when they had stood. He heard them now as he walked back to his chalk, a noise like the weir below Ostrand bridge, and under it somewhere the Stone's enormous voice saying one word over and over. He did not look up. His left forearm had four lines across it, and he could not have closed the hand to save his life; his shoulder had counted one push, and his hip was done with him.

Ephram stood at the far chalk, soaked and grinning, with a red mark on his wrist.

He had learned. Cael could see it in how the man set his feet. He had learned in three exchanges what a rule of the house had kept from him for three years: that a lane was a line, and could be read; that the push took a heel, if the heel went down slowly; and that the boy in front of him was not quick, but early, so that no chain of cuts would close on a man who knew each link before it was forged.

So he would not chain. Cael read it in his weight before Rooke's hand had moved. Ephram was going to do the simplest thing a Blade could do: he would wait for a moment when there was only one place Cael could be, and then put everything he had into a single cut at that place, shoulders and all, the plain entry from the first year.

"Exchange."

He walked. It was the old walk, at every angle, with the lath low, but he was not walking to learn weight now. He was laying lines. He turned his wrist and there was one across the middle of the oak, level, a bar across a doorway, and he turned it again and there was a second, slantwise, from the rim wall to the first. He could not hold more than two at once; Cael saw that, and saw that he did not need to. He was building a corner out of two lines and four feet of sunk stone wall.

Cael went back before it. Nine. A burst along the wall, and the hip gave him nothing in return but pain. Ephram turned with him, unhurried, and the lines turned too, and he spent a tenth to stay ahead of them. He had never gone past nine on any floor.

He put the push into the oak under the walking heel, and Ephram stepped over the place as a man steps over a puddle. It took the shoulder four to learn that he had seen it coming. He tried it a fifth time, at the load, at the edge of his reach, and the boards jumped a foot to the left of a boot that was no longer there.

Then the wall was at his back, with a flight of steps on his right hand, and a line lay on each side of him. Ephram stood ten feet off, and his heel was up. There was one place left, and both of them knew where it was.

Cael asked the air to lean.

He laid it out of the corner, down the only gap, fifteen feet along the foot of the wall. The dust woke in the joints and ran, and his ears closed and opened. It was a line a blind man could have read, and Ephram read it.

His heel went down. It went down hard, for the end of the lane, with the hip coming after it and the shoulders dropped and all of him behind the edge. It was the plain entry, the whole weight at once; he had seen a lane once, and he knew to the foot where it would put a man down.

He could not take it back.

Cael did not step into the lane.

He had a breath, perhaps, before the bill. He took the read back off the air and went the other way, a single stride, straight at the man, inside the arc of the cut, to the place where the lath's root would pass at the top of its swing, a foot from Ephram's own shoulder. He set his feet there. He put up his left forearm as a post, and he shut the give.

He sent the load to the floor. It was Brom's gift, the first thing he had ever taken from Brom, and he had watched it practised in a harness room for a year: nothing in the whole of him pushing back, only somewhere for the weight to go, down the forearm and the shoulder, down the back, through both heels into sprung oak.

The cut arrived.

It had been meant for empty air fifteen feet away. It met a thing that did not give, a hand's breadth from where it began, with a Blade's whole committed body still coming on behind it. Cael felt the edge go into his forearm to the bone, it seemed, and stop. He felt the load go through him and into the boards, and the oak took it and held.

It could not go on, and so it went back.

He heard it. He had heard the same sound once in a chalk cut, under the noise of Iron Skin holding: a click, and a give. Ephram's right arm stopped being a thing with an edge on it, and the lath fell out of his hand and rang on the oak.

Cael's right palm was flat on his chest. He had put it there in the same beat, as he had meant to.

"Touch," said Rooke.

Ephram went down on one knee.

He did not cry out. He knelt on the pale boards with his right arm hanging from the shoulder at an angle no arm should hang at, and his face the colour of the chalk, and the Crown yard, which had been roaring, made a sound like a bellows let go.

Then the bill came in, and everything Cael owned went slow.

*One.* He tried to get down beside him and his knees bent a beat after he asked. *Two.* "Healer," Rooke was saying, in the same voice in which he had called the touch, and a spare grey woman was already off her bench and coming along the stand with her bag. *Three.* Somebody very large had come over the rim wall without troubling about the steps.

"Don't move it," said Brom. He was on his knees on the oak with one hand flat between Ephram's shoulder blades. "I've had one out. It goes back. It goes back worse if you help."

"That's the door," said Ephram.

He said it through his teeth, with his eyes shut, and Cael, slow, kneeling at last, thought that he had not heard right.

"Every Blade's got a door in him. They tell you in the second year. They don't tell you where." Ephram opened his eyes, which were wet with pain, and quite clear. "I've wondered where mine was for three years. Nobody on this hill was ever early enough to stand in it." He breathed. "Did you know it would do that?"

"No," said Cael. *Six. Seven.* The word came a beat late, and it was true. He had known that a commitment could not be taken back, and he had not once asked himself where it went if it was met. "I didn't price it. I priced my arm."

"Good," said Ephram. "I'd not have liked it if you'd known and done it anyway." He turned his head a little toward the stand. "Coach. Call it. I'm not having it set till it's called."

Rooke looked down at the two of them from the edge of the stand with his glass in his hand. Whatever he felt, his face showed about as much as his slate did.

"Two touches to one," he said, to twelve hundred people. "The enrollee."

Then the healer had Ephram's wrist in one hand and his elbow in the other, and Brom had him round the chest. She said something short, and there was a second sound like the first, and Ephram made a noise that he afterward denied.

*Ten.*

At eleven Cael could have stood, and he stayed where he was.

He read it through twice.

Nine.

*Iron.* It was the lightest thing he had been handed since before Norhold, and Karis's curve had been right again, for it had come in a breath. *Edge-declaration.* That was the edge that stood clear of the wood because a man said so. *Line-reading.* It was the thin bright rule that had ended on his third button. *Contact range.* He had five lines across his forearm that said the same.

And at the top, where a name went, *unnamed*, as it had said on the road. It always said so. He had sat on a chalk bank and grieved that he would never be able to find the woman it had come from.

He knew this one's name, and where he slept, and what he whistled on a stair, and that he could not get the third line of a song right. Ephram was eighteen inches from him, having his arm bound, and he could have said it now. That was the difference, and it was the cost. He had written it down the night before at full weight, and it weighed exactly what he had written.

He read the last line a third time.

*Earnest — non-hostile.*

He had not known the notices had those words in them. The one before had said *combat*. The storm had come out of a Gold who was trying to beat him, and the road out of a woman who meant to put him in a wagon. This had come out of a man who had asked for the bout because he wished to know the truth about a friend, and who had fought with everything he had and wished him no harm in the world. Whatever kept the notices had known the difference, and it had a word for it.

"Coach."

It was Rooke's clerk, the thin anxious young man who had kept the visitors' ledger. He had come to the edge of the stand with a book open on each forearm, and he looked from one to the other like a man holding two plates.

"Which book, Coach? It isn't a program bout, so it oughtn't to go in the program's. And it isn't a ladder bout, so I don't see how it goes in the house's. There's no head for it in either."

Rooke did not turn round. He was watching Brom and the healer get Ephram to his feet.

"Both. Enter both."

"And for him? There's no column that—"

"As chalked."

The clerk looked up at the first board, at the two words Rooke had written there in the dark, and then he sat down at the long table and dipped his pen. He wrote it twice, once in each book, as a man does who has been told to get a thing down where nobody can rub it off.

Cael stood up, and his legs allowed it.

At the far end of the stand, Gault had not moved from beside his case. The magister was looking across the oak at him, steadily, as he had looked across a trestle every week for two years with three cards and a glass. He had signed a figure for every one of those weeks, and every figure had been earned on the house's floors, to the decimal, exactly as it was written; and he had just been shown what they had been figures of.

He did not take out his small book. For once he did not seem to need to write a thing down in order to have it.

He nodded. It was one small exact nod, the kind he gave a card that had been added right, and Cael nodded back, and found that he had to look somewhere else quickly.

The yard was a great deal of noise. He had expected that, and had not expected the kinds. The Current girl was standing on her bench with both fists in the air and tears running down her face, and seemed not to know about the tears. The assistant had her hands over her mouth. Half of Ephram's cohort were over the rail and round him, and the porter stood by his pail with his cap in his hand, blowing his nose and speaking severely to the ground. Brom walked beside the healer with the captain's good arm across his shoulders, and his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them; he had said once that a man who looks at a hurt only teaches it to be looked at.

Karis was not crying. She was on her feet, clapping, with her pale fingers flat, and her face was the one she wore when a count came out.

Lira was not crying either. She had her arms folded and her chin up, and she was looking round the tiers at twelve hundred faces, one after another. She had sat on a painted floor and told him she knew what their faces would do, and she seemed to be checking it.

At the top of the west tier the seat was empty. The grey man had gone, with his stick, to catch a bag. And at the end of the stand, by the porter's boards, there was nobody.

---

It took the Crown yard the better part of an hour to empty.

He sat on the lowest step of the east flight while it did, because the healer had told him to sit somewhere and hold his arm above his heart. She had looked at the forearm and whistled through her teeth, and felt along both bones, and said that nothing was broken and that it would be the colour of a plum by morning and stay so for a fortnight. She had bound it, and then she had looked at the way he was sitting and asked which hip. He had told her, and she had said that she supposed there was a shoulder as well.

"And behind the eyes," said Cael.

"I don't strap those," said the healer, and went after her other patient.

The town went first. It went up the tiers and out by the yard gate in a slow loud river, wool-factors and carters and clerks, and he heard his name in it, the registry's and the hill's, and a good many guesses, and let them go by. The lake house's Blade came down to the rim before he left and stood looking at the oak for some time with his lath across his shoulders; then he looked at Cael, and laughed, and shook his head, and went away.

The house went more slowly, for it did not seem to know how to leave. First-years stood about in knots on the tiers, re-fighting the fourth exchange with their hands. Nobody came down the steps to him, and that was Lira's doing, who had put herself at the head of the east flight with her arms folded.

Withrow was the last.

She had not left the registrar's chair. She sat in it, upright, with her hands in her lap, while the stand emptied round her and Bracken went down with the clerk's two books under his arm, and she went on sitting until the tiers were bare stone again and the porter had begun at the top with his broom. Then she stood.

She did not come to the steps. She looked at the oak, the whole length of it, as a woman looks at a room she has kept for a long time, and up at the first board, where four lines stood in a coach's capitals. Then she looked at Cael on his step, with his arm bound.

Her face was not the registry face, and he did not know what it was. He thought that she had seen a thing on her own oak that explained two years of an enrollee looking ordinary, and that she was adding it, quite calmly, to the things she had decided not to ask about.

She inclined her head to him. It was not the registry's depth; it was a good deal less, and it was meant.

Then she went out by the north arch, alone, and she was the last out of the yard.

On the tier above him the Current girl had come back for a scarf. She stopped beside Lira, red-eyed and hoarse.

"I've trained on this hill with him for two years," she said. "I've watched every wing-three card there was. I never saw one bit of that. Not one." She wiped her nose on her wrist. "Where's he been *keeping* it?"

Lira did not answer the question. She looked down the steps at him, and then out over the empty oak.

"That's the first time this school has ever seen him," she said.

---

Ephram came back when the light had begun to go yellow.

He came down the steps slowly, with his right arm bound across his chest in a sling of the healer's linen and his coat hung over the shoulder. He had washed. He carried the porter's chalk pail in his left hand, and he was carrying it badly.

"There's a custom," he said. "You'll not know it. You've never been on a board." He set the pail down on the stand. "Whoever loses on the oak chalks it. With his own hand, the same day, where the other can see him do it. It's older than the brass on the Copper crown. The porter does the rest of the year, but he doesn't do that."

"You've one arm."

"I've the wrong one. Hold the ladder."

Cael held the ladder, with his own one good hand. Ephram went up three rungs of it, left-handed, as carefully as a man crossing ice, and stopped level with the head of the third board, where the Iron column began.

His own name stood there. *Ephram. Blade. Rank Six.* It had stood there, in one porter's chalk or another's, since before Cael came up the road.

He took a lump of chalk out of his coat pocket and looked at the name for a moment. Then he reached up above it, to the bare black strip between the name and the board's painted heading, and drew a line. It was short and level, the width of the column, and it wavered, because it was drawn with the wrong hand.

He wrote nothing above it.

"I asked the porter this afternoon what he'd put," said Ephram, to the board. "He said there wasn't a column, and he'd want a fifth board, and the house wouldn't vote him the paint." He looked at his line. "So that's a rule, with the space over it left. Anybody who reads this board next year will see that the head of the Iron column has something above it, and that nobody could put a name to it. The first-years will ask. I'll tell them."

He came down the ladder, one rung at a time, and stood on the stand with chalk on his fingers.

"I said last night I wanted to find out," he said. "Well, I have. I had one touch off you, while you were still being careful, and I'll keep that one. I had none after. I've a shoulder that wants a month." He turned the chalk over in his left hand. "I was the best fighter on this hill for three years, by the only measure the hill has. And the measure was short the whole time, and I'm the man who kept the chart that proves it. Ninety-eight entries. I ruled the line myself."

He looked up at the wavering rule above his name.

"Still glad."

He said it plainly, as he would have read out a rota.

"I thought I would be. I wasn't sure. You don't know a thing like that until it's cost you something, and I'd rather it had cost me a shoulder than have gone on not knowing." He put the chalk in his pocket. "Go and be whatever that was, somewhere there's room for it. I'll be here. Somebody has to tell them it happened, and get the third line wrong."

Cael stood with his hand still on the ladder.

It was in him, new, housed for the moment where nothing else was, a thing with an edge and a rule to it. It had come out of the man in front of him an hour ago, and he could have said so in a dozen words. *There's something I took. You should know what you gave.*

Ephram would have listened. He would have gone still, and thought about it, and very probably said that he was glad of that as well; he had turned his back on a table of papers twice, for a friend's sake, without being told what was on them.

And then he would have known. He would have been a cohort captain at a chartered house who knew what the registry's unclassified enrollee was, and what he had carried east, and he would have known it on the day a district officer rode up to the gate, and for every day after. He was staying. He had a board with his name on it, and they would come and ask him, courteously, what he had seen.

Cael did not say it.

"You've a look on you," said Ephram.

"I'm writing something down."

"You always are." Ephram picked up his coat with the wrong hand and got it over his shoulder at the second try. "Don't make me sound clever in it. I walked into a post."

He went up the steps, slowly, and at the top he began to whistle. It was the song from the residence windows, and the third line was wrong.

*Nine. Taken in earnest from a friend. He asked for the fight and not for this, and gave it without knowing that he gave it. Iron weight. It came quietly, like a rule laid along a line already drawn. *He isn't told. That was priced last night and it's owing from today. It doesn't go yellow in a fortnight. He chalked a rule over his own name with his wrong hand and said he was still glad, and I stood there holding his ladder with a thing of his in me and said I was writing something down.*

*Why not. One: it's on no paper the registry holds, and a man with a pencil had the top of the west tier.

# TEST 2 — Book 5 Climax Match: Daeva (shakedown clean-room draft)

<!-- SCENE-START -->

The tunnel under the Continental ground kept the cold of old stone, and the crowd above it made a sound like weather.

Cael walked toward the light with his hands unwrapped. The stewards had offered him the linen twice — white, with academy colors at the wrist — and twice he had declined, because the colors marked a tier cohort, and there was no cohort to mark. The second steward hadn't argued. He'd only looked at Cael the way people had looked at him all week: as a clerical error that had learned to walk.

The ground opened around him, sixty paces of oval floor under an afternoon sky. Grey slabs of shatter-slate, fitted tight, quarried soft on purpose so that Gold-tier force would crack them visibly and the judges could score what it touched. A ring of white boundary stone set flush at the edge of the field. On the north rim, the judges' station stood raised behind a rail — three judges, three exchange-slates, three sticks of chalk. Above and behind them the academy sections climbed in tiers of banners, every registered academy on the continent, because the Continental came only once in three years and this was its last afternoon.

[NEW-CANON: shatter-slate arena floor — slabs engineered to fracture visibly under tier-grade force, for scoring. NEW-CANON: exchange-slate scoring — judges chalk each exchange as technique class + executor's tier, weighted by tier differential.]

Halcenvane's section made a brave narrow noise. The rest of the ground had come to see the technicality. Tournament law required an academy to field any practitioner on its enrollment record — a clause written to stop academies from hiding their weak — and Karis had read it until it bent the other way and put him on the card. Nobody had thanked her aloud. On the bracket sheet his line carried no tier color, only a clerk's neat hedge: *entered unclassified; weighted at Copper baseline.* A courtesy fiction, so the arithmetic could run.

He found, without meaning to, the officials' gallery beside the judges' station, and Archmarshal Vastin in it — grey coat, hands folded, watching the field the way a man attends an audit. Cael looked away first.

Eight days he'd heard the same word in the concourses. *Tierless.* Thrown as a verdict, not a description. He'd stopped correcting the tone, because underneath it he carried his own answer, worn smooth: he had beaten every fighter they had ever put in front of him at his own level. Circuit, academy, Crown. Whatever his level was, he had cleared it. The word for everyone within reach had always been *next.*

Daeva came out of the south tunnel to a sound the weather couldn't hold.

Nineteen years old. Gold Rank 3. The youngest Gold-tier practitioner in continental record, and she crossed the slate without hurry, stopped exactly on her mark, and set her feet under her shoulders with her weight centered and nothing spent on the crowd. Cael read the walk the way he read everything. Economical. Conservative. A prodigy who intended to remain one, on the single afternoon she had everything to lose and nothing to win that anyone would count.

[NEW-CANON: Daeva — Force Path, Gold Rank 3. Signature declaration: condensed planes of force ("steps") she can stand on and launch from, visible as shimmer-panes with bright rims; secondary use as thrown force-lances. Proposed limits, witnessed in-scene below: maximum three planes held at once; a plane must anchor by a drawn line to whole, unbroken stone beneath it; after the third plane she must return to ground to re-anchor.]

The marshal called the terms from the field's center: match by scored exchanges; the white ring concedes the exchange; the count concedes the match. He withdrew. The ground went quiet enough that Cael heard the chalk shift in the head judge's fingers.

Daeva opened with a declaration he had only ever read about.

The air two paces ahead of her hardened. A pane of nothing, visible only where light bent along its rim, tilted like a stair no builder would risk — and she stepped onto it, up onto it, above him, and the lance came off her palm down an angle no floor-bound fighter learns to answer.

He moved on the read, not the sight. The edge-read had ridden in his shard structure since Kindling — a half-beat's warning when a committed strike stopped being a question — and it bought him the half-beat. The lance took the slab he'd stood on and the slab answered the way it was quarried to: a white spiderweb of fracture, loud as a dropped plate.

Chalk moved on the north rim. Hers.

[NEW-CANON: Cael's entering fragment loadout, three pieces, each costed. (1) Edge-read, original Kindling shard: half-beat warning on committed attacks; overuse cost — a splitting pressure behind the eyes. (2) Wind-adjacent burst, integrated from Lira, on-page in Book 3: one hard lateral acceleration; cost — scorched lungs, minutes of shallow breath. (3) Stone-adjacent forearm, integrated from a Halcenvane Stone-cohort demonstration: one block's worth of hardened arm; cost — the arm rings numb after, grip unreliable.]

The second exchange taught him her ceiling. She built a step, then a second above it, then a third — and never a fourth. From the third she always came back down, and she always came down onto whole stone, never onto the cracked slabs. He filed it. He was losing, and filing.

The third exchange nearly ended him. Her lance levered a slab up out of the floor entirely, a grey door standing on its hinge of rubble, and he put it between them because it existed; her next lance sheared it through the middle, and he rode the topple — let the falling half hide him for the one breath it took to spend the wind. Lira's wind, taken watching her fly, two years old in him now and still not his shape. It threw him four paces left out of the lance's line, and then the cost came due and his lungs shrank to fists, and he finished the exchange breathing like a man rationing air, one pace inside the white ring where she had been herding him all along.

Chalk. Hers.

Then the long middle of it, the stretch where the match became a single sustained sentence with no place in it to breathe: she rose and struck and re-rose, three steps built and burned and rebuilt, lances coming down the diagonals while he gave ground along the ring's curve and spent the stone forearm to stop the one strike he couldn't slip — grey arm, ringing block, elbow to fingertips gone numb as a hanged bell — and gave more ground, and watched her land, and land, and land, always on the unbroken slabs, until the sky behind her seemed like territory she held and the floor itself declined to be his ally, cracked everywhere he'd been driven and whole everywhere she chose.

She landed again.

And there — the fourth time, the fifth — he saw it clean: she had formed a step above the broken field once, early, and the pane had shivered, its rim stuttering like a lamp in wind, and she had let it die and rebuilt a pace east, over whole stone. Her steps drank from the floor. Anchor to sound slab, or no step at all. The broken field — the wreckage the two of them had made — she treated like water.

So he fought her for the floor instead of the match. He couldn't crack slate; she could, and did, every time she missed him, and he began spending his misses like coin, drawing lances down into whole slabs, backing, drawing again. It cost him the chalk — every exchange scored hers, the slate arithmetic weighing Gold over Copper-baseline until his column read like an apology — and it bought him a widening lake of fractured grey where her stair could not stand.

She saw it, of course. Gold Rank 3 at nineteen sees everything. She stopped missing, and stopped chasing, and held the whole eastern third of the field, patient as winter, needing nothing from him but time.

He understood, somewhere in the sixth exchange, that his plan had the wrong shape — that every plan he'd ever fought had really been one plan: find the level, learn the fighter inside it, outdecide them within reach. She declined to have a level he could stand on. He had been fighting as if a floor ran under the two of them, some shared footing the match rested on. She fought from the air. There was no floor.

There had never been one under him, either.

The thought didn't arrive like grief. It arrived like a door unlocked from the far side, and behind it stood the one thing he did that no tier did, the thing that had eaten Lira's wind and a Stone cohort's arm and kept them: he could take what he witnessed. He had never taken from Gold. He had never taken mid-fight, with the ability's owner actively trying to end him. And watching had a price no fighter pays willingly — you cannot watch a thing wholly and evade it too.

He stopped evading.

She built the stair to finish it — first pane condensing over whole stone at the field's eastern edge, and he stood flat-footed in his lake of broken slate and watched the way he'd learned to watch anything he meant to keep: the line she drew down from the pane to the sound slab beneath, taut as a plumb-string of bent light; the pane thickening along it; her weight arriving; the whole grammar of the thing. Her lance found him standing still. It opened his ribs along the left side, a hot shallow line, the cost of the seeing — and he got what he paid for.

Unnamed. Force-adjacent. Standing surface, self only.
Integration: partial. Duration: undetermined.
Tier equivalent: unknown.

The notice hung in his eye alone, irregular as all of them, and he did not have time to argue with it, because she was coming down her stair with the match's end in her hand.

He stepped up to meet her.

One pace of air held him.

The crowd's weather stopped. Not fell — stopped, the way a held breath stops, ten thousand people declining to spend the next second. He felt the anchor-line run down from the sole of his boot to the broken slab below — *broken,* cracked white, and the step held anyway, and some far shelf of his mind filed that, too, filed it hard — and he took the second step, and the third, rising along the line of her descent, tierless, unwrapped, standing on her own declaration between her and the sky.

Daeva's eyes went wrong. Just for a beat. The first unhurried girl on the continent, hurrying.

Then the price called itself due.

FRAGMENT SILENT.
Unnamed. Wind-adjacent. Capacity exceeded.
Restoration: undetermined.

Blood came off his lip before he understood his nose had started. The numb arm dropped dead to his side; the pressure behind his eyes went from splitting to white; and the third step held, but he didn't. His legs simply left the ledger. He dropped through empty air onto the broken field, and the ground he'd spent the whole match cracking took him without kindness.

Daeva checked her final lance mid-cast — the single uneconomical act of her afternoon — and put it past his head into the ring-stone, where it cratered white dust out of the boundary.

The marshal's count reached him through the floor. He got an elbow under himself at four. At seven the elbow quit. At ten the match went where the record would always have said it was going.

He lay on his back in the wreckage and watched the dust settle out of a sky that had held his weight, three steps' worth, longer than any tier had ever agreed to.

The head judge's chalk did not move.

He could see the station from where he lay, tilted, rail and slate and three figures who had scored sixty years of Continentals between them. One had the rulebook open and turned pages with a wet thumb, forward, then back, then forward again to the same place. One leaned across the rail arguing at the marshal in fragments that reached Cael flat on the ground — *log it as what, the line wants an executor tier, the line has always wanted an executor tier* — and the marshal, waiting for the exchange record he needed to close the match, received nothing at all. The head judge stood with her hand raised to signal the final scores, the way she had raised it all afternoon.

The crowd's noise came back wrong, roar curdling into question, ten thousand people asking their neighbors what column that went in.

Daeva stood on whole stone with her win, looking not at the judges and not at the crowd but at the air north of the broken field, where nothing at all still held the shape of three steps going up.

The hand did not come down.

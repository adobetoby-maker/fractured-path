## Book Promise

Make the reader feel the weight of history — the sense that the present is built on something that was deliberately buried.

## Protagonist Arc Statement

*I know the system was built to exclude me* → *the system was built specifically to prevent me.*

The arc statement is staged honestly, in the log, at both poles, and the distance between them is the whole book. The first sentence is where Book 6 and Book 7 left him: the system had no seat for him — no tier, no rank, no file he can see — no evaluation pathway — he was *left out*, an omission, a case the instruments could not parse. That belief is true and the book lets it stand for fourteen chapters, because from the outside exclusion and prevention look identical — a locked door is a locked door whether you were forgotten or feared. Then, in a pre-Compact archive beneath a border city, a damaged four-hundred-year-old record turns the omission into an intention: the classification system was not a structure that happened to have no room for him. It was a structure *built to make certain he never occurred* — designed, deployed, and maintained for four centuries with exactly one purpose, to prevent the reemergence of what Cael is. The second sentence is written in Chapter 16, over a table of primary sources, in a hand that has stopped being steady. The difference is precise and the book refuses to let it blur: *excluded* means they had no place for you; *prevented* means you were the reason they built the place. He turns twenty the day before he learns it. The book gives him that on purpose.

## Treswick, the Deepstacks & the Bearing — Reference (established across Parts 1–2, referenced throughout)

**Before the Paths (the title's referent — an object and an argument, per the series' title-as-argument precedent):** Two things wearing one phrase. First, the literal era: the time *before the Path system existed* — four centuries back, before the Architect deployed the classification apparatus, when what Cael is walked the continent under the old word, [UNBOUND]. Second, the argument the book makes about the present: everything in Valdris — the tiers, the registry, the Arbiter at every Kindling, the wall Cael was expelled over — was *built on top of* a buried thing, laid down deliberately by someone who wanted the thing underneath to stay buried. The book's sentence in one phrase: the present is not the natural order; it is a lid, and the reader spends twenty-four chapters watching the companions pry up an edge of it.

**Treswick `[NEW-CANON]` (the border city — PROVISIONAL):** The last governed city on the frontier — old, walled, straddling the Registry Line where the maintained roads give out and the void roads begin. It is a Compact city: it has a registry office, an Arbiter station, tiered districts, and city-access-by-rank, which makes it the first place since the academy above Ostrand where Cael's [SHATTERED] classification is a live legal hazard — he cannot enter the inner districts, and an Arbiter station is a thing to be walked wide of, not toward. But it is a *frontier* Compact city, not the armed heartland the asset-restriction guards (that is deep west; nobody goes there): Treswick tolerates edge-territory traffic because it lives on it — carters, brokers, unranked labor, the ferry trade — and a crew that keeps its head down can work there. The Path system has deformed it exactly the way the series shows institutions (charter §6.5): the city is built in concentric tiers up a bluff, the registry office rations district passes by rank, and — the seam this book needs — the city is *old enough to predate the Compact*, so beneath the modern registry, in the bluff's foundations, lie the **Deepstacks**: the archives of whatever governed here before standardization, sealed, uncatalogued, and legally nobody's. Treswick's argument in one image: a Compact registry office built directly on top of a pre-Compact archive it has never read.

**The Deepstacks `[NEW-CANON]` (the pre-Compact archive — PROVISIONAL — the book's institutional machine, treated the way Book 2 treated the circuit and Book 7 treated the board: rules, access, failure modes):** The sub-foundation archive beneath Treswick's registry — records from before the standardization directive, in scripts and formats the Compact never converted, kept because no one ever ordered them destroyed and no one ever needed them. Access is the chapter's institutional problem: the Deepstacks are technically Compact property, gated behind a district pass Cael cannot legally hold, staffed by no one, catalogued by no one, and entered in practice through the old scholars' provision — the same historical-precedent seam Karis has used since Book 4 — which admits *registered historical-studies practitioners*. Which Karis is not, out here, and which **Cleon** is. The Deepstacks are where the buried thing is buried: not hidden, just un-read for four hundred years, because reading them requires a Path-studies scholar, a researcher who knows what she is looking at, and a reason to look — and until the Quieting, no one had all three at once.

**Cleon `[NEW-CANON]` (the historical-Path-studies practitioner — PROVISIONAL — the book's specialist, the old-yard-owner convention applied to scholarship):** Sixties. Rune Path, Bronze-tier, a registered historical-Path-studies practitioner who has spent thirty years in the Deepstacks and the region's older ruins reconstructing what Path-craft looked like before the classification system flattened it — work the Compact tolerates as harmless antiquarianism and funds not at all. He is the third keeper-of-a-record the series has given after Vell and Pike, and the first whose record is *history* rather than a ledger. He is not an ally recruited and not a mystery to be solved; he is a professional who has been waiting his whole career for someone to walk in with the right question, and Karis walks in with three sets of carvings copied off fused-stone floors and the word she has been holding since Book 6. Cleon's function: he can read the *structure* of old inscriptions where Karis can only read their strata; he recognizes the Fallow Ring / Long Stair / Drowned Hall carvings as *instructions*; and his Deepstacks, cross-referenced, hold the primary source. His independent-want note: he does not want to leave the Deepstacks or join anyone — he wants the work *finished* and *believed*, and he has learned, over thirty years, that no one in the Compact will ever believe it. Differentiation from prior specialists: where Oryn approaches Cael as a patient who doesn't work, Cleon never examines Cael at all — Cael is beneath his notice; it is the *carvings* Cleon reads, and the book keeps him pointed at the page, not the boy, which is exactly why Cael can be in the room.

**Rune Path `[NEW-CANON]` (new Path defined this book — PROVISIONAL; from the excluded Path-name roster, now instantiated):** Inscription architecture. Where Ember declares ignition, Iron Skin density, and Tide current, Rune declares *structure*: the perception of how a made system is put together — which mark in an inscription bears load and which is ornament, whether a sequence is a record (describing) or an instruction (commanding), where a constructed pattern would fail. Its civil face is authentication and restoration: Rune practitioners verify old documents, reconstruct damaged inscriptions, and are the trade that tells you whether a seal is real. Its diagnostic technique, the analogue of Oryn's reading, is contact or close-range attention to an inscribed surface — Cleon calls it "seeing the bones of a thing." Limitations, weaknesses, costs (charter §6.1, stated before the fragment is load-bearing): Rune reads *structure, not meaning* — it can tell you a passage is an instruction and cannot tell you what it instructs; it works on *made* systems only (inscriptions, constructed patterns, worked stone), never on living architecture — which is the limit that matters, because when Cael turns the integrated fragment inward it finds his interior legible *as if it were made*, and neither he nor the book is permitted to say what that implies; it costs attention and time, not reserve, so it is cheap where Tide is dear, and it is useless in a fight. Inside quiet ground: untested here, deliberately (Cael carries it into no Quiet site as a Rune practitioner would — the one time it matters is turned on himself, Ch22, outside any perimeter).

**Force Path (Teague's Path — canon by name since Book 7's reference; mechanics PROVISIONAL, defined here per arch review H06, stated BEFORE Ch19's integration):** Impulse architecture. Where Iron Skin declares density and Iron Wall declares a held plane, Force declares *push*: a kinetic impulse delivered through the practitioner's own frame at contact or a body's length — the shove that moves a cart, the strike that breaks a brace, the throw. Civil face: labor — lifting, breaking, hauling; the trade that clears a road. Limits, weaknesses, costs (charter §6.1): line-of-body only — Force goes where the body is pointed and no further, no reach, no thrown effect; it needs something to push AGAINST — a Force practitioner on nothing solid is a man waving; and the cost is RECOIL: a fraction of every impulse comes back through the frame that delivered it, which is why a Force practitioner's joints go first and why Teague at forty has a wrist that clicks (stated Ch17, paid Ch19 as 'a Force-line recoil-cost paid by Teague'). Useless at range, useless against what does not resist, and, integrated as a fragment (Ch19), partial and paid in Cael's own frame — the Ch21 exchanges are constrained by all three.

**Iron Wall Path (Vastin's Path — canon from the Bible, first shown on the page — PROVISIONAL mechanics):** Vastin is Silver-tier, Iron Wall Path (Bible §Archmarshal Vastin). Distinct from Brom's Iron Skin: where Iron Skin densifies the *body* (a man made harder), Iron Wall projects *fixed defensive structure* — a braced plane, a held line, ground that does not give — at the cost of mobility and reserve; the declaration roots the practitioner and everything behind him and cannot be carried on the advance.

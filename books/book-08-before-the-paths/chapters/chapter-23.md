# Chapter 23 — The First Quiet

The haze had gone with the night, and in the morning of the three hundred and seventh the thing was there, across the plain, where the line said it would be, and he catalogued it from the plain's edge before anyone had spoken, because he had wanted to see it since a landing in the snow and the seeing should be entered before the wanting got to it.

Low. That was the first fact, and the one he had not expected: a thing a half-day off across flat ground, and it did not stand up against the sky as the Span's crown had; it lay down. A wide grey lid on the plain, level, with a rim round it that broke the level — not a wall; a row of somethings, short and blunt, like teeth worn to the gum, going round all of it at the edge of what the eye could resolve. Nothing on it. Nothing over it. The plain went to it from every side without a hill or a fold, as if the ground had been worn down to meet it, and the sun came up behind it along the bearing and lit the rim first, and the lid was grey, and then the sun was over it and the whole thing was the colour the plain was, and would have been hard to see if you had not known where the line went.

"A thousand," Karis said, beside him. She had not got the cord out. She had the map, and the straightedge from Ternhall along the line, and her eye, and she said the number as she said a number she was going to check. "Across. Two and a half of the Ring. Near enough. I'll know by dark."

The rest was walking, and they went down onto the plain, eleven and a mule, in the order the plain took them, which was any order, because there was nothing on it to draw for.

---

He logged the half-day as a half-day, one entry at noon, because the plain was the plain and the thing was not and he did not want them on the same line.

Flat. Grass to the knee, going pale as it went east, and then not grass; shale; and then, in the last mile, a thing under the shale that was not shale, that the boots found before the eyes did — a hardness, level, that the shale lay on in a skin the wind moved. Rock. Not stone laid; rock, the plain's own, ground down. Karis went to one knee at the first of it and put her bound hand flat on it and her good hand beside it and looked along the surface to the rim a mile off and said, "It's been worn to the floor. The whole plain. From here in." She stood up. "Water doesn't do that. Wind doesn't do that in the time there's been wind."

"What does," Brom said.

"I don't know. Time. More than the office has a column for." She went on.

He catalogued the eleven on the plain's rock, because it was the last halt before the thing and he wanted the count. Brom with the forearm bound and the good one carrying, on a foot that had walked to a city and in one and out of it. Lira with two arms and the third box, and her eyes on the rim ahead and not on him, because she had looked at him at a pass's mouth and had said nothing and had decided to go on saying it until he said something first. Karis with the case and the map, reading the rock as she read everything, for the strata, with the bound hand holding the notebook she had been told not to write in. Seln at the back, where the count was kept, with nothing on the plain to keep it against. Vastin on the stick, the twenty-eighth day of a month, walking as a man walks who has two days left on an order and means to keep them. Oryn with the roll on her back and the mule's rope in her hand, three days past the end of the road that had been hers, walking east because a man's ribs were owed a reading she had said she would not take until he had stood where he was going, and because ten people had walked into four quiet places and she had been in reach for one. Teague and his three — the tall woman with the spear; the broad man on the leg that bore today, as she had said it would, and limped worse, as she had said it would; the older woman with the cloth bag — walking a stride apart from the six, as a crew walks that has buried one of its own the day before and has not yet found the distance it wants to walk at. And himself, with the middle box, and the brass in his coat, and the ribs saying their one thing at every stride, and thirteen things held to nothing, and the count.

Eleven. He entered it at noon, and did not enter the twelfth, because the twelfth was under stone at a pass's mouth and the log had a line for that already and did not need another.

The rim, at a mile, resolved. Uprights. That was the word for what they had been; they were stumps now, the height of a knee at their lowest and the height of a man's waist at their highest, grey, seamless, of the stone that had no seams, going round the whole of the thing at a spacing he could not yet count, weathered on their outer faces to a smoothness that had rounded every edge that had ever been on them, and cut, on their inner faces, where the weather had not had them, with marks.

And the floor.

He saw it from the last hundred strides, between two stumps, and stopped, and so did everyone, because it was the fourth site and the Ring and the Stair and the Hall laid together and pressed flat: a level, seamless, grey floor, a thousand metres across by a woman's eye, with nothing on it — no ring, no stair going down, no hall's walls, no arch — nothing at all, from the stumps at its rim to the middle, five hundred strides off across it, which looked, from here, like more floor. The rock of the plain came up to the stumps and stopped at the same level as the floor, so that the floor was not raised and not sunk; it was the plain, made into a floor, and the plain had been worn down to be level with it, or the floor had been laid to be level with the plain, and he did not know which and neither did Karis, and she said so.

"The oldest thing I've stood near," Brom said. He had gone to the nearest stump and put his hand on its outer face and moved the hand over the rounding the way a hand goes over a horse's shoulder to price it. "Older than the walls at Treswick. Older than the bridge under them. Older than anything I've priced, and I've priced a tiered hall." He took the hand off. "You could put your whole life on that stump's outside and not wear it that much."

Karis had the cord out.

---

She paced it along the line from outside, as she had paced the fourth, because that was how you paced a thing you had decided not to go into yet, and it took the afternoon, and he catalogued the afternoon by the count, because the count was the thing that was going to be entered.

Lira on the line. She had the job from the Ring and the Stair and the fourth: walk the perimeter with her sigil lit, a stride at a time, until it went out, and step back until it came on, and call it. She did it as she had done it at four sites, and it went out at the stride, at a place a pace inside the stumps' inner faces, and she said "Gone," and stepped back, and said "Back," and Karis put a stone down, and they went on. Cord and stride and stone. Brom marking. Seln at the mule with the case, keeping the count of the plain, which was eleven and a mule and nothing else, because there was nothing else, because there was nothing on the plain to hide anything behind, and Seln, who had spent fifteen years using the ground, stood in the one place on the continent where there was no ground to use and did not like it and said so once, to the mule.

Teague's three sat at the rim while the cord went round, on the plain's rock outside the stumps, and did not help and were not asked to, because the pacing was the six's and the contract had said so. The tall woman watched Lira's sigil go out at the stride and come on at the stride back, twenty times, and at the twentieth she looked at her own hand, at the mark on it, and closed the hand, and did not open it again that afternoon. The broad man sat with the leg out and the crossbow across his knees and looked at the floor like water he had been told was deep. Teague stood. He did not sit all afternoon; he stood at the place where the path had come in between two stumps and read the floor as he read a job, and Cael, passing with the cord's far stone, saw him do it, and saw him come to the end of the reading with nothing moved, because there was nothing on the floor to move anyone to, and saw him not like that, and enter it, in his way, which was to stand there longer.

The line was sharp. It was a hair sharp, as it had been at every site: a stride outside, Lira's sigil lit; a stride inside, gone; nothing between. The circle was perfect. Karis said so at the twentieth cord and again at the eightieth, in the voice for a finding, because a circle that stays perfect across eighty cords is a circle and not a guess. And it was larger, and it was older, and she had the first from the cord — "A thousand and twelve. Across. Call it a thousand" — and the second from the stumps.

She read the stumps between cords. Every one she passed. The marks on the inner faces were the same make; he had known that from the first between the shale and the rim, and knew it now from twenty of them: the rows, the bearers, the lean. But she did not read the marks. She read the *cuts*. She put her good hand into a row on a stump's inner face and ran a finger along the cut's bottom, and did it at the next stump, and the next, and at the twelfth she said the thing.

"Deeper."

"The cuts?"

"The cuts. Every floor I've charcoaled, the cut's the same depth — a made thing's cut, the tool's width, one pass. These are that, and then they're worn, at the bottom, where nothing's touched them since, in a way the Ring's aren't and the Span's aren't and the Hall's aren't. The cut was made and then the stone went on being stone under it for longer than the others have been stone at all." She took the finger out. "And the layer under it. Look." She did not point; she put the straightedge from Ternhall flat against the stump's foot where the rock of the plain met it, and there was a line in the rock, a hair below the stump's base, that went round the stump and off across the plain's rock as far as the eye went — a stratum, a change in the grey, that the stump sat on and the floor sat on and everything else on the whole plain sat above. "That's under all of it. That's older than the floor. And the floor's sitting on it, cut to sit on it, the way the next layer's laid so the one above it fits. This is the first one." She said it in the voice, and then she said the rest, which was the part that was hers. "I'm not saying what it's the first of. I'm saying that by the strata this floor was cut before the others, and by the cuts these marks were made before the others, and that a thing that's written once and copied is written first somewhere, and this is where. The others are copies. This is nearer what they copied." She looked at the stump. "That's a finding about rock. It's not a finding about anything else."

"Noted," said Cael, and Vastin, a stride behind him, said nothing, which was the man having learned when a word was already taken.

The bearing ended.

That was the last thing the pacing found, and it found it at the far side, at the hundred and fifty-ninth cord, when the circle was closed and Karis laid the straightedge along the map's line for the last time on this side of anything. The line ran from the west, through four circles, to this one, the fifth, and through it, to the middle — and stopped. She laid the edge past the middle and there was nothing on the far side of the fifth circle for it to touch: no sixth; no distance on any sheet; nothing in the index; nothing on the plain, which went on east, level, worn, empty, to a horizon that had nothing on it. Oldest-last. The end of the line.

"It's the end," she said. "There's nothing past it. Not on the paper and not on the ground." She folded the map. "I've paced it. A thousand across, sharp, perfect, oldest by the strata, and at the end of the line. That's all that the outside can say."

He wrote it at the mule, standing, in the plain hand, because a thing paced should be entered before it is stood in.

*Three hundred and seventh day. The court — the fifth, and the last; the end of the line. A thousand and twelve across by the cord; sharp; perfect; oldest by the strata; the marks the first cut. Eleven and a mule at the line. A hundred and twenty-three in hand. Nineteen. Thirteen. Nothing past it.*

The council took less than a sunset, as the one on a crest above the fourth had, and it was the same council with the answer the other way round, and he catalogued that, because a thing decided twice in two directions is a thing whose reasons should both be on the page. On the crest there had been five, and no healer within four days, and a circle twice the size of anything they had entered, and three sentences of reasons not to, and they had not. At the rim there were eleven, and a healer a stride off, and a circle two and a half times the Ring, and the same three sentences, and one more, which was that the line ended here and there was nothing past it to walk to instead. Nobody said the fourth sentence. Karis folded the map. Lira looked at him. That was the council, and it had decided what it had decided on a bridge and at a fire and over a map, which was that he was going in, alone, and that the eleven were going to stand at the line while he did, with their Paths gone at a stride and a healer's hands useless a stride in, because that was the only way it could be done and every one of them had known it since a landing in the snow.

---

The line, at dusk, with the whole company at it.

He had wanted this before the middle, and had said so, and they had given it him: eleven people at the rim of the oldest quiet, a stride outside the stumps, in a row, as ten had stood inside an iron a pace apart at a Span; and he catalogued them at it, one each, because it was the count and the count was going to be the thing he left here when he walked away from it.

The sigils went first. He watched them go, because a healer had said it was what she read for and a woman with a cord had said it was the finding she was afraid of what came after: every mark on every hand at the line going dark at the stride, at the same stride, at the same instant — Lira's, and Brom's, and the tall woman's and the broad man's and Teague's, and Vastin's, on a hand that had held a plane across a stream-bed the day before, and Karis's, on a hand that was bound, and Oryn's. The same. A hundred lamps. He did not say the rest of it and neither did she, and she looked at her own dark hand as she had looked at a column, and entered it, and drew nothing.

Lira. She stood at the line and looked at all of it — not the middle; the *whole*, the rim going round in its stumps and the floor lying level to the far side and the plain beyond — as she had looked at the tops of three walls from a road, and at the Ring from a rim, and at a ladder from a registry's yard, and every one of those times she had looked away, deliberately, because she had a thing about the tops of things and had had it since a hall. She did not look away. She stood with her two hands at her sides and her sigil dark and looked at the largest made thing on the continent for as long as she wanted to, and then she said, to it, "Right," and it was all that she had to say to it, and it was enough.

Brom. He went to the stump nearest him and put his bound forearm on it, and his good hand, and pushed, once, not with anything — with his weight, as a man tests a post — and it did not move, and he had not thought it would, and he said, "Older than the walls. Older than anything I've priced," a second time, because he had said it once at the rim and it was truer at the line, and then he sat down on the plain's rock with his back to the stump and the forearm on his knee and looked at the floor as he looked at a fence he had been paid to walk, and was, Cael thought, the only one of the eleven who was entirely at his ease, because a thing that old was a thing that had been made, and Brom priced made things, and this one was simply the largest.

Karis. She had the notebook in the bound hand and the pencil in the other and she was not writing. She was standing at the line with her hand on a stump's inner face, in a row of marks, with the cut's bottom under her finger, and she said, to the stump, in the small voice, "It's the first one," and did not say what of, and did not write it down, because she had written it down at the twelfth stump and a thing written once does not need writing at the line.

Seln. He stood at the end of the row, at the plain's edge of it, with the cache on his back and his eyes on the plain and not on the floor, because somebody had to and it was always him, and he gave the count as he gave it at every halt, to the plain, flatly. "Nothing behind. Nothing beside." He turned, then, and looked at the floor, once, the whole width of it, as a man looks at the one room he has ever been in with no door. "Nothing ahead but that."

Vastin. He came to the line last, on the stick, on the twenty-eighth day of a month, and stood at it, at a stumps' width from Cael, and looked at the mark on his own hand, which was dark, and had been dark at four lines this year and had been lit at every other line of his life, and he said the one sentence, and it was not the one Cael had expected, and it was the right one.

"I stood at a line for forty years," Vastin said, "and told people which side of it they were. I never once asked who drew it." He looked at the floor. "This is where they drew it."

Oryn went in.

One stride. She did it as she did everything, square to it, with her hands out of her coat and the roll on her back, over the line between two stumps and onto the floor, and stood there, and held her hands out from her sides a little, open, and Cael saw her feel them go — the thing she owned, the current that ran to what was hurt and arrived, going out of her hands at the stride as a lamp goes — and she stood with the useless hands a moment, on the oldest floor, and then stepped back, one stride, over the line, and the hands were hers again, and she closed them.

"Nothing," she said. "Everything, round it." She looked at Cael. "Same as you."

Nobody answered that. He did not, because he did not know what she meant by it and did not want to, yet, and she did not say it again, because a healer says a finding once, and it stood there between the two of them at the line, unanswered, and the book of the year did not have a column for it, and he let it stand.

Teague went in one stride, first, alone, with the other hand out — the good one — and set his feet as he set them at a cart, and the hand was a hand, and the line from heel to palm was a man's arm and nothing else, and he stood inside with that for the time it took to know it and stepped back over the line between the two stumps.

He did not go in again. He stood a stride back from the line with his three — the tall woman with the spear grounded and lifted, the broad man on his stick with the leg that bore today, the older woman with the cloth bag — and read his people in the old order, face after face, and came to the gap, and went past it, and looked at the floor, and said, at the size he said a term: "My crew's stood at four of these. It's stood at enough." He looked at Cael. "Field's mine. This isn't field." And that was the contract, kept to its last line, by a man who had lost one of four to a line the day before and was not going to put the three across another, and Cael entered it as the contract's best clause, and the man's.

Then the thing began.

He did not know what else to call it and he did not call it anything. He was standing at the line with the straightedge from Denvash in his hand — he had taken it out without deciding to — and the thirteen ringing faint in him as they rang at every rim, and the still place at the middle of them doing what it did, and the ribs saying their one thing; and under the ribs, at the place, something *turned*. Toward him, or him toward it; he could not have said which, as he could not have said whether the rows on the stumps leaned east because they were going or because they had come. He did not have a word for it and did not reach for one. He held the straightedge and did not move and did not enter it, because it had not finished beginning, and a cataloguer does not enter a thing that has not finished.

Lira, beside him, said the thing she had said at the Ring, in the autumn, at the first line any of them had ever stood at, and she said it as she had said it then, plainly, to him.

"Every other one, yours worked and ours died."

"Yes."

"You think this one's different."

He looked at the floor. At the middle of it, five hundred strides off across the seamless grey, where there was nothing, where the line ended, where a thing that had been quiet since before there was paper to say so was lying level with the plain in the last of the light, not knowing he was there.

"I think this one's the first one they made," he said. "I think if any place on the continent is going to tell me what I am, it's the one they built to make sure I'd never be."

He stepped over the line.

His Path did not die. It also did not *notice* it should have.

"Wait here," he said, and did not look back, and walked toward the center of the oldest quiet, alone.

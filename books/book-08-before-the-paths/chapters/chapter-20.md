# Chapter 20 — The Cordon

The camp on the three hundred and fifth was the first one on the bearing that had no site in sight of it, and he catalogued that first, because it was the first time since a ford that he had lain down with nothing made within a day's walk, and the country had a different sound.

Dry ground, rising. The Span's far rim was behind them by half a day, and the land east of it went up in long steps of grass and shale toward a line of hills that had a notch in it, low and square, where the bearing went; and Seln had looked at the notch from the last step at noon, for a long time, with his hands in his coat, and had said "Pass," and nothing else, and had not looked at it again, which from Seln was a paragraph. Beyond the hills, by the office's distance from a hold, the point. A day, or two. Karis had said she would pace it and she had not paced it yet, because the pass was between her and it, and she did not pace a thing she had not stood on.

Twelve, and a mule, and a fire on the shale with the wind off the hills taking the smoke away east ahead of them, as if the smoke had somewhere to be. The broad man on a stick — Vastin's second one, cut on the crest and given without comment, so that there were two men on sticks at the fire and neither of them mentioned it. Teague's arm in Oryn's wrapping, elbow to wrist, and the hand at the end of it doing nothing, because she had told it to. Karis's hand bound and the notebook in it. Vastin, the twenty-sixth day of a month. Brom with two bruises that he had not shown anyone and that Oryn had read through the coat and left alone, because a bruise was a bruise. Oryn, two days past the end of the road that had been hers and one day into the road that was not, walking as she walked, on her terms, which she had not restated, because she had said them at a ford and once at a ford was a term.

And the blanket.

Karis had laid it out at dusk without saying she was going to, as she laid everything out, and by the time the fire was a fire the blanket had the whole of the year on it and he catalogued it before she started, because she was going to change it, and he wanted the before.

The carter's sheet, with the four circles and the pencil line through them and the blank sheet pinned to its edge with the line running on across it to the divider's point. The kit's ledger, open at the index. The Compact's records in their rows — five rows now, because the Span's sheets had a row of their own, and the sheets that had never been laid out at a spring had a row too, at the end, that she had not read aloud to anyone. The fifty-two sheets of copied walls from the autumn, rolled, and the Span's piers copied that morning, flat, weighted at the corners with shale. The leaf's copy, in her hand and his, with the gap drawn. The list of parts. The dividers, closed. The two brass edges, side by side along the line.

Everything they had found, on one blanket, on a shale step, with the wind going east over it, and a woman with one working hand about to reconcile it.

"I'll say what I've got," Karis said, to the blanket, "and then I'll say what it means, which is less, and then I'll stop."

---

She started with the piers.

The Span's carvings were the same make as the four floors — that was not a finding; he had known it from the rim and she had known it from the charcoal — but there were more of them, on the Span, than on any floor, and they were in rows on the piers where the floors' had been in rows on the walls, and Cleon's board read them, the bearers in their order, the lean of them left to right, and the lean went the same way the arch went. Along the bearing. East.

"The floors face the line," she said. "I had that from the Hall. I've got it four times now. Every one of them's cut so the rows lean along the bearing, as the arch runs. Whoever cut them knew where the next one was."

"Or where the last one was," Vastin said.

"Or that." She did not look up. "The rows don't say which way's first. Cleon said an instruction leans toward where it's going. I can't tell you whether the going was east or the coming was west. I can tell you the rows agree with the line, and the line agrees with the ages, and the ages run east."

She laid the index against the records, then, and the records against the walls, and it was the work of an hour and he watched the whole hour and did not enter any of it, because it was a reconciliation and a reconciliation was one entry, at the end, when the columns agreed.

They agreed. The dead man's grades in the kit's index were the office's marks on the sheets, first and second and third, and the office's dates by first sheet were the ages the walls' rows leaned along, and the fourth site's radius on the carter's sheet was the fourth site's radius on the office's, twice the others, and the stake count on the iron was the stake count in the ledger, and a distance from a hold on the office's sheet with the stroke and two bars was the distance she walked the dividers across from that hold, and the point came down where it had come down at Lowmarch, past the fourth, on the line, in the straightedge's shadow.

She put the point there, this time, with the pencil, and drew a circle round it the size of her thumbnail, because a thing that has been found the same way twice from two sources is a thing you can draw.

"There," she said. "Fixed. Past the hills. On the office's distance and the office's bearing and the dead man's grade and the walls' lean, and I'll pace it when I'm standing on it and not before." She put the pencil down. "That's what I've got."

No one took it up.. Teague, across the fire, who did not read, had watched the whole hour as he watched a job, and looked at the circle now as he had looked at a stake, and said nothing, because the reading was hers and he had said so at a plank.

"Now the other thing," Karis said.

---

She turned the records to the fifth column.

He knew the column. He had known it since a spring: the office's abbreviation, every sheet, every year, that Seln had read for them as *not asking* — the sigil going out at the line, every practitioner, every site, the same distance, the same instant. He had known, too, where the sentence went that Karis had begun over that column at the spring and had stopped, with her pencil down and her hand off the sheet and a rule said aloud in the small voice, because he had heard her not go there twice, and had entered the not-going both times.

She went there now.

"Every sheet," she said. "Every perimeter. Every practitioner the office ever sent to a line with a sigil, and every one of ours, and Teague's four at the Hall in the autumn, and the seven on the shelf yesterday who wouldn't step over a stake. It's the same. Not the same *kind* of thing — the *same*. The same stride. The same instant. A Silver goes at the line at the stride a Copper goes at. An Iron goes where a Tide goes. A man forty years on a Path goes where a boy a month on one goes, and neither of them goes a hair before the other, and neither of them a hair after, and it doesn't matter who they are, or what they've done, or how they were taught, or whether they believe in it." She put her good hand flat on the column. "I've got it on more than twenty sheets in an office's hand and on every page of my own since the Ring and on four floors of walls that say nothing about it at all, and I said it once at a spring and stopped, and I'm not stopping now, because a finding you stop three times is a finding you're afraid of, and I'm not afraid of it. I'm afraid of what comes after it, and that's a different thing."

She said it.

"They fail the way a hundred lamps fail when one wire's cut. Not the way a hundred souls fall silent."

The fire went on. The wind took the smoke east.

"That's the finding. That's all of it. A hundred people, a hundred Paths, a hundred lives spent on them, and they all go out at once, at the same place, at the same stride, as if somebody had cut one thing. Not a hundred things. One. And I don't know what the one thing is, and I'm entering it as an observation I can't explain, because a person's — " she stopped, and found the word she wanted, which was the office's, and used it as if it were a stone she had picked up and did not like the weight of — "a person's *spiritual entity*, which is the phrase, which is the thing they tell a boy at fourteen he's been given, doesn't have a *wire*. A soul doesn't fail identically. A soul fails the way the person does. Differently. Late or early. Bravely or not." She took the hand off the column. "These don't. So either the phrase is wrong about what the thing is, or I'm wrong about what a wire is, and I can't tell which from a blanket, and I'm not going to pretend I can."

Lira, across the fire, said nothing, and looked at her own two hands. Brom looked at the fire. Oryn, who had read flow all her life and had said on a bench that the thing at the middle of a man was not it, looked at Karis as she had looked at a hold-woman's hand — a thing being read, by someone else, correctly — and said nothing either. Seln was looking at the hills.

Vastin said: "If a man designed the apparatus, then the sigils — "

"A measurement is a fact about a ruler," Karis said. "Not about the hand that held it."

She said it over him, not loud, in the small voice, and he stopped, because it was her rule and she had said it at a table at Thornwater and at a spring and now at a fire, and a man who had spent forty years making findings into orders knew a rule when he heard one and knew that this one had been kept longer than any he had ever written.

"I know where that sentence goes," she said, to him, not unkindly. "I've known since the leaf. You know where it goes. Everyone at this fire knows where it goes. There's a leaf that says a man made the measure and the seat and the tier and the mark, and set an apparatus over the land, and there's a column that says every mark goes out like a lamp when a wire's cut, and there's a word on both that's the same word, and the sentence that joins them is about six words long and I could say it in my sleep. I'm not going to." She opened the notebook, with the bound hand, and turned to a page, and wrote on it, and turned to another page, and wrote on that. "Two findings. Two pages. What the leaf says, which is a record and says what it says. What the column says, which is a measurement and says what it says. I've put them on separate pages and I'm not drawing a line between them, because a line's a thing you draw when you've measured both ends, and I've measured one end and read the other, and reading isn't measuring. I've spent a year at the foot of a stair learning the difference. I'll not throw it away at a fire because the sentence is easy."

Vastin looked at her a long moment. Then he said, "Noted," and it was the third time, and he meant it as she meant her rule.

Cael entered it. He entered the finding, in her words, because they were exact. And under it he entered the thing that had not been done: that a line had been reached for by a man with forty years of reaching, and not drawn by a woman with one, and that the not-drawing was all that the year had been for, and that he had watched it hold at the hardest place it had yet been asked to, and it had held.

He did not draw it either.

He could have. He had a thing on his own page that nobody at the fire had, from a spring, from a row of dates — a deep-layer sheet that said *six years* and was five years and a hundred-some days, and a day he knew to the hour that lay under the office's rounding like a stone under a river — and he could have laid it beside the two pages and made a third, and the third would have made the line shorter, and he did not, because it was his and it was a count and not a finding, and because a man who has just watched a discipline hold does not go behind it with his own key. He entered that he had not. That was three things not done at one fire, by three people, and he counted them, and let the count stand.

---

Vastin went on reading after the others had stopped.

He had the last row — the sheets that had not been laid out at a spring, the ones with no site on them — and he read them as a man reads a file he has filed a thousand of, which was fast, and without moving his lips, and with his thumb keeping the place, and Cael, across the blanket with the log open on his knee and nothing entered on it for a quarter of an hour, watched him find the thing, because a man finding a thing in a file has a face for it, and Vastin's had not changed in forty years.

"Here."

He did not point. He had learned that from Seln, in a fortnight, and Cael entered that too.

"A directive. Not a survey; an order about the surveys. Six years back, at the head of the file, in the office's ruling hand — the hand that writes the forms and not the hand that fills them." He read it in the register he had once read everything in, which was the register of a man who had been read to all his life by paper and had read back. "*Findings under the fifth column to be held. Not for onward transmission. Not for citation. Not for the seat.* A seal. A rank I'd have saluted, once." He turned the sheet. "And on the back — " He stopped.

"On the back," Cael said.

"A second hand." Vastin turned the sheet to the fire so that the light went through it and the two hands lay one over the other in the paper. "Filed under it. Same date, or near. *Objection entered: the holding of a finding is not the finding's disproof. Refer to the seat.* A different seal. A rank I'd have saluted the same." He put the sheet down on the row, face up, the order side, and looked at it as he had looked at a stake on a crest. "Somebody wanted this buried. Somebody else put it in writing that it shouldn't be. That's not a monolith. That's a crack."

Nobody asked him what part. He did not say. He had been forty years in the thing and had two seals in his head that he could have named, and he did not name them, and Cael watched him not do it and understood that it was not discretion. It was that the man did not yet know what he would do with the names if he said them, and had learned, in a fortnight, at a table and a counter and a cut, not to say a thing he did not know what to do with.

"Six years," Karis said. She had the deep-layer sheet under her hand, the one from the spring with the office's *six years* on it in the office's rounding, and she looked at it beside the directive and beside the index, and did the thing she did, which was to say what the papers said and not one word past it. "Six years back, somebody measured all of it, and graded it, and ordered the grades to be held. Somebody was administering this — the sites, the marks, all of it — recently enough to keep records, and to fight over the records, and to seal the fight. Documented as *stable*. Somebody's still writing that word down." She looked at the fire. "The Compact. That's who keeps records. That's who has a ruling hand and a seat and a seal. I'm not going to say *someone else*, because I've no sheet for someone else, and I've a whole file for them."

"The Compact," Vastin said. He said it as a man says his own name in a room where it is no longer his. "Yes. Part of it."

That was all that he said about the crack. He did not pry it. He put the sheet back in its row, the order side up, and closed the row, and sat back on the shale with the stick across his knees, and Cael saw him decide, as he had seen him decide at the back of a column and at a plank and at a stake, and the decision was not to decide, and it was the right one, and it was entered.

---

Oryn did the broad man's leg by the fire when the blanket was folded.

Day one of three. She had the leg between her hands for as long as she had had it on a slope, and came out of it grey, and said, in the register, "It's knitting. It'll bear on the third day and not before, and if you put it down on the second I'll read that too," and the broad man, who had not said a word to any of the six since a marsh, said, "Aye," which was Brom's word, and Brom, at the fire's edge, looked up at it, once, and looked down, and the two of them had exchanged a syllable across two crews and would not exchange another.

The older woman with the cloth bag bound it after. Oryn let her. That was the third thing between the two healers, and it was not a look and not a nod; it was one of them putting her hands in the other's work, at the other's leave, and Cael entered it as the largest thing yet, and the least said.

Teague's arm she read and did not touch. "Tomorrow's a pass," she said. "I know what a pass does to a man who leads from the front of it. Lead from the front of it with the other hand." Teague said "Noted," which was the third time from him too, and Oryn said, "I'm going to have to find another word," and did not, and went to the mule.

The watch had gone east. That was the last thing on the shale before the fire went down, and it was Seln's, and he said it as he said things, to the hills, from the camp's edge, with the cache beside him.

"Seven went along the shelf yesterday. Seven came off it at the east end and went up, onto the ground we're on, ahead of us. I've had the count at every halt today and it's twelve and a mule and nothing behind. Nothing beside." He did not turn round. "Nothing ahead that I can see. Which is the thing about ahead."

"The pass," Cael said.

"The pass is a place I'd put seven people if I had seven people and knew twelve were coming. I'd not put them at a barricade again. They learned the barricade." Seln looked at the notch in the hills, black against a sky that still had some light in it, and then at the fire, which was the first time all day he had looked at anything that was not the notch. "I looked at it. That's all. I've looked at it and I don't like it and I'll look at it again in the morning from nearer, and if I like it less I'll say."

That was Seln's whole report, and it was more than he usually gave, and Cael entered it under the observation he could not file, beside the feeling of being known, which he had carried since a bridge and which had not gone away on a shale step with nothing made within a day of it, and which was, he thought, the same feeling as a man on a crest saying *the pass*, from the other side.

---

He and Karis were the last two at the fire, over the map, because they were always the last two at a table with a map on it, and had been since a ring.

She had the reconciled sheet flat on the blanket's last fold, with the four circles and the line and the fifth circle, thumbnail-sized, past the hills, and the straightedge from Denvash laid along the line because he had laid it there, and the edge touched the fifth circle as it touched the other four, without a hair of daylight, dead along, and neither of them said anything about that, because they had said everything about that there was to say at a landing in the snow.

"It's here," Karis said. "Third-tier, documented four hundred years back — as old as the record, as old as the Architect, as old as the machine. The first one they made, or near it."

"And it's on the line."

"It's the *end* of the line. Oldest-last. Everything before it was practice, or maintenance, or — I don't have the word. This is the one that's been quiet since before quiet had a name." She looked at him. "You still want to stand in the middle of it."

"More than I've wanted anything since I was fourteen."

"Why."

He told her, because it was Karis, and because she had asked the way she asked a stone how far it was from another stone, which was a question that deserved a number.

"Because every other site, my Path worked while everyone's died. I want to know what the *oldest* one does to me. I think it's going to tell me something the records can't."

He was right. He did not yet know how right.

# Chapter 19 — The Sunken Span

He logged the leg as a leg, in five entries, one a night, because the road was the road and the sites were not, and he wanted the two kept apart on the page.

*Two hundred and ninety-ninth.* East from the ford on the bearing, twelve and a mule, the Stair road under them to the Stair's bowl by the last of the light — the rim, the floor below it with its terraces going down, the iron a stride apart round it as the registry had set it; not entered; Karis at the rim with the cord for the hour there was, checking a number she already had; camp on the rim. *Three hundredth.* The road to Oryn's last three holds turned north off the bearing at a cairn at noon, and she looked at it once, straight, and walked on east with the roll on the mule, and nobody said anything, because the terms had been said and the day she had to was not this one. The Hall's marsh by dusk the same day; the stone at its edge where he had read himself twice by a fire in the autumn. *Three hundred and first. Three hundred and second.* Past the marsh, on the bearing, three days of ground none of the six had walked and Teague's four had walked once, in the autumn, carrying a healer to a rival's shoulder; two camps with no water. *Three hundred and third.* The crest.

He entered, under each date, the same line, because it was true under each: *known*. Not followed — Seln had the count at every halt and there was nothing behind — but read, off a sheet, by somebody who had the sheet, and he did not stop entering it, because a feeling that recurs is a fact about the road.

---

The crest at dusk on the three hundred and third, and the thing below it, and he catalogued both, because one of them had not been there in the autumn.

The circle first, as it had been. Eight hundred metres across by Karis's cords, twice the others; the ground dropping away inside it on the north side into a sink, a bowl where the earth had gone down and stayed down, and at the middle, four hundred metres in past the iron, the shape against the last of the sky — not a mound, not a hall; a curve, with a crown taller than a man, and dark under it where the sky came through. The iron round the whole of it, a hand's height out of the ground, a pace apart, the number and the stroke-and-bar on every stake, and the line Lira had called in the autumn a hundred and ninety paces below the crest, where it had been.

And then the thing that had not been there.

A post. Timber, split and set, on the perimeter's shelf outside the iron, where the crest's only path came down between the sink's lip on the one hand and the line on the other — a shelf a cart's width wide, and the post built across it. A hut, low, turf on the roof. A stack of kit under a hide. A fire, in the open, with a pan on it, and a barricade of two timbers braced on posts across the path where the shelf narrowed, and behind the barricade and round the fire, seven people in hoods, and two of the seven with crossbows spanned and laid across their knees, and none of them looking at the crest, because a watch that has been told what is coming looks at the road and not at the sky.

"They knew," Karis said, beside him, in the voice for a finding.

"They've always known," Seln said. He had come to the crest's lip on his stomach, as he came to everything, and had the nearest stake in his eye from ninety paces, and read it as he read a lock. "That iron's been in the ground years. Not one winter. The scale's through it." He did not say how many years, because he did not have the number and would not make one. "The hut's a season. The fire's tonight. They've been here since the ice went out and they've been told to expect a crew off this crest, and they've been told how many, and it's the wrong number."

"How wrong," Teague said. He had come up the crest last, as a captain does, and had read the post before he lay down.

"They've room round that fire for a watch that expects six," Seln said. "Not twelve."

Teague looked at it a while longer. Then he said, to Cael and to nobody: "Drawn?"

"Drawn," Cael said, and drew it, because a fight is drawn before it is fought, and because a captain who did not read had asked the one on the crest who did.

The shelf. A cart's width, with the sink on its left hand — a drop of three men's height onto loose stone — and the iron on its right, and a stride beyond the iron the line, where ten of the twelve on this crest would go dark. The post across the shelf. The barricade across the shelf's narrowest place, with the hut behind it and the fire behind that. Seven. Two crossbows. A practitioner among them, by the way one of the seven stood at the barricade's end with his hands empty and his weight set, and low, by the fact that he was here; nobody posted a Silver to a hut. Nobody could go round. There was no round. Sink, or line.

"They've the ground," Teague said. "We've the numbers, and numbers don't count on a cart's width."

"No."

"Then somebody goes through the middle."

"Somebody does." Cael looked at the barricade, and at the man's wrapped wrist on the turf beside him, and did not look at the wrist a second time, because the man had priced it and it was his. "Nobody declares. Nobody dies. They're seven people on a posting. Noted?"

Teague looked at him, and it was the look from a marsh, the one that read a job off a face. "Noted," he said, and Cael entered it beside Vastin's.

---

They went down at first light on the three hundred and fourth, in the order the shelf took them, because the shelf was the thing they were going to be in.

Brom first, with the kit off and the box down on the crest and his shoulders bare of anything but himself, because a man who was going to be a rock did not carry. Then Teague, and Cael at his left hand, a stride back, to watch the exchange. Lira behind them, the flat of the blade out. The tall woman with the spear, then Vastin with the stick, then the broad man with the crossbow, then the young one beside him, because a man on a stick has a man beside him; then Karis with the case under her arm, on the path and on the count, because a wall counted who walked a site and she was the one who had found it; then Seln — and Seln was not on the path; Seln had gone off the crest's east side in the dark an hour before, and was somewhere along the perimeter with the cache, and Cael had a count of nine on the path and one that was not, as he had had at a cut. Oryn and the older woman a hundred paces up the path with the roll open on a rock.

The watch saw them at two hundred paces, which was late, because they had been looking at the road and the crew had come down off the crest's face. The crossbows came up. The five without them went to the barricade in a line, with the empty-handed one at its end, and the hooded man at the middle of the barricade said the thing a Compact watch says, which is a declaration, at the size a declaration is said, and it went up the shelf to the nine of them, and nobody answered it, because nobody declared.

The first bolt came at a hundred. Brom took it on the shoulder that was not bound — turned into it, as he turned into everything, and the bed of him went hard under the coat as Iron goes, and the bolt went off him at an angle into the sink — and did not stop walking, and the second bolt went past Cael's ear by a hand and into the turf, and the crossbows went down to be spanned again, and that was the first exchange, and the watch had it.

They had it. Seven people on a cart's width with a barricade and a drop and a line, and nine coming at them with nowhere to go but through. The numbers were nothing. The ground was everything. The watch had been given a place where what was coming did not matter, and had built across it, and had not run, and he did not think less of them for any of it, and entered that.

Then Teague went through the middle.

He walked the last thirty paces as he had walked across a meadow to a cart, without hurrying, and Cael walked at his left hand a stride back and watched. The five at the barricade set themselves behind the two timbers. The crossbows came up again, spanned, and the bolts came, and Brom, at Teague's right hand now and half a pace ahead, took one on the forearm as a man takes a thing he has decided to take, and the other went between them, and then Teague was at the barricade, and put his palm flat on the upper timber, at the height of his own chest, and set his feet.

Cael saw the feet. He had seen them in a meadow and he saw them now: square, a stride apart, the weight on the balls of them, the whole of the man from the heels up through the hips and the shoulder to the flat of the hand lined up as if a straightedge had been laid down him. The empty-handed man at the barricade's end saw it too, a half-breath late, and moved, and the timber went.

Not the timber. The barricade — both timbers and the posts that braced them and the two men who had their shoulders to the back of it — went, a body's length, which was the length of the man from heel to palm, backward off its footing and into the men behind it and down, and the shelf where the barricade had been was shelf, and Teague stood on it with his hand still out.

And Cael, at his left hand, a stride back, had it.

Not the man's. The *adjacent* thing, the thing that lived at a stride's distance from a Path and was not it, that he had been picking up off people since a training floor at fourteen and had twelve of; and he had it as he had had the eleventh in a cut and the twelfth in a chamber, in the middle of the minute where he was not looking, except that this time he had been looking, for the whole of the exchange, from a stride, at a line running from a man's heels to his palm, and the line had gone into him along its own length and rung.

*Force-adjacent. Teague. Partial. Bronze. Impulse along the line of the frame at contact: from the ground, through the man, out at the hand, and nothing to either side of the line. Needs a thing to push against — the barricade, the ground under the feet; nothing at a distance; nothing from the air. A body's length. The cost back along the same line, every time, at the place the line comes out. Engagement field: close; braced. Tier equivalent unknown. Deployment: none. Thirteenth.*

Teague shook the wrist. It clicked, and then the elbow did, which it had not in the meadow, and the man's face did nothing, and he took the cloth out of his own coat this time, because the young one was on the path behind him beside a man on a stick, and wrapped the wrist and did not wrap the elbow, because there was not time, and said, to the shelf ahead, "Through," and went.

---

The watch reformed. He had known they would; a watch that does not run at a barricade does not run at the loss of one. They went back along the shelf past the hut to the fire, the five and the two with the crossbows, and the crossbows were spanned again by the time the nine had come through the place where the barricade had been, and the seven stood across the shelf at its widest, in a line, with the sink at their left and the iron at their right, and the empty-handed one at the iron end with his weight set, and the crew stood across the shelf thirty paces off in their own line, and it was the first exchange again with the ground fifty paces further along.

Except that the ground was not the same fifty paces along. Past the fire the shelf narrowed again, where the hut's far wall came out to the sink's lip, to a stride and a half between the drop and the iron — the pinch the post had been built in front of — and beyond the pinch the shelf was the perimeter road, a pace wide, running away east along the stakes. A line that gives ground along a shelf gives it toward the pinch, because there is nowhere else on a shelf for ground to go.

He looked at the iron.

He had looked at it from a crest in the autumn with five and had not gone over it, for three sentences of reasons that had been true. He looked at it now from a shelf with nine, and the reasons were still true, and there was one more, which was that the seven people across the shelf had been told what the line did and would not step over it, because their Paths would die at a stride, and everything they were had been built on Paths since they were fourteen; and the nine on this side had been over three such lines in the autumn and had come out of all three, and knew to the stride what it cost, and it cost the same thing it would cost the watch.

Nothing was gained inside. He wanted that entered before he did it, and he entered it, standing on the shelf with a crossbow spanned thirty paces off: the ones who went over would gain nothing over the iron. Lira's speed would be a woman's. Vastin's wall would not come. Every one of them would be, a stride past the iron, exactly what the seven would be a stride past it, which was bodies and steel and the decision to stand there; and the only difference on the whole shelf would be that they had stood there before, three times, and had decided it three times, and the seven had been told about it and had not. What the inside gave was not strength. It was *width*. A shelf was a cart's width, and a line across it was seven; a line that went two strides past the iron was wider than the shelf, and a line wider than the shelf was a thing seven people on a shelf could not face and could not get round.

"By count," he said. Not loud. "Five over the iron, two strides in, level with the four on the shelf. Then we walk. Nobody strikes. Nobody stops walking."

He went first, because it had never done anything to him.

He stepped over a stake numbered thirty-four with the stroke and the bar beside the number, and took two strides, and the twelve went on ringing in him faint as they had on three floors, and his own Path, which was nothing, was nothing, and he stood inside the line on the fourth site with his back to four hundred metres of quiet and his face along the shelf to seven people, and said, "One."

"Two," Lira said, and came over, and he felt her Arbiter go at the stride, from the outside, as a thing goes that has been switched off.

"Three." Karis, with the case under her arm, because the wall counted who walked a site and she had found it, and her sigil went at the stride and she did not look at her hand; she looked at the shelf, for the strata.

"Four." The young one, because Teague had looked at him once and the look had said *go*, and he went over as the young go over things, all at once, and stood inside with nothing in him and did not know yet what that was.

"Five." Vastin, over the iron with the stick, on a knee that had been told a month, standing inside a line where a wall could not come, with nothing in front of him but the decision to, which was, Cael thought, the thing the man had been for all along and had spent forty years being for the wrong people.

Five inside, two strides in, and four on the shelf level with them — Brom at the sink's lip with the rock in his hand, the broad man beside him with the crossbow across his back and the stick, Teague and the tall woman between them and the iron — and the line ran from the drop across the whole of the shelf and two strides into the quiet, and nothing working in five of them but the one who did not count, and steel, and their feet.

They walked.

Not fast. A stride, and a stride, along the shelf toward the fire and the seven at it, nine people in a line wider than the ground, with the five on the inside walking on stone where a Path went out like a lamp, and the seven watching them do it. The crossbows could reach. They did not shoot, because a watch that shoots into a line and drops two of nine has seven of nine still walking, and a line that is still walking is a line you have to stand in front of after, and everyone on the shelf knew it. The seven gave ground. They did not decide to; a line does not decide to give ground to a wider line, it does it, a step at a time, backward, past the fire, past the hut's near wall, past the pan, to where the hut's far wall came out to the sink's lip and the shelf was a stride and a half wide, and there the seven stopped, because seven cannot go backward through a stride and a half in a line, and the nine stopped ten paces off, because the walking had been the thing and the thing was done.

The watch had the pinch. Behind them the perimeter road ran away east a pace wide along the stakes — a way out for seven people one at a time, with nine standing at their backs while they went. On their left, the drop. On their right, the iron, and past the iron the one place on the shelf with room in it, four hundred metres of it, level and quiet, where five people were standing with their Paths gone and their feet on the ground and nothing in their faces, and which the seven had been told they could not stand on, and had believed.

He counted the seconds, because they were the price and the price should be counted. The empty-handed man at the iron end took a step toward the stake and stopped at it as a man stops at the edge of a roof. Four seconds. Six. Nine. The five stood inside, and did not move, and did not declare, and did nothing at all that was not standing there, at the one place on the whole shelf the seven had room to go and would not, and the four on the shelf stood, and the seven stood at the pinch with the road at their backs.

At eleven seconds the hooded man in the middle said, across the ten paces, not loud, "You'll die in there."

"We've been in three times," Cael said.

That was the whole exchange. The man looked at the stake. He looked at his own two crossbows, and at the empty-handed one at the iron's edge, who had not gone over and was not going to, and at the five inside, who were not going to come out until he had decided, and who could stand there, on nothing but their feet, for as long as it took him, because they had done it before and he had not; and at the road at his back, a pace wide; and he decided, and it was the right decision, and Cael entered it as one. The watch went east. Along the perimeter road, past the pinch, in order, one at a time, with the crossbows last and spanned and the empty-handed man last of all, walking backward the length of the hut before he turned, and they did not run, and nobody pursued them, because nobody had been going to, and at two hundred paces they were hoods on a perimeter and then they were the perimeter, and the post was a hut and a fire with a pan on it.

The crews had removed the watch's ability to hold the approach by making the only safe retreat a line the watch would not cross and the crews would. He wrote that sentence that night as it was, because a fight that can be said in one sentence is a fight that was won by a decision, and the decision had been the seven's as much as his.

The broad man went over the sink's edge at the fourth second.

Cael had not seen it; he had been counting, and looking at the watch. He heard it — a foot on the lip, the limp set wrong on loose ground at the shelf's edge where the line had ended at the sink, on a leg that did not walk well — and the man was gone, over, onto the slope three men's height down, and the loose stone went with him, and Teague, on the shelf a stride from him with the wrapped wrist at his side, did not turn round, because his crew was not the thing on the shelf that wanted doing, and the tall woman did not either, and Cael entered that as the hardest thing he had watched a captain do all year, and the most correct.

Oryn was already coming down the path.

---

She mended him at the sink's edge, on the slope, with the older woman with the cloth bag holding his shoulders and the rest of the crew on the shelf above, not looking, as a crew does not look at a healer's work.

The leg. The bad one, the one with the limp, broken above the ankle on the stone where he had landed, and she had it between her hands before the crew had finished coming back over the iron, and held it, and Cael, on the lip, a stride and a half off, which was her distance, saw her face do the reading's work, and then do the other work, the current going *to* the thing that was hurt and arriving and doing what it did. It took longer than a carter's hand, and when Oryn let go the leg was a leg with a break in it that had been set and knit as far as a healer's current could knit a thing in one holding, and she said, in the register, "That'll bear in three days. Not before. It'll limp worse than it did. You'll not thank me for it and I'll not ask you to," and sat down on the slope, all at once, the colour gone out of her, and put her hands on her knees, and did not get up for a quarter of an hour by his count.

That was the price, and she did not hide it. "Nobody died of that," she said, when she could stand, to the older woman, who had not asked, and the older woman nodded, once, which she had not done at the ford, and that was the second exchange between the two healers, and it was a nod.

Teague's arm was the other price. He had it under his coat by the time Oryn came up the slope, and she said, "Arm," and he said, "Contract," and she said, "I don't read contracts. Arm," and read it — the wrist, the elbow, the shoulder, along the line — and said, "You've done that twice in a fortnight. A third time on that arm and I'll set it in a splint. That's not a healer's opinion. That's a fact about a line." Teague said, "Noted," and she said, "Everybody says that now," and wrapped the elbow properly.

---

Brom's bill came in before noon. Two bolts on Iron, and Iron is a bed and not a coat; the bed goes hard and the body under it pays after, and by the time the pan was on the fire the forearm was the colour of the sink's stone from the wrist to the elbow and the shoulder that was not bound had a fist-sized place on it that was going the same way. Oryn read both, with her hands, and said, "Bruise. Deep. A week of colours and it'll carry the whole week," and Brom said, "Aye," and put the box on the other side, which was the bound side, and said nothing about that either.

They went in at noon. Nine of the count, Karis and the young one among them, with Seln come in along the perimeter — nine and not ten, because the broad man stayed on the slope below the hut with the older woman with the cloth bag, whose binding was on his leg and whose hands were the ones he trusted, and the mule tied beside him, because a mule does not care about iron and would not have gone over it for anyone — and Oryn, and Karis with the case, and the young one with the box.

Four hundred metres of quiet, over ground that went down toward the sink's bowl and then along the bowl's rim, with the shape at the middle getting a shape as they came. And then it had one, and he catalogued it from the rim, because he had wanted to stand in the middle of it since the autumn and was going to, and wanted the outside first.

A span. A bridge — an arch, of the grey stone that had no seams, rising off two piers set into the sink's two sides where the ground had dropped away, and going over, the crown of it taller than a man against the noon sky and the dark under it where the bowl went down; the fourth floor, and not a floor. A thing built to cross a gap that had not been there when it was built, or had; he did not know, and Karis, at the rim with the cord already out and the notebook in the bound hand, did not know either, and said so, and went to the piers.

The carvings were on the piers. The same make — he had seen four floors' worth and knew the hand as he knew Hesk's — cut into the seamless stone in rows on the inner faces of both piers, where a man standing under the span would see them and a man standing on it would not, and Karis had the charcoal against the first sheet before Brom had the sheet flat, and her body did the thing it did at a source, and Cael left her to it, because that was hers, and went up.

There were no steps. He walked up the arch as a road rises, to the crown, and stood there, at the middle of the fourth site, twice the size of the others, with the bowl under him and the bearing under his feet, because the arch ran along it — Karis had the straightedge on the map at the pier's foot and called it up to him, "Through," and it was through, dead along, the span's line and the sites' line the same line, and the line going on off the far pier east across the bowl's far rim into ground nobody had walked.

Nothing happened to him.

He had known it would not. It had not on three floors and it did not on a fourth, twice their size; the thirteen rang on in him faint, and the still place did what it did, and he was, as on every floor, the one thing on it that the floor had no argument with. He entered it. It was the same entry as three times before.

The thirteenth listened. That was the one new thing, and he had the word from the autumn: not a fragment held to nothing, this one; a fragment that *wanted*, faintly, as the Tide one had wanted its minute — a line from the heel to the hand that had gone into him along its own length and now lay along that length in him and listened for a thing to push against, and the ribs, which had been quiet since a marsh, said, very quietly, that they knew what it would cost when it found one. He noted it, and gave it nothing, and stood on the crown until Karis called "Done" from the pier, and walked down.

He wrote the log at the fire below the crest, at the hut the watch had left, with the pan washed and the iron a pace apart running away into the dark both ways, and the shape four hundred metres in with the stars through the arch of it, in the plain hand.

*Three hundred and fourth day. The fourth site, the Sunken Span; the watch's fire. A hundred and twenty-three in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Nineteen. Thirteen. Twelve of us; the broad man on a leg that bears in three days; Teague's arm; Oryn a quarter of an hour on a slope. Nobody dead. Nobody declared.*

*Twelfth—no, count it again—thirteenth. Wind, Pressure, Iron, Compression, Ember, Shadow, Storm, Anchor, Blade, Tide, Iron Wall, Rune, Force. Thirteen things that aren't a Path, and I got three of them in one city and one bridge. Rate's climbing and I'm writing that down because Hesk taught me to note the trend. The Span's the fourth site and the Compact fenced it, years ago by the scale on the iron, and left men on it. They knew. They've always known. The bearing runs through it and keeps going, oldest-last, and the oldest one's out there in the deep edge, quiet since they built the machine. One more site. I've wanted to stand in the middle of it since Karis said *someone made this.* Tomorrow I find out what standing in the middle of the first one feels like.*

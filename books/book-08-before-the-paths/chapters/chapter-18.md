# Chapter 18 — The Route North

Millrace was a ford with a wheel on it, and he heard the wheel before he saw the roofs, as he had heard it in the autumn, and he catalogued the difference, because there was one.

The Stair road went south out of Lowmarch along the river's high bank for a morning and then left the river and climbed, and from the top of the climb the country opened, and the road ran along the ridge's shoulder with a stream coming down beside it, and where the stream met a second one there was the race cut in stone and the wheel turning in it, slow, and the eleven roofs, and the ford below them shallow over gravel. The same. But there had been five and a mule coming down off the shoulder in the autumn, and there were eleven now and no mule, because the mule was hers since a fork and was in the ford-keeper's shed where she had left it, and the ford-keeper at the mill's door with flour to the elbow counted them as he had counted five, and arrived at a number he had not had to arrive at before, and said it to the healer's back.

"Eleven off the ridge."

"I know," Oryn said, without turning.

She had a woman's hand between her palms. A hold-woman's, by the coat — the fingers gone stiff and the wrist with it, an old thing and not a wheel's work — and she did not look up at eleven people coming down onto her ford, because a healer with a hand between her palms does not look up, and Cael, at the bank's edge, a stride and a half off, which was her distance and had become his, did not expect her to, and stood, and catalogued the lean-to while he waited, because that was what the distance was for.

The turf roof on poles. The plank table scrubbed white. The roll open on it in her order. The pan of water. The stool she was not using. And the line, which was six people when he counted it and five by the time he had, because she had done one while he counted, as she always had. Nothing in it had changed since the autumn except the season and the count of the crew on the bank, and he entered that, because a place that does not change is a fact, and there were going to be more of those on this road than he had been used to.

She let the hand go. The fingers opened, slowly, all the way, which they had not been going to do when they came in, and the hold-woman looked at them as people looked at their own hands on Oryn's table, and Oryn said, "Work them. Every morning. In warm water, not hot. They'll go stiff again if you don't and I'll not be here to do it twice," and the woman said something Cael did not hear, and went, and Oryn drank from the pan, not much, and put it down, and looked up.

She looked at the bank. She counted. He watched her do it, because he had watched her count a crew on a bank before and knew the order she took them in: Lira, whose arm she had priced at a fork; Brom, whose shoulder she had not been allowed to bind; Karis, whose hand was bound and had not been when she left; Seln; and then the man with the stick, and the stick, and the knee under the trouser, and the look went to the knee and stayed there a moment longer than it had on anything else, and then came to Cael, and stopped.

"Six," she said, and he said it back.

"And five that aren't." The look went past him, to the road, where Teague's four stood with the tall woman's spear grounded and the young one looking at the ford as the young look at everything, and to the fifth, the older woman with the cloth bag, who was looking at the lean-to as a woman looks at another woman's table. Oryn looked at her. The older woman looked back. It lasted as long as it took, and neither of them nodded, and neither of them looked away first, and then the older woman looked at Brom's shoulder, once, as she had looked at it in a marsh and across a room, and Oryn looked at it too, and then they had both looked at the same thing and priced it the same, and that was the whole of the exchange between the two healers on the bearing, then or after, and Cael entered it as complete.

"The rest," Oryn said, to the bank, to the six of them, in the register, "can wait till I've done the line. Sit down. Not you." That was to Vastin. "You stand there and don't put the stick down, because you've been putting it down, I can see it from here, and I said a month."

"Nineteen days," Vastin said.

"Then it's eleven more. Stand there."

He stood there. It was, Cael thought, the first order the man had taken since a cut without walking it back, and he took it from a woman at a plank table with her hands in a pan, and stood, and did not say anything about it, and Cael noted the date.

---

She did the line. Five, then four — the old woman's knee among them, done again as she had said at this ford in the autumn she would, the ladder order repeated and, by the woman's face, refused again — then the small ones, in the register, one hand each for the small ones and the current for none of them, because none needed it and she did not have it to spend, and Cael sat on the bank with the log and did not open it, and watched a woman do the thing she owned.

Then she came to the bank.

She did not sit. She stood at the edge of it with the roll on the table behind her and the ford at her back, square to the six of them as she stood square to everything, and said the terms, and he had known she would, because she had said them at a fork and at a table and by post, and a woman who says a thing three times at three sizes is a woman who has decided that it needs saying until nobody can mistake it.

"I'm on my route." She said it to the six of them and past them to the road, where Teague stood, because Teague was the one on the road who had not heard it said. "Seven holds north of here owe me a visit and I've walked four of them since the fork and there are three I haven't, and there's an old woman at Thornwater with a knee that held through the winter and a ladder she'll not stop climbing. That's mine. Nobody at this ford gets a share of it." A breath. "Your bearing runs east. So does the road to the last three holds, for two days, and then it doesn't. I'll walk the two days because they're on my way. I'll walk further than that because there's a man on this bank with ribs I've been owed a reading of since a marsh, and because ten people are going into two places that stop Paths at a stride, and I've seen what that costs, and I'll not have it said I was a day north when it cost it." She did not look at Teague when she said the next thing, and it was for him. "I'm not a recruit. I've never been one. I'm a stop on my own route, and the route's mine, and the day I have to turn north I'll turn north, and nobody at this ford will ask me not to, because the one who'd have the right to has already said he won't."

She looked at Cael, then.

"On the road, if there's road," he said. "Not before."

"Good," she said.

Teague, on the road, said nothing, which was the right answer, and Cael watched him hear it and file it, because the man filed things too, in his way — a healer of no Path and a healer of Tide, and both of them on their own terms, and neither of them his to move — and the tall woman moved the spear a hand's width, and that was Teague's crew hearing it, and it was done.

Lira, from the bank: "Sixth chair."

"Sixth chair," Oryn said. "On my terms. Which are the only kind." And then, because the terms were said and the rest was work: "Brom. Shoulder."

---

She read them in order, on the plank table, with the light going, and asked each of them first, as she asked every time — "May I?" — and each said yes in their own register: Brom with a grunt that was a word, Lira with a nod she turned into one, Karis with "Yes" and no look up from the notebook, Vastin with "You may," which was the form's word and the only one he had; and he catalogued each one, because each was a cost he had carried in the log since the autumn and a healer's hand on it was the entry that closed the row.

Brom's shoulder. She put the flat hand on it, as she had wanted to at a marsh and had been told *binding, not hands* by a woman with a cloth bag, and she held it there, and came out of it, and said, "She was right. It wanted leaving alone and it was left alone and it's a shoulder. Carry with the other one another month. You will anyway." Brom said, "Aye," which was his whole speech on the subject of his shoulder for the year.

Lira's arm. Two fingers on the wrist, the flat hand on the forearm, the count: "Fifteen days at the fork. It's had thirty-three. It's an arm." She looked at Lira's two hands, both at her sides and neither in her coat. "You listened when it told you the second time."

"Once," Lira said.

"Once is once more than I priced." That was all of it, and Lira took it as praise, because from Oryn it was.

Karis's hand. The bound one, the cut across the back of the left hand from a step's edge in a cut, eight days old, bound by Brom with thread and a strip of shirt and his teeth, and Oryn unbound it and looked at it a long time in the going light and said, "Who sewed this."

"Brom."

"With what."

"Thread. His teeth for the knot."

Oryn looked at Brom, once, and said nothing, and rebound the hand with her own linen, tighter and flatter, and said to Karis, "It'll close. It'll leave a line. Don't write with it for a week — the other hand. You've got one." And Karis, who had been writing with it for eight days and would not stop for a week, said "Noted," which from Karis was not agreement, and Oryn heard that it was not and let it go, because a healer picks the orders she enforces.

Vastin's knee. He came to the table with the stick and she said, "Sit," and he sat, and she put both hands on the knee through the trouser and then said, "Trouser," and he rolled it, and the knee was a knee with a binding on it that had been put on at a cut nineteen days ago by the hands that were on it now, and she read it. It took longer than the others. He watched her face, because her face did the reading's work in the register her voice did not, and it did not change, and then it did, a little, at the corner, and she took her hands off.

"It held in a cut," she said.

Vastin did not say anything.

"You stood on it. With the stick off the ground. You've done it once, and it was in a cut, and you did it because a cut was where it went and you wanted to stand on the place." She did not ask him. She had read it. "It held. That's not the same as the month being up. The month's the month. Eleven more days on the stick and then I'll say, and if you put it down before I say I'll know, because I'll read it, and it'll tell me."

"Noted," Vastin said, and she looked at him. "That's new."

"It's a useful word."

"It's his." She tilted her head at the bank, at Cael, and rebound the knee, and it was done, and the man with the stick went back to standing where he had been told to stand, and stood.

Then she wiped her hands, and looked at the pan, and did not drink from it, and said, to the ford, "Cael. Bench. Lie still when I say."

---

He lay still.

He had lain still for nobody, in five years, except her, and for her three times, on a sack and on a table and at a marsh's edge, and each of those had been a minute at her count, and this was not going to be a minute, and he knew it before she put her hands on him, because she had said *the long one* at a fork and had meant it, and because she had carried this reading on her route since the autumn and was going to have it whole.

The bench was the lean-to's, against the back wall, under the turf, out of the ford's noise. He lay on it on his back with the log on the ground beside his hand where he could reach it and would not, and his coat open, and the straightedge and the dividers taken out of it and put on the plank table in her order, because she had said *nothing in the coat* and had meant the brass. The light was nearly gone. She did not light the lantern. She sat on the stool beside the bench and said, "May I?" because she said it every time, and he said, "Yes," and she put two fingers on his wrist, as she began every reading, and waited for the count to settle, and it settled, and then she put the flat hand on his ribs, on the left, low, where the front hand had been on a table in the autumn and the ribs had been quiet since a marsh, and went in.

He felt her go. He always had; he had told her so, at a fork, and she had said *I know*. Her current came in through the flat hand as it came in through everything, steadily, without a check in it, and went down through the ten — the twelve — as water goes down through a bed of stones, finding the ways between, and he lay still and let it, and did not go with it, because that was the term and he did not shave terms.

It took a long time.

He did not count it. He had decided not to before she put the hand down, because a count was a thing done, and lying still was the absence of a thing done, and he could not do both. So he lay, and the ford ran outside, and the wheel turned, and the light went, and somewhere on the bank Brom said a word to Lira and Lira did not answer it, and the flat hand on his ribs did not move, and Oryn's breathing beside him went slow, and slower, and stopped being a thing he could hear, and he understood that she was as far in as she had ever been in anyone, and that she was going round.

Going round. He felt her do it. Her current, which ran to things — that was Tide; a current that ran *to* whatever was hurt, and arrived, and did the work — ran down through the twelve to the middle, to the place, and did not arrive. It went round. As the twelve went round. As everything went round, and had, as far back as he could feel, and he felt her feel it, from his own side: the current bending where the bed bent, and the bed bending because of a stone that the river had gone round for so long that the going-round was the river's shape, and the stone at the middle not doing anything, because doing was not what it was.

She stayed there. A long time. He felt her try — not push; she did not push; a healer who had spent her life reading current did not push a current at a thing that was not one — but *stay*, at the edge of it, with all that she was held still at the bank of a thing that had no bank, and read it as nothing, and read the everything around it, and read the nothing again, and it was the same.

And while she stayed there, because he was lying still and had nothing to do with his hands or his count or his eyes, and because a man who owns twelve things and has been told to lie still with them does not stop owning them, he turned the twelfth.

He did not decide to. He entered that afterward, honestly: he had not decided. It had turned as a floor leans that you have stood on for an hour; the eye going along a row whether he told it to or not. Cleon's inside. The lean. He had had it four days and had turned it on nothing since a board, because there had been nothing made to turn it on, on a road; and here, lying still under a turf roof with a healer's current going round a place in him, the twelfth turned, by itself, inward, toward the place, because the place was where everything in him was looking, and the twelfth looked where he looked.

It should have found nothing. That was the card: *made things only; not a river; not a man.* He was a man. The still place was in a man. A record of a thing that was not made would read as what a heap read as, which was nothing with a shape, and he lay and waited for the nothing, and it was the only time in the reading that he was not lying still, and he knew it, and could not stop it any more than he could stop his eye going along six marks in a row.

It did not read as nothing.

It read as *still*. That first, and the same as Oryn's finding, and he held there, because it agreed with a healer and a thing that agrees with a healer is a thing you can enter. Still. Not flowing. Not empty. And then, under the still, as six marks on a board had been six marks and then were six marks with a direction — not a movement; a *lean* — the still place leaned.

Not toward anything. He could not have said toward. It was not a row; it did not go left to right; there was no bearer and no borne. But it was not a heap. A heap sat, and this did not sit; it was *set*, as a thing is set that somebody has put where it is so that a next thing can sit on it, and the twelfth, which could tell him a thing was an instruction and could not tell him what it instructed, told him that the still place at the middle of him had the inside of a made thing, and would not tell him what it was made for, and he lay under the turf roof with a healer's hand on his ribs and did not move, and did not breathe differently, and did not let it into his face, because her fingers were on his wrist and she would feel a check in him as she felt everything, and there was no check, because he had lain still for her before with a thing found in him and this was only another one.

He did not name it. He had no name that was not a guess. He had said so to her in a yard and it was on a page.

But he heard Cleon, at a table with a cup: *If that fragment ever reads something for you that shouldn't be made and reads it anyway — Don't tell me. Don't tell anyone. Just remember I said it.* He had entered it at the time under the observation he could not file. He filed it now. It had been an instruction, and it had leaned, and he had not seen the lean until the thing it pointed at was under his own ribs.

The eyes were paying already, in the dark, which was the one place they could pay unseen: the lean-to's open side gone to a smear, the lantern that was not lit no darker for it, and he let them pay and did not blink against it, because a blink is a thing a healer's fingers feel.

Her hand came off.

He felt her come up out of it — not fast; a healer does not come up out of a long reading fast — and the fingers left his wrist last, as they always did, and he heard her breathe again, and the stool creaked, and she sat a while in the near dark with her hands in her lap before she said anything, and he lay still, because she had not yet said he could stop, and did not.

"Up," she said.

He sat up. He picked up the log from the ground and did not open it.

She said it plainly, in the register, as she said every finding, and she said it looking at him and not at the ford, which was not the register and was the other thing.

"There's a place in you I can't read because it isn't *flow*. I've spent my life reading flow. Whatever that is, it's not the kind of thing my Path was built for."

He said, "Noted," because it was the word, and because it was true, and because it was the same finding she had given him at a marsh with less certainty and had now given him with all of it, and a finding that has been made certain is a different entry from one that has been made.

"It hasn't changed." She looked at her hands. "Not since the autumn. Not a hair. I went as far in as I've ever gone in anyone and I stayed as long as I've ever stayed, and it's a still place that everything goes around, and I can tell you every current in you to the bank, now, all twelve — twelve; you've two I didn't have in the autumn, and one of them's not for fighting and I don't know what it's for and I'll not ask — and I can't tell you one thing about the place they go around, because there's nothing there for a healer to read. Nothing. And everything, round it." She stood up. "That's my finding. It's the same finding. It's not going to be a different one, because I've read it as deep as I can read, and that's the bottom of what I am."

He did not tell her.

He entered that too, that night, honestly, and the reasons in their order, because a thing withheld from a healer who has just gone as deep as she can go on your behalf is a thing that wants its reasons written down. Cleon had said not to, and Cleon had been right about the stair. He had no words that were not a guess, and he had told her once that he would not guess. And she had gone to the bottom of what she was, and had come up with her own finding, whole, and it was hers, and a man does not lay a rival instrument's reading over a healer's the minute she has finished, on her own bench, in the dark, as if her finding were the lesser one because it had found nothing. It had found nothing. That was a finding. The other had found a lean. That was a finding too. Two instruments, one place, two findings that did not agree, and he did not know which was the true one or whether both were, and he was not going to decide it on a bench, and he was not going to make her carry a thing she had not found and could not read.

So he said "Noted," again, and she said "Good," and put the roll on her back by its strap, and that was the reading.

Lira came in from the bank, then, with the light gone, and stood in the lean-to's open side, and said to Oryn, not to him, "There's a thing we read in Treswick. About the marks. About him. You'd want it, I think. Or not."

Oryn looked at her. Then at Cael, on the bench, with the log shut on his knee.

"Is it a finding?"

"It's a leaf," Lira said. "Older than the board. It says what the thing was built for."

"Then it's not mine." Oryn took the pan and poured it out on the ground, and set it on its rim to dry. "Tell me on the road, if there's road. Not at my table. My table's for things I can put my hands on." She looked at Cael once more, and the look was the look she had given a knee: a thing read and priced and not finished. "Lie still when I say. That's still the term."

"Noted," he said.

"It's the word," she said, and went out to the bank to see about the mule.

---

They went at dawn on the two hundred and ninety-ninth, and he catalogued them from the ford, because it was the first time the count had been what it was and he wanted it.

Twelve, and he took them in the order the road took them, because the road was the thing they were going to be in.

Teague first, on the road above the ford, with the wrist wrapped and the hand that was on the end of it hanging as it hung, and the four of his behind him — the tall woman with the spear grounded and lifted, the broad man with the crossbow across his back and the limp set for the day, the young one with a pack that was too big for him and was going to get lighter, and the older woman with the cloth bag, who did not carry a weapon and never had. Then the crew's: Karis with the case and the map, and the map with the straightedge's line on it — he had laid the brass from Denvash along the bearing the night before by the lantern, and she had drawn along it, once, with the pencil, from the fourth circle out onto the blank sheet to the divider's point and past it, a line true to a hair on paper across a country that had none — and the bound hand holding the notebook with the other one writing, because a week was a week and she had not agreed to it. Brom with the kit and the first box. Lira with the third box and two arms. Seln at the back, where the count was kept. Vastin with the stick, day twenty, on the road, where a stick could be put down and would not be. And Oryn, with the roll on her back by its strap, and her hands out of her coat, walking as she walked to everything, square to it, on a road that ran east for two days with her holds and then did not, and she had said so, and was walking it anyway, on her terms, which were the only kind.

And himself, with the middle box, and the brass in his coat, and twelve things held to nothing, and a place at the middle of them that one instrument said was nothing and one said was made, and neither of them his to decide.

Twelve on the road, two of whom were on no wall's count — a healer of Tide and a healer of no Path, on either end of the ten that were, walking the same bearing for reasons that were their own — and a mule — hers since a fork, and back on the bearing because she was — which had Karis's boxes on it now beside her roll, because Oryn had put them there herself at the ford and had said nothing about it and the mule had not minded.

The bearing ran east from the ford, past the Stair's bowl and the Hall's marsh, five days to the fourth site with the Compact's cordon round it, which none of them had crossed, and past that, on the line, to a point in a straightedge's shadow that had been quiet since there was paper to say so. The sun came up along it. He had not planned that; a bearing that ran east had the sun come up along it every morning, and he entered it anyway, because it was true this morning and he was the one who wrote down what was true.

A healer's route and a researcher's archive, on one mule, on one road, walking the same way for as long as the road ran the same way; and the twelve of them behind it, in the order the road took them, going east into the light with the wheel turning behind them in its race, slow, and the ford-keeper at the mill's door with flour to the elbow, counting them out, and arriving at the number, and saying it to nobody.

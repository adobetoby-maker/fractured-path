## Greyvane Academy Reference (established in Part 1, referenced throughout)

**The campus:** A former guild waystation complex on a hill town three days east of Ardenmere, converted to an academy sixty years ago. Minor-tier: one training hall that would fit inside a corner of a major academy's grounds, a lecture wing, a residence wing, an archive that predates the academy itself (the waystation's records room, never emptied), and a walled training yard. Greyvane's entire identity is that it takes students the tier system has mispriced — late Kindlers, disputed classifications, re-certification cases. It survives on being right about people other institutions were wrong about.

**Key institutional facts:**
- *Provost Naveth:* Runs Greyvane. 60s, formerly a mid-tier academy administrator who took the Greyvane post as a demotion and made it a philosophy. Cautious by temperament, principled by conviction — the tension between those two is his whole arc this book. He approved Quenna's recruitment of Cael knowing it was legally fragile.
- *Quenna:* Carried over from Book 2 (LOCKED — see State Ledger). Senior teaching-practitioner, Silver-tier, irregular-Path development assessment. She administers the demonstration-provision track and is Cael's assessor of record. Her professional reputation is staked on this working.
- *Instructor Wray:* Senior defensive-branch instructor, Shield Path, Bronze-tier. Brom's primary instructor. Practical, unimpressed by lineage, the first formal teacher Brom has ever respected. (Deliberately NOT an Iron Path practitioner — see naming constraints. "Iron Skin" is always spelled in full.)
- *Archivist Prynn:* Keeper of the Greyvane archive, including the founding-era waystation records. 70s, precise, territorial about the collection. Becomes Cael's registry-law guide in Part 3.
- *Edran:* Student, Glass Path, Iron-tier Rank 2. Greyvane's top-ranked enrolled student before Karis transfers in. Initially hostile to an "observer" holding training-floor privileges; his arc bends from resentment to grudging witness — he testifies to fact, not friendship, at the hearing.

**The three enrollment tracks (established Book 2 Ch22–23, executed here):**

| Practitioner | Track | Mechanism | Ongoing requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cael | Demonstration-provision | Written for unkindled prospects under observation; language covers "any practitioner without a standard Arbiter-issued classification" | Monthly demonstration assessment before Quenna's panel |
| Lira | Re-certification candidate | Provision for practitioners expelled from a prior academy (Fenmark) seeking restored certification standing | Monthly reassessment; must demonstrate continued advancement |
| Brom | Standard enrollment | Ordinary application; Copper-tier, clean record, demonstrated performance | Standard coursework and evaluation |

The asymmetry is structural and deliberate: Brom is the only one of the three whose presence at Greyvane is unambiguously legal. Lira's is legal but probationary. Cael's is a workaround wearing a category as a coat. The book's external arc is the coat coming off.

**Companion differentiation note (Karis / Lira):** Karis and Lira must read as clearly distinct from Karis's first page. The differentiation is enforced structurally, not just descriptively: through Part 1 and most of Part 2 they occupy separate scene-spaces (Lira's chapters are the re-certification arc and training-partner scenes; Karis's chapters are the research arc and assessment scenes) and separate registers (Lira is kinetic, shorthand-familiar, a year and a half of shared history; Karis is methodical, formal, negotiating access she has not yet earned). They are not written into the same scene until Chapter 6, and when they are, the scene is explicitly about the difference between them. Lira trains *with* Cael. Karis studies *what Cael is*. Neither would ever be mistaken for doing the other's job.

## Chapter Breakdown

### Part 1 — Greyvane (Chapters 1–8)

---

**Chapter 1 — The Gate**
~4,600 words

The road from Book 2's final page runs directly into this chapter — same three travelers, same easy pace, no time skip. Three days east of Ardenmere, Greyvane appears the way minor institutions do: smaller than the word "academy" promised, and more real for it. Cael's narration does the arrival the way he does everything — cataloguing. The wall is old waystation stone. The gate is newer. The gate clerk has a list, and their names are on it, which is its own small shock: the first door in Cael's life that was opened *before* he reached it.

Intake runs three tracks in three lines, and the chapter stages the asymmetry immediately. Brom's paperwork takes eleven minutes — application, record, stamp, done; he is, for the first time in the series, the *simple* case, and his flat satisfaction at this is quietly funny. Lira's takes an hour: her Fenmark record has traveled with her file, and the re-certification provision requires her to sign an acknowledgment of the expulsion she has spent three years refusing to acknowledge as legitimate. She signs it. Her jaw does most of the talking.

Cael's takes the rest of the day. Quenna walks him through it personally. The enrollment document classifies him as an *unclassified observer* — a category written for children who have not yet Kindled. Cael, fifteen, Iron-equivalent by a circuit's honest ledger, four fragments in a binder in his pack, reads the category line three times. Quenna, evenly: "The provision's language is what protects you. The provision's intent is what you'll be asked about if anyone ever looks closely." Cael: "Someone will look closely." Quenna: "Yes. Our job is to be established fact by the time they do."

*Close on:* Cael signing. His handwriting is careful. The category above his signature is a lie, and everyone at the table knows it, and the ink dries anyway. He writes in his log that night: "Enrolled. The word is doing a lot of work."

**Chapter 2 — Minor-Tier**
~4,600 words

Full establishment of Greyvane — geography, faculty, institutional character — built the way Book 2 Chapter 2 built the Ironyard: around Cael walking the place with a question. His question here: *why did this academy take the risk?*

Provost Naveth is introduced answering it, in his office, without being asked. He has clearly prepared this speech and clearly needed to give it to himself as much as to Cael. Greyvane is minor-tier and always will be; it cannot compete for the students the system prices correctly, so it competes for the ones the system prices wrong. Its alumni ledger is its argument: late Kindlers who reached Bronze, disputed classifications overturned, careers built out of other institutions' rejections. "Every student in this building is someone's clerical error," Naveth says. "You are simply the largest one we have ever admitted." It is not warmth. It is a business model with a conscience, and Cael — who has spent two books learning to trust systems only when he can see their incentives — finds it more reassuring than warmth would have been.

The chapter tours the training hall, the lecture wing, the yard, and the archive (Prynn introduced briefly, guarding her records room like a border). It establishes academy rhythm: lecture blocks, supervised training floors, the monthly assessment calendar posted in the main hall with Cael's demonstration dates on it, in public, where anyone can read them.

*Key beat:* Instructor Wray meets Brom on the defensive-branch floor, watches him work for five minutes, and says: "Whoever trained you taught you to survive being underestimated. I'm going to teach you what to do when you're not." Brom, later, to Cael, with the specific care of a person handling an unfamiliar object: "I think I have a teacher." First time in the series he's said the word without irony.

**Chapter 3 — Three Tracks**
~4,600 words

The first weeks, structured around the divergence. For the first time since Book 1, the three of them are not living inside the same institution on the same terms — they're living inside the same institution on three *different* terms, and the chapter lets that reshape the daily texture.

Brom disappears into standard coursework and comes back talkative — a word never previously applicable to Brom — about formal declaration theory, which he has spent his whole life deriving from scratch and is now being handed in organized form. His verdict: "Half of it is wrong. The wrong half is the half about ceilings." Filed, lightly, against his long arc.

Lira's re-certification track is harder than advertised. The provision grants academic access but marks her file at every turn — separate sign-in on the training floor, an assessor's initial required beside her bout results. The academy is not cruel about it; the *procedure* is cruel on its own, without needing anyone's help. The chapter gives her one scene alone with the notice board, looking at her name with the provisional marker beside it, and one scene refusing to let it matter: she takes the floor against Greyvane's second-ranked student and wins with the not-holding-back circuit style that no academy taught her. The floor goes quiet. Fenmark's records say Copper-tier. The floor just watched something else.

Cael's observer status is the strangest of the three: he may attend any lecture, use the library and archive, and train — but only under assessor supervision, only in scheduled sessions, never in open student bouts. He is present everywhere and eligible nowhere. He does what he does with every constraint: inventories it, finds its edges, and begins quietly filling twenty-three pages of the observation notebook with the declaration architecture of every Path on the training floor — more Path variety in two weeks than the circuit showed him in a year.

*Close on:* the Power Log, new entry: "Greyvane inventory, week two: eleven Paths I've never seen at close range. The observer category was meant as a cage. It's a vantage point."

**Chapter 4 — Demonstration**
~4,600 words

Cael's first monthly demonstration assessment — the mechanism his entire enrollment rests on, staged in full so the reader understands its rules before the book starts bending them.

The format: a closed session in the training hall. Panel of three — Quenna presiding, Wray, one rotating faculty seat. Cael must demonstrate "consistent, observable practice." He is not required to name his Path. He is required to show that what he does is real, repeatable, and controlled. The tension of the chapter is calibration: every capability he shows becomes institutional record, and institutional records travel. He demonstrates the Wind-adjacent evasion framework and the Iron-adjacent surface read — his two most stable fragments — at deliberately conservative deployment, and holds back the Pressure-adjacent, the Compression-adjacent, and everything about acquisition. Showing enough to pass while banking everything that matters: the skill Vell's circuit spent a year teaching him, transplanted into a room with higher stakes.

The panel passes him. Wray's comment for the record: "Unorthodox architecture. Consistent execution. No safety concerns." Quenna's, carefully neutral: "Assessment satisfied." Afterward, privately, she tells him the truth: "The first assessment proves you're real. The ones that matter are later — when someone hostile is reading the file."

*Key beat:* The academy rumor ecosystem ignites. Students watched the panel walk to a closed session and walked out having passed *someone*, and the observer with no Path on his residence card is the only candidate. Edran, publicly, in the yard: "Observers don't demonstrate. What exactly is he?" Nobody answers. That's the problem — nobody *can*.

**Chapter 5 — Karis**
~4,600 words

Karis's formal introduction. The chapter opens with a limited third-person window into Karis — the same technique used for Coss in Book 1 and Brom in Book 2 Chapter 8 — before returning to Cael.

Karis is 16. Ember Path, Iron-tier Rank 3, top-ranked student at Ternhall Academy — a genuinely good institution, three tiers of prestige above Greyvane. Her window establishes what kind of mind she is: she keeps research notebooks the way Cael keeps observation notebooks, except hers are about Path theory itself — declaration structure, tier mechanics, the places where the standard model's predictions fail. Eight months ago, a Ternhall instructor mentioned, as a curiosity, a circuit rumor out of Ardenmere: an unclassifiable practitioner beating formally ranked opponents. Karis pulled every thread of the rumor she could reach. What she found didn't fit the standard model — didn't fit *any* model — and Karis has organized her entire life around the conviction that anomalies are not noise. They are the data that matters. She filed for transfer to Greyvane the week Cael's enrollment was quietly listed. Ternhall thought she was destroying her career. She thinks she's the only person on the continent standing next to the most important open question in Path theory.

The meeting, back in Cael's POV: she finds him in the archive, introduces herself with her full ranking the way people do when they're used to it opening doors, and then does the thing nobody has ever done. She asks permission. "I transferred here because of you. I want to study what you are. Formally, with your consent, on your terms, and you see everything I write." Cael's whole history says *being studied is what the Compact does.* His whole history also says nobody from the Compact ever asked. He doesn't say yes. He says: "Why should I trust you?" Karis, without hesitation: "You shouldn't yet. Trust is a finding, not a premise." It is the single best possible answer she could have given him.

*Close on:* Cael's log: "Ember Path, Iron 3, walked away from a better school to look at me. First person to treat what I am as a question instead of a verdict. Deciding what that's worth."

**Chapter 6 — Terms**
~4,600 words

The negotiation — and the chapter that puts Karis and Lira in the same room for the first time, on purpose, about exactly that.

Cael doesn't decide alone; that stopped being how his life works somewhere in Book 2. The four-way conversation in the residence common room is the chapter's spine. Brom is immediately, unreservedly in favor: "I've been trying to solve him by instinct for a year. She has a method. I want to compare results." Lira is the holdout, and the scene is honest about why: not jealousy of Karis, wariness of the *category* — every institution that ever looked closely at Cael was gathering evidence for something. Lira and Karis face each other directly, and the scene draws the line the whole book will keep: Lira has fought beside Cael, bled beside him, read the Power Log because he handed it to her. Karis has a hypothesis. "You want data," Lira says. "We're a family." Karis, who is constitutionally incapable of pretending otherwise: "Yes. And I'm asking to be allowed to earn my way from the first thing toward the second." Lira sits with that. Then: "Terms. Real ones. On paper." Coming from Lira, this is a yes.

The terms, drafted by all four: Karis sees demonstrations, not the Power Log (the log stays with the two people who earned it — B2 continuity held). Nothing is written that names acquisition or mechanism. Everything Karis writes, Cael reads. Either party ends it with a word. Karis signs without hedging, and then does something that recalibrates the whole room: she hands over her *own* research notebooks, all of them, unprompted. "Symmetry. You should be able to study the person studying you."

*Key beat:* First controlled observation session. Karis watches Cael run his evasion framework, writes for forty minutes, and asks one question no one — not Lira, not Brom, not Quenna — has ever asked: "When you use it, does it feel *borrowed*?" Cael doesn't answer. The question follows him for three chapters.

**Chapter 7 — Tracked**
~4,600 words

Compact escalation. The monitoring apparatus catches up to the fact that its subject has crossed from an unranked district into a registered academy — a jurisdictional phase-change that converts him, in procedural terms, from *someone to watch* into *someone inside a structure the Compact regulates*.

The chapter runs the two-track structure of Book 2's Chapter 7. The Compact track, limited window: Coss receives the location confirmation — Cael's name on a registered academy's enrollment submission, flagged automatically at the registry intake layer. For Coss this is the moment two years of patient file-keeping becomes actionable: a [SHATTERED] entity inside a Compact-chartered institution is not a monitoring question anymore, it is a compliance question, and compliance is a domain where a Warden's authority has teeth. He requests the assignment formally. It is granted. The window closes on Coss doing something he has not done since Denvash: packing for travel.

Greyvane's track: a Compact records request arrives — enrollment documentation, provision citations, assessment records. Naveth's response is a masterclass in institutional stalling: everything provided, nothing volunteered, every document technically responsive and maximally unhelpful, delivered at the exact pace regulation permits and no faster. Cael watches the provost work and revises his estimate of the man upward. "You've done this before." Naveth: "Son, this academy's entire admissions model generates paperwork disputes. I have been slow-walking the Compact since before you Kindled." 

*Plant (Book 6, maintenance):* the records request that comes back to Greyvane carries a processing annotation neither Naveth nor anyone at Greyvane can parse — a priority routing code that doesn't match the request's stated monitoring level. Naveth assumes bureaucratic noise and files it. The reader, who saw Book 2 Chapter 15, knows what a Level 4 shadow looks like falling across a page.

*Close on:* Cael's log: "They know where I am. They always knew where I was. What's changed is that where I am now has walls they think they own."

**Chapter 8 — The Observer's Floor**
~4,600 words

Part 1's closing chapter — the observer category tested in public, and the doorway to Part 2 opened.

The friction that's been building since Chapter 4 breaks: Edran files a formal student petition challenging Cael's training-floor privileges. His argument is not stupid, which is what makes it dangerous — the observer provision was written for the unkindled, floor access under it assumes no meaningful capability, and "whatever the observer is, he is not that." Edran's real grievance is rank-holder's vertigo: he earned his standing under rules that Cael's existence suggests are incomplete. The petition forces Naveth to do something rather than nothing.

The something, engineered by Quenna into an opportunity: a supervised exhibition spar, Cael versus Edran, full faculty supervision, publicly witnessed, formally logged as a demonstration assessment — converting the challenge into exactly the "demonstrably observable practice" his provision requires. The bout is the chapter's centerpiece: Edran's Glass Path is precise, fast, edged — the best formally trained opponent Cael has ever faced in a sanctioned space. Cael wins it the way he won the circuit: six days of observation, the compound gaze, a seam in Glass Path's recommitment timing between second and third declarations, patience until it appears. He is deliberate about *how* he wins — conservative fragment deployment, nothing the panel hasn't already logged. The victory is complete and quiet.

Edran, after, on the floor — echoing a posture the reader knows from Brom, but landing differently: he doesn't sit down next to Cael. He stands, breathing hard, and says: "Whatever you are, it's real. I withdraw the petition." Not friendship. Witness. It will matter at the hearing.

*Close on:* Quenna, filing the assessment record, to Cael: "That satisfies this month. Next month, I want to try something with structure." She looks across the hall at Karis, who has watched the entire bout writing continuously. "She's already asked."

### Part 2 — Ember (Chapters 9–16)

---

**Chapter 9 — Method**
~4,600 words

The research arc begins in earnest. Karis's controlled sessions become part of the week's architecture — and the chapter uses them to do openly what the series has been doing quietly for two books: examine the acquisition pattern.

Karis works from observables only (per the terms — she has never seen the Power Log and doesn't ask). Her data: Cael's capabilities cluster around the signatures of people he has spent sustained time observing at close range. Her stated hypothesis, delivered to Cael and Lira and Brom with academic formality in the common room: "Whatever your architecture does, observation is upstream of it. Every capability I can document corresponds to prolonged exposure to a practitioner. If exposure is the input, the process should — in principle — be *directable*." The room goes still. She has, from the outside, with no access to the log, reconstructed the outline of the thing the three of them have been circling for two books. Brom, softly: "Told you she had a method."

Cael's internal movement is the chapter's real content. Directing it has never occurred to him as a possibility — the fragments *arrive*, they have always arrived, on their own schedule, like weather. The idea that the weather might have a handle on it is destabilizing precisely because of what it implies: if it can be aimed, then what happened with Lira's Wind, with Feryn, with Brom, with Reydan, was never random. And if it was never random, he is not a person things happen to. He is a person who does things — and has been, without consent, to people he cares about. He takes this to Lira alone, at night, the way he took the first disclosure to her in Book 1. "I might have been taking from you on purpose without knowing it was on purpose." Lira, steady, after a long moment: "You told me what you knew when you knew it. Keep doing that, and there's nothing to forgive." It is the Book 1 Ch16 covenant, renewed under heavier load.

*Close on:* the Power Log — the entry that titles the internal arc: "If it can be directed, I have to learn to direct it. Not to have more. To stop not-knowing what I'm doing. Not-knowing is the version that eventually gets someone killed."

**Chapter 10 — What Fenmark Kept**
~4,600 words

Lira's chapter — the re-certification arc's centerpiece, keeping her and Karis's tracks structurally distinct at the exact point the research arc is heating up.

Lira's first formal reassessment under the re-certification provision. The procedure requires her originating academy's records, and Fenmark's file arrives at Greyvane in full for the first time: the expulsion documentation Lira has never actually read. Quenna, administering, gives her the choice of reviewing it before the assessment. She almost says no. She reads it.

The file is worse than she expected in a way she didn't expect: it's *thin*. The insubordination finding that ended her academic life runs four paragraphs. No hearing transcript, because there was no hearing — Fenmark's disciplinary provision for Copper-tier scholarship students required none. Three years of her life redirected by four paragraphs and a signature. The chapter sits with her in that — the discovery that the institution that broke her didn't even do it *carefully* — and then watches her put it down and walk onto the assessment floor. The reassessment itself is the answer: she demonstrates advancement so far beyond her file's Copper ceiling that Quenna's panel logs the session with the notation "advancement inconsistent with recorded tier; recommend continued track with priority review." It is the academy system, for the first time in her life, writing down that her file might be wrong.

*Key beat:* Cael finds her afterward. She hands him the four paragraphs. He reads them twice, and says the exact right thing, which he learned from her: "The records know what happened here. Nobody gets to say otherwise." Vell's words, passed forward. Lira laughs — actually laughs — and keeps the copy. "Evidence," she says. "For when it's obvious."

*Secondary thread:* Brom, in the chapter's margins, hits the first real wall of his formal education — Wray identifies a structural flaw in his self-taught force-redirect that only shows against Silver-tier-speed opponents. Nobody at Greyvane can attack fast enough to train him past it. Filed openly: Greyvane's ceiling is real, and it is not only Cael's problem. (Book 4 pressure, planted early.)

**Chapter 11 — Controlled Conditions**
~4,600 words

The attempt-and-failure chapter. Cael tries to direct an acquisition under controlled conditions — and the failures teach the rule the revelation will later confirm.

The setup, negotiated carefully and consented to explicitly: Karis offers herself as source. Structured sessions — she runs Ember Path declarations at demonstration intensity while Cael observes with directed intent, trying to do deliberately what has only ever happened on its own. Session after session: nothing. He can observe, he can catalogue, he can predict her ignition timing to a quarter-second — no fragment, no notice, not even the pre-acquisition texture he has learned to half-recognize. Karis documents every null result with undiminished enthusiasm ("a boundary condition is a finding"), which Cael finds first alien and then, reluctantly, steadying.

The chapter works the pattern honestly, through the Power Log: every acquisition he has ever received came under genuine engagement — Lira's real sparring, Feryn's real bout, Brom's full-intensity sessions, Reydan's climax fight. Never from the audience. Never from a demonstration. Hypothesis, written in Cael's careful hand: "Observation is necessary but not sufficient. The architecture doesn't take what's shown. It takes what's *used against me*. Stakes are part of the mechanism." He doesn't yet know if the rule is real. Part 2's climax is its test.

*LOCKED thread touched, not advanced:* In one late session, Karis — thorough — asks about anomalies, and Cael tells her about session nine: the Tide-adjacent reading Brom's pressure-read caught, unreproduced for a year despite methodical attempts. They spend an evening on it. Karis's conclusion, frustrated and honest: "It doesn't fit your acquisition pattern. No Tide practitioner, no sustained exposure, no stakes. It shouldn't exist." Cael: "It exists." Karis: "Then something about the model is incomplete, and I can't see the missing piece from here." She marks it in her notebook with a symbol she reserves for exactly one category: *real, unexplained, keep*. It is not resolved. It is not reframed. It stays exactly as open as Book 2 left it.

**Chapter 12 — Ember Architecture**
~4,600 words

The preparation chapter — mirroring the Book 2 rhythm (Ch9, Ch19) of study before a defining fight.

Quenna authorizes the next demonstration assessment as a full supervised training match: Cael versus Karis, formal conditions, faculty panel, logged. The framing is institutional — the panel wants to see the observer against Iron-tier at match intensity — but everyone inside the research understands the second experiment running underneath: genuine engagement, real stakes, an opponent he has spent six weeks in sustained close observation of. If the stakes hypothesis is right, the conditions have never been better. Karis consents on the record, in writing, in her own terms: "I want to be the first documented source. If it works, the documentation matters. If it doesn't, that matters more." Lira, reading the consent note, to Cael, dry: "She writes a better waiver than Fenmark writes an expulsion."

The study content: Ember Path at Iron-tier, done with the depth Book 2 gave Iron Skin. Karis's variant is control-dominant rather than output-dominant — precise ignition points, sustained heat channels, a lattice of small commitments instead of large releases. Her tell architecture is the cleanest Cael has ever charted, because she trained at a real academy and real academies train the tells *in*: standardization is legibility. His fight problem is real regardless of the experiment — her lattice punishes his evasion framework's seam worse than anything since Reydan, and Lira drills him for it without mercy.

*Key beat:* the night before. Cael and Karis, archive table, notebooks closed for once. Karis: "Tomorrow, don't fight the experiment. Fight me. If your rule is right, the experiment only works if you forget it's happening." Cael, after a moment: "That's the first coaching you've ever given me." Karis: "It's the only kind I have."

**Chapter 13 — Directed**
~4,600 words

The revelation chapter. The book's first central scene, and the payoff of two books of accumulated ambiguity — positioned at Chapter 13 in deliberate echo of Book 2's fragment chapter.

**The match, full choreography.** Training hall, faculty panel, formal conditions. **First exchange:** Karis's lattice does what the drills predicted — she cuts the training space into heat channels, denies his evasion angles, forces him to spend the Wind-adjacent framework faster than it can be spent. She wins the exchange going away. She is better at match intensity than any of them modeled from her demonstrations, which is itself a finding: Karis at real stakes is a different practitioner than Karis at documentation intensity. **Second exchange:** Cael adjusts — Iron-adjacent surface read to feel ignition points forming, Compression-adjacent to survive the channel he chooses to cross instead of evade. He takes real heat doing it and closes to contact range once. It costs more than it gains. His mobility is degrading. He is, by every honest read, losing.

**Third exchange:** the moment. Inside her lattice, out of angles, at genuine stakes, sustained observation running at maximum because his survival requires it — Cael stops trying to escape her architecture and does what he has spent six weeks learning her deeply enough to do: he *reaches for it*. Consciously. Intentionally. Directed. Not metaphor — the interior action the controlled sessions could never complete, completing, because every condition the null results mapped is finally present at once. He takes a fragment of the Ember architecture as it is being used against him, and he *deploys it in the same exchange* — a single small ignition-point of his own, crude, partial, absolutely unmistakable, breaking her lattice's timing from the inside. Karis — who is mid-fight, and who is also the one person in the room who fully understands what she just witnessed — stops fighting. She stands in the wreck of her own lattice and starts laughing, breathless, delighted, before the panel has processed anything.

The match is called a draw on interruption. Nobody involved cares. The notice arrives while Cael's hands are still shaking:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Ember-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
Note: ignition-point component. Single-channel, contact-adjacent range.
Acquisition: directed.
```

**Chapter 16 — Three Weeks**

**Chapter 17 — Registry Law**
~4,600 words

The study chapter — three weeks of preparation compressed into the book's most unusual training montage, textured exactly like Cael's fight-prep chapters, because to Cael it *is* one.

Prynn's archive becomes the training floor. The chapter takes the Compact registry code seriously as worldbuilding — the reader learns it alongside Cael, in the compound-gaze style: the code's architecture (charter law → classification schedule → practitioner obligations → enforcement provisions), its history of amendments, its load-bearing definitions. And the seam, found the way he found Keth's seam and Edran's seam — by reading until the pattern shows its joint: **the classification schedule is a closed, enumerated list.** Every Path, every tier, every provisional and disputed category the Compact has ever asserted authority over is *named* in the schedule, because enumeration is how the code grants jurisdiction. [SHATTERED] is not in the schedule. It appears in the code exactly nowhere. It exists only in Arbiter output and enforcement custom — a flag the system emits, that four hundred years of practice treated as a classification, that the law never actually defined. The machinery that expelled him from Denvash ran on precedent and fear. It never ran on text.

Prynn, across three weeks, becomes the chapter's quiet second character — territorial archivist thawing into co-conspirator, because Cael treats her records the way Vell taught him to treat records: as the truth about people, kept safe. Her line, late, dry as dust: "Sixty years I've kept these shelves. You're the first person to read the code the way it's written instead of the way it's enforced."

*Key beat:* Cael drafting the argument's first full outline and hitting its terrifying implication — he isn't arguing that his enrollment is valid. He's arguing that the Compact's authority over [SHATTERED] has *never* been valid. If the argument wins, it doesn't just save one enrollment. It puts a crack in the machine, on a public record, with his name on it. He sits with the outline a long time. Then he keeps writing.

**Chapter 19 — The Older Word**
~4,600 words

The [UNBOUND] discovery chapter — the book's planted mystery, staged late, exactly as the series bible requires: something Karis literally finds, and copies, before losing access.

The mechanism: the filed challenge entitles the Compact to inventory all enrollment-relevant academy records, and the inventory sweep reaches Prynn's archive — including the founding-era waystation records that predate the academy, the charter, and the Compact's last registry standardization. Prynn fights the scope of the seizure clause by clause and loses; the pre-standardization materials fall under a catch-all provision. The record: a waystation registrant's entry, four centuries old, in the pre-registry hand. A traveler assessed by the old terminology. The entry describes what the assessor witnessed — capabilities across multiple Path signatures, no single classification applicable — and gives the designation the old system used for it: **[UNBOUND]**. And then, in a later annotation in a different hand, the line that stops Karis's breath: *"Per standardization directive, prior designation [UNBOUND] to be re-entered under flag [SHATTERED]; original terminology discontinued."* Not two words for two things. Two words for the *same* thing — one from before the system, one from after, and the after-word chosen by someone, on a directive, for a reason nobody recorded. Karis copies the full passage by hand, twice, checking every abbreviation against Prynn's key, finishing as the Compact clerks reach that shelf. The record goes into a sealed box under Compact stamp. She watches it go, her copy inside her notebook, inside her coat.

That night, the scene the whole discovery exists for: she shows Cael. Two independent records. Two cities. Same buried word. Karis, at a whisper, the researcher's whole soul in it: "Someone renamed you four hundred years before you were born. *Shattered* is an editorial choice. The question is whose." Neither of them can answer it. Neither of them will for a long time. LOCKED open — the copy travels forward; the understanding does not.

*Close on:* Cael declining to use it. This is older than their text. We keep it until we understand it." Into the notebook it goes. The reader files the click for five books.

**Chapter 20 — Eve**
~4,600 words

The night-before chapter — the argument finished, the fighter's ritual observed, each companion giving Cael one thing.

The argument's final shape, rehearsed in the common room with Karis playing magistrate (pedantically, gleefully) and Brom playing Coss (unnervingly well): concede the category, contest the jurisdiction. **Chapter 21 — Adjudication**
~4,600 words

The hearing opens — the Compact's case in full, and the chapter engineered to make the reader believe, at its final page, that Cael is going to lose.

The staging: Greyvane's lecture hall converted to a hearing chamber, public gallery packed — students, faculty, and (the chapter notes with circuit-trained eyes) several credentialed observers from institutions that have no obvious business here; the record is public and word has traveled. Yorlan presiding, Ilsev and a third assessor empaneled, Havel recording. Coss presents for the Compact.

And Coss is *good* — the book pays the antagonist the respect of competence. His case is clean, documented, and, on its own terms, entirely correct: the provision's text, its legislative intent (he produces the drafting records; Naveth visibly did not know the Compact had them), the enrollment's plain-face invalidity, precedent after precedent of category-violation enrollments voided. He examines Quenna and lands the assessment records' careful drafting as evasion. He examines Naveth and makes the provost's honest hedging sound like consciousness of guilt. Midway through, the gallery's energy curdles; Greyvane's students came to watch their anomaly win and are watching him be dismantled instead. *Close on (the pivot, held to the last page):* Yorlan, closing the session: "The respondent will present his defense of the enrollment at next sitting." Cael stands. A full second of absolute silence. Ilsev looks up from her notes for the first time all day. Coss's expression does something small and complicated — the look of a man watching the one argument he'd worried about walk onto the record. Session ends. The gallery detonates into noise.

**Chapter 22 — No Path Given**
~4,600 words

The title chapter. The hearing's second sitting — the argument in full, and the book's structural and emotional climax.

Cael argues it himself — fifteen, alone at the respondent's table, three weeks of Prynn's archive in a stack of tabbed pages he barely touches because it's all resident, the way Lira's evasion patterns became resident: drilled past memory into movement. The chapter gives the argument real airtime, beat by beat, in the series' fight-choreography register — because that's what it is, the compound gaze applied to law, and the prose should make the parallel unmistakable. **First movement:** concession. He concedes everything Coss proved. The provision was written for the unkindled; he is not unkindled; the enrollment, as categorized, is invalid. The gallery stops breathing. Coss goes very still — total concession is the one opening he has no prepared answer for. **Second movement:** the foundation question. Voiding an enrollment is an exercise of enforcement authority; enforcement authority, per the code's own architecture, extends to *classified practitioners*; classification is created by the enumerated schedule — "and I ask the panel to find [SHATTERED] in it." He reads the schedule. Aloud. Every category, every provisional designation, every disputed-status clause, the amendment history. It takes a long time. That is the point. [SHATTERED] is not there. **Third movement:** the seam, driven home. The flag is Arbiter *output*, never defined, never enumerated, never granted jurisdiction — four hundred years of enforcement custom built on a word the law never wrote down. "The Compact says no path was given to me. I'm holding them to it. No path given — no authority attached. Their rules don't contain me. They never did."

Coss's rebuttal is the anticipated one — custom, implication, the absurdity of a flag with no legal home — and it dies where the rehearsal predicted: on Ilsev. The panel's deliberation questions are the climax-inside-the-climax, and Ilsev's are surgical, aimed at both sides equally, and fatal to exactly one of them, because her entire characterization since Book 1 — *procedurally scrupulous, follows text where text goes* — now pays off as the hinge of the series' first institutional victory. Her question to Coss: "Cite the schedule entry, Warden. Not the practice. The entry." He cannot. There isn't one.

**The ruling**, Yorlan, colorless to the last, and all the more devastating for it: the challenge is *dismissed for want of established jurisdiction*. The panel does not validate the enrollment — enrollment standing is remanded to the academy as an internal matter (Naveth will have it re-papered by nightfall). The panel finds only, narrowly, precisely, that the Compact has not established authority to void it — "the respondent's classification status being undefined under the current schedule." Contested, not resolved: the crack, not the collapse. On the record. In public. Forever.

*Close on:* the gallery's roar, and under it, the quiet series of images the book has earned: Lira on her feet first; Brom nodding once, slowly, the arbiter of interesting problems watching his problem win; Karis writing even now, tears entirely unacknowledged on her face; Quenna's long exhale; Prynn, in the back, dust-dry and incandescent. And Cael at the table, fifteen years old, hands flat on the wood, understanding — before anyone reaches him, in the last breath of stillness he'll get — that he just did something no [SHATTERED] practitioner in four recorded instances ever got the chance to do: he made the system say, in its own voice, on its own record, that it never had the right.

**Chapter 23 — The Record Is Public**
~4,600 words

The cost chapter — the victory's second face, and the book's antagonist-track resolution and Book 4 handoff.

Coss's exit, given the weight a two-book adversary has earned. He requests five minutes with Cael — off the record, witnessed by Quenna at Cael's insistence — and spends them not on grievance but on warning, because Coss's defining trait was always that he does the job honestly: "Understand what you won. He files his final report as contact of record — the chapter's limited third-person window watches him write it, factual, unembellished, professionally ruinous to himself, ending with a recommendation he knows will be ignored: *subject is a boy; the anomaly is the schedule; recommend registry review, not escalation.* Then the window pulls back one level higher than the series has ever gone: an office, unnamed; a request slip for the complete Greyvane adjudication transcript; a new file cover, cut clean.

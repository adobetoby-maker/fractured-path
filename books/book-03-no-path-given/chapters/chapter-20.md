# Chapter 20 — Eve

They ran the argument for the last time after dinner, in the common room, with the lamps burning low enough that the windows had gone dark and reflective instead of showing the yard beyond them.

Karis played magistrate. She was, predictably, the best possible person for the role — pedantic in exactly the way a real magistrate would be pedantic, gleeful in a way no real magistrate would ever admit to, interrupting Cael mid-sentence with objections she'd clearly been saving up for days. She had also, being Karis, prepared: she sat behind a stack of books arranged into something approximating a magistrate's bench, and she had written out Yorlan's opening formalities verbatim from the first sitting's public transcript, and she delivered them in a flat, colorless voice that was close enough to the original that Brom, entering with tea, stopped in the doorway and briefly checked the room for actual Compact officials.

"The panel doesn't care how the argument feels to you, Cael. It cares whether the citation is load-bearing. Cite the schedule section again. Slower."

"Section two, subsection four. Enforcement authority attaches to classified practitioners. Classification is defined at charter level —"

"Defined where at charter level. Don't wave at the charter. The charter is four hundred pages. Point."

"Definitions clause. *Classification, for all purposes under this charter and its subordinate provisions, shall mean designation under the Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings, as amended.* Shall mean. It's a closed definition. There is no classification outside the schedule, as a matter of the charter's own language."

"Better." Karis made a note with a flourish that no real magistrate would have survived making. "Now the part where you insult four centuries of my colleagues. Delicately."

Brom played Coss, and it was, everyone agreed, unnerving how well he did it. He'd spent three weeks watching the man work — the deposition, the pre-hearing filings, the quiet procedural pressure Coss applied without ever once raising his voice — and he'd absorbed something of the rhythm the way he absorbed everything physical, by watching until his body understood it before his mind did. He didn't imitate Coss's voice. He did something stranger and more effective: he sat the way Coss sat, paced his sentences the way Coss paced them, and let the stillness do the rest.

"You concede the enrollment's invalid," Brom said, in that borrowed flat authority that made Cael's stomach tighten every time despite three weeks of practice. "So what's left to argue?"

"Jurisdiction. You're assuming the Compact has authority to void an enrollment held by someone under its classification system. I'm telling you it doesn't have authority to void anything held by someone its classification system doesn't contain."

"The flag exists."

"The flag is Arbiter output. It's not a classification. Classification is created by the enumerated schedule — every Path, every tier, every provisional and disputed category the Compact has ever claimed authority over, all of it named, all of it written down. [SHATTERED] isn't in the schedule. It's never been in the schedule. Four hundred years of enforcement custom built on a word the law itself never defined." He said it the way he'd said it a hundred times in the archive, alone, to Prynn, to the empty shelves — and it still landed differently out loud, in front of people, with Brom's borrowed flatness staring back at him like the real thing. "You can't expel me under rules that don't contain me."

"Then nothing contains you." Brom leaned forward slightly, and this was new — an angle they hadn't rehearsed, arriving in Coss's register from wherever Brom's instinct for pressure lived. "That's what you're asking the panel to find. That there is a category of practitioner the law cannot touch at all. No obligations, no oversight, no recourse for anyone you harm. You're asking three Compact officials to sign their names to a hole in the world. Why would they."

The room went quiet. This was the question underneath the question — not a legal objection, an institutional one, the fear that would be sitting in the panel's chairs tomorrow whether anyone voiced it or not.

Cael took his time, because the rehearsal existed for exactly this, and because the answer had to be built now, tonight, or it would be built badly tomorrow in front of people who mattered.

"Because I'm not asking them to create the hole," he said, slowly, feeling for the joints of it. "The hole already exists. It's existed for four hundred years. The only thing I'm asking them to do is stop pretending it doesn't — because the pretense is the dangerous part. Right now, everything done to people like me is done outside the law while wearing its coat. No defined process, no defined limits, no appeal, because you can't appeal a rule that was never written. If the panel finds the schedule doesn't contain me, that's not the creation of a gap. That's the discovery of one — on the record, where someone with drafting authority can finally fix it properly. The remedy for a hole in the law is to write the law. It has never once been to enforce the hole as if it were law."

Brom held the Coss-stillness a moment longer. Then he dropped it, and was Brom again, and nodded once. "That's the answer," he said. "That one. If they ask why they should sign their names to it, that's the one you give."

Karis, still magistrate, still merciless: "Objection anyway. The panel could simply rule that the flag implies membership in the schedule by custom — that four hundred years of treating [SHATTERED] as a classification is itself the classification, whether or not anyone ever wrote it down."

This was the vulnerability. They'd found it together, three nights ago, in a session that had run past midnight and left Cael staring at the ceiling of his room for an hour afterward turning it over. It was the one place the argument could actually break, and Karis never let him forget it, drilling the counter into him the same relentless way she drilled everything: patiently, precisely, until he could deliver it half-asleep.

"Custom is not code," Cael said. "If custom were sufficient, the schedule wouldn't need to exist at all — the Compact could simply point at practice and call it law. It doesn't do that, because it can't. That's exactly why the schedule was written the way it was written: enumerated, closed, precise. Every amendment in its history is the Compact declining to rely on custom — every time practice and text diverged, they amended the text, because they knew which one governed. And that's exactly why Assessor Ilsev's seat exists. Someone on that panel is paid, precisely, to know the difference between what the code says and what people have always assumed it says. If the panel rules on custom instead of text, they're not applying the law. They're admitting they can't find it."

Silence, for a moment. Karis broke it first, dropping the magistrate's voice entirely. "That's it. That's the whole shape of it. If you can say that last part without flinching in front of an actual panel instead of me, you win."

"If," Brom said, half back in Coss's register, "the panel wants the schedule to say what it doesn't say. That's the part nobody can promise."

"No," Cael agreed. "Nobody can promise that." He closed the notebook he'd been reading from, though he hadn't needed to read from it in over a week — the argument lived somewhere past memory now, the way Lira's evasion patterns lived past memory, drilled down into the kind of knowing that didn't require looking anything up. "But I can promise I said it right. That's the part that's actually mine."

They ran it twice more anyway — the concession, the definitions clause, the schedule, the custom counter — because Karis's position on rehearsal was theological and non-negotiable, and because, as she pointed out while resetting her book-bench for the final pass, "tomorrow you get one delivery in front of the only audience that counts, and tonight you get as many as you're willing to earn."

On the last pass, nobody objected to anything. The argument went through whole, beginning to end, in the low lamplight, to a common room gone quiet, and when it finished, none of the three of them said anything for a moment, because there was nothing left in it to fix and all of them could hear that, and hearing it was its own strange, weightless feeling — the feeling of a preparation actually ending.

Quenna appeared in the doorway near the end of the hour, drawn by the lamplight or by an administrator's instinct for where the important thing in her building was happening. She didn't come in. She listened from the doorway to the last minute of the final pass, and when it was done she said only, in the direction of the room rather than any one person in it: "Second sitting convenes at first bell. The respondent speaks from the floor, standing, and may not be interrupted during his primary presentation — that's charter procedure, and Yorlan follows procedure. Whatever you have, you'll get to say all of it." A pause, and then, before she withdrew: "Sleep. All four of you. The argument's finished. Carrying it past this point just wears the finish off."

---

Lira found him afterward, out past the residence wing, and didn't say anything about the rehearsal at all. She just held out a hand toward the training yard, the gesture so familiar he was moving before he'd consciously decided to follow her.

The yard at dusk was empty, the last of the light going gold and then grey the way it always did over walls that had seen more evenings like this than either of them had lived years. She didn't run him through anything from the archive, didn't ask him a single question about registry law or classification schedules or Ilsev's seat. She just took her stance, the old familiar one, the one from before either of them had ever heard the word Greyvane, and waited for him to match it.

They sparred lightly. Familiar patterns — his evasion framework against her Wind-adjacent forms, patterns so old between them they barely counted as fighting anymore, more like a conversation conducted in movement instead of words. She didn't press him hard. She didn't need to. Three weeks of archive dust came out of his shoulders one exchange at a time, the tightness he hadn't noticed accumulating in his neck and spine easing loose the way it only ever eased in motion, against someone who knew exactly how much pressure to apply and exactly when to ease off it.

"You've been someone else for three weeks," she said, catching her breath after a long exchange, not quite looking at him. "Careful. Precise. Reading everything like it might bite you. That's not wrong, for what you've been doing. But it's not the whole of you either."

"I know."

"I don't think you do, entirely. That's why I brought you out here instead of just telling you." She circled him, slow and easy, the last light catching the side of her face. "Whatever happens in there tomorrow — the argument, the ruling, whichever way it goes — this is real. This yard. This fight. Start from that. Not from the schedule, not from Coss, not from four hundred years of a word nobody wrote down properly. Start from the thing that's actually true regardless of what any panel decides."

He came at her again, and this time he felt it land the way she meant it to — not a correction to his argument, which needed no correcting, but a correction to the ground he was standing on while he made it. There was a difference, she was telling him without telling him, between a boy who needed the ruling to know what he was and a boy who already knew and was simply informing the panel. The first one could be broken in that room tomorrow. The second one couldn't — not by any ruling, because no ruling had made him.

They ran the old patterns until the light had gone fully grey, and when they finally stopped, breathing hard, grinning at each other in the way that came from having fought someone you trusted completely, the archive's weight had genuinely lifted. Not gone. Set down somewhere he could pick it back up tomorrow, lighter for having been put down at all.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me after you win." She started toward the residence wing, then stopped, looking back at him with something unguarded in her face that she didn't often let show all the way through. "Do you remember what you said to me, after I read the Fenmark file? *The records know what happened here. Nobody gets to say otherwise.*" She let it sit a beat. "Tomorrow the record finally works for one of us. Make it say something worth keeping." Then, because she was Lira and the moment had held its full weight for exactly as long as she permitted moments to: "You're going to win, Cael. Not because the argument's clever. Because it's true, and you're the only person who's ever been positioned to say it out loud. Go rest."

---

Brom caught him in the corridor outside his room, holding a single folded page, and for a moment neither of them said anything, which was, between the two of them, a whole conversation in itself.

"Here," Brom said finally, holding it out.

Cael unfolded it. It was Brom's handwriting, careful in the way of someone unused to writing things meant to last — dated, and beneath the date, an account of the third exchange of the first fight the two of them had ever had, back before either of them had known what the other one was, back when Brom had still been the mysterious stranger at the edge of a training yard and Cael had still been figuring out what he was allowed to want out of a friendship built on getting hit by someone who watched him too closely.

The account was precise, almost clinical, the way Brom described anything he'd taken the trouble to actually understand: what he'd seen Cael do, the exact sequence, the moment the fight had stopped following the rules Brom understood and started following rules he didn't. He hadn't flattered either of them. He'd recorded his own errors in the exchange with the same evenness as everything else, and the evenness was what made the page feel like what it was — not a tribute, a *witness statement*, made by a man who considered accuracy the highest form of respect he had to offer. It ended with a single signed line beneath the account itself.

*In case you ever need a witness to what you are, from someone who was trying his best to beat it.*

"You wrote this down," Cael said, when he trusted his voice again.

"A while ago. I've had it for a while. Never knew what it was for." Brom shrugged, the economical shrug of someone uncomfortable with how much weight the moment had accumulated without his permission. "Seemed like it might be for this."

"It's not evidence. It's not anything they'll accept at the hearing."

"I know. It's not for the hearing." Brom held his gaze, steady, patient, the way he held everything that mattered to him. "It's for you. In case tomorrow goes badly and you start doubting whether any of it was real. Somebody wrote it down. Somebody who was there, and who has no reason to lie about it, because I lost that fight and admitting what happened doesn't make me look any better." He nodded once, like that settled something, and turned to go. Then, over his shoulder: "Whatever they rule tomorrow. That page doesn't change."

Cael read it three more times before he set it down, and did not, in the end, put it in the log or the binder or anywhere it might get filed among the evidence. He put it in his pocket instead, close, where he could feel the fold of it if he needed to.

---

Karis found him last, in the archive, where he'd gone to return a stack of Prynn's reference volumes he no longer needed for the argument — the argument was finished, drilled, as ready as three weeks and a careful mind could make it, and there was something almost ceremonial in returning the last of the borrowed books, closing that door on his own preparation. Prynn accepted the stack without comment, checked each spine against her slips, and said only, at the end, "Shelf them yourself. You know where they live now." Which was, from Prynn, roughly a knighthood.

Karis was waiting at the table they'd claimed as theirs since the first week, her research notebooks stacked in front of her in the order she always kept them, oldest to newest, eight months of a life reorganized around a rumor that had turned out to be entirely, spectacularly true.

"I have something for you," she said, and slid the topmost notebook across the table — not opened to a random page, but to the last one, the conclusion page, the place her research had, for the moment, come to rest.

He read it. It was short. It was, in Karis's economical hand, the entire eight months distilled down to a single line, dated the day of his fifth fragment, the day the hypothesis had stopped being a hypothesis and started being a fact she'd watched happen in front of a faculty panel.

*Findings to date: the phenomenon is a person. Study continues.*

"That's it?" he said. "Eight months, and that's the conclusion?"

"That's the conclusion that matters." She watched him read it a second time, her expression doing something complicated — not quite vulnerable, not quite proud, some third thing that belonged only to her. "I came here to study a phenomenon. I found out, somewhere in the last three weeks, that the phenomenon has a grandfather who writes him letters, and a friend who wrote down a fight he lost because he thought you might need it someday, and someone who spars with you at dusk to remind you what's real underneath an argument. Phenomena don't have those things, Cael. People do." She closed the notebook gently, as if closing it too hard might undo the conclusion written inside it. "I updated my methodology. I thought you should see where it ended up."

"Study continues," he read aloud.

"It does. I have no intention of stopping." She said it plainly, without apology, and he understood, sitting there, that this was as close as Karis Dellenmoor came to saying something she couldn't quite bring herself to say the way Lira or Brom might have said it. It was, in its own precise and careful way, exactly enough.

He stood to go, and stopped at the end of the table, because there was one more thing and tomorrow it would be too late to say it cleanly.

"You know the argument's yours too," he said. "Half the counters. The custom objection. The rehearsals. If it holds tomorrow, it holds because you spent three weeks refusing to let it be weaker than it could be."

Karis considered this with the seriousness she gave everything, which was total.

"Noted," she said. "For the record: the argument is the respondent's own work. The panel should be told so." A pause, and the smallest possible fraction of a smile. "The methodology, however, was excellent, and the methodologist expects to be cited in your memoirs."

---

He was alone by the time he got the Power Log out, sitting at the small desk in his room with the lamp turned low and the window showing nothing but his own reflection against the dark yard beyond it. Tomorrow the Compact would hear the argument he'd spent three weeks building. Tomorrow he would stand in a hearing chamber and tell a room full of people who had the formal authority to end his enrollment that their own authority had never actually reached him at all.

He opened the log and wrote, in the hand he saved for the entries that had to outlast the mood they were written in:

*Tomorrow I argue that their system never contained me.*

He sat with the sentence a moment, and then, because the strangest part of the whole three weeks deserved to be written down honestly, added the rest.

*Strange discovery of the archive: I'm not angry about the argument. The system that flagged me, expelled me, and hunted me also never actually claimed me. Fourteen-year-old Cael needed to hear that. Tomorrow the Compact hears it instead.*

He closed the log, turned down the lamp, and lay in the dark a long while before sleep found him — not anxious, exactly, though the anxiety was there, familiar, present the way it had been present the night before every real thing that had ever happened to him. Underneath it, something steadier. A page folded in his pocket. A notebook's last conclusion, dated and signed. A yard at dusk that had put the weight of three weeks down where he could pick it back up in the morning, lighter for having set it down at all.

Whatever the panel ruled tomorrow, he understood, drifting toward sleep, none of it would change what was already true tonight.

He had five things that weren't a Path. An argument that was actually his. And three people who'd stood next to him for long enough to know exactly what they were vouching for.

It would have to be enough. He suspected, lying there in the dark, that it already was.

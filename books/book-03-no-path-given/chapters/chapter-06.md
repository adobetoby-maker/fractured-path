# Chapter 6 — Terms

Cael had stopped deciding things alone somewhere over the past year and a half, though he could not have named the exact day it happened. It had not felt like a decision at the time. It had felt like a habit forming the way habits usually formed for him — quietly, through repetition, until one day he noticed the shape of it and realized it had been there for months.

So when Karis found him in the lecture wing's stairwell the morning after the archive — waiting at the landing, he noted, rather than intercepting him mid-flight, a positional courtesy he doubted was accidental — she didn't ask whether he'd decided. She asked something else.

"Before you answer me," she said, "I'd like to put the question to the people you'd consult anyway. All of them, in one room, where they can object to my face instead of to your account of my face. Your account would be accurate. It still wouldn't be me." She adjusted the strap of her bag, the first unnecessary movement he'd ever seen her make. "If I'm going to be refused, I'd rather be refused on primary evidence."

He said yes before he'd fully worked out why it mattered so much that she'd asked at all. It mattered. He wrote it in the log that night, a small addition to a much larger entry: *She could have pressed me alone. She asked to bring it to the table instead. She knows what the table is without having been told. Noted.*

The table, when it convened, was the low scarred one in the residence common room that had apparently hosted every important conversation Greyvane's students had ever needed privacy for — initials and dates burned and gouged into its edges going back decades, a stratigraphy of other people's alliances. The evening crowd had thinned; a pair of second-years played tiles by the fire with the studied deafness of students who understood that not listening was the common room's most enforceable law. Brom got there first and claimed the corner seat with his back to the wall, an old habit from circuit venues that Cael doubted he'd ever consciously examine. Lira came in second, dropped into the seat across from Karis's empty chair before Karis had even arrived, and said nothing, which was a statement in itself.

"You're already braced," Cael said.

"I'm already informed," Lira said. "Brom told me what she asked you. I've had a night to think about it."

"And?"

"And I'm going to listen before I decide anything, which is more generous than it sounds, because I already know what I think." She folded her arms, not unkindly, the way people did when they wanted their reservations on the record before anything else got said. "I want to hear her say it herself."

Karis arrived on the fourth bell, precisely, a folder under one arm and an expression that Cael was beginning to recognize as her version of nervous — not anxious exactly, but keyed to a pitch of readiness that suggested she'd rehearsed several openings and discarded most of them.

---

"I asked Cael for permission to study him," she said, once she'd taken the empty chair and set the folder on the table without opening it. No preamble beyond that. She had apparently decided, somewhere between the archive and this room, that the direct approach was the only honest one available to her. "He asked me why he should trust me, and I told him trust was a finding, not a premise. I still believe that. But I understand that isn't only his decision to weigh. It's yours as well." She looked at Lira first, deliberately, and then at Brom. "I'd rather have this conversation with all three of you than get his agreement and discover afterward that I'd only earned a third of the trust I needed."

Brom, who had been turning a training-hall chalk stub over in his fingers without seeming to notice he was doing it, set it down.

"I'm in favor," he said. Flat, immediate, no visible deliberation behind it at all — the certainty Brom reserved for things he'd already decided before the meeting started. "I've been trying to solve him by instinct for a year. Watching what works, guessing at why, adjusting when I guess wrong. It's how I've always learned anything — Velmere didn't teach me to think about a problem, it taught me to survive one until survival started looking like skill." He nodded once at Karis, an oddly formal gesture from a boy who rarely bothered with formality. "She has a method. An actual one, built out of research and documentation instead of instinct and repetition. I want to see what a method finds that instinct missed. That's not a small thing to me."

"That's exactly the problem," Lira said.

She said it evenly, without heat, which somehow made it land harder than if she'd raised her voice. Cael watched her turn to face Karis directly — not hostile, not performing anything for the room, just giving the moment the full weight of her attention because she'd decided it deserved that much.

"I'm not against you," Lira said. "I want to say that plainly, so it doesn't get lost in whatever comes next. I don't think you're lying about your reasons. I've watched you in the archive for two weeks — you work like someone who actually cares what the answer turns out to be, not someone who already decided what the answer should be and is hunting for evidence to match it. That's rarer than you'd think, among people who study things." She paused, choosing the next part with visible care. "But I've watched a lot of institutions look closely at Cael. Every single one of them was gathering evidence for something. The certification board. The academy record system in Denvash. The Compact's monitoring file, which followed him here before he even finished unpacking. Every one of them called it observation. Every one of them was building a case." She held Karis's gaze without flinching from it. "You want data. I understand why you want it — I even think your reasons are good ones. But wanting data is still wanting something *from* him. And I've spent a year and a half being one of the people standing between him and everyone who wants something from him. Forgive me if I'm slow to hand a new person a seat at that table."

The room went quiet for a moment that felt longer than it probably was.

"You want data," Lira said again, quieter this time, the accusation sanded down to something closer to a simple statement of fact. "We're a family."

Cael watched Karis absorb this. He had, by now, spent enough time around her to recognize how she processed a hard truth — no flinch, no rush to soften it, just the methodical working-through she gave an unexpected result in one of her research sessions, checking it from every angle before she decided what to do with it.

"Yes," Karis said, finally. No hedge in it, no attempt to make the word smaller than it was. "You're right. I want data. I've wanted data about him since I first heard the rumor in Ternhall's east lecture hall, eight months ago, and I have not stopped wanting it since. I'm not going to sit at this table and pretend otherwise, because I think you'd see through the pretending in about half a sentence, and because I don't actually believe pretending would earn me anything worth having." She took a breath. "But I'm not asking you to accept that I already have what you have. I know I don't. I've fought beside no one in this room. I've bled for nobody at this table. I haven't earned the trust that gets a person handed something like a log kept in someone's own hand, written for no one else's eyes." A glance at Lira, brief, that told Cael she knew more than she'd been told directly — that she'd pieced together, from context and careful listening, that something like that existed even without ever having seen it. "I'm asking to be allowed to earn my way from the first thing toward the second. Not granted it. Earned. On whatever terms you decide are fair, and revoked the moment I stop deserving them."

Lira sat with that for what felt, to Cael, like a genuinely long time. He watched her weigh it — not performing consideration, actually doing it, setting the offer against a year and a half of learned wariness and finding, apparently, that it held up under the weight.

She looked at Cael, once, before she answered — not for permission, he understood, but for confirmation of something she wanted to see in his face before she committed her vote to it. Whatever she was looking for, she found. He hadn't said a word through the whole exchange, and he realized, meeting her eyes, that his silence had been its own testimony: the Cael of a year and a half ago would have been managing this conversation, steering it, guarding its exits. This one had sat still and let the people who loved him argue about his safety in front of him, because he trusted the argument to come out somewhere he could live with. Lira had watched him learn to do that. She'd been most of the reason he had.

"Terms," she said at last, turning back to Karis. "Real ones. Written down. Not a handshake and a good feeling."

Coming from Lira, Cael understood, this was as close to a yes as the moment was ever going to get.

"For the record," Cael said then, because the record was about him and he'd decided somewhere in the last minute that it should hold his voice too, "I want this. Not just permit it. Want it." Three faces turned to him with three different qualities of surprise. "I've spent a year and a half studying what I am with a sample size of one and no training in how to study anything. She's spent five years learning how to ask questions properly. If there are answers, I'd rather we found them on purpose, with terms, at a table like this — before somebody else finds them without asking." He looked at Lira as he said the last part, and watched her hear the argument that would actually move her: not curiosity. Defense.

"That," Lira said quietly, "is the first reason anyone's given tonight that I can't argue with."

---

The terms took the better part of an hour to draft, and Cael found himself, halfway through, doing something he rarely did in a group conversation: mostly listening, letting the negotiation happen around a subject he technically was rather than steering it as a person making demands. It felt strange in a way he didn't have a clean word for yet. He wrote *strange, not bad* in the margin of his notebook while Brom and Lira argued a clause with Karis, and left it at that.

The first real disagreement came over the Power Log itself. Karis raised it plainly, without apology, because she had apparently decided that pretending not to want the thing she wanted most would only cost her credibility she needed for everything else. "I understand it's not on the table. I'd be a poor researcher if I didn't at least ask why, once, so I understand the boundary instead of just obeying it."

"Because it isn't research to us," Lira said. "It's the two of us telling each other the truth when nobody else was going to. You can study what it produces. You don't get to read how it got written."

Karis considered this with the same unblinking attention she gave a difficult passage in an old text. "That's a fair answer," she said, eventually. "I withdraw the question. I won't ask again." And she didn't — not that day, not in any session Cael would sit through in the weeks that followed, though he noticed, more than once, the held stillness that came over her whenever the log was mentioned in passing, the visible effort of a mind choosing not to follow a thread it clearly wanted to pull.

The middle clauses went faster, in the rhythm negotiations found once everyone had established what they were actually protecting. Lira wanted a witness provision — any session, any of the three of them could attend, unannounced, and Karis agreed before Lira had finished the sentence, on the grounds that observed observers behaved better and she intended to be the best-behaved observer on record. Cael himself raised the one nobody had thought of, because a year and a half of watching institutions had taught him where arrangements actually failed: not at the signing, at the ending. "If it ends," he said, "whichever of us ends it — what happens to the notes?" The table went quiet while Karis thought about it, longer than she'd thought about anything else that hour, because the honest cost of the answer was visible on her face before she gave it. "They stay with you," she said finally. "All copies. If I walk away, I walk away from the data too. That's the only version where your veto means anything." She wrote the clause herself, and Cael watched her hand slow slightly on the words, a researcher signing away the right to her own findings, and understood he was watching the price of the arrangement being paid in advance, where everyone could see it.

Brom's contribution was smaller and, in its way, more exacting: he wanted the *consent in the moment* clause written so that Cael could withdraw from any single session without withdrawing from the whole arrangement — a distinction Karis's first draft hadn't made, and one Brom insisted on with a stubbornness that surprised even Lira. "He should be able to say not today without it meaning not ever," he said, and nobody at the table argued the point, least of all Karis, who wrote the clause in herself, in her own hand, and had Cael read it before anyone else did.

The final shape, once they'd worn the edges off it: Karis would observe demonstrations, training sessions, and anything Cael consented to in the moment — but not the Power Log itself, which stayed exactly where it had always stayed, in the hands of the two people who'd earned it the hard way, one entry and one year at a time. Nothing Karis wrote down would name the acquisition process directly, or speculate in writing about mechanism beyond what she could document from the outside. Everything she wrote, about him, Cael would read before it went anywhere else — not as approval, Karis clarified, unprompted, but as a standing right to know what was being recorded about him, which she considered a baseline courtesy that should have applied to every institution that had ever studied him and somehow never had. And either side could end the arrangement with a single word, no explanation required, no penalty attached.

Karis read the terms twice, out loud, the second time slower than the first, the way Cael imagined she checked her own research notes for errors before committing to a conclusion. Then she signed the page without a single moment of visible hesitation — a firm, even signature that looked like it belonged to someone years older than sixteen — and did something none of them had asked for and none of them had anticipated.

She reached into the folder she'd carried in and set a stack of her own notebooks on the table. Six of them, worn at the corners the way things got worn when they were carried and consulted daily, spines cracked at points that suggested certain pages had been returned to again and again.

"What's this," Lira said.

"Symmetry," Karis said. "My research. All of it — every theory, every dead end, every place my thinking about Path mechanics has been wrong before and every place it might still be wrong now. You're granting me access to study him. It seemed dishonest to keep the reverse door locked." She pushed the stack slightly toward the center of the table, an offering rather than a demand. "You should be able to study the person studying you. I'd want that, in your position. I think anyone reasonable would."

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Lira reached out, took the second notebook from the stack rather than the first — a small randomization Cael suspected was deliberate — and opened it somewhere in the middle. She read a page in silence. Turned it. Read another.

"This is your handwriting at what, twelve?" she said.

"Eleven. The early entries are embarrassing. The embarrassment is part of what I'm handing over." Karis said it evenly, but Cael, watching her, saw what it cost — the same held-breath quality he'd felt signing his own name under a category that wasn't true. Five years of being wrong in writing, pushed across a scarred table to three strangers who owed her nothing.

"There's a page here where you crossed out your own conclusion and wrote *I wanted this to be true, which is why it isn't* in the margin," Lira said, not looking up.

"Page ninety-one of that volume. Yes."

Lira closed the notebook, set it back on the stack, and looked at Karis for a long moment with an expression Cael had seen exactly twice before — once across a fire in Ardenmere, once on a training floor in the rain. Recalibration, performed in public, refusing to hide itself.

"All right," she said. That was all. From Lira, it was a paragraph.

Cael looked at the notebooks a moment longer. He had spent a year and a half learning that the people who wanted something from him rarely offered anything back that cost them real vulnerability in return. This was not a small gesture. It was, he understood, watching Brom pick up the top notebook and start reading, in no hurry at all, possibly the single most disarming thing anyone outside his own small circle had ever done in front of him.

He wrote it down that night, in the log, under the heading he'd kept since Greyvane's second day: things that were changing that he wanted to remember had changed. *Karis handed over five years of her own thinking without being asked. I don't know yet what that's worth. I'm inclined to think it's worth quite a lot.*

---

The first controlled observation session happened three days later, in the training hall's smallest supervised section, on a schedule Quenna had approved without comment beyond a raised eyebrow that suggested she'd expected this development well before it arrived. She'd read the terms page before approving it — all of it, twice, in front of them, which took long enough that Cael began to wonder whether she was going to raise an institutional objection — and had then handed it back with the only assessment she apparently felt the document required: "Whoever drafted the termination clause has a future in provision law." Karis had not quite managed to keep the pleasure off her face, and Cael had filed the exchange as the first time he'd ever seen the two of them measure each other: the assessor of irregular development and the researcher of broken models, two professionals recognizing, across a wide gap in age and station, the same taste for load-bearing language.

Lira came to the session. Unannounced, per her clause, which Cael suspected was half the point — the clause worked, look, here it was working. She took a place at the section's rail with her arms folded, said nothing to anyone, and stayed the full hour. Karis acknowledged her presence with a nod and adjusted absolutely nothing about her own conduct, which was, Cael understood, the correct response and possibly the only one Lira would have accepted.

Cael ran his evasion framework — the Wind-adjacent movement patterns that had carried him through most of a year and a half of circuit work, the ones so deeply worn into his body by now that he barely had to think through them consciously anymore. He ran the whole sequence three times through: the slow version first, joints loose, each transition exaggerated so the underlying shape stood out the way a diagram stood out from a photograph; then the working version, the one he'd actually used against Reydan and a dozen circuit opponents before him, fast enough that a spectator without training would only register motion rather than method; then a third pass, slower again, at Quenna's request from the section's edge, so Karis could watch the same architecture at two speeds and note where it changed and where it didn't.

Karis sat with her notebook open and wrote, without pause, for the better part of forty minutes. He learned things about her method just from its sound: the pen ran in long unbroken passages while he moved and went silent the instant he stopped, which meant she wasn't describing what he did — anyone could describe — she was tracking something continuous, some quantity that existed only while the framework was live. She didn't ask him to repeat anything, though he half expected her to. She didn't interrupt to clarify a point, though he could see her hand pause once or twice, pen hovering, before she apparently resolved whatever question had occurred to her without needing to voice it. She simply watched, with the same total, uninterrupted attention he'd once brought to eleven Paths in a training hall he had no standing to fight in, and filled page after page with observations he couldn't read from where he stood and didn't try to. There was something almost restful in being watched that carefully by someone who wasn't judging him for anything — not for his footwork, not for the strangeness of a framework nobody at Greyvane could quite classify, not for the fact of what he was. Just watching. Recording. Treating the whole of him as information worth the trouble of getting right.

He caught himself, twenty minutes in, adjusting his stance slightly to make a transition clearer for her — not performing, exactly, but aware of being observed in a way that made him want to be observed *well*, the way he imagined a musician might play a passage more carefully for an audience that actually understood the instrument. He wrote that down too, later, as its own small data point about himself: *I wanted her to see it right. Not to impress her. To be understood correctly. There's a difference and I'm not sure I could explain it to anyone who hasn't spent a year and a half being misread.*

When the session ended, she closed the notebook, capped her pen with the small deliberate click that he'd already learned meant she'd finished a thought completely rather than merely paused it, and asked him a question.

"When you use it," she said, "does it feel borrowed?"

Cael stood there, breathing still slightly elevated from the drill, and understood immediately that this was not a casual question. Lira had watched him run this same framework a hundred times and never once asked what it felt like from the inside. Brom, who studied him by instinct more thoroughly than anyone alive, had never phrased the question this way — as though the movement itself might carry the memory of where it came from, might feel, in the doing of it, like something that belonged first to someone else. Even Quenna, whose whole professional attention was aimed at what he was, asked only questions with observable answers. This one had no observable answer. It could only be answered from the one vantage point in the world that nobody else could occupy, and Karis had walked straight to that vantage point on her first session and knocked, politely, on the door.

He opened his mouth. He did not have an answer ready, and he had learned, across a year and a half of practice, not to manufacture one just to fill a silence that made him uncomfortable.

"I don't know," he said, finally, which was true, and which was also, he understood even as he said it, not going to be the last time he thought about the question.

Karis nodded, unbothered by the non-answer, the way someone looks who'd expected exactly this result and logged it as data rather than disappointment. She made no note of his silence. She didn't need to. Cael understood, walking back toward the residence wing with the question still sitting somewhere behind his ribs, that she had simply planted something and was content to let it grow on its own schedule.

Lira caught up with him in the corridor outside the hall, falling into step the way she had on a hundred walks home from a hundred venues. She'd heard the question — the rail was close enough. She didn't ask him what his answer was. She asked the other thing, the Lira thing, the thing that cut past the philosophy to the load underneath it.

"You went somewhere, when she asked that. Behind your eyes. Somewhere I couldn't follow." It wasn't an accusation. It was a report. "Just tell me when you're back."

"I'm back," he said, and was mostly telling the truth.

"Mm." She let it go, which was its own gift, and then, at the residence wing door, without looking at him: "For the record — it doesn't look borrowed. From the outside. A year and a half I've watched you run that framework, and for at least a year of it, it's looked like nothing in the world but yours." She went in ahead of him and left him standing with that in the doorway, which he suspected she'd also done on purpose.

He wrote it in the log that night, the only entry that mattered from the whole day.

*Does it feel borrowed. I don't know. Lira says it doesn't look it. Looking and feeling aren't the same measurement, and I notice I'm reaching for hers because I can't take mine.*

Underneath, smaller:

*I'm going to know eventually, whether I want to or not.*

He was right. It would be a while before he found out — longer than he expected, and not in the way he expected either.

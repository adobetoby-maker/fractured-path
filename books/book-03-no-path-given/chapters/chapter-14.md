# Chapter 14 — What It Costs

Greyvane, the morning after, was an academy trying very hard to pretend it was thinking about anything else.

Cael felt it the moment he stepped into the refectory: the conversational weather shifting around him, tables going a half-tone quieter as he passed and a full tone louder behind him, the sidelong traffic of glances that never quite arrived. A first-year stared at him openly over a porridge spoon until her tablemate elbowed her. Two older students at the far wall were arguing in intense whispers over something one of them was diagramming in spilled tea, and Cael did not need the compound gaze to know what the diagram was of. By the door, posted since dawn, someone official had put up a notice reminding the student body that formal assessment outcomes were matters of record and speculation about them was discouraged — which was, as institutional gestures went, roughly as effective as posting a notice discouraging weather.

Edran intercepted him at the yard crossing, fell into step for exactly four strides, and said, without preamble and without looking at him: "I'm not asking. I want you to know that I'm not asking, and that not asking is currently the hardest thing I have ever done." A beat. "The withdrawal stands. Whatever you are, it's still real. It's just—" He stopped himself, redrafted. "It's just *more* real than my last estimate." And he peeled off toward the lecture wing, leaving Cael to file the exchange under the growing category of things Edran did that were not friendship and were not nothing.

The summons to Quenna's office was waiting on his door by the time he went back for his kit.

The record she filed on the match was, Cael understood the first time he read it, a small masterpiece.

*Atypical adaptive response, consistent with previously documented irregular architecture. No safety violation noted. Assessment satisfied under demonstration provision.*

Every word of it was true. None of it said anything. He read it twice in her office the morning after, the same careful economy of language he'd watched her use on his enrollment paperwork weeks before — *the provision's language is what protects you* — and understood that she'd spent the night doing for the panel exactly what she'd once told him the system would eventually ask of him: being established fact, in writing, before anyone hostile came looking. *Atypical adaptive response* could describe a clever feint. *Consistent with previously documented irregular architecture* chained it backward to every prior assessment, so that yesterday became not a new fact but a footnote to old ones. A hostile reader would find nothing to grip. A neutral reader would find nothing at all. He was beginning to understand that Quenna's flat administrative prose was a martial discipline in its own right, and that he had been watching a master work at match intensity for weeks without recognizing the tier.

"That'll hold," she said, watching him read it. "Wray signed it without comment. The rotating seat didn't ask a single question, which tells you how little anyone in that room wanted to be the person who asked."

"And you?"

She sat down across from him, and for a moment the careful drafting fell away entirely. He had seen Quenna tired before, and Quenna guarded, and Quenna professionally opaque. He had not seen this before — Quenna with the paperwork set down, looking at him across the desk the way a person looks at a weight they have already accepted and are now re-estimating.

"I don't need to know what happened in the third exchange," she said. "I have theories. I'm not going to write them down, and I'm not going to make you say them out loud, because the moment either of those things happens, this stops being something I can protect and starts being something on a record." She let that sit. "What I need to know — not for the file, for me — is whether you can *not* do it. In front of people who wish you harm. Because the version of you that reaches for someone's ability mid-exchange without deciding to first is a version that gets flagged the instant it happens somewhere I can't draft around."

The question landed like a stone dropped into a well he hadn't known was that deep. He sat with it, and made himself actually examine it rather than reach past it for reassurance, because it deserved examination: yesterday, in the third exchange, had there been a moment where not-doing it was available? He replayed the half-second honestly — the closing channels, the reach, the word *directed* arriving in his head at the moment of the act. He had chosen it. He was nearly sure he had chosen it. But *nearly* was doing weight-bearing work in that sentence, and the difference between a choice and a reflex that felt like a choice afterward was exactly the difference Quenna was asking about, and he did not have the data. One trial. One directed acquisition, under conditions engineered to produce it. Nobody sane built a safety claim on a sample of one.

He wanted to say *yes*, because yes was the answer that let her keep sleeping and let him keep his enrollment and let the record stay clean. He said, instead, the true thing, because she'd earned the true thing and because lying to Quenna felt like a different category of mistake than lying to almost anyone else.

"I don't know yet."

She held his eyes a moment. Something in her face registered the answer — not approval exactly, but a grim species of respect, the acknowledgment of a person who would rather have an honest problem than a comfortable lie on file. "Find out before the Compact does," she said, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it followed him out of the office and sat with him for the rest of the day like a stone he hadn't agreed to carry.

---

The misfires had already happened by then. That was the part he hadn't told her, and the not-telling had its own weight.

Morning training, first light, the yard still cold enough that breath showed: he and Lira, the usual patterns, the familiar circuit-honed rhythm they could both run half-asleep. Third sequence in, he'd called on the Wind-adjacent framework for a standard redirect — his oldest fragment, the one Lira had carried into him a year and a half and a whole life ago, the one that answered before he finished asking, always, the way his own hand answered — and for half a second, nothing came. Not slowness. Absence. He'd moved on bare reflex instead, taken Lira's strike on the shoulder, and stood there in the cold with his heart going hard for reasons that had nothing to do with the hit.

"Again," Lira had said, watching his face rather than his stance, because she missed nothing.

They ran it again. The framework answered clean, as if nothing had happened. Eight sequences later it happened a second time — a half-second's lag between intent and evasion, a gap that would have cost him a bout against anyone faster than a training partner — and then answered clean again, and kept answering clean for the rest of the session, while Lira invented reasons to keep the intensity down and neither of them said the thing they were both circling.

She said it at the end, racking the practice batons, her back to him, voice pitched for two people only even though the yard was empty.

"Twice in one session. In eighteen months I've seen that framework miss exactly never." She turned. "Is it the new one? Did taking it knock something loose?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. It's also the only answer I have for anything today."

"Then here's mine." She handed him his coat. "No full-intensity anything until it's answered clean for three straight days. Not with me, not with anyone, not for any panel. If Quenna schedules something, you tell her you need the days, and if you won't tell her, I will." She held his eyes until he nodded. "You spent yesterday finding out you can reach into yourself mid-fight. You don't get to spend today pretending that came free. The log gets the misfires, dated. Both of them."

"They're already in."

"Of course they are." Something eased in her face, a quarter-inch. "That's the one part of this I never have to check."

He'd written it down before breakfast, dated, timed, with the care he gave every entry that mattered. *Fragment behavior destabilized approximately eighteen hours post-directed-acquisition. Wind-adjacent misfired twice in morning training; resolved by session's end. Iron-, Pressure-, Compression-adjacent: answering normally, checked individually. Ember-adjacent: present, partial, untested — not touching it until the others settle. Cause: unknown. Suspected: the interior action required to direct an acquisition draws on the same architecture that holds the others steady, and asking it to do something new costs something borrowed from what it already does.*

He didn't know if the hypothesis was right. He knew, with more certainty than he wanted, that he'd reached into himself mid-fight and done something no one — not Vell, not Quenna, not the four recorded [SHATTERED] instances the registry's brief cold entry had told him about, back when he'd first gone looking for others like himself in the months after his own Kindling — had ever documented doing on purpose, and he'd done it on instinct plus six weeks of study, without knowing what it would cost until the cost had already been spent. The misfires were the bill arriving. Two misfires, small, resolved — this time. He had altered something in his own architecture, irreversibly, mid-fight, on a hypothesis, and the architecture had wobbled for a day, and he had no way of knowing whether the wobble was the whole price or the first installment.

The old fear surfaced, familiar as an old bruise: *this is how it goes wrong. Not from malice. From doing something real without understanding it first, and finding out the shape of the mistake only after it's already happened to someone.*

---

And underneath the misfires, all day, there was the other presence — the fifth weight, the new one, the one he was deliberately not touching and could not stop being aware of.

It did not feel like the others. That was the finding he kept circling and kept declining to write down in full, because he didn't have language for it yet that wouldn't overclaim. The four older fragments sat in him like tools in a familiar roll — distinct, inert until called, wholly his in the way a thing becomes yours after enough use that you stop noticing the seam where it joined you. The Ember-adjacent fragment was still all seam. It sat warm. Not hot, not active, nothing the panel's instruments or anyone's eyes would ever register — just faintly, persistently warm, the way a stone holds the afternoon into the evening, and there was a signature in the warmth that he recognized, because he had spent six weeks learning to recognize it: precision, patience, small commitments immaculately kept. It felt like her. Partial and crude and torn at the edges by the taking, and even so, unmistakably, it carried the discipline of the person it had come from, twelve years of Ternhall training folded into a single ignition-point the way a handwriting carries a whole hand.

He had taken pieces of four people before this and never once felt the person in the piece — or never let himself look. He was looking now. He didn't know yet what it meant that he could see her in it. He suspected, uneasily, that it had always been true of all of them — that some part of Lira's speed and Feryn's weight and Brom's patience and Reydan's ferocity had come along inside the fragments, riding under the capability like a watermark, and that he had spent a year and a half carrying his people with him in a sense far more literal than the sentence usually meant.

He was still turning that over when Karis found him at the archive table that evening, and for the first time since she'd introduced herself there seven weeks earlier, she didn't have a notebook open.

He'd seen almost nothing of her since the match — a fact which, once he noticed it, he realized was itself remarkable. Karis had spent seven weeks being scrupulously, structurally present: sessions, meals, the common room, the archive, her attendance as regular as the water clock. For two days she had been a rumor. Brom reported sighting her once, crossing the yard at speed with her coat collar up. Lira, asked whether she'd seen her, had said "she's writing something she doesn't want witnessed," with the confidence of a woman who had spent two days doing exactly that herself once, over four paragraphs from Fenmark.

Now she sat down across from him, folded her hands on the empty table where the notebook should have been, and looked at the space it should have occupied.

"I need to tell you something," she said, "and I need you to let me finish before you say anything, because if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve, and I've been building up to this for two days."

He nodded.

"I designed that session as an experiment. I told you that going in — I want to be the first documented source, if it works the documentation matters, if it doesn't that matters more — and I meant every word of it when I said it. I've spent eight months treating you as the most important open question in the field I care about, and I was proud of that, and I still am." She was watching her own hands rather than him, which was new; Karis, in his experience, watched everything except her own hands. "What I didn't account for is what it would feel like to *be the data*. To have something of mine — a piece of the thing I trained twelve years to be able to do — sitting inside someone else now, permanently, because I consented to a session and the session worked exactly the way I'd hoped it would."

"Karis—"

"I said let me finish." She took a breath. "It doesn't distress me. That's the part I've spent two days sitting with, because I expected it to, and it didn't, and I don't fully trust a reaction I can't explain to myself yet. There's a version of this where I should feel violated, or diminished, or at minimum strange about it, and instead what I feel is—" She stopped. Started again. "I feel *included* in something. Which is not a scientific finding. It's not something I know how to write into a report. It's just true, and I've been carrying it alone for two days because I didn't know how to say it without it sounding like something it isn't."

She reached into her coat and handed him a single folded page. Not the two pages of data he'd expected — a single paragraph, in her small, exact hand, that he understood, reading it, she'd rewritten more than once. The paper itself testified to the rewriting: a crease pattern that said it had been folded, unfolded, and refolded many times, corners softened by a pocket, one edge bearing the ghost of an earlier sentence scraped carefully away.

*Session subject reports no distress at the outcome. This finding does not fit any framework I currently have for interpreting it, and I am choosing to record that honestly rather than force it into language that would make it sound more resolved than it is. What I can say is this: I do not feel studied by him. I have not, since the third exchange, felt like the experiment. I am not certain what that makes me instead, but I intend to find out, and I would like to find out from inside the circle rather than outside it, if that is something the four of you would consider allowing.*

Cael read it twice. He was aware of the archive around them, the shelved and boxed centuries of other people's recorded lives, Prynn's lamp burning somewhere back in the stacks — a room built for exactly this, for the moment when the thing that mattered most got written down and handed over. He looked up, and found her watching him now, the notebook-open steadiness gone from her entirely, replaced by something that looked, on Karis, almost unbearably like nerve.

"You're asking to actually be one of us," he said. "Not the research arrangement. The other thing."

"I'm asking," she said, "because I don't know how else to ask it, and I've never had to ask before — at Ternhall, belonging was a ranking. I was simply *in*, because I was good enough to be in. This is different, and I don't have a method for it, and I hate not having a method, and I'm asking you anyway."

He held the page and understood, with a clarity that had been assembling for seven weeks, what he was holding. Terms, again — but inverted. The first document between them had been armor: clauses and boundaries and a word that could end everything, drafted by four wary people against the possibility of harm. This one was the opposite of armor. One paragraph, no clauses, no exit provision, asking for the single thing no document could secure. She had written the most defenseless sentence of her life in the same exact hand she used for boundary conditions, and folded it, and carried it in her coat for two days, and he thought of Lira on a rooftop a year and a half ago, and of how the things that changed everything were always, in the end, someone deciding to hand someone else the unguarded version.

"The answer isn't only mine to give," he said, honestly. "You know that. You wrote it that way — *the four of you.* I'll carry it to the people whose answer it also is."

"I know," she said. "That's why I wrote it that way." And she left him with the page, which was its own message: she wasn't taking it back to redraft. It was finished. It was filed. It was the finding.

Lira read it that night in the common room, turning the page over once and reading it in the same careful silence she'd given Karis's handed-over notebooks weeks before, and said, finally, not to Cael but half to herself: *"Well. There it is, then."* Not resistance. Not quite welcome yet, either. The sound of a line moving, an inch at a time, the way lines actually moved when they moved for real reasons instead of declared ones. Brom read it standing, nodded once at the middle of it and once at the end, and handed it back with the verdict Cael suspected he'd reached weeks before anyone else: "Took her less time than I thought."

---

He spent the rest of the night alone with the harder reckoning, the one that had been circling since the moment the fragment notice printed itself into his hands on the training hall floor, and had only tightened its orbit through Quenna's question and the morning's misfires and the folded page now sitting with Lira.

Three people had spent today telling him, each in their own dialect, what yesterday had cost. Quenna's dialect was institutional: a record that would hold only as long as he never again did the thing in front of the wrong witnesses, and a question — *can you not do it* — that he could not answer. Karis's dialect was the strangest and the kindest: a cost she had audited in herself for two days and found transmuted into something she wanted more of, which was its own vertigo, because it meant the mechanism didn't only take — it bound, in both directions, in ways none of them had language for yet. And his own dialect was the oldest one he had, the notebook dialect, misfires dated and timed: the architecture itself, presenting an invoice written in half-second absences, for an action he had taken without ever seeing a price list.

He made himself write out what he actually knew, in columns, because columns disciplined a frightened mind better than prose did. Known: directed acquisition was possible, once, under conditions of sustained observation plus genuine stakes. Known: it carried a destabilization cost to the existing fragments, duration roughly a day, severity two misfires, this time. Known: the panel had seen the effect and not the mechanism, and the record was drafted, and the record would hold unless he handed someone a reason to reopen it. Unknown: whether he could refuse the reach if the conditions assembled themselves again without his consent — in a real fight, against a real enemy, in front of exactly the witnesses Quenna feared. Unknown: whether the cost scaled, and along what axis, and whether some future taking would ask a price the older fragments couldn't pay. Unknown, last and largest, written slowly: what the mechanism was *for*. A thing with a handle was a tool. Tools had purposes. Somebody's purposes. He looked at that line longer than any of the others, then drew a small box around it, which was his private notation for *not yet* — the notation he'd once used for the Tide-adjacent anomaly, and for the older word in Vell's archive, and for every door he'd found before he'd found its key.

Then he got out the Power Log and wrote the sentence that had been assembling all day, because plain was the only way he knew how to hold something this large.

*I need to understand what I'm actually doing before it gets someone killed.*

He looked at the sentence a long time. It was the truest thing he had written since the night after Karis's hypothesis, and it was not finished, and he knew it was not finished, and he sat with the pen until honesty caught up with precision. Then, because precision mattered more than comfort, he made himself finish it.

*Not me. I've already decided I can carry whatever this costs. The someone I'm afraid for is the three people who keep standing next to me while I figure out what "carry it" actually means.*

---

Brom found him there, past the hour when the residence wing's corridors went quiet, and said almost nothing for a long while — sat on the floor across from him, back against the wall, the way he used to sit in a market square that felt a lifetime distant now, saying nothing because nothing needed saying yet. It was a posture with a whole history in it, that sitting: the arbiter of interesting problems, arrived at his customary post, in no hurry at all. The candle worked through a knuckle's width of its length. Somewhere below, the building ticked and settled.

When he finally spoke, it wasn't about the misfires, or Quenna's question, or the page folded in Cael's pocket that he hadn't yet decided how to answer.

"Back in Ardenmere," Brom said, "I told you that someday you'd get to choose the word for what you are. Instead of it being chosen for you." He nodded once at the log, at the sentence still wet on the page. "You just started choosing."

Cael looked at what he'd written. It didn't feel like triumph. It felt like the first honest weight he'd ever put a real number on, and it felt, somehow, better for being honest than it would have felt to leave it vague and comfortable. Yesterday he had learned that he could reach. Today he had learned what reaching cost, and who the cost could land on, and that the learning itself — the slow, dated, timed, unglamorous accounting of it — was the only version of this power that he could live inside. Anyone could take. The choosing was in the ledger.

"It's a heavier word than I expected," he said.

"Most of the good ones are," Brom said, and didn't move, and stayed exactly where he was until the candle burned down far enough that neither of them needed to say anything else that night.

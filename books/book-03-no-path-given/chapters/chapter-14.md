# Chapter 14 — What It Costs

The record Quenna filed on the match was, Cael understood the first time he read it, a small masterpiece.

*Atypical adaptive response, consistent with previously documented irregular architecture. No safety violation noted. Assessment satisfied under demonstration provision.*

Every word of it was true. None of it said anything. He read it twice in her office the morning after, the same careful economy of language he'd watched her use on his enrollment paperwork weeks before — the provision's language is what protects you — and understood that she'd spent the night doing for the panel exactly what she'd once told him the system would eventually ask of him: being established fact, in writing, before anyone hostile came looking.

"That'll hold," she said, watching him read it. "Wray signed it without comment. The rotating seat didn't ask a single question, which tells you how little anyone in that room wanted to be the person who asked."

"And you?"

She sat down across from him, and for a moment the careful drafting fell away entirely.

"I don't need to know what happened in the third exchange," she said. "I have theories. I'm not going to write them down, and I'm not going to make you say them out loud, because the moment either of those things happens, this stops being something I can protect and starts being something on a record." She let that sit. "What I need to know — not for the file, for me — is whether you can *not* do it. In front of people who wish you harm. Because the version of you that reaches for someone's ability mid-exchange without deciding to first is a version that gets flagged the instant it happens somewhere I can't draft around."

He didn't have an answer. He wanted to say *yes*, because yes was the answer that let her keep sleeping and let him keep his enrollment and let the record stay clean. He said, instead, the true thing, because she'd earned the true thing and because lying to Quenna felt like a different category of mistake than lying to almost anyone else.

"I don't know yet."

She held his eyes a moment. "Find out before the Compact does," she said, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it followed him out of the office and sat with him for the rest of the day like a stone he hadn't agreed to carry.

---

Karis found him at the archive table that evening, and for the first time since she'd introduced herself there seven weeks earlier, she didn't have a notebook open.

"I need to tell you something," she said, "and I need you to let me finish before you say anything, because if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve, and I've been building up to this for two days."

He nodded.

"I designed that session as an experiment. I told you that going in — I want to be the first documented source, if it works the documentation matters, if it doesn't that matters more — and I meant every word of it when I said it. I've spent eight months treating you as the most important open question in the field I care about, and I was proud of that, and I still am." She was watching her own hands rather than him, which was new; Karis, in his experience, watched everything except her own hands. "What I didn't account for is what it would feel like to *be the data*. To have something of mine — a piece of the thing I trained twelve years to be able to do — sitting inside someone else now, permanently, because I consented to a session and the session worked exactly the way I'd hoped it would."

"Karis—"

"I said let me finish." She took a breath. "It doesn't distress me. That's the part I've spent two days sitting with, because I expected it to, and it didn't, and I don't fully trust a reaction I can't explain to myself yet. There's a version of this where I should feel violated, or diminished, or at minimum strange about it, and instead what I feel is—" She stopped. Started again. "I feel *included* in something. Which is not a scientific finding. It's not something I know how to write into a report. It's just true, and I've been carrying it alone for two days because I didn't know how to say it without it sounding like something it isn't."

She reached into her coat and handed him a single folded page. Not the two pages of data he'd expected — a single paragraph, in her small, exact hand, that he understood, reading it, she'd rewritten more than once.

*Session subject reports no distress at the outcome. This finding does not fit any framework I currently have for interpreting it, and I am choosing to record that honestly rather than force it into language that would make it sound more resolved than it is. What I can say is this: I do not feel studied by him. I have not, since the third exchange, felt like the experiment. I am not certain what that makes me instead, but I intend to find out, and I would like to find out from inside the circle rather than outside it, if that is something the four of you would consider allowing.*

Cael read it twice. He looked up, and found her watching him now, the notebook-open steadiness gone from her entirely, replaced by something that looked, on Karis, almost unbearably like nerve.

"You're asking to actually be one of us," he said. "Not the research arrangement. The other thing."

"I'm asking," she said, "because I don't know how else to ask it, and I've never had to ask before — at Ternhall, belonging was a ranking. I was simply *in*, because I was good enough to be in. This is different, and I don't have a method for it, and I hate not having a method, and I'm asking you anyway."

He thought of Lira, later that night, turning the page over once and reading it in the same careful silence she'd given Karis's handed-over notebooks weeks before, and saying, finally, not to Cael but half to herself: *"Well. There it is, then."* Not resistance. Not quite welcome yet, either. The sound of a line moving, an inch at a time, the way lines actually moved when they moved for real reasons instead of declared ones.

Cael didn't answer Karis that night. He told her, honestly, that the answer wasn't only his to give, and that he'd carry the page to the people whose answer it also was. She accepted this the way she accepted most things — with the patience of a researcher who understood that the most important findings were rarely the fast ones.

---

He spent the rest of the night alone with the harder reckoning, the one that had been circling since the moment the fragment notice printed itself into his hands on the training hall floor.

The directed acquisition had not been free. He'd known that in the abstract the instant it happened — some cost had to sit on the other side of doing, consciously, a thing his architecture had only ever done to him before — but knowing it in the abstract and cataloguing it in fine detail were different disciplines, and he'd spent the day doing the second one with the same cold precision he brought to everything that frightened him.

The Wind-adjacent framework, his oldest fragment, the one Lira had carried into him a year and a half and a whole life ago, had misfired twice in morning training. Small failures — a half-second's lag between intent and evasion, the kind of gap that would have cost him a bout against anyone faster than a training partner — and both times, when he reached for the framework afterward to check it, it had answered clean, as if nothing had happened. He'd written the misfires down anyway, dated, timed, with the same care he gave every entry that mattered. *Fragment behavior destabilized approximately eighteen hours post-directed-acquisition. Resolved by the following morning. Cause: unknown. Suspected: the interior action required to direct an acquisition draws on the same architecture that holds the others steady, and asking it to do something new costs something borrowed from what it already does.*

He didn't know if the hypothesis was right. He knew, with more certainty than he wanted, that he'd reached into himself mid-fight and done something no one — not Vell, not Quenna, not the four recorded [SHATTERED] instances the registry's brief cold entry had told him about a year and a half ago on the morning before his own Kindling — had ever documented doing on purpose, and he'd done it on instinct plus six weeks of study, without knowing what it would cost until the cost had already been spent.

The old fear surfaced, familiar as an old bruise: *this is how it goes wrong. Not from malice. From doing something real without understanding it first, and finding out the shape of the mistake only after it's already happened to someone.*

He got out the Power Log and wrote the arc statement plainly, because plain was the only way he knew how to hold something this large.

*I need to understand what I'm actually doing before it gets someone killed.*

He looked at the sentence a long time. And then, because precision mattered more than comfort, he made himself finish it honestly.

*Not me. I've already decided I can carry whatever this costs. The someone I'm afraid for is the three people who keep standing next to me while I figure out what "carry it" actually means.*

---

Brom found him there, past the hour when the residence wing's corridors went quiet, and said almost nothing for a long while — sat on the floor across from him, back against the wall, the way he used to sit in a market square that felt a lifetime distant now, saying nothing because nothing needed saying yet.

When he finally spoke, it wasn't about the misfires, or Quenna's question, or the page folded in Cael's pocket that he hadn't yet decided how to answer.

"Back in Ardenmere," Brom said, "I told you that someday you'd get to choose the word for what you are. Instead of it being chosen for you." He nodded once at the log, at the sentence still wet on the page. "You just started choosing."

Cael looked at what he'd written. It didn't feel like triumph. It felt like the first honest weight he'd ever put a real number on, and it felt, somehow, better for being honest than it would have felt to leave it vague and comfortable.

"It's a heavier word than I expected," he said.

"Most of the good ones are," Brom said, and didn't move, and stayed exactly where he was until the candle burned down far enough that neither of them needed to say anything else that night.

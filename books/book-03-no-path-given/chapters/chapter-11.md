# Chapter 11 — Controlled Conditions

The proposal came from Karis, on the third evening after the observation sessions had started producing more questions than either of them knew what to do with, delivered with the flat clarity she brought to everything that mattered to her.

"I want to offer myself as a source. Formally. Controlled conditions, my own declarations, demonstration intensity, and you try to take something on purpose." She said it the way she said most things — no preamble, no softening, the sentence arriving fully built. "If your architecture can be directed, this is the cleanest possible test. If it can't, we'll have learned that too, and it will still be worth knowing."

Cael sat with the offer longer than she seemed to expect him to.

It was not a small thing she was proposing, and he understood — from a year and a half of learning to weigh what people offered him against what it cost them — that she understood that too, even if her voice gave away nothing of the weighing. She was offering to be deliberately, repeatedly, studied *from the other direction*. Not the researcher watching the phenomenon. The phenomenon's raw material, handed over on a schedule, for as long as it took.

"Why would you agree to that."

"Because the alternative is waiting for it to happen to someone by accident again, the way it happened with Lira, with Feryn, with Brom, with Reydan." She did not flinch delivering the list, which told him she had done her homework past what the terms technically allowed her to know. "None of them consented to being a source in advance. I can. That's not nothing, Cael. That might be the only ethically clean version of this experiment anyone is ever going to be able to run."

"And if it works."

"Then we have proof, and I've contributed something real to the only question I actually care about." A pause, the first hesitation he'd seen from her since the archive. "And if it doesn't work, we've eliminated a variable, and I'd still rather know than not know. I don't function well around unresolved boundary conditions. You may have noticed."

He had noticed. He said yes, on terms both of them wrote down before the first session, in the same careful language Lira had insisted on back in the common room: Karis's consent stood for each session individually, renewable, revocable with a word; nothing about mechanism would be written in any record either of them showed anyone else; Lira and Brom would know the sessions were happening, in general shape, without needing every detail.

The first session was, by any honest accounting, uneventful.

---

They ran it in one of the smaller training rooms off the main hall, chosen for privacy rather than size — a bare rectangle with good light and nothing on the walls, the kind of room built to hold a demonstration rather than a fight. Karis stood at one end, ran her Ember Path declarations at the intensity she used for formal assessment, and Cael watched from the other, doing the thing he had never once, in a year and a half of fragments arriving unbidden, deliberately tried to do.

He reached for it.

He was not entirely sure what *reaching* meant, mechanically. He had spent a year and a half cataloguing what acquisition felt like after the fact — the sense of something settling into a space that had been empty, a texture he had learned to half-recognize in the seconds before a fragment notice arrived — but he had never once initiated it, and he discovered, standing in that bare room watching Karis's ignition points bloom and fade with perfect, repeatable precision, that wanting something to happen and knowing how to make it happen were not remotely the same skill.

Nothing happened.

He watched her run the same declaration eleven times, cataloguing the ignition timing down to what felt like a quarter-second of consistency, holding his attention on her with the same total focus he'd once held on Reydan's third exchange, on Feryn's shoulder, on every moment that had ever produced something. Nothing arrived. No texture. No pre-acquisition sense of a space opening. No notice.

"Nothing," he said, at the end of it, more to his own notebook than to her.

"A null result is data." Karis was already writing, unbothered, the tight economy of her handwriting the only sign she was working at all. "We now know demonstration-intensity declaration, sustained visual observation, and directed intent are not, on their own, sufficient conditions. That's not nothing. That's a boundary."

"It feels like nothing."

"It feels like nothing because you were hoping for something." She looked up at him with an expression that on anyone else he might have called sympathy, and on Karis registered as something closer to genuine scientific respect for the difficulty of the thing he'd just attempted. "Hope is not a research methodology. We run it again tomorrow."

They ran it again the next day. And the day after that. Karis, tireless in a way that Cael found first alien — nobody had ever brought this much undimmed enthusiasm to watching him fail at something — and then, slowly, across the better part of two weeks, steadying. There was a kind of relief, he came to understand, in a null result that came from someone who had already decided in advance that a null result would still be worth having. He had spent most of his life being studied by people for whom his failures confirmed something they'd already believed about him. Karis's failures confirmed nothing except that the boundary of the mechanism sat somewhere she hadn't found yet.

By the end of the second week, he had a stack of null sessions eight deep, and a growing, uncomfortable certainty that the thing he was trying to prove wasn't going to prove itself this way.

---

He worked the question the way he worked everything that mattered, which was on paper, at night, with the Power Log open on the desk and the fragment binder beside it, going back through every acquisition he had ever logged and asking, plainly, what they had in common.

Lira's Wind-adjacent had come during a real bout, back in Ardenmere, evasion patterns deployed against him at genuine speed, with genuine stakes — a sparring session that had mattered, that had cost something if he'd lost it badly enough. Feryn's Pressure-adjacent had come from a real fight, fourth exchange, a shoulder against his that had carried actual weight behind it. Brom's Iron-adjacent had come from full-intensity training, the kind where getting it wrong meant getting hurt. Reydan's Compression-adjacent had come from the climax of a circuit match with a scout in the crowd and everything riding on the outcome.

Four fragments. Four moments of genuine engagement. Zero moments of quiet observation from a safe distance, no matter how sustained, no matter how carefully cataloged.

He wrote the pattern out in full, in his careful hand, and then wrote the hypothesis underneath it, because writing a thing plainly was always the way he made himself actually believe it.

*Observation is necessary but not sufficient. Every fragment I have ever received came from genuine engagement — real stakes, real risk, something that mattered in the moment it happened. Watching Karis run drills at demonstration intensity, safely, with nothing on the line, has produced nothing in two weeks of trying. The architecture doesn't take what's shown to it. It takes what's used against me. Stakes are part of the mechanism, not incidental to it.*

He looked at the sentence for a long time. It was, he understood, either the missing piece of a year-and-a-half-old puzzle or a convenient story he was telling himself to explain a run of failed experiments. He didn't yet know which. He wrote beneath it, smaller: *Don't know if this is true yet. Will know when something with real stakes tests it.*

He didn't say the last part out loud to anyone, but he understood, closing the log that night, that some part of him was already waiting for the test to arrive.

---

It was late in the third week, in the last hour of a session that had produced nothing new, that Karis — thorough in the particular way that made her impossible to have half a conversation with — asked about the thing he had been half-hoping she wouldn't ask about and half-hoping she would.

"Your fragment log," she said, closing her notebook with the careful precision she gave everything. "Four confirmed. Is that the complete inventory, or is there something you haven't told me because it doesn't fit anywhere?"

He thought about deflecting. He didn't, because a year and a half of practice had taught him that the people worth trusting were the ones who could handle an honest *I don't know*, and he had already decided, somewhere in the archive on the first day, that Karis was one of them.

"There's an anomaly. A session with Brom, months ago now. A single reading, Tide-adjacent, that came through his pressure-read for less than a second and then never repeated. I've tried to reproduce it a dozen times since. Nothing."

"Tell me everything you remember about the conditions."

He told her. The sparring session, unremarkable by every measure that had ever produced a fragment before or since — no elevated stakes, no genuine danger, nothing that matched the pattern he'd just spent two weeks confirming. A moment of contact through Brom's pressure-read, and then, for less than a heartbeat, something that felt like water moving where there should have been none.

Karis was quiet for longer than he'd ever seen her quiet, turning the description over with the close attention she gave to a genuine problem rather than a confirmed hypothesis.

"It doesn't fit your acquisition pattern," she said finally, and there was real frustration in it — not at him, but at the shape of the thing itself. "No Tide practitioner present. No sustained exposure. No stakes, by your own account. Every rule we've just spent three weeks confirming, and this breaks all of them at once. It shouldn't exist."

"It exists."

"I know it exists. That's the problem." She said it without heat, the frustration of someone whose model had just been handed a data point it had no room for. "Either the pattern we've built isn't the whole pattern, or there's a second mechanism operating alongside the first one that we haven't isolated yet, or — and I don't love this option — the sample size on your anomaly is one, and one data point can't build a theory around itself. Something about the model is incomplete, and I genuinely can't see the missing piece from here."

"So what do we do with it."

"We keep it." She opened her notebook again, flipped to a page near the back that he hadn't seen before, and made a small mark beside the single line she'd written about session nine — a symbol he didn't recognize, precise, deliberate, clearly not decorative. "I have exactly one category in this entire notebook that gets this mark. It means: real, unexplained, keep. It's not a failure state. It's not a hypothesis waiting to be confirmed. It's a fact I don't understand yet, filed honestly as a fact I don't understand yet, instead of forced into an explanation that would make me feel better and teach me nothing true."

He looked at the mark for a long moment. It was, he thought, the most honest thing anyone had ever done with the anomaly — not explaining it away, not dismissing it, not pretending three weeks of careful experimentation had somehow accounted for it. Just marking it as real and leaving it alone.

"How many other things have that mark," he asked.

"None, until tonight." She closed the notebook. "You should feel free to take that as either flattering or alarming. I haven't decided which it is myself."

---

He wrote about it that night, in the Power Log, in the same careful hand he used for everything that had earned the weight of being written down properly.

*Three weeks of controlled sessions. Zero directed acquisitions. The pattern holds: every fragment I've ever received came from genuine stakes, real engagement, something that mattered when it happened. Karis's demonstration intensity, however sustained, doesn't produce the conditions the architecture requires.*

*The Tide-adjacent anomaly remains exactly as unexplained as it was the night it happened. Karis marked it "real, unexplained, keep" — the only entry in her notebook with that classification. I find I trust that mark more than I would trust an explanation. An explanation this early would almost certainly be wrong. The mark, at least, is honest.*

*Hypothesis stands, untested against real stakes: observation is necessary but not sufficient. Stakes are part of the mechanism. I don't know yet if that's true. I'll know when something tests it properly.*

He closed the log and sat with the binder a while longer, the four fragment entries and the one anomaly, all of it sitting exactly where careful, patient, controlled experimentation had left it: real, mostly explained, one piece stubbornly, honestly unresolved.

He had learned, across a year and a half of cataloguing things he didn't understand, that the unresolved pieces were rarely the ones that stayed unresolved forever. He had also learned, more slowly, that rushing them rarely helped. He put the notebook away, and let the anomaly sit exactly where Karis had marked it: real, unexplained, and — for now, deliberately, disciplined about the difference between patience and avoidance — kept.

# Chapter 11 — Controlled Conditions

The proposal came from Karis, on the third evening after the observation sessions had started producing more questions than either of them knew what to do with, delivered with the flat clarity she brought to everything that mattered to her.

They had been packing up — chalk dust on her sleeve, the high window gone dark, the water clock in the corridor marking an hour later than either of them had noticed arriving — and she had stopped with her notebook half into its case, and set it back down on the table, and squared it against the edge, which he had learned was what she did with her hands when she was about to commit to something her mind had already finished committing to.

"I want to offer myself as a source. Formally. Controlled conditions, my own declarations, demonstration intensity, and you try to take something on purpose." She said it the way she said most things — no preamble, no softening, the sentence arriving fully built. "If your architecture can be directed, this is the cleanest possible test. If it can't, we'll have learned that too, and it will still be worth knowing."

Cael sat with the offer longer than she seemed to expect him to.

It was not a small thing she was proposing, and he understood — from a year and a half of learning to weigh what people offered him against what it cost them — that she understood that too, even if her voice gave away nothing of the weighing. She was offering to be deliberately, repeatedly, studied *from the other direction*. Not the researcher watching the phenomenon. The phenomenon's raw material, handed over on a schedule, for as long as it took. Every practitioner he had ever taken from had been taken from without warning, without consent, without either of them knowing the taking was possible. She was volunteering to stand at the front of that line knowing exactly what stood at the end of it, because she had reconstructed the end of it herself, from the outside, with a notebook and two months of timing runs.

"Why would you agree to that."

"Because the alternative is waiting for it to happen to someone by accident again, the way it happened with Lira, with Feryn, with Brom, with Reydan." She did not flinch delivering the list, which told him she had done her homework past what the terms technically allowed her to know. "None of them consented to being a source in advance. I can. That's not nothing, Cael. That might be the only ethically clean version of this experiment anyone is ever going to be able to run."

"And if it works."

"Then we have proof, and I've contributed something real to the only question I actually care about." A pause, the first hesitation he'd seen from her since the archive. "And if it doesn't work, we've eliminated a variable, and I'd still rather know than not know. I don't function well around unresolved boundary conditions. You may have noticed."

He had noticed. He noticed most things, and Karis's relationship to open questions was not subtle: she circled them the way other people circled debts.

He didn't answer that night. He took it to the wall outside the residence wing instead, because the covenant said *tell her what you know when you know it*, and what he knew now was that the question from the common room had stopped being hypothetical. Lira listened to the whole shape of it with her arms folded and the night coming down cold around them.

"She offered," Lira said, when he'd finished. "You didn't ask."

"She offered. I haven't said yes."

"Say yes." She caught his look and held it. "I told you to go figure out whether it's real. This is what figuring out looks like when someone hands you the honest way to do it. She knows what she's volunteering for better than any of us did — that's the entire difference, and it's not a small one." A pause. "Terms on paper, though. Again. Every time. That part doesn't ever get skipped because you've all gotten comfortable."

Brom's contribution, delivered the next morning over breakfast with the gravity of a formal ruling: "She studied the thing for two months and then walked toward it. That's either the best judgment at this academy or the worst, and either way I want to see the data." Which was, from Brom, a benediction.

So he said yes, on terms both of them wrote down before the first session, in the same careful language Lira had insisted on back in the common room: Karis's consent stood for each session individually, renewable, revocable with a word; nothing about mechanism would be written in any record either of them showed anyone else; Lira and Brom would know the sessions were happening, in general shape, without needing every detail. Karis read the terms twice, signed, and added one line of her own at the bottom in her tight economical hand — *sessions end immediately on any sign of involuntary effect on the source* — which Cael read three times, because it was the first rule anyone had ever written to protect someone from him, and he had complicated feelings about how glad he was that it existed.

The first session was, by any honest accounting, uneventful.

---

They ran it in one of the smaller training rooms off the main hall, chosen for privacy rather than size — a bare rectangle with good light and nothing on the walls, a room built to hold a demonstration rather than a fight. Karis stood at one end, ran her Ember Path declarations at the intensity she used for formal assessment, and Cael watched from the other, doing the thing he had never once, in a year and a half of fragments arriving unbidden, deliberately tried to do.

Her declarations were worth watching for their own sake, which did not help. He had seen Ember Path work before — twice in the circuit, at a distance, output-heavy practitioners who treated the discipline as a delivery system for heat — but Karis's variant was a different animal entirely. She built ignition points the way a jeweler set stones: a bloom of light no bigger than a coin, held at a fixed coordinate in the air, sustained, precise, extinguished on a count. Then two at once. Then a channel between them, a seam of shimmering distortion that connected point to point like a drawn line, held steady while the air above it wavered and the smell of hot stone drifted across the room. Nothing wasted. Nothing decorative. Every commitment small, complete, and immaculately timed. His observation notebook filled with the architecture of it almost against his will — ignition latency, sustain cost, the little preparatory stillness in her off hand that preceded every bloom by the same interval, every time.

And underneath the watching, he reached for it.

He was not entirely sure what *reaching* meant, mechanically. He had spent a year and a half documenting what acquisition felt like after the fact — the sense of something settling into a space that had been empty, a texture he had learned to half-recognize in the seconds before a fragment notice arrived — but he had never once initiated it, and he discovered, standing in that bare room watching Karis's ignition points bloom and fade with perfect, repeatable precision, that wanting something to happen and knowing how to make it happen were not remotely the same skill. He tried attention, total and undivided, the compound gaze at full aperture. He tried something like invitation — holding the empty space where a fifth fragment would sit, if fragments sat anywhere, and presenting it, absurdly, like a held-out hand. He tried want, plain and unadorned, and was mildly ashamed of how easy that one was to produce. He tried, near the end, simply mimicking the interior stillness that had preceded the arrivals he could remember best, the way you'd rebuild a sneeze from memory.

Nothing happened.

He watched her run the same declaration eleven times, mapping the ignition timing down to what felt like a quarter-second of consistency, holding his attention on her with the same total focus he'd once held on Reydan's third exchange, on Feryn's shoulder, on every moment that had ever produced something. Nothing arrived. No texture. No pre-acquisition sense of a space opening. No notice. Just a boy standing in an empty room, watching a girl light small precise fires, wanting.

"Nothing," he said, at the end of it, more to his own notebook than to her.

"A null result is data." Karis was already writing, unbothered, the tight economy of her handwriting the only sign she was working at all. "We now know demonstration-intensity declaration, sustained visual observation, and directed intent are not, on their own, sufficient conditions. That's not nothing. That's a boundary."

"It feels like nothing."

"It feels like nothing because you were hoping for something." She looked up at him with an expression that on anyone else he might have called sympathy, and on Karis registered as something closer to genuine scientific respect for the difficulty of the thing he'd just attempted. "Hope is not a research methodology. We run it again tomorrow."

---

They ran it again the next day. And the day after that.

They varied everything a controlled framework allowed them to vary, because Karis was constitutionally incapable of running the same failure twice without changing a parameter. Distance: he watched from across the room, from three paces, from close enough to feel the heat of the channels on his face, close enough that Lira, told about it afterward in general shape, gave him a long look and said nothing. Duration: short sessions, long ones, one marathon afternoon that left Karis pale with sustain-cost and Cael with nine pages of architecture notes and nothing else. Declaration type: single points, paired points, channels, the beginnings of the lattice-work she was famous for at assessment, each variant catalogued, each one producing in Cael a fractionally deeper understanding of Ember architecture and not one flicker of anything more. He learned her tells to a depth he had never reached on any practitioner — the off-hand stillness, the half-breath before a sustain, the way fatigue showed first in her extinguish timing, a lag of instants that she couldn't feel and he couldn't miss. By the second week he could predict her ignition to a quarter-second, could have drawn her declaration tree from memory, could close his eyes and reconstruct a session from its sounds alone.

None of it mattered. The architecture, whatever it was, sat behind his sternum like a door with no handle on his side.

Karis documented every null with undiminished enthusiasm, which Cael found first alien — nobody had ever brought this much undimmed energy to watching him fail at something — and then, slowly, across the better part of two weeks, steadying. "A boundary condition is a finding," she said, more than once, entering another nothing into her ledger with the satisfaction of a surveyor driving a stake. There was a species of relief, he came to understand, in a null result recorded by someone who had already decided in advance that a null result would still be worth having. He had spent most of his life being studied by people for whom his failures confirmed something they'd already believed about him — assessors, examiners, the Compact's whole patient apparatus of foregone conclusions. Karis's failures confirmed nothing except that the boundary of the mechanism sat somewhere she hadn't found yet. Under her instruments, even his nothing was worth writing down accurately. He had not known, before Greyvane, that being measured could feel like that.

By the end of the second week, he had a stack of null sessions eight deep, and a growing, uncomfortable certainty that the thing he was trying to prove wasn't going to prove itself this way.

"We're missing a condition," Karis said, at the end of the eighth null, flexing the ache out of her writing hand. "The pattern's real — I'd stake the whole notebook on the exposure correlation. But exposure at this intensity isn't completing whatever the circuit is. Something else closes it, and it isn't present in this room."

"Any candidates?"

"Several. None I can produce under controlled conditions without violating either the terms or basic ethics." She said it evenly, but she was looking at him when she said it, and he understood that she had already seen the shape of the missing condition, the same way she saw everything — early, clearly, and from the outside — and was declining to name it before the data did.

He named it himself, three nights later, alone.

---

He worked the question the way he worked everything that mattered, which was on paper, at night, with the Power Log open on the desk and the fragment binder beside it, going back through every acquisition he had ever logged and asking, plainly, what they had in common.

Lira's Wind-adjacent had come during a real bout, back in Ardenmere — and he made himself reconstruct it honestly, not the summary but the moment: her evasion patterns deployed against him at genuine speed, the yard's packed dirt under his feet, the hot embarrassment of being outclassed and the hotter need not to be, a sparring session that had mattered, that had cost something every time he lost it badly. Feryn's Pressure-adjacent had come from a real fight, fourth exchange, a shoulder against his that had carried actual weight behind it, in a yard where nobody was pretending. Brom's Iron-adjacent had come from full-intensity training, the sessions where Brom had stopped being careful with him because Cael had asked him to stop, where getting a read wrong meant taking a hit that echoed in his bones for a day. Reydan's Compression-adjacent had come from the climax of a circuit match with a scout in the crowd and everything he'd built riding on the outcome — had come, he could admit now, at very nearly the last moment it could have arrived and still saved him.

Four fragments. Four moments of genuine engagement. Zero moments of quiet observation from a safe distance, no matter how sustained, no matter how carefully documented. The audience had never once been paid. Only the participant.

He wrote the pattern out in full, in his careful hand, and then wrote the hypothesis underneath it, because writing a thing plainly was always the way he made himself actually believe it.

*Observation is necessary but not sufficient. Every fragment I have ever received came from genuine engagement — real stakes, real risk, something that mattered in the moment it happened. Watching Karis run drills at demonstration intensity, safely, with nothing on the line, has produced nothing in two weeks of trying. The architecture doesn't take what's shown to it. It takes what's used against me. Stakes are part of the mechanism, not incidental to it.*

He looked at the sentence for a long time. It had the feel of a true thing — it snapped into the year-and-a-half-old puzzle with the small click he'd learned to trust — and he distrusted the feel precisely because he wanted it so much. It was either the missing piece or a convenient story he was telling himself to explain a run of failed experiments, and the difference between those two was not something a desk could settle. He wrote beneath it, smaller: *Don't know if this is true yet. Will know when something with real stakes tests it.*

He didn't say the last part out loud to anyone, but he understood, closing the log that night, that some part of him was already waiting for the test to arrive. He understood, too — filing it where he filed the things about himself he didn't like but declined to lie about — that the waiting had a color of anticipation in it, and that a wiser or more frightened boy would have been only afraid.

---

It was late in the third week, in the last hour of a session that had produced nothing new, that Karis — thorough in the way that made her impossible to have half a conversation with — asked about the thing he had been half-hoping she wouldn't ask about and half-hoping she would.

The session had wound down early; her sustain reserves were low from a morning assessment, and they'd spent the last while at the table instead, cross-referencing her boundary ledger against his session notes, an exercise that had become its own quiet ritual — two notebooks open, two hands writing, the water clock dripping in the corridor. She finished a page, and closed the notebook with the deliberate exactness she gave everything, and looked at him.

"Your fragment log," she said. "Four confirmed. Is that the complete inventory, or is there something you haven't told me because it doesn't fit anywhere?"

He thought about deflecting. The question sat right at the edge of the terms — she wasn't asking for mechanism, wasn't asking for sources, was asking only whether the inventory she'd been allowed to see was shaped like the whole truth — and a version of him could have answered it narrowly and been technically honest. He didn't, because a year and a half of practice had taught him that the people worth trusting were the ones who could handle an honest *I don't know*, and he had already decided, somewhere in the archive on the first day, that Karis was one of them.

"There's an anomaly. A session with Brom, months ago now. A single reading, Tide-adjacent, that came through his pressure-read for less than a second and then never repeated. I've tried to reproduce it a dozen times since. Nothing."

Karis went very still, the stillness of a pointer marking a bird. "Tell me everything you remember about the conditions."

He told her. All of it, or all of it that existed to tell, which was the frustrating part — the sparring session, unremarkable by every measure that had ever produced a fragment before or since. No elevated stakes, no genuine danger, nothing that matched the pattern he'd just spent two weeks confirming at such cost. An ordinary working afternoon, Brom's pressure-read engaged the way it had been engaged a hundred times before and since. A moment of contact, and then, for less than a heartbeat, something that felt like water moving where there should have been none — a sensation with no business existing, gone before he could even turn his attention fully toward it, leaving nothing behind. No notice. No fragment. No repetition, ever, in a dozen deliberate attempts under conditions reconstructed as faithfully as his notes allowed.

She took him through it twice more, from different ends — start from the sensation and work backward, start from the morning of that day and work forward — hunting for a variable he might have stopped seeing through familiarity. Weather. Meals. Injuries. What Brom had been working on that week. Whether anyone else had been present, or nearby, or recently departed. Whether anything, anything at all, had been different. He answered everything, and the answers built exactly the picture his notes had always built: a day distinguished by nothing except the one indistinguishable second at the middle of it.

Karis was quiet for longer than he'd ever seen her quiet, turning the description over with the close attention she gave to a genuine problem rather than a confirmed hypothesis.

"It doesn't fit your acquisition pattern," she said finally, and there was real frustration in it — not at him, but at the shape of the thing itself. "No Tide practitioner present. No sustained exposure. No stakes, by your own account. Every rule we've just spent three weeks confirming, and this breaks all of them at once. It shouldn't exist."

"It exists."

"I know it exists. That's the problem." She said it without heat, the frustration of someone whose model had just been handed a data point it had no room for. "Either the pattern we've built isn't the whole pattern, or there's a second mechanism operating alongside the first one that we haven't isolated yet, or — and I don't love this option — the sample size on your anomaly is one, and one data point can't build a theory around itself. Something about the model is incomplete, and I genuinely can't see the missing piece from here."

"So what do we do with it."

"We keep it." She opened her notebook again, flipped to a page near the back that he hadn't seen before, and made a small mark beside the single line she'd written about session nine — a symbol he didn't recognize, precise, deliberate, clearly not decorative. "I have exactly one category in this entire notebook that gets this mark. It means: real, unexplained, keep. It's not a failure state. It's not a hypothesis waiting to be confirmed. It's a fact I don't understand yet, filed honestly as a fact I don't understand yet, instead of forced into an explanation that would make me feel better and teach me nothing true."

He looked at the mark for a long moment. It was, he thought, the most honest thing anyone had ever done with the anomaly — not explaining it away, not dismissing it, not pretending three weeks of careful experimentation had somehow accounted for it. Just marking it as real and leaving it alone. He had carried session nine for months the way you carried a stone whose weight nobody else would credit. Watching her enter it into the most disciplined notebook he'd ever seen, under the most exacting category that notebook contained, was — he searched for the honest word and found it — validating. Not answered. Witnessed. There was a difference, and he was old enough now to know which one was actually on offer, and to take it.

"How many other things have that mark," he asked.

"None, until tonight." She closed the notebook. "You should feel free to take that as either flattering or alarming. I haven't decided which it is myself."

---

He wrote about it that night, in the Power Log, giving it the full deliberate entry he reserved for the things that had earned one.

*Three weeks of controlled sessions. Zero directed acquisitions. The pattern holds: every fragment I've ever received came from genuine stakes, real engagement, something that mattered when it happened. Karis's demonstration intensity, however sustained, doesn't produce the conditions the architecture requires.*

*The Tide-adjacent anomaly remains exactly as unexplained as it was the night it happened. Karis marked it "real, unexplained, keep" — the only entry in her notebook with that classification. I find I trust that mark more than I would trust an explanation. An explanation this early would almost certainly be wrong. The mark, at least, is honest.*

*Hypothesis stands, untested against real stakes: observation is necessary but not sufficient. Stakes are part of the mechanism. I don't know yet if that's true. I'll know when something tests it properly.*

He closed the log and sat with the binder a while longer, the four fragment entries and the one anomaly, all of it sitting exactly where careful, patient, controlled experimentation had left it: real, mostly explained, one piece stubbornly, honestly unresolved. Down the corridor, someone's lamp still burned — Karis's, almost certainly, her ledger of boundaries growing by one honest page. Three weeks of nothing, and yet the nothing had a shape now, and the shape pointed somewhere, and the somewhere had stakes in it.

He had learned, across a year and a half of writing down things he didn't understand, that the unresolved pieces were rarely the ones that stayed unresolved forever. He had also learned, more slowly, that rushing them rarely helped. He put the notebook away, and let the anomaly sit exactly where Karis had marked it: real, unexplained, and — for now, deliberately, disciplined about the difference between patience and avoidance — kept.

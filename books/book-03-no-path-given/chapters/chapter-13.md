# Chapter 13 — Directed

The morning of the match, Cael woke before the bell and lay still for a while in the grey, taking inventory the way he did before anything that mattered — hands, breath, the four settled weights of the fragments where they lived, each one answering when he turned his attention to it, the way banked coals answered a poker. Wind-adjacent, recovered from the week's drills. Iron-adjacent, steady. Pressure-adjacent, steady. Compression-adjacent, steady. And the fifth space, the one that wasn't a space, the one he had spent three weeks learning the exact shape of by failing to fill it. He did not reach for it. Karis's coaching had been unambiguous on the point, and he had decided, somewhere in the night, to obey it with the totality he usually reserved for Vell's rules: from the moment he left this bed, there was no experiment. There was a match against the best formally trained opponent he had ever faced, and he was, by every sober accounting, likely to lose it, and his entire job was to make losing it as expensive as possible.

He dressed, wrapped the forearm guards, and went down to a breakfast at which nobody discussed anything at all. The residence hall around them was louder than usual — the match had been the academy's only topic for two days — and their table sat inside the noise like a stone in a stream, parting it. Karis ate at a separate table, alone, by her own arrangement; she had informed them the night before, with great formality, that fraternizing with the opposition on match morning was methodologically unsound, and Lira had thrown a roll at her, and she had caught it without looking and eaten it, and that had been, all four of them understood, the actual goodbye.

The walk to the training hall took four minutes. Lira spent them on his left, saying nothing. Brom spent them on his right, saying nothing. At the hall doors, where the noise of the gathering gallery came through the wood like surf, Lira finally delivered the whole of her tactical counsel: "Feet first. Everything else second." Brom considered the door, then Cael, then produced his own: "She's proud of the geometry. Be somewhere the geometry isn't finished." Then the two of them went in ahead of him, to take the rail, and left him in the doorway with his kit and his breath and the enormous ordinary fact that whatever happened in the next hour, there were two people at that rail who had walked him to the door.

The training hall had never felt smaller.

That was the first thing Cael catalogued, standing at his mark with the panel's table set up along the north wall — Quenna, Wray, and a rotating faculty seat filled today by an instructor Cael only half-recognized from the lecture wing — and Karis across from him at her own mark, already settled into the low, coiled stillness she carried into everything she took seriously.

The hall itself had been reset for formal conditions: the training posts racked away, the floor swept and chalked with the match boundary, the panel's ledgers open and squared. Beyond the boundary, the gallery rail was as full as he had ever seen it. Quenna had predicted a crowd and the crowd had arrived early — students three deep at the rail, a scatter of faculty who had no procedural reason to be there, Edran at the near corner with his arms folded and his attention already running at bout-speed. And at the rail's far end, exactly where he'd known they would be, Lira and Brom: Lira leaning forward with both forearms on the rail in her reading-the-floor posture, Brom planted beside her like load-bearing masonry. Neither of them waved. All three of them had said everything there was to say at breakfast, which had consisted, in total, of Lira putting an extra roll on his plate and Brom saying "eat it," and that had been the correct amount.

Quenna read the conditions into the record in her dry procedural voice — supervised training match, three-exchange structure, faculty discretion to halt, logged as a demonstration assessment under the observer provision — and the words went past Cael like weather on glass, because he was watching Karis, and Karis was watching him, and the two of them were conducting the real pre-match conversation, which had no words in it at all. Six weeks of study stood on both sides of the floor. Her notebooks knew his recovery intervals. His notebook knew her ignition latency. Somewhere beneath the formal staging, running silent, was the other thing — the experiment neither of them would name in front of a panel, the rule waiting for its test — and her last coaching sat on top of all of it like a seal: *don't fight the experiment. Fight me.*

He let the experiment go. He was surprised how much like exhaling it felt.

He had watched her run drills at demonstration intensity for six weeks. He had studied her tell architecture until he could have drawn it from memory, clean lines, precise ignition points, a lattice instead of a blaze. He had told himself, walking in, that six weeks of watching had to count for something.

It counted for less than he expected, and more than he understood, and both of those facts were true at once by the end of the first exchange.

"Begin," Quenna said, and dropped her hand.

---

**First exchange.**

Karis moved the way her research notes moved — economically, with no wasted motion, everything in service of a structure she had already decided on before the first step landed. She did not charge him. She *positioned* him, and she began doing it from the very first beat, which was the first thing the six weeks had not prepared him for: there was no probing phase, no polite opening survey. She came out of her mark already mid-argument.

The opening points went down exactly where Brom's warning said they would — in the quiet part, cheap and dim, laid behind her own footwork like a woman dropping coins she intended to collect later. Cael saw them. That was the bitter marvel of it: he saw every single one, named them as they bloomed, *point, point, point,* the observation running clean and useless, because seeing a promise being made and being fast enough to stop its keeping were different currencies, and she was spending faster than he could counter. She cut the floor into channels with small, precise bursts of heat that closed his angles one at a time, the way a good editor closed loopholes in an argument, until the only ground left to him was the ground she had chosen to leave.

He tried the Wind-adjacent framework first, because it was his best tool and because six weeks of watching had taught him nothing yet about what her real speed looked like at genuine stakes. It was not enough. She read his evasion the way she read everything — documented it, adjusted to it, closed the channel he'd used before he could use it twice. Her first connection lit while he was still mid-redirect, a seam of shimmer snapping across the floor a half-step behind his heel, close enough that the heat of it climbed his calf like a hand, and the gallery made a sound, low and involuntary, the noise a crowd makes when it stops being sure it's watching a demonstration. He redirected again, and the redirect worked, and cost him a fraction more than the last one, and bought him a fraction less. He spent the framework faster than he had ever spent it against anyone, burning through angle after angle just to stay ahead of a lattice that seemed to already know where he was going to be — because it did know; that was what the lattice was *for*; every escape he executed had been priced into the geometry before he chose it.

She won the exchange going away. Not narrowly. Going away.

The halt was called on position — his back to the boundary chalk, three lit channels between him and any floor worth having — and he stood at the edge of her fourth channel, breathing harder than the exchange should have earned, and understood the first real finding of the match, the one none of them had modeled from her demonstration sessions: Karis at documentation intensity was a careful, controlled researcher, testing hypotheses one variable at a time. Karis at genuine stakes was something else. Something faster, hungrier, more willing to spend everything on a single correct read. The extinguish-lag he'd charted so patiently was gone. The tidy interval between commitments was gone. Six weeks of quarter-second measurements, and the fighter across the chalk had a wartime economy the peacetime books had never shown him. He had spent six weeks studying a version of her that did not, it turned out, actually exist under pressure.

At the rail, Lira's expression had not changed, which was how he knew it was bad. Wray was writing steadily. The half-recognized instructor from the lecture wing had stopped writing entirely.

"Reset," Quenna said, and they reset.

---

He used the reset the way Vell had taught him to use resets — inventory first, feelings later. Wind-adjacent framework: spent hard, answering slow, a tool he could draw maybe twice more at full speed before the speed stopped being full. Floor position: recoverable. Read on the opponent: obsolete, being rebuilt live. The version of Karis in front of him laid points earlier, connected them sooner, and spent sustain-cost like a woman with reserves she had never shown at assessment — which meant the documentation intensity he'd studied had been, all along, a fraction of her, the way his conservative panel demonstrations were a fraction of him. They had been studying each other's public drafts. The match was the first honest edition either of them had seen.

*Out-early her,* Brom's voice said, from somewhere in the resident part of him. He rolled his shoulders, and stopped trying to win the geometry, and started planning to survive it instead.

---

**Second exchange.**

He adjusted, because adjusting was the only thing he knew how to do when a first read failed this badly. He brought the Iron-adjacent surface read online, feeling for the shape of ignition points as they formed rather than waiting to see them declare — and the world changed texture. The read gave him her points as pressure before they were light, a bloom of density in the air a half-second ahead of the flame, which was not much, but which was, against a lattice this precise, the difference between reacting and merely surviving. For the first time in the match, he moved *before* her floor moved, sliding out of a channel's line while it was still a promise, and he saw her register it — a single recalibrating flicker across her face, the researcher noting an anomaly at combat speed — and lay the next three points in a pattern he hadn't charted, because she was adjusting too, because of course she was adjusting too.

He brought the Compression-adjacent framework in behind the read, not to evade the channels this time but to survive crossing one directly, taking the heat rather than dodging it, because dodging had already proven too slow. He picked the channel himself — the shortest seam, the one her geometry least expected to be challenged, because no sane fighter crossed fire to stand closer to the person making it — hardened everything the Compression fragment knew how to harden, and went through.

The crossing was two steps and it rewrote his understanding of what her smallest channel carried. Heat went up his left forearm like a debt being collected all at once, through guard-cloth, into skin, a burn he would be feeling for days — and he was through it, inside the grid's middle distance where none of her assemblies wanted him, and he closed to contact range for the first time in the match, one honest reach that made her give ground for the first time, that forced the lattice to convulse around its own maker and re-form three paces back.

It worked, in the narrow sense that he was still standing and she had yielded a step. It cost more than it earned. The forearm told him so, in the flattest possible terms, exactly how much margin he had left to work with.

Not much. His mobility was degrading. The Wind-adjacent framework, spent hard in the first exchange, was answering slower now, a fraction of a second behind where it needed to be, and the Compression crossing had drawn down reserves he could not draw down twice. He ran the arithmetic the way he ran everything, even mid-fight, even with his own skin protesting the decision, and the arithmetic came back plain: by any honest read, he was losing. Not dramatically. Just steadily, the way a structural failure was steady right up until the moment it wasn't. The exchange halt found him inside her middle distance with nothing left to do there, and the panel logged it closer than the first — closer was not close.

Across the floor, Karis reset her stance, and for just a moment — half a breath, no more — he saw something in her face that wasn't triumph. It was closer to concentration. She was not gloating. She was working, the same way he was working, rebuilding him in her model the same way he was rebuilding her in his, two notebooks fighting at the speed of hands. And underneath the concentration, buried where probably only he could read it, something that in a less disciplined face would have been hope — because she knew the arithmetic too, knew he was running out of everything the panel could see, and she, alone of everyone at that rail and that table, knew what the rule said about moments when everything ran out.

The fact of that, small and strange, was the last clear thought he had before the third exchange started.

---

**Third exchange.**

She came to finish it, and the finishing was beautiful, if you were in a position to appreciate it, which he was not, because he was inside it.

He made her work for the early ground, because making her work was all he had left and he spent it honestly. The surface read bought him three escapes that should have been impossible, sliding him out of forming channels on pressure alone, feet moving on Lira's drilled arithmetic — *pick faster, correctness later* — and for perhaps a quarter of the exchange he was somewhere the geometry wasn't finished, exactly as instructed, a fly declining to land anywhere long enough to be mapped. The gallery had gone entirely silent by then, the deep bout-silence he knew from the circuit, a hundred people breathing quietly around a single moving problem. But the framework was answering slower with every call on it, each redirect arriving a shaved fraction later than the last, and Karis was doing what Karis did with data: compensating. She stopped aiming where he was. She started aiming where his lateness would put him.

The points went down in pairs now, faster than the surface read could price them, her whole first-exchange patience converted into closing speed. She walled his left with a channel he could not afford to cross a second time, folded his right with two more, let him spend a slow Wind-adjacent redirect escaping a connection that turned out to be bait, and used the redirect's cost to lay the true assembly behind him, and by the time his framework had recovered enough to answer again there was nothing left for it to answer with. He was inside her lattice when it happened. Out of angles — the third channel closing on his left, the fourth already forming ahead of him, nowhere honest left to go that didn't cost him something he couldn't afford to spend twice. Genuine stakes, the sort the controlled sessions had never once managed to manufacture no matter how carefully Karis structured them. His own survival, for the length of that one exchange, actually on the line, in a way three weeks of null-result sessions in a quiet room with a consenting research subject had never come close to replicating.

Time did the thing it did in the deep of fights, going wide and slow, every fraction of a second suddenly furnished with room to think in.

He had spent six weeks learning her. Not documenting her — Karis did that, meticulously, in notebooks he had read every page of. He had spent six weeks *watching* her, the way he watched everything, the compound gaze running underneath every session whether either of them had named it that or not: the shape of her ignition points, the rhythm of her channel-cutting, the precise interval between one commitment and the next, the little preparatory stillness in her off hand that he had seen ten thousand times and could feel, right now, through the Iron-adjacent read, gathering half a breath ahead of the fourth channel's completion. He knew her architecture the way he knew his own notebook. He had simply never had a reason, until this exact half-second, to reach for it.

So he stopped running.

It was not a decision in the way decisions usually felt to him — weighed, considered, written down afterward in careful handwriting. It was closer to the thing that had happened with Lira's Wind in a training yard a year and a half ago, except this time he was awake for it in a way he had never quite been awake for it before, and the being-awake was the entire difference, the way being awake was the entire difference between falling and diving. Every condition the null results had mapped was finally present at once, and some assembled part of him — the part that had run eight useless sessions in a bare room, the part that had written *stakes are part of the mechanism* in lamplight and underlined nothing because he hadn't dared believe it yet — recognized the completed circuit in the same instant it closed. He stopped trying to escape her architecture and reached, instead, directly into the shape of it — the ignition point she was in the process of forming, the heat gathering at a single precise location the way her whole lattice always gathered heat, controlled, structured, *hers* — and he took it.

Consciously. Intentionally. Directed, the word arriving in his own head at the exact moment the thing itself happened, as if some part of him had been waiting three weeks of null results just to finally have a sentence for what this was supposed to feel like.

What it felt like — and he would spend pages on this later and never fully land it — was not theft, and not borrowing, and not any of the words he had spent a year and a half auditioning. It felt like comprehension with weight behind it. Like the difference between reading a word and saying it. Six weeks of her architecture, resident in him as observation, turned over all at once into something that had *grip* — and along the turning, for one instant, a channel between them that ran in a direction no fight had ever run, through which came heat, and structure, and the precise jeweler's discipline of a girl who set fires like stones, partial, crude, torn at the edges because he had no idea yet how to take cleanly.

It was not clean. It was not, by any measure Karis's own research notes would have used, a controlled acquisition — a single small ignition-point instead of the whole architecture, deployed in the same breath he took it because there was no time to do anything else with it. He turned, closed the gap she had spent the whole match keeping him out of, and answered her fourth channel with a spark of her own fire, small and rough and absolutely unmistakable, a coin of light igniting at a point *he* chose, in a lattice that had priced every possibility except its own language spoken back to it — breaking the timing of her structure from the inside in a way no evasion, no surface read, no careful survival tactic had managed in two full exchanges.

The fourth channel guttered. The assembly behind him, suddenly missing its keystone beat, fell out of sequence and died back into sixteen separate small fires with nothing to say to each other.

Karis stopped fighting.

She stood in the wreck of her own fourth channel — heat failing where it should have held, the whole careful structure of the exchange broken by something that had no right to exist inside it — and Cael watched the person who had spent eight months chasing a rumor across a continent, who had transferred out of a better school to stand in this exact room, understand *exactly* what had just happened to her a full two seconds before anyone else in the hall did. He watched the understanding arrive in stages, each one legible because he had spent six weeks learning to read her: the fighter's shock, first, at a lattice broken; then the researcher's audit, the lightning-fast rundown of alternative explanations being checked and discarded; and then the third thing, the thing underneath both, rising through her face like heat through a channel.

She started laughing.

Not politely. Not the careful, contained amusement she brought to everything else. She stood there, breathless, flushed from the exchange, one hand braced on her knee, laughing the way a person laughed when something they had spent their whole life hoping was true turned out to actually be true, right in front of them, close enough to touch — laughing with her whole body, in front of a faculty panel and a packed rail, entirely careless of both for the first time in her documented existence.

The gallery did not know what it was watching. Cael registered that distantly, through the ringing in his own head: the rail full of faces caught between alarm and awe, students who had just seen the observer conjure fire out of a category that had no fire in it, Edran gone very still and very pale with the look of a man whose models had failed a second time in one season. At the far end of the rail, Lira had both hands locked on the wood. Brom had not moved at all, but Brom's stillness had grades, and this was the deepest one.

"Match halted," Quenna said, already on her feet, already moving toward the floor with an expression Cael would spend a long time afterward trying to properly name — something with too many layers in it for one face, procedure and calculation and, underneath, a thing that might have been fear wearing the uniform of calm. "Interruption. Called a draw."

Nobody in the room seemed to care about the ruling in the slightest.

---

Cael's hands were shaking by the time the notice arrived.

He was sitting on the training hall floor by then — he did not entirely remember sitting down — with the burn on his forearm announcing itself in long slow pulses and the hall's noise washing around him like weather from another county: the panel conferring in low urgent voices at the table, Wray's pen scratching, the gallery's murmur climbing toward a boil and being ordered down by somebody official. He felt it start somewhere behind his sternum, the settling arrival he had felt four times before in his life and had believed, all four times, was weather — and had just enough presence of mind left to get the binder open before his hands stopped being reliable for anything more precise than holding a page still.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Ember-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
Note: ignition-point component. Single-channel, contact-adjacent range.
Acquisition: directed.
```

*Acquisition: directed.*

He read it four times, sitting on the training hall floor with the panel still talking somewhere behind him and Karis still catching her breath a few feet away, because four fragments across a year and a half had never once carried that field, and he had genuinely, honestly, not known until this exact moment that the field existed at all. Every notice he had ever received had ended at the note line. This one had a fifth line, appended in the same flat administrative voice as all the rest, as though the system had been holding the field in reserve the entire time — as though somewhere in whatever ledger the notices came from, there had always been a column for this, sitting empty through Lira and Feryn and Brom and Reydan, waiting for the first entry that qualified. Not a name for the fragment. A name for the *act* — the system itself, in its flat administrative language, confirming that this one, unlike every other, had been chosen.

He understood, sitting there, reading the word a fifth time because four had not been enough for it to fully land: what he did was not random. It never had been. Every fragment before this one had simply arrived on a schedule he'd mistaken for weather, because he had never once, in a year and a half, tried to reach for the handle. The system had known the difference all along. The system had a *field* for the difference. He had spent a year and a half being told what he was by institutions that had no word for him at all, and the only ledger that had ever described him accurately had been waiting, patient as an archivist, for him to do the one thing that would let it use its most important word.

He had a handle now.

Karis dropped down beside him, still catching her breath, still radiating the charged electric stillness of a person whose life's central question had just answered itself in front of a faculty panel. Her lattice-hand was trembling very slightly — sustain-cost, or its opposite, or something no ledger had a column for yet. She didn't say anything for a moment. Neither did he. There didn't seem to be a sentence built for exactly this.

"Directed," she said finally, reading the notice over his shoulder, her voice doing something complicated between awe and pure researcher's satisfaction. "It has a *field* for that."

"It has a field for that," Cael agreed.

At the boundary chalk, held there by decorum and nothing else, Lira and Brom stood waiting. Lira caught his eye across the floor and asked the whole question with her eyebrows, and he answered it by holding up the binder, just slightly, just for her — and watched her read the gesture, and understood it, and press one hand flat against her own sternum for a moment, which was not a signal they had ever agreed on and did not need to be. Brom looked from the binder to Karis's wrecked lattice, still smoking faintly in patches across the floor, and nodded once, the slow arbiter's nod, the one that meant a question he had been holding open for a year had just been ruled on.

Across the hall, the panel's conference was breaking up; Quenna was coming toward them, ledger in hand, official language already assembling behind her eyes, and beyond her the gallery was emptying its noise out into the yard where it would become, by supper, six contradictory stories and one accurate one that nobody would believe. There would be questions. There would be records, drafted carefully; there would be a conversation about what the panel had seen and what it would say it had seen; there would be, he knew with a cold clear edge under all the shaking, consequences he could not size yet, because a thing seen by a hundred people could never be fully unseen. All of it was coming, and all of it was real, and none of it could be permitted to arrive before this.

He got the Power Log out right there on the training hall floor, because he understood, with the strange lucidity that only ever showed up in the minutes right after something changed everything, that this was not an entry that could wait until evening. His hand was still not entirely steady. He wrote it anyway, careful, deliberate, the same hand he used for everything that mattered.

*Fifth fragment. First one I chose. Everything before this was the architecture acting. This one was me.*

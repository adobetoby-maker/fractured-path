# Chapter 5 — Karis

Karis Dellenmoor had built her entire academic life around a single conviction, and she had never once needed to defend it out loud, because at Ternhall nobody had cared enough to argue.

*Anomalies are not noise. They are the data that matters.*

She kept two notebooks, and had since she was eleven. The first was the one everyone at Ternhall expected — bout logs, declaration-timing charts, the ordinary architecture of a top-ranked student's advancement. The second was the one nobody at Ternhall had ever asked to see, because nobody had thought to ask what an Iron-tier Rank 3 practitioner did with her evenings besides train. The second notebook was full of failures. Not her own — the standard model's. Every place a Path's textbook behavior had refused to hold: an Ember practitioner whose ignition curve bent the wrong way under fatigue, a Shield Path that degraded faster than the formula predicted against sustained pressure, small stubborn discrepancies that every instructor she'd ever asked had waved off as measurement error. Karis did not believe in measurement error. She believed in models that weren't finished yet, and in herself as the kind of person who might finish one.

She had been eight months into a slow, private catalogue of exactly these discrepancies when a Ternhall lecturer — Master Cordwell, a dry man who liked showing off in front of second-years — had mentioned, mostly as color, a rumor drifting up from the southern circuits. Something out of Ardenmere. An unranked fighter, a boy nobody could classify, beating practitioners who by every formal measure should have handled him without difficulty. Cordwell had told it as a joke about provincial exaggeration. Karis had written it down as data before he'd finished the sentence.

It took her three weeks to find the first real thread and four months to stop finding contradictions in it. The rumor was inconsistent in every detail that didn't matter — his name spelled two different ways, his age reported anywhere from thirteen to sixteen, the exact bouts he'd supposedly won varying depending on which circuit gossip you traced it to. It was consistent in exactly the details that did. An unclassified fighter. Multiple documented wins against ranked opponents, across what looked, from the fragments she could piece together, like more than one distinct combat signature. No Path name anyone would attach to him. No explanation anyone offered that survived being asked a second question.

She had read every scrap of it twice, built a working file, and reached the only conclusion her method permitted: whatever this was, it did not fit the standard model. It did not fit any model she had access to. And a boy who broke every predictive framework she owned was not a rumor to be dismissed. He was the most important open question in Path theory she had ever come within reach of, and he was, improbably, in the same country she was, at an academy three tiers beneath Ternhall's prestige, admitting students the good schools had already given up on.

She filed the transfer request the week his name appeared, quietly, unremarked, on a minor-tier academy's enrollment record she had flagged an alert for months earlier.

Her advisor had called it career suicide, in those words, in his office, with the weary patience of a man trying to talk a promising student out of throwing something away. Her parents had been gentler and more worried. Karis had listened to all of it and signed the transfer paperwork anyway, because none of them understood what she understood: she was not leaving a good school for a worse one. She was leaving a settled question for the only unsettled one that had ever mattered to her, and she intended to be standing next to it when it resolved.

---

Cael found her before she found him, technically, though she would later insist the sequencing didn't matter — she'd known she was going to seek him out from the moment her transfer was approved, and finding herself instead in the archive on the same afternoon he happened to be there simply meant the universe had saved her a day's search.

He was at Prynn's long reading table with a stack of academy bulletins he had no real reason to be reading except that reading things was what he did with unstructured attention, and he noticed her before she said anything — the particular quality of someone crossing a room with intent rather than wandering it, a directness that most Greyvane students, still adjusting to him, hadn't yet worked up to.

"You're Cael."

It wasn't a question, which told him something before she said anything else. "I am."

"Karis Dellenmoor. Ember Path, Iron-tier Rank Three, formerly of Ternhall." She said the ranking the way people said things they were used to opening doors — not boastfully, exactly, but with the flat efficiency of someone establishing credentials because credentials were the fastest available shorthand for who she was. "I transferred here because of you."

Cael set the bulletin down. He had, in the two weeks since his first demonstration assessment, absorbed enough of the rumor ecosystem circulating about him to know roughly what shape this conversation usually took — curiosity dressed as small talk, or suspicion dressed as politeness, or occasionally, from a handful of students, something closer to fear that never quite said its own name. This did not sound like any of those.

"Because of me."

"I've spent eight months reconstructing what I could of your circuit record from secondhand accounts, none of which agreed with each other on anything except the outline. I want to study what you are. Formally. With your consent, on terms you set, and you see everything I write about you before anyone else does." She said all of this without a single pause for effect, the way someone recites a thing they've rehearsed until the rehearsal has worn all the drama off it. "I'm not asking you to explain yourself right now. I'm asking whether you'd consider letting me try to understand it properly, on the record, as a formal research relationship rather than whatever the rest of this academy is doing, which is mostly staring."

Cael looked at her for a long moment.

He had spent two years being observed. The Compact had observed him — quietly, then less quietly, filing reports on a boy who hadn't asked to be a data point in anyone's registry. Coss had observed him, patient and procedural, the way a warden observes a problem he intends to solve eventually. Even the people who loved him had, in their ways, watched him closely enough to notice when something didn't fit — Hesk cataloguing an irregular assessment, Lira clocking a pattern in a sparring match before either of them had words for it. Being watched was, if he was honest with the notebook the way he tried to always be honest with the notebook, the single most consistent experience of his entire life.

Nobody had ever asked.

Not the Arbiter who'd flagged him. Not the assessor who'd filed the paperwork. Not Coss, for all his careful gentleness, who had walked Cael out of Denvash's certification office as a matter decided rather than a matter discussed. Every eye that had ever turned toward him with the particular weight of trying-to-understand had turned there because an institution required it, not because anyone had stopped to ask whether he'd consent to being looked at that closely.

"Why should I trust you?" he said.

He watched her consider the question seriously — not performing consideration, actually doing it, the small pause of someone checking her own answer against her own standards before she offered it to someone else.

"You shouldn't," she said. "Not yet. Trust is a finding, not a premise. I don't expect you to extend it to me because I asked nicely. I expect to have to earn it, the way I'd earn any other conclusion — by being right about small things first, consistently, until being right about small things becomes evidence you can use." She held his gaze without flinching from it. "I'm not going to promise you I'll never get anything wrong about you. I'm going to promise you that when I do, I'll show you the mistake instead of hiding it, because a hidden mistake is worse for both of us than an admitted one."

Cael sat with that for a while.

It was, he understood, the single best possible answer she could have given him — not because it was reassuring, though it was, in its own dry way, but because it was honest about the actual shape of the problem. She hadn't offered him certainty. She'd offered him a method, and an explicit account of how the method could fail, and an explicit promise about what she'd do when it did. It was, in its structure, not unlike the way Quenna had talked to him about the observer category on his first day — the truth, delivered plainly, with the hard parts left in rather than sanded off.

"I haven't said yes," he said.

"I know. I'm not expecting an answer today." She glanced, briefly, at the bulletin stack he'd abandoned, and then back at him, with something that might have been the beginning of a smile if she'd let it get that far. "I did want you to know the question exists. Most people who look at you the way this whole academy has been looking at you for two weeks never actually ask it. I thought that ought to count for something, even before you decide."

She left him with that — no pressure to answer, no lingering to see how he took it, just the plain fact of the offer set down between them and the space to consider it that she'd promised him she'd give.

---

He walked to the training yard afterward without any particular destination, the way he still did when he needed to move faster than his own thoughts, and turned the conversation over from every angle he could find.

There was a version of caution available to him here, and it would have been the reasonable version. Two years of institutional attention had taught him that people who wanted to study him rarely wanted what they said they wanted, and that the space between a stated intention and its eventual use was exactly where he tended to get hurt. Karis was, by her own account, going to write things down about him — capabilities, patterns, whatever she could observe of an architecture he barely understood himself. Written records traveled. He knew that better than almost anyone his age had reason to.

But there was another fact sitting next to the caution, and he made himself hold both of them at once rather than let the louder one win by default: she had asked. Every institutional eye that had ever turned toward him had turned there as a matter of process, a thing done *to* him under the cover of procedure that never once paused to check whether he'd have said yes if given the choice. Karis had walked up to him in an archive and made the choice explicit, offered him the veto before she'd taken a single note, and told him upfront exactly how she planned to fail him if she did.

He didn't know yet whether that made her trustworthy. He knew it made her different from everyone else who'd ever wanted to know what he was, and difference, in his experience, was worth taking seriously before deciding what to do with it.

That evening he found Lira on the low wall outside the residence wing, and told her the whole exchange nearly word for word, the way he'd gotten in the habit of doing with things that mattered before he'd finished deciding what he thought about them.

"What do you think," he said, when he'd finished.

Lira considered it for longer than he expected. "I think," she said finally, "that everyone who's ever wanted something from you has wanted it without asking, and she asked. That's not nothing. It's also not everything." She looked at him sideways. "You don't have to decide tonight. She said that herself, apparently."

"She did."

"Then don't." Lira bumped his shoulder with hers, the old easy gesture from a hundred low walls in two other cities. "Sit with it. You're good at sitting with things until you actually know what you think about them, instead of just what you're supposed to think."

He wrote it in the log that night, in the careful hand he kept for the entries that mattered, sitting on the edge of his bed with the lamp turned low.

*Ember Path, Iron 3, walked away from a better school to look at me. First person to treat what I am as a question instead of a verdict.*

He looked at the sentence a while, the way he looked at most sentences that weren't quite finished yet, and then added the line underneath it, smaller, the one that was actually true.

*Deciding what that's worth.*

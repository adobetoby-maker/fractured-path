# Chapter 5 — Karis

Karis Dellenmoor had built her entire academic life around a single conviction, and she had never once needed to defend it out loud, because at Ternhall nobody had cared enough to argue.

*Anomalies are not noise. They are the data that matters.*

She kept two notebooks, and had since she was eleven. The first was the one everyone at Ternhall expected — bout logs, declaration-timing charts, the ordinary architecture of a top-ranked student's advancement. The second was the one nobody at Ternhall had ever asked to see, because nobody had thought to ask what an Iron-tier Rank 3 practitioner did with her evenings besides train. The second notebook was full of failures. Not her own — the standard model's. Every place a Path's textbook behavior had refused to hold: an Ember practitioner whose ignition curve bent the wrong way under fatigue, a Shield Path that degraded faster than the formula predicted against sustained pressure, small stubborn discrepancies that every instructor she'd ever asked had waved off as measurement error. Karis did not believe in measurement error. She believed in models that weren't finished yet, and in herself as someone who might finish one.

The first notebook had made her Ternhall's best student. She understood, with the unsentimental clarity she applied to everything including herself, that the second one was the reason she trained as hard as she did on the first. Rank was not the point. Rank was *access* — to better instructors, better libraries, better opponents whose Path behavior was worth charting, and eventually, if she kept the trajectory, to the archives and assessorial records where the discrepancies she collected might resolve into something with structure. She had worked this out at thirteen, the way other students worked out which instructors graded leniently: the system rewarded excellence with proximity to its own machinery, and its machinery was the thing she wanted to look at. So she was excellent. It had never once occurred to her to be excellent for its own sake, and the handful of classmates who'd sensed this about her had found it faintly unnerving, the way one is unnerved by a person who runs every race and does not care about ribbons.

Her Ember work was, by every formal measure, exemplary — control-dominant, precise past her tier, ignition points placed like punctuation. Her instructors called it discipline. It was actually curiosity wearing discipline's uniform: she had made her own Path the first entry in the second notebook, and every drill was also an experiment, every training session a paired trial with herself as both subjects. She knew her own tells better than her examiners did. She had documented them. It struck her as an obvious thing to do, and it had never stopped striking her as strange that no one else seemed to do it.

She had been eight months into a slow, private catalogue of exactly these discrepancies when a Ternhall lecturer — Master Marlowe, a dry man who liked showing off in front of second-years — had mentioned, mostly as color, a rumor drifting up from the southern circuits. Something out of Ardenmere. An unranked fighter, a boy nobody could classify, beating practitioners who by every formal measure should have handled him without difficulty. Marlowe had told it as a joke about provincial exaggeration. Karis had written it down as data before he'd finished the sentence.

It took her three weeks to find the first real thread and four months to stop finding contradictions in it. She worked the way she always worked: sources ranked by distance from the event, claims cross-indexed, every assertion tagged with who had said it and what they'd have gained by saying it. She wrote to a cousin who traded through Ardenmere twice a season and got back three paragraphs of secondhand tavern accounting. She paid a records-copying service an amount she declined to enter in either notebook for transcriptions of the circuit's public bout ledgers, which arrived incomplete, misordered, and more valuable than everything else combined — because ledgers didn't exaggerate. Ledgers just disagreed with reality in ways that couldn't be explained, which was her whole field of interest.

The rumor was inconsistent in every detail that didn't matter — his name spelled two different ways, his age reported anywhere from thirteen to sixteen, the exact bouts he'd supposedly won varying depending on which circuit gossip you traced it to. It was consistent in exactly the details that did. An unclassified fighter. Multiple documented wins against ranked opponents, across what looked, from the fragments she could piece together, like more than one distinct combat signature. No Path name anyone would attach to him. No explanation anyone offered that survived being asked a second question. She built a page she titled, with the restraint she considered a methodological obligation, *Candidate explanations*, and spent a winter killing its entries one by one. Concealed dual-Path lineage: didn't survive the bout ledgers, the signatures were too far apart. Fraudulent record-keeping: didn't survive the number of independent witnesses. Exceptional single-Path versatility: didn't survive her own reconstruction of the Feryn bout, which she had rebuilt from four partial accounts into a timing chart that sat on her desk for a week like an insult. Nothing survived. That was the finding. In eight months of work, the only hypothesis she could not kill was the one she had been trained never to write down: *the model is missing a category.*

She had read every scrap of it twice, built a working file, and reached the only conclusion her method permitted: whatever this was, it did not fit the standard model. It did not fit any model she had access to. And a boy who broke every predictive framework she owned was not a rumor to be dismissed. He was the most important open question in Path theory she had ever come within reach of, and he was, improbably, in the same country she was, at an academy three tiers beneath Ternhall's prestige, admitting students the good schools had already given up on.

She filed the transfer request the week his name appeared, quietly, unremarked, on a minor-tier academy's enrollment record she had flagged an alert for months earlier.

Her advisor had called it career suicide, in those words, in his office, with the weary patience of a man trying to talk a promising student out of throwing something away. He had laid out the arithmetic as if arithmetic were the obstacle: Ternhall's placement record against Greyvane's, the doors an Iron-tier Rank 3 from a first-rank institution walked through against the doors that same ranking opened from a minor-tier academy nobody could place on a map. "You are trading a career for a curiosity," he'd said, and Karis had spent a moment genuinely admiring the sentence — it was compact, it was quotable, and it was wrong in a way he had no instrument for measuring. She had tried, once, to show him the second notebook. He'd leafed through four pages of it with polite incomprehension and handed it back with advice about focus. That had been the moment, she understood later, that the decision actually got made; everything after was paperwork.

Her parents had been gentler and more worried, and harder, because their worry was about her rather than her prospects, and had no arithmetic she could refute. Her mother had asked one question — "What if he's just a boy, and the mystery is bad record-keeping?" — and Karis had given the honest answer, which was that then she would have spent a year establishing that, documented it properly, and transferred back with the best-verified negative result of her career. Her mother had looked at her for a long moment and said, "You've already decided," which was true, and had been true, if Karis was rigorous about it, since the winter the last candidate explanation died.

She had listened to all of it and signed the transfer paperwork anyway, because none of them understood what she understood: she was not leaving a good school for a worse one. She was leaving a settled question for the only unsettled one that had ever mattered to her, and she intended to be standing next to it when it resolved.

Greyvane, when she arrived, was smaller than its correspondence had implied and better organized than its reputation had. She catalogued the campus in an afternoon, submitted her transfer records to a clerk who blinked twice at the Ternhall seal and once, longer, at her rank, and was assigned a room, a schedule, and a training-floor slot with an efficiency she noted approvingly in the margin of her day-log. She did not seek Cael out that first week. She watched the rumor ecosystem instead — it told her things about how the academy held him that no interview would have — and she made herself finish unpacking, register for lectures, and demonstrate for her new instructors like an ordinary transfer, because she wanted the record to show, when anyone later asked, that Karis Dellenmoor had arrived as a student and not as a stalking question. The distinction would matter. She had a researcher's faith that distinctions, kept carefully enough, always eventually mattered.

---

Cael found her before she found him, technically, though she would later insist the sequencing didn't matter — she'd known she was going to seek him out from the moment her transfer was approved, and finding herself instead in the archive on the same afternoon he happened to be there simply meant the universe had saved her a day's search.

He was at Prynn's long reading table with a stack of academy bulletins he had no real reason to be reading except that reading things was what he did with unstructured attention, and he noticed her before she said anything — the unmistakable carriage of someone crossing a room with intent rather than wandering it, a directness that most Greyvane students, still adjusting to him, hadn't yet worked up to. Prynn, at her desk by the door, had marked the trajectory too; Cael caught the archivist's eyes tracking the newcomer across her territory with the alertness of a border official watching someone walk straight past the checkpoint, and filed the observation for whatever it would later be worth.

She was perhaps a year older than him, dark-haired, dressed with the functional neatness of someone who had decided once, years ago, what she wore and had not spent a further thought on it since. She stopped at the correct conversational distance — precisely the correct distance, he noticed, neither the crowding of the curious nor the buffer people left around him when the rumors got to them first — and stood there with her hands empty and visible, which he only registered as deliberate much later, when he understood how much of her was deliberate.

"You're Cael."

It wasn't a question, which told him something before she said anything else. "I am."

"Karis Dellenmoor. Ember Path, Iron-tier Rank Three, formerly of Ternhall." She said the ranking the way people said things they were used to opening doors — not boastfully, exactly, but with the flat efficiency of someone establishing credentials because credentials were the fastest available shorthand for who she was. "I transferred here because of you."

Cael set the bulletin down. He had, in the two weeks since his first demonstration assessment, absorbed enough of the rumor ecosystem circulating about him to know roughly what shape this conversation usually took — curiosity dressed as small talk, or suspicion dressed as politeness, or occasionally, from a handful of students, something closer to fear that never quite said its own name. This did not sound like any of those.

"Because of me."

"I've spent eight months reconstructing what I could of your circuit record from secondhand accounts, none of which agreed with each other on anything except the outline. I want to study what you are. Formally. With your consent, on terms you set, and you see everything I write about you before anyone else does." She said all of this without a single pause for effect, the way someone recites a thing they've rehearsed until the rehearsal has worn all the drama off it. "I'm not asking you to explain yourself right now. I'm asking whether you'd consider letting me try to understand it properly, on the record, as a formal research relationship rather than whatever the rest of this academy is doing, which is mostly staring."

Cael looked at her for a long moment.

He had spent a year and a half being observed. The Compact had observed him — quietly, then less quietly, filing reports on a boy who hadn't asked to be a data point in anyone's registry. Coss had observed him, patient and procedural, the way a warden observes a problem he intends to solve eventually. Even the people who loved him had, in their ways, watched him closely enough to notice when something didn't fit — Hesk cataloguing an irregular assessment, Lira clocking a pattern in a sparring match before either of them had words for it. Being watched was, if he was honest with the notebook the way he tried to always be honest with the notebook, the single most consistent experience of his entire life.

Nobody had ever asked.

Not the Arbiter who'd flagged him. Not the assessor who'd filed the paperwork. Not Coss, for all his careful gentleness, who had held the file on him as a matter decided rather than a matter discussed since the morning of his Kindling. Every eye that had ever turned toward him with the weight of trying-to-understand had turned there because an institution required it, not because anyone had stopped to ask whether he'd consent to being looked at that closely.

"Why should I trust you?" he said.

He watched her consider the question seriously — not performing consideration, actually doing it, the small pause of someone checking her own answer against her own standards before she offered it to someone else.

"You shouldn't," she said. "Not yet. Trust is a finding, not a premise. I don't expect you to extend it to me because I asked nicely. I expect to have to earn it, the way I'd earn any other conclusion — by being right about small things first, consistently, until being right about small things becomes evidence you can use." She held his gaze without flinching from it. "I'm not going to promise you I'll never get anything wrong about you. I'm going to promise you that when I do, I'll show you the mistake instead of hiding it, because a hidden mistake is worse for both of us than an admitted one."

Cael sat with that for a while.

It was, he understood, the single best possible answer she could have given him — not because it was reassuring, though it was, in its own dry way, but because it was honest about the actual shape of the problem. She hadn't offered him certainty. She'd offered him a method, and an explicit account of how the method could fail, and an explicit promise about what she'd do when it did. It was, in its structure, not unlike the way Quenna had talked to him about the observer category on his first day — the truth, delivered plainly, with the hard parts left in rather than sanded off.

"I haven't said yes," he said.

"I know. I'm not expecting an answer today."

"What would it look like?" he asked, because the shape of a thing mattered more to him than its label, and she had offered a label. "Practically. If I said yes. What happens on an ordinary day of being studied by you?"

"Less than you'd think." She answered at once, which told him she'd already designed it. "Observation sessions, scheduled, at your convenience — I watch, I take notes, I don't touch, I don't test. Questions afterward, which you can decline one at a time or all at once. No questions about anything you haven't chosen to show. I write up what I observe; you read it before it exists anywhere but my hand. If a single page ever goes anywhere you haven't approved, you'll have the proof in your own files, because you'll have copies of everything." A beat. "And you can end it. Any day, with a word, no reason owed. That has to be structural, not polite — a right you hold, not a courtesy I extend. It's not generosity. Consent you can't withdraw isn't consent, and data gathered without consent is contaminated. I have no use for contaminated data."

It was the first time anyone had ever described protecting him as a matter of experimental hygiene, and he was surprised to find he liked it better than kindness. Kindness could waver. Method was load-bearing.

She glanced, briefly, at the bulletin stack he'd abandoned, and then back at him, with something that might have been the beginning of a smile if she'd let it get that far. "I did want you to know the question exists. Most people who look at you the way this whole academy has been looking at you for two weeks never actually ask it. I thought that ought to count for something, even before you decide."

She left him with that — no pressure to answer, no lingering to see how he took it, just the plain fact of the offer set down between them and the space to consider it that she'd promised him she'd give.

---

He walked to the training yard afterward with no destination in mind, the way he still did when he needed to move faster than his own thoughts, and turned the conversation over from every angle he could find.

There was a version of caution available to him here, and it would have been the reasonable version. Two years of institutional attention had taught him that people who wanted to study him rarely wanted what they said they wanted, and that the space between a stated intention and its eventual use was exactly where he tended to get hurt. Karis was, by her own account, going to write things down about him — capabilities, patterns, whatever she could observe of an architecture he barely understood himself. Written records traveled. He knew that better than almost anyone his age had reason to.

But there was another fact sitting next to the caution, and he made himself hold both of them at once rather than let the louder one win by default: she had asked. Every institutional eye that had ever turned toward him had turned there as a matter of process, a thing done *to* him under the cover of procedure that never once paused to check whether he'd have said yes if given the choice. Karis had walked up to him in an archive and made the choice explicit, offered him the veto before she'd taken a single note, and told him upfront exactly how she planned to fail him if she did.

He didn't know yet whether that made her trustworthy. He knew it made her different from everyone else who'd ever wanted to know what he was, and difference, in his experience, was worth taking seriously before deciding what to do with it.

So he did the thing he did with everything he needed to understand before committing to: he watched her.

She had a training-floor slot three mornings a week, posted on the roster like anyone else's, and an observer barred from open bouts had every legitimate reason to be standing at the rail during open hours. He gave her two full sessions of the same attention he'd given the Glass Path student and the Blade pair, and what he saw went into the notebook under a fresh heading, because it deserved one. Her Ember work was unlike any fire he'd seen on the circuit — no broad releases, no theatrical heat, almost nothing that read as *attack* in the grammar he was used to. She worked in points. Small, exact ignitions, placed and held and released on timings so regular he could have set the training hall's bells by them, each one a commitment the size of a coin where most Ember practitioners he'd watched spent commitments the size of doors. Control-dominant, he wrote, and then, watching longer: *not control as restraint — control as vocabulary. She has more words than anyone I've charted. She just refuses to shout any of them.*

The second session, she saw him. Of course she saw him; he hadn't hidden, and she was, by her own account, a person who noticed things for a living. She registered his presence at the rail with one glance between drills, and then — this was the part he wrote down twice — she did nothing differently. No sharpening for the audience, the way the Glass Path student had sharpened. No angling her best work toward his sightline. She finished the session exactly as she'd run the last one, packed her kit, and left with a nod in his direction that acknowledged everything and performed nothing.

*She knows being watched changes people,* he wrote that night. *She's trained it out of herself. Which means she decided, at some point, that clean data mattered more than looking good — and then did the work.* He looked at the entry, aware it was the most favorable thing he'd written about anyone since Brom's first fight, and made himself add the balancing line, because balance was the discipline: *Or she performs non-performance. Two more sessions.*

The two more sessions said it wasn't a performance. Nobody held a register that steady for four mornings for the benefit of one boy at a rail.

That evening he found Lira on the low wall outside the residence wing, and told her the whole exchange nearly word for word, the way he'd gotten in the habit of doing with things that mattered before he'd finished deciding what he thought about them.

"What do you think," he said, when he'd finished.

Lira considered it for longer than he expected. "I think," she said finally, "that everyone who's ever wanted something from you has wanted it without asking, and she asked. That's not nothing. It's also not everything." She looked at him sideways. "You don't have to decide tonight. She said that herself, apparently."

"She did."

"Then don't." Lira drew one knee up onto the wall and wrapped her arms around it, settling in the way she did when a conversation had turned out to be longer than the evening had promised. "I'll tell you the part that snags for me, since you're going to hear it from me eventually anyway. It's not her. From everything you've said, she's more honest about what she wants than anybody who's ever come at you. It's the category. *People who write things down about you.* Every bad thing that's ever happened to you started as something somebody wrote down." She said it without heat, staring out at the dark yard. "That's not an argument. It's a scar talking. I know the difference. I'm just telling you the scar's there, so when I make faces about this later, you'll know which part of me is making them."

"Noted," Cael said, and meant the word in the full weight his notebook had given it.

"And Cael." She waited until he looked at her. "If you do this — do it the way she offered it. Terms. Written. Real ones. Not because she'll cheat. Because the next person who wants to study you won't ask, and when that day comes, you'll want to be able to point at a piece of paper that shows what asking looks like."

He sat with that longer than anything else said that day. Trust Lira to find, in a single evening, the version of the question that made it about every day after this one.

"Sit with it," she said again, and bumped his shoulder with hers, the old easy gesture from a hundred walls in two other cities. "You're good at sitting with things until you actually know what you think about them, instead of just what you're supposed to think."

Brom's contribution arrived unprompted, at breakfast the next morning, delivered between mouthfuls with the timing of a man who had been thinking all night and saw no reason to announce the fact. "The Ternhall transfer. The one the second-years can't stop explaining to each other." He didn't look up from his plate. "She watched my floor session two days ago. Took notes the whole hour. Afterward she asked me one question about my force-redirect — where I'd learned to delay the second commitment." Now he looked up. "Nobody's ever asked me that. Wray hasn't asked me that. It's the right question. It took me two years to figure out it was even a question." He returned to his food, and added, in the tone of a man entering a fact into a shared ledger: "Whatever you're deciding about her, put that in the scales."

Cael put it in the scales.

He wrote it in the log that night, in the careful hand he kept for the entries that mattered, sitting on the edge of his bed with the lamp turned low.

*Ember Path, Iron 3, walked away from a better school to look at me. First person to treat what I am as a question instead of a verdict.*

He looked at the sentence a while, the way he looked at most sentences that weren't quite finished yet. A verdict, he thought, was a thing done to you — arrived, sealed, survivable only by appeal. A question was a thing you could participate in. He had been a verdict since the morning of his Kindling: [SHATTERED], four instances, file closed. Every person since who had surprised him into trusting them — Hesk, Lira, Vell, Brom, Quenna in her cold-ledger way — had done some version of the same thing, which was to treat the verdict as unproven. Karis had gone one step further than any of them. She'd treated it as *unasked*.

That was the thing he kept circling, lying there with the lamp low. Not whether she was safe. Nobody was safe; safety wasn't a property people had, it was a property terms had, which was probably why Lira had said the word twice. The thing he kept circling was that for eight months, somewhere three tiers of prestige away, a person he'd never met had looked at the wreckage of his record and concluded — against her advisor, against her parents, against the entire pricing system of her world — that he was worth more, not less, for refusing to fit. He didn't know yet what to do with being valued for exactly the thing he'd been discarded for. It was going to take more than one evening.

He added the line underneath the entry, smaller, the one that was actually true.

*Deciding what that's worth.*

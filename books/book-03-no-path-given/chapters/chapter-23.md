# Chapter 23 — The Record Is Public

The hall had emptied by the time Coss found him.

Not quickly — a hearing gallery this size took the better part of an hour to fully clear, students and faculty and the handful of credentialed observers all filing out in the loose, easy disorder of people who had just watched something they would spend the rest of the season talking about. Cael had stayed near the respondent's table longer than he needed to, partly because Lira and Brom and Karis had wanted a few minutes with him before the common room swallowed the rest of the evening, and partly because some part of him had known, without being told, that this conversation was still owed before the day could actually end.

Coss approached alone, his aide and his document case both absent for the first time since he'd walked through Greyvane's gate three weeks before. "Five minutes," he said. "Off the record. I'd ask Assessor Quenna to witness it, if you're amenable — I have no interest in either of us describing this conversation differently later."

Cael looked at him for a moment. Two years of history sat in the space between them, and none of it had ever included Coss asking permission for anything. "Quenna," he said, and she came, and stood a little apart, close enough to hear, far enough to make clear she wasn't a participant.

"Understand what you won," Coss said, without preamble, because that had always been his way — no warming up, no softening the entry, just the thing itself, delivered plainly. "Yesterday you were a monitoring file at my grade. A [SHATTERED] entity, unclassified, tracked but not escalated, the kind of anomaly that gets reviewed once a year by someone who has forty other files exactly like it and mostly hopes they all stay quiet. Today you are a public adjudication record that says the Compact's own classification schedule has a hole in it, and your name is the hole." He said this without triumph and without bitterness, the flat register of a man reciting a fact he had already made complete peace with. "Files at my grade get read by people like me, Cael. Records like yours get read by everyone above me."

"You're telling me I made it worse."

"I'm telling you that you made it *true*." Coss held his gaze, steady, calm in the way that had always made him harder to read than a man who raised his voice. "There's a difference. Worse implies you'd have been better off losing. You wouldn't have been. Losing meant expulsion, and expulsion meant exactly the kind of quiet, permanent removal from every institutional record that makes a person very easy to make disappear from, later, when someone decides disappearing is convenient. What you did today is the opposite of quiet. It's the opposite of easy to make disappear. That has a cost. It is not the same cost as the one you avoided."

Cael turned this over, the way he turned over everything Coss said, because two years had taught him that the man rarely spoke without meaning every clause of it. "You still think I should be worried."

"I think you should understand precisely what you're worried about, which isn't the same thing as being told to be afraid." Coss glanced once toward the doors, where the last of the gallery's stragglers were finally clearing out. "I filed my final report this afternoon. Contact of record, complete history, nothing embellished and nothing omitted — I've never once lied in a Compact filing and I wasn't going to start on the day I lost an argument to a fifteen-year-old. My recommendation was that the subject is a boy, the anomaly is the schedule, and the correct response is registry review, not escalation." A short, humorless breath, not quite a laugh. "I already know it will be ignored. I filed it honestly anyway. That's the job too, whatever else the job turns out to be."

He didn't say anything else for a moment, and in the space he left, Cael found himself doing something he hadn't expected to do: believing him completely, and being unsettled by the belief rather than reassured by it. A man who did his job honestly, all the way through, including the parts of it that cost him something — that was a harder thing to hold at arm's length than a man who lied.

"Thank you," Cael said finally, because it seemed like the only accurate thing available to say.

"Don't thank me for the report. Thank me for telling you what it means." Coss straightened, the conversation visibly closing in him the way a door closes on a well-oiled hinge, without a sound. "Five minutes," he said again, to Quenna this time, a small courtesy, and then he was walking away across the empty hall, toward whatever came next in a career that had just absorbed a public loss it would not soon forget.

---

Somewhere above the district where Coss kept his office — in a location the file itself would never specify, in a hand that would never sign anything with a name a later reader could trace — a request slip crossed a desk that had processed a great many request slips before this one, and would process a great many more after.

*Complete transcript, Greyvane adjudication hearing, [SHATTERED] enrollment challenge. Full record, all sessions, all exhibits.*

The request was approved in the ordinary way requests of its kind were approved: without comment, without delay, filed into a system built for exactly this — moving information upward without anyone below a certain level ever needing to know where, precisely, upward ended. A new file cover was cut. It was cut clean — no annotation, no priority flag, nothing that would have caught Havel's eye or Ilsev's, nothing that resembled the kind of irregularity either of them had learned to notice. Just a folder, and a name typed onto its spine that was not Cael's, because the file that mattered here was not a person's file. It was a schedule's.

The hand that filed it left no other trace. The office had no other visible occupant. If the record kept any account of who sat at that desk, it was not an account anyone below that desk would ever be shown.

Somewhere, quietly, a file was opened. Nobody at Greyvane would know it for years, if ever.

---

The monitoring changed within the week, and it changed in a way Cael could count.

He had spent two years learning the shape of being watched at a distance — the particular texture of a Compact presence that preferred not to be noticed, that operated through slow paperwork and patient, occasional visits rather than anything that announced itself. He knew that shape the way he knew a tell, the way he knew the rhythm of a fighter he'd studied long enough to predict. What replaced it, in the days after the hearing, did not match that shape at all.

He counted three new faces in the market square outside Greyvane's gate in a single afternoon, none of them making any real effort at concealment, all of them present again the next day in slightly different positions, the kind of rotation a team used when it wanted coverage rather than invisibility. He counted a fourth watching the training hall's public entrance from a bench that had never, in months of his own careful cataloguing of the district, hosted anyone sitting still for that long before. He counted the frequency of unfamiliar faces passing the archive's outer door — not Prynn's usual visitors, not academy staff, people whose business at Greyvane he couldn't place because they had none, only a reason to be standing somewhere with a clear line of sight.

The count kept coming up wrong. Too many watchers for a passive posture. Too coordinated for anything but a resourced operation, the kind that required someone, somewhere, to have decided the resourcing was worth spending. He had learned to read surveillance the way he read a fighter's tells, patiently, over two years, and everything he was reading now told him the same thing: this was not the shape of *watch and wait*. This was the shape of *watch, and be ready.*

He wrote it into the Power Log without self-pity, because self-pity had never once been useful to him and he saw no reason to start now.

*They know my name. All of them, or near enough — everyone who reads a hearing transcript up whatever chain those things travel. That was always the price of the argument. I understood the price before I made it. The argument was still worth making. Both of those things are true, and I don't think I get to have only one of them.*

---

He found the other three in the common room that evening, and found Karis, in particular, at the long table with the hearing transcript spread in front of her, weighted flat at both ends with a stack of her own notebooks, working through it with the particular intensity she brought to anything she'd decided mattered.

"You should be celebrating," Lira said, from the couch, where she was very deliberately not doing anything that resembled work. "You won. Historically, by all accounts. There's a party happening in the residence wing that neither of us is at because you're doing homework."

"I'm not doing homework." Karis didn't look up. "I'm doing the thing that matters more than the party, which is understanding exactly what we won, because I don't think it's what everyone downstairs thinks it is."

Cael sat across from her. "Tell me."

"The ruling is jurisdictional." She turned the transcript toward him, tapping a passage near the end. "The panel didn't say your enrollment is valid. They said the Compact hasn't established authority to void it. That's not the same finding. A jurisdictional hole survives exactly as long as nobody with drafting authority bothers to close it — and someone will, eventually, because a hole this visible doesn't stay unpatched forever once enough people know it's there. And your observer workaround itself is finished either way. Naveth can re-paper your enrollment status internally as many times as he wants, but the provision that got you in the door was never built to survive being read this closely by this many people. It died in that hearing room today, whatever the verdict said."

"So what do I need."

"Something positive. Not an absence of authority — an actual, affirmative basis for being where you are." She pulled one of her own notebooks closer, opened to a page dense with her handwriting, older ink than the rest, weeks of accumulated marginalia. "I keep finding references, in the founding-era material, to how institutions evaluated practitioners before the registry standardized everything — before the schedule existed at all, when the only real test was whether you could demonstrate what you claimed. If there's a recoverable form of that precedent, a real one, documented well enough to survive scrutiny — that's sturdier than any workaround. Sturdy enough, maybe, for somewhere better than here. A real mid-tier institution, somewhere, that could actually hold you the way Greyvane never quite could."

"You haven't found it yet."

"No." She said it plainly, without frustration, the way she said most things that were simply true. "I've found the shape of where it should be. That's not nothing. It's also not finished." She closed the notebook, finally looking up at him properly. "I'll keep looking. It's not a today problem. It's a next-year problem, if we're careful, and we're going to be careful."

Brom, from the doorway, arms full of something that smelled distantly of the residence wing's celebration, dropped into the chair beside Lira. "You're doing the thing where you solve tomorrow's problem instead of enjoying today's win," he said, to Karis, without any real heat in it. "I've watched Cael do it for months. It's less charming when there's two of you."

"Someone has to," Karis said, entirely unbothered.

Lira looked between the four of them — the transcript, the notebooks, the celebration audibly continuing down the hall without them — and something in her face settled into the particular fondness she reserved for exactly this configuration of people. "You realize," she said, to Cael, "that you're now the most famous clerical error on the continent."

"Naveth will be proud."

"Naveth," Brom said, "is hiding under his desk."

"Naveth," Karis said, not looking up from her notes, the faintest edge of a smile finally showing, "re-papered your enrollment before dinner. I checked. He's proud *under* the desk. Those aren't contradictory states. I'd know — I've been both this week."

Cael looked at the four of them, at the transcript and the notebooks and the party they weren't at, and understood, sitting there with a Compact file opening somewhere above his reach and watchers counting themselves into new patterns outside the gate, that he had never once regretted an argument this much and been this certain, at the exact same time, that he would make it again.

He got out the log, and wrote that down too, because it seemed like the kind of thing worth having in careful handwriting, for whenever he needed to remember it was true.

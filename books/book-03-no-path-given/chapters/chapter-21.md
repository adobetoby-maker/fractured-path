# Chapter 21 — Adjudication

Greyvane's lecture hall had never held this many people at once, and it showed in the particular strain of a room asked to be something it wasn't built for — extra benches carried in from the training hall, the gallery packed shoulder to shoulder, students and faculty and, scattered among them with the deliberate stillness of people trying not to be noticed, a handful of credentialed observers from institutions that had no obvious business at Greyvane at all. Cael counted six of them before he stopped counting, because the count itself was telling him something he already understood: the record was public, and word had traveled further than three weeks should reasonably have allowed.

He sat at the respondent's table with Quenna beside him, Naveth a row back, and Prynn further back still, arms crossed, dust-dry and unreadable in the particular way she'd been unreadable all week. Yorlan presided from the raised table at the front, flanked by Ilsev on one side and a third assessor Cael didn't recognize on the other. Havel sat below them at the recording desk, pen already moving before anyone had said a formal word.

Coss stood at the delegation's table, document case open, and did not look at Cael once as the session opened. Cael understood the omission as its own kind of respect. This was not personal for Coss. It had never been personal. That was, in its way, the whole argument Cael was about to spend a day watching demonstrated in exhaustive, professional detail.

"The Compact's case," Yorlan said, without preamble, the flat colorless economy of a man who had opened a thousand hearings and saw no reason to make this one longer than it needed to be. "Proceed."

---

Coss was good.

Cael had known this, in the abstract, for two years — had built his own preparation around the assumption that Coss would be good, because underestimating him would have been its own kind of failure. Watching it in practice, across a full session, was still something else entirely. Coss did not raise his voice. He did not misstate a fact, or reach for a characterization the record didn't support, or overplay a single point beyond what it could actually carry. He laid out the Compact's case the way a mason laid stone — one piece at a time, each one load-bearing, nothing wasted.

The provision's text, first: read into the record in full, the language plain and unambiguous on its face, written for practitioners who had not yet undergone Kindling assessment. Then the legislative intent behind it — and here Coss produced something that made Naveth's expression shift, almost imperceptibly, in the row behind Cael: the original drafting records, decades old, establishing beyond argument that the provision had been built exclusively for unkindled children under academic petition, never intended to cover anyone already classified under any registry designation, however irregular. Naveth had not known the Compact still held those records. Cael filed the small, cold fact of that away for later.

Then the enrollment itself, examined on its plain face against the provision's actual language, and found, inevitably, invalid — Coss did not need to argue this point so much as simply demonstrate it, laying the category beside the fact of Cael's Kindled, registered status and letting the mismatch speak for itself. Then precedent, case after case, category-violation enrollments voided at other academies under nearly identical circumstances, none of them contested successfully, all of them decided in a matter of sessions rather than weeks.

He examined Quenna next, and here the questioning sharpened without ever becoming unkind. He walked her, carefully, through the assessment records she'd filed across the semester — *unorthodox architecture, consistent execution, no safety concerns* — and asked her, with the mild, devastating precision of a man who already knew the answer, whether the language had been chosen to accurately describe what she'd observed, or to describe as little as possible while remaining technically true. Quenna did not flinch. She also did not have an answer that improved the record.

He examined Naveth after that, and the questioning found the provost's honest uncertainty and turned it, gently, methodically, into something that read on the record like consciousness of guilt — every hedge, every careful qualification Naveth had genuinely meant as caution rather than concealment, reframed by careful sequencing into the shape of a man who had known, from the start, that what he was doing wasn't defensible.

By the middle of the afternoon, Cael could feel the gallery's mood shifting behind him — a curdling, subtle but unmistakable, the particular energy of a crowd that had come to watch their anomaly win and was instead watching him get dismantled in exhaustive, patient, entirely correct detail.

---

Edran testified near the session's end, called as a witness of fact regarding the exhibition bout, and gave his testimony exactly the way he'd promised he would: plain, unembellished, no more and no less than what he'd actually witnessed. He described the match honestly — the seam Cael had found, the way it had broken his Glass Path's timing, the clean and total nature of the loss. He did not soften it. He did not need to.

Coss took the honest testimony and turned it, with the same careful patience he'd brought to everything else, into one more stone in the wall. "So the entity currently enrolled under a provision written for children with no meaningful capability demonstrated, in a sanctioned academy match, capability at what level, in your professional assessment?"

"Iron-tier, at minimum," Edran said, because it was true, and because he'd promised Cael honesty rather than advocacy. "Possibly higher. I couldn't say for certain."

"Thank you." Coss let the answer sit a moment before turning back to the panel. "One more nail, Magistrate, in a coffin that was already fully built."

By the time Yorlan called the session's close, the Compact's case sat on the record whole, clean, and — by any honest read of the room — entirely unanswered.

"The respondent," Yorlan said, gathering his papers with the flat, colorless economy that had governed the entire session, "will present his defense of the enrollment at next sitting."

Cael stood.

The room, which had spent an entire day settling into the comfortable rhythm of watching a case be built, went very still at the sound of a chair scraping back that nobody had expected to hear yet.

"I won't be defending the enrollment, Magistrate."

Yorlan paused, papers half-gathered, and looked at him properly for what felt like the first time all day.

"I'll be challenging the Compact's jurisdiction to void it."

The silence that followed lasted a full second — long enough that Cael, standing there with his heart doing something complicated behind his ribs, had time to actually feel it happen. Ilsev looked up from her notes, sharp and sudden, the first time all day her attention had visibly left the page in front of her. Across the delegation's table, something moved behind Coss's expression — small, complicated, gone almost before it arrived, the look of a man watching the one argument he had genuinely worried about finally, formally, walk onto the record.

"Noted," Yorlan said, after a moment, in a voice that gave away nothing at all. "Session is closed. Second sitting, tomorrow, first bell."

He struck his small gavel once.

The gallery detonated into noise.

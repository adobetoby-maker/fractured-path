# Chapter 10 — What Fenmark Kept

The reassessment had been on the main hall calendar for three weeks, posted in the same public column as Cael's demonstration dates — *L. — re-certification track, formal reassessment* — and for three weeks Lira had been preparing for it the way she prepared for everything, which was to say visibly, methodically, and without once admitting out loud that it mattered.

Cael had watched the preparation the way he watched everything, and had kept his notes on it to himself, because some observations were owed privacy even from the observer. She had added a second morning session to her training schedule. She had stopped reading in the common room in the evenings and started reading in her own room instead, which meant she didn't want witnesses to how late the lamp burned. Her sentences had shortened by roughly a third — he had not actually measured this, but he would have bet the notebook on it — and her sparring had picked up an edge of economy, nothing wasted, nothing decorative, the style of someone stripping a thing down to what would hold under inspection. She was fine. She said she was fine every time Brom asked, which Brom did with the flat regularity of a man taking soundings, and it was even mostly true. She was fine in the sense that a drawn bow is fine.

Then, on the Tuesday of the assessment week, Quenna sent word that Lira should come to the archive annex before the midday meal, and that she could bring whoever she wanted or no one, and Lira had looked across the common room at Cael and said only, "Come stand somewhere in the room," which was as close as she ever came to asking.

The annex was a small chamber off the archive proper that Quenna used for the administrative business of the irregular tracks — a table, four chairs that didn't match, a shelf of provision binders, and Prynn's territorial disapproval seeping through the wall like damp. The envelope was already on the table when they came in. Sealed, stamped, addressed in a clerk's hand that had never once thought about the person whose life it was labeling.

Quenna set it in the middle of the table between them without comment, and Lira looked at it the way she looked at opponents she hadn't decided yet whether to underestimate.

"That's Fenmark's," Quenna said. "The full record. It's a requirement of the re-certification provision — your originating academy has to submit the complete file before a reassessment can be scheduled. You're entitled to review it before we begin." She paused, and the pause was doing more work than the sentence around it. "You're also entitled not to. Some candidates prefer to walk in without it."

Lira looked at the envelope for a long moment. "What happens if I don't read it?"

"We proceed with the assessment. The file gets entered into your record here regardless. You'd simply be finding out what it says at the same time everyone else who ever reads your file does — after the fact, from people who already have opinions about it."

"That's not really a choice."

"No," Quenna agreed. "It's a version of one."

Cael, standing by the archive doorway where he'd drifted after his own morning session, watched Lira's hand hover over the envelope and not move for long enough that he understood she was doing the thing she always did before a bout she wasn't sure she wanted — running the calculation twice, checking whether wanting to know and being ready to know were actually the same question. Two years, he thought. She had spent two years not reading this document, and the not-reading had been a wall she built herself, brick by brick, out of the entirely reasonable suspicion that whatever Fenmark had written down would be worse to know than to imagine. He knew better than to say anything. He'd learned that from her, the same year she'd learned patience from him — the long apprenticeship they'd served in each other's silences, back when silence was most of what either of them had to offer.

She picked it up. She broke the seal with her thumbnail, one clean motion, the way she did everything she'd decided to do.

She read it.

---

It was thin.

That was the first thing, and somehow the worst thing, in a way Lira hadn't braced for. She had braced for a great many versions of this document, over two years of not reading it. She'd braced for cruelty — some assessor's contempt preserved in official language, the institutional voice curling its lip at a scholarship girl who didn't know her place. She'd braced for distortion — the incident rewritten, her correction inflated into an outburst, witnesses paraphrased into a story that wasn't the one she'd lived. She had braced, above all, for weight: pages of testimony, a record of deliberation, the machinery of an institution actually reckoning with what it was doing to a fourteen-year-old girl who'd corrected an examiner in front of a room. Something heavy enough to have needed all this time to lift.

What she held instead was four paragraphs.

She counted them twice. She turned the sheet over, looking for the rest, and found the reverse blank except for a filing stamp. She checked the envelope for additional pages and found the transmittal slip and nothing else. Four paragraphs, one signature, one citation. The whole of it could be read aloud in under a minute. She knew, because some cold cataloguing part of her mind — a part she'd caught from Cael, probably, the way you caught a person's gestures after enough years — was timing it as she read.

The finding was insubordination. Undefined beyond a single sentence describing the incident — a Wind Path candidate's evaluation, a disputed technique, a correction offered without deference to the assessor's authority — followed by a citation to Fenmark's disciplinary code, section and subsection, and a determination. That was all the incident got: one sentence. The room she remembered in such dense detail — the light through the tall windows, the examiner's ring tapping the lectern, the exact half-second of quiet after she'd said *that's not how the third form loads, though* and the longer quiet after she'd demonstrated why — had been compressed to nineteen words by someone who hadn't been there. No hearing transcript. No statement from her. No record of anyone having asked her, at any point in the process, to explain what she'd meant or why she'd said it.

"There wasn't a hearing," she said, out loud, to no one in particular, reading the same four paragraphs a second time in case she'd missed a page. "I remember there being a room. People. I thought that was a hearing."

Quenna, quiet, let her keep reading rather than answering immediately.

She remembered the room. She'd furnished two years of midnight arguments with that room — rehearsed better answers for it, imagined the faces of the three seated adults she'd assumed were weighing her fate, wondered which of them had argued for her and lost. She had assumed someone argued for her and lost. It had mattered, somehow, assuming that.

"There wasn't a hearing," Lira said again, and this time it wasn't a question, because she'd found the line — a single administrative note near the bottom, easy to miss if you weren't looking for exactly this — stating that Fenmark's disciplinary provision for Copper-tier scholarship students did not require a formal hearing for findings below a certain severity threshold. Insubordination, apparently, fell comfortably under it. "They didn't need one. There's a rule that says they didn't need one."

The room she remembered had been three adults telling her a decision that was already made. The weighing she'd imagined had never happened anywhere, in any room, on any afternoon. There had been nothing to weigh because nothing was required to be weighed. Scholarship students below the threshold got the abbreviated procedure, and the abbreviated procedure was a form, and the form was four paragraphs long.

She set the file down on the table with the exact care of someone who wanted to throw it and had decided, deliberately, not to.

"Two years," she said. "Two years of every conversation I've had with myself about what I did wrong, what I should have said differently, whether I deserved it — organized around four paragraphs and a room that wasn't even a hearing." She looked at Quenna. "They didn't even do it carefully. I built an entire case against myself out of something they didn't think was worth doing properly."

"No," Quenna said. "They didn't."

Lira laughed, once, a short and humorless sound. "I don't know if that's better or worse."

"It's neither," Quenna said. "It's just true. What you do with it is a separate question, and it's the one the assessment floor is actually going to ask you."

Lira sat with that for a moment. Then she squared the four paragraphs against the table edge, set them back in their envelope, and stood, and Cael — who had spent the whole reading not moving, holding the doorway like a post — watched her do the thing that he would try, later, in the log, and fail, to fully describe: she put it down. Not away. Down. The distinction was visible in her shoulders, in the way the drawn-bow quality resolved into something plainer and more dangerous. She had walked into the annex carrying two years of a question. She walked out carrying an answer she didn't like, and answers, even bad ones, weighed less. Answers you could work with.

"What time is the panel," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"Two bells," said Quenna. "Eat something first."

---

She ate something first. She let Brom sit across from her while she did it and talk, at uncharacteristic length, about a formal-declaration lecture, and Cael understood that both of them knew exactly what the talking was for — ballast, ordinary noise, the sound of a life continuing on either side of the thing at two bells — and that this was Brom's gift given the only way Brom knew how to wrap one.

At the training hall, she stopped once at the notice board, where her name hung with the provisional marker beside it that the re-certification track required, the little bracketed annotation that followed her onto every sign-in sheet and every bout ledger. She looked at it the way she'd looked at it once before, weeks ago, when she thought no one was watching. Then she went in and wrapped her hands.

"You don't have to watch this one," she told Cael at the floor's edge, cinching the wrap with her teeth. "I know they run long."

"I've watched every bout you've fought for a year and a half," he said. "I'm not going to start missing them at the interesting ones."

Something crossed her face that was almost the real laugh, banked for later. "Interesting," she said. "That's one word for it." And she walked onto the floor.

---

The reassessment ran the standard format — demonstration bouts against two Greyvane students at graduated intensity, a formal panel observation, a closing evaluation of technique and advancement against her file's recorded baseline. Quenna presided with two faculty flanking her, ledgers open, and the required assessor's initial-box waiting at the margin of every line the way it always waited on Lira's paperwork, the procedure's small constant reminder that she was here on sufferance and schedule. Cael watched from the floor's edge, the same place he'd watched her win against Greyvane's second-ranked student weeks before, and understood within the first exchange that this was not going to be a repeat of that afternoon's quiet, contained victory.

This was Lira deciding to be unmistakable.

Her first opponent was a third-year, Copper-tier and solid, a Current Path student with a textbook advance pattern — and the word *textbook* was the whole problem, because Lira had just spent a morning learning exactly how much a textbook weighed. She gave him the first exchange to declare himself, the way she gave every opponent the first exchange, reading. Then she stopped giving him anything. She closed distance faster than the standard evasion framework predicted, arriving inside his second declaration before it finished loading, and the bout's remainder was a lesson in the gap between intention and execution — she read his commitments before they fully declared and punished that gap every single time it opened, with a precision that had nothing circuit-taught about it anymore. It had become entirely, unmistakably hers: the not-holding-back style that no academy had trained into her and no academy had managed to train out. The third-year fought gamely and lost cleanly and left the floor looking like a man who wanted to check the posted tier listings against his own memory.

The panel's pens moved. Cael, watching the pens as much as the floor, saw one of the flanking faculty write something, cross it out, and write something longer.

The second bout, against a stronger opponent — a fourth-year at Iron-tier's lower edge, faster, cagier, briefed by the first bout's example — ran longer and cost her more. He refused the close-distance game, kept his declarations short and his exits shorter, made her chase, and for two exchanges the fight was genuinely even, the hall's attention tightening around it as the outcome stopped being narrative and became mechanical. She took a real hit crossing the second exchange — a grazing release across the shoulder that would bruise for a week — and a second one three moves later, blocking late on purpose to buy an angle, and she spent both of them without hesitation, the way a person spends money they came intending to spend, because slowing down had never been the point. The point, Cael understood, watching her absorb the cost and keep the pace, was arithmetic performed in public. Every judge on that panel needed to watch her advance so far past a Copper ceiling that the ceiling itself became the thing that required explaining — needed to sit there with her file open in front of them, four paragraphs and a recorded tier, while the floor showed them a practitioner the file could not possibly contain.

By the third exchange of the second bout, the floor had gone quiet in the way floors went quiet when something was being settled that had nothing to do with the immediate fight. Students had drifted in from the yard and the lecture wing — word traveled fast in a small academy — and they lined the rail in silence, and the silence had a texture Cael recognized from the circuit — the sound of an audience recalibrating what it thought it knew.

She finished it with the same move she'd have used a year and a half ago in a market-town training yard, and the fact that it worked against an Iron-tier fourth-year in a chartered academy's hall was, in its way, the entire argument.

Quenna's panel closed the assessment with a notation Cael read over her shoulder before he could stop himself, because reading things over shoulders was a habit a year and a half of circuit work hadn't cured him of: *Advancement inconsistent with recorded tier. Recommend continued track with priority review.*

He read it twice, to be sure of the words, and then looked at the words a third time anyway.

It was, he understood, the first time in Lira's life that an academy's own system had written down, in its own official language, that her file might be wrong. Fenmark had written four paragraphs about her in two minutes and let them stand for two years. Greyvane had watched her for one afternoon and put a contradiction on the record. The contradiction wouldn't resolve anything by itself — records never did, by themselves — but it existed now, initialed and dated, and things that existed in records had a durability that even institutions couldn't easily take back. He knew that better than almost anyone alive. His whole life at Greyvane was balanced on it.

---

He found her afterward on the wall outside the training hall, the file still in her hand, not put away. She'd cleaned up and changed, but she was holding her shoulder in the careful way of someone deciding how much a bruise was going to be allowed to matter, and the light was going long and amber across the yard, and she was watching nothing in particular with an expression that had been through several drafts since morning.

"You saw the notation," she said, before he'd sat down.

"I did."

"It doesn't fix anything." She looked at the four paragraphs again, and this time her expression had settled into something steadier than the raw thing it had been that morning. "The finding still stands. Fenmark hasn't retracted anything. I'm not un-expelled. I just have a second piece of paper now that says the first piece of paper doesn't add up." She weighed the file in her hand, tilting it as if the contradiction might slide audibly from one end to the other. "I don't know what to do with two contradicting official records about the same person."

Cael thought about this for a moment, in his usual manner with things that mattered — carefully, and out loud, because he'd learned that some thoughts only finished forming once he'd said the beginning of them.

"Vell told me something once," he said. "Back in Ardenmere. I'd found something in her archive that contradicted what the Compact's own registry said about a fragment I'd logged. I asked her which one was true." He paused, and for a moment the hill town's evening was overlaid with a records room three days west, dust in slanted light, a woman who guarded the truth about people like grain against winter. "She said the records know what happened. Not what should have happened, not what the paperwork wanted to have happened afterward. What actually happened. Nobody gets to argue with that just because they wrote something different down somewhere more official."

Lira looked at him for a long moment.

"The records know what happened here," she said, testing the words. "Nobody gets to say otherwise."

"That's the version I'd use."

She was quiet, and then she did the thing he hadn't expected, which was laugh — not the short humorless sound from the archive, but something real, something that came up out of her like it had been waiting for permission. "Evidence," she said, folding the four paragraphs carefully and tucking them inside her coat rather than the file they'd come from. "For when it's obvious."

"For when what's obvious?"

"That they were wrong." She said it simply, without heat, the way a person states a fact they've finally finished arguing with themselves about. "I'm not filing a formal dispute. Not yet. Maybe not ever — Quenna's right that today's notation does more for me here than winning an argument with Fenmark would. But I'm keeping this." She patted the coat. "For the day it stops being about me proving something to an institution, and starts being about an institution finally admitting something to me."

Cael nodded. He didn't have anything to add to that, which was, he was learning, sometimes the correct response. There was a version of him — a year younger, maybe less — that would have tried to improve the moment with analysis, would have pointed out the parallels between her paperwork and his, mapped their two file-shaped wounds against each other until the mapping crowded out the feeling. He let the parallels sit unspoken instead. She knew them. He knew she knew them. The whole architecture of what they were to each other had been built by people who never needed the obvious said aloud.

---

Brom found them a while later, coming up the path from the defensive wing with his gear bag and less than his usual ease, and the news he brought sat differently against the afternoon's shape — quieter, but real.

"Wray watched my redirect form today," he said, dropping onto the wall beside them. "Found something."

"Found what," Lira asked.

"A flaw." He said the word like he was still getting used to the shape of it in his own mouth. "The self-taught version — the one I built alone, before any of this — it holds against anything I've faced at circuit speed. Against Silver-tier speed, it doesn't. There's a gap between when I read the redirect and when I execute it. Small. Milliseconds. Doesn't matter against Copper or Iron. Would matter enormously against anyone faster."

Cael turned toward him. In a year and a half he had watched Brom absorb losses, absorb insults, absorb a whole city's worth of underestimation, all with the same geological patience. He had never once watched Brom report a structural flaw in his own foundation, because until today no one alive had been qualified to find one.

"Can Wray fix it?"

"She says she can teach me to see it. Fixing it is going to take opponents fast enough to actually punish the gap in training, so I learn to close it under real pressure instead of theory." He looked out across the yard, toward the training hall where the afternoon's last sessions were still running. "Nobody here is fast enough. Not Wray, not the floor's best student, not anyone Greyvane has on its roster."

The three of them sat with that for a moment, the plain weight of a true thing nobody wanted to be true.

"So it's a wall," Cael said finally.

"It's a wall," Brom agreed. "Not yours. Not something that comes from what you are or what the Compact thinks about it. Just an ordinary wall, the sort training runs into eventually if you get good enough. Greyvane got me this far. It's not going to get me past this gap. I don't think it can."

He said it without complaint — Brom rarely complained about anything, on principle, the way some people refused to raise their voice — but there was something underneath it that Cael recognized, because he'd been sitting with a version of the same thing himself since he'd walked through Greyvane's gate. This place had done exactly what it promised to do. It took the mispriced and re-priced them honestly, and it had re-priced all three of them, and honest pricing cut in both directions: it had also, in doing its work, shown each of them precisely where its promise ran out. An academy that survived on being right about people other institutions were wrong about could tell you what you were worth. It could not always afford what you were worth. That was a different ledger, and nobody at this table owned it.

Lira reached over and knocked her shoulder against Brom's, once, the way she did when words weren't going to improve on the gesture — then winced, because it was the bruised shoulder, and did it again anyway with the other one, on principle, which got the closest thing to a smile out of Brom the afternoon had produced.

"Filed," she said. "Not today's problem. Today's problem got solved fine."

"Today's problem got solved," Brom agreed. "This one's waiting its turn."

They sat there a while longer, the three of them, watching the yard empty out toward evening — students crossing toward the refectory in twos and threes, a groundskeeper racking the training posts, the amber light going bronze and then grey — one wall behind them that had finally admitted it might have been wrong about something, and another wall ahead of them that hadn't admitted anything yet, because it didn't know it was a wall at all.

Cael wrote it down that night, in the log, in the plain evening entry he kept for the days that changed the shape of things.

*Lira's file lied by omission for two years, and today the record finally caught up to itself. Brom's wall is real and it isn't anyone's fault. Both of these are true on the same afternoon. I'm starting to think that's just what growing up inside institutions actually looks like — some walls fall because you finally push hard enough, and some walls stay standing because nobody built a door, and you learn to tell the difference by which kind makes you angry and which kind just makes you patient.*

He read it back, added nothing, and closed the notebook. Somewhere down the hall, he could hear Lira laughing again, softly, at something Brom had said — the real laugh, the one that had been waiting two years for a piece of paper thin enough to finally let it out.

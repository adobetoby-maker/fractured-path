# Chapter 10 — What Fenmark Kept

The file arrived on a Tuesday, in a sealed envelope Quenna set on the table between them without comment, and Lira looked at it the way she looked at opponents she hadn't decided yet whether to underestimate.

"That's Fenmark's," Quenna said. "The full record. It's a requirement of the re-certification provision — your originating academy has to submit the complete file before a reassessment can be scheduled. You're entitled to review it before we begin." She paused, and the pause was doing more work than the sentence around it. "You're also entitled not to. Some candidates prefer to walk in without it."

Lira looked at the envelope for a long moment. "What happens if I don't read it?"

"We proceed with the assessment. The file gets entered into your record here regardless. You'd simply be finding out what it says at the same time everyone else who ever reads your file does — after the fact, from people who already have opinions about it."

"That's not really a choice."

"No," Quenna agreed. "It's a version of one."

Cael, standing by the archive doorway where he'd drifted after his own morning session, watched Lira's hand hover over the envelope and not move for long enough that he understood she was doing the thing she always did before a bout she wasn't sure she wanted — running the calculation twice, checking whether wanting to know and being ready to know were actually the same question. He knew better than to say anything. He'd learned that from her, the same year she'd learned patience from him.

She picked it up. She read it.

---

It was thin.

That was the first thing, and somehow the worst thing, in a way Lira hadn't braced for. She'd spent two years imagining the document that ended her academic life as something substantial — pages of testimony, a record of argument, the weight of an institution actually reckoning with what it was doing to a fourteen-year-old girl who'd corrected an examiner in front of a room. What she held instead was four paragraphs.

The finding was insubordination. Undefined beyond a single sentence describing the incident — a Wind Path candidate's evaluation, a disputed technique, a correction offered without deference to the assessor's authority — followed by a citation to Fenmark's disciplinary code, section and subsection, and a determination. No hearing transcript. No statement from her. No record of anyone having asked her, at any point in the process, to explain what she'd meant or why she'd said it.

"There wasn't a hearing," she said, out loud, to no one in particular, reading the same four paragraphs a second time in case she'd missed a page. "I remember there being a room. People. I thought that was a hearing."

Quenna, quiet, let her keep reading rather than answering immediately.

"There wasn't a hearing," Lira said again, and this time it wasn't a question, because she'd found the line — a single administrative note near the bottom, easy to miss if you weren't looking for exactly this — stating that Fenmark's disciplinary provision for Copper-tier scholarship students did not require a formal hearing for findings below a certain severity threshold. Insubordination, apparently, fell comfortably under it. "They didn't need one. There's a rule that says they didn't need one."

She set the file down on the table with the exact care of someone who wanted to throw it and had decided, deliberately, not to.

"Two years," she said. "Two years of every conversation I've had with myself about what I did wrong, what I should have said differently, whether I deserved it — organized around four paragraphs and a room that wasn't even a hearing." She looked at Quenna. "They didn't even do it carefully. I built an entire case against myself out of something they didn't think was worth doing properly."

"No," Quenna said. "They didn't."

Lira laughed, once, a short and humorless sound. "I don't know if that's better or worse."

"It's neither," Quenna said. "It's just true. What you do with it is a separate question, and it's the one the assessment floor is actually going to ask you."

---

The reassessment ran the standard format — demonstration bouts against two Greyvane students at graduated intensity, a formal panel observation, a closing evaluation of technique and advancement against her file's recorded baseline. Cael watched from the floor's edge, the same place he'd watched her win against Greyvane's second-ranked student weeks before, and understood within the first exchange that this was not going to be a repeat of that afternoon's quiet, contained victory.

This was Lira deciding to be unmistakable.

She fought the first bout at a pace that made her opponent's Copper-tier training look exactly as thin as Fenmark's file, closing distance faster than the standard evasion framework predicted, reading commitments before they fully declared and punishing the gap between intention and execution with a precision that had nothing circuit-taught about it anymore — it had become entirely, unmistakably hers. The second bout, against a stronger opponent, ran longer and cost her more, and she took two real hits doing it and didn't slow down, because slowing down had never been the point. The point, Cael understood watching her, was that every judge on that panel needed to watch her advance so far past a Copper ceiling that the ceiling itself became the thing that required explaining.

By the third exchange of the second bout, the floor had gone quiet in the way floors went quiet when something was being settled that had nothing to do with the immediate fight.

Quenna's panel closed the assessment with a notation Cael read over her shoulder before he could stop himself, because reading things over shoulders was a habit a year and a half of circuit work hadn't cured him of: *Advancement inconsistent with recorded tier. Recommend continued track with priority review.*

It was, he understood, the first time in Lira's life that an academy's own system had written down, in its own official language, that her file might be wrong.

---

He found her afterward on the low wall outside the training hall, the file still in her hand, not put away.

"You saw the notation," she said, before he'd sat down.

"I did."

"It doesn't fix anything." She looked at the four paragraphs again, and this time her expression had settled into something steadier than the raw thing it had been that morning. "The finding still stands. Fenmark hasn't retracted anything. I'm not un-expelled. I just have a second piece of paper now that says the first piece of paper doesn't add up." She turned the file over in her hands. "I don't know what to do with two contradicting official records about the same person."

Cael thought about this for a moment, the way he thought about most things that mattered — carefully, and out loud, because he'd learned that some thoughts only finished forming once he'd said the beginning of them.

"Vell told me something once," he said. "Back in Ardenmere. I'd found something in her archive that contradicted what the Compact's own registry said about a fragment I'd logged. I asked her which one was true." He paused. "She said the records know what happened. Not what should have happened, not what the paperwork wanted to have happened afterward. What actually happened. Nobody gets to argue with that just because they wrote something different down somewhere more official."

Lira looked at him for a long moment.

"The records know what happened here," she said, testing the words. "Nobody gets to say otherwise."

"That's the version I'd use."

She was quiet, and then she did the thing he hadn't expected, which was laugh — not the short humorless sound from the archive, but something real, something that came up out of her like it had been waiting for permission. "Evidence," she said, folding the four paragraphs carefully and tucking them inside her coat rather than the file they'd come from. "For when it's obvious."

"For when what's obvious?"

"That they were wrong." She said it simply, without heat, the way a person states a fact they've finally finished arguing with themselves about. "I'm not filing a formal dispute. Not yet. Maybe not ever — Quenna's right that today's notation does more for me here than winning an argument with Fenmark would. But I'm keeping this." She patted the coat. "For the day it stops being about me proving something to an institution, and starts being about an institution finally admitting something to me."

Cael nodded. He didn't have anything to add to that, which was, he was learning, sometimes the correct response.

---

Brom found them a while later, and the news he brought sat differently against the afternoon's shape — quieter, but real in its own way.

"Wray watched my redirect form today," he said, dropping onto the wall beside them without much of his usual ease. "Found something."

"Found what," Lira asked.

"A flaw." He said the word like he was still getting used to the shape of it in his own mouth. "The self-taught version — the one I built alone, before any of this — it holds against anything I've faced at circuit speed. Against Silver-tier speed, it doesn't. There's a gap between when I read the redirect and when I execute it. Small. Milliseconds. Doesn't matter against Copper or Iron. Would matter enormously against anyone faster."

"Can Wray fix it?"

"She says she can teach me to see it. Fixing it is going to take opponents fast enough to actually punish the gap in training, so I learn to close it under real pressure instead of theory." He looked out across the yard, toward the training hall where the afternoon's last sessions were still running. "Nobody here is fast enough. Not Wray, not the floor's best student, not anyone Greyvane has on its roster."

The three of them sat with that for a moment, the plain weight of a true thing nobody particularly wanted to be true.

"So it's a wall," Cael said finally.

"It's a wall," Brom agreed. "Not yours. Not something that comes from what you are or what the Compact thinks about it. Just an ordinary wall, the kind training runs into eventually if you get good enough. Greyvane got me this far. It's not going to get me past this gap. I don't think it can."

He said it without complaint — Brom rarely complained about anything, on principle, the way some people refused to raise their voice — but there was something underneath it that Cael recognized, because he'd been sitting with a version of the same thing himself since he'd walked through Greyvane's gate. This place had done exactly what it promised to do. It had also, in doing that honestly, shown all three of them precisely where its promise ran out.

Lira reached over and knocked her shoulder against Brom's, once, the way she did when words weren't going to improve on the gesture.

"Filed," she said. "Not today's problem. Today's problem got solved fine."

"Today's problem got solved," Brom agreed. "This one's waiting its turn."

They sat there a while longer, the three of them, watching the yard empty out toward evening — one wall behind them that had finally admitted it might have been wrong about something, and another wall ahead of them that hadn't admitted anything yet, because it didn't know it was a wall at all.

Cael wrote it down that night, in the log, the way he wrote most things that mattered.

*Lira's file lied by omission for two years, and today the record finally caught up to itself. Brom's wall is real and it isn't anyone's fault. Both of these are true on the same afternoon. I'm starting to think that's just what growing up inside institutions actually looks like — some walls fall because you finally push hard enough, and some walls stay standing because nobody built a door, and you learn to tell the difference by which kind makes you angry and which kind just makes you patient.*

He closed the notebook. Somewhere down the hall, he could hear Lira laughing again, softly, at something Brom had said — the real laugh, the one that had been waiting two years for a piece of paper thin enough to finally let it out.

# Chapter 15 — Warden Coss

The gate clerk had never seen the credentials before, and it showed.

Cael watched it happen from across the yard, close enough to read the shape of the exchange without hearing the words: a man in traveling clothes, unremarkable at a glance, holding out a document case and waiting while the clerk turned a small stamped seal over in her hands longer than she needed to, the particular hesitation of someone checking a thing against a memory that didn't have anywhere to file it. She said something. He answered, patient, and produced a second paper from the case — a formality, the kind of extra proof a careful traveler carried for exactly this situation — and after that the gate opened the way it opened for anyone the list expected.

Cael didn't need to see the second paper to know who was standing at the gate. He had spent two years learning the shape a certain kind of patience made in a doorway, and this was that shape, older now, traveled, but built the same way underneath.

He wrote nothing down. There wasn't time, and there wasn't a version of this that needed the notebook to be real.

---

Naveth's runner found him twenty minutes later, in the archive, where Prynn was in the middle of explaining a filing convention Cael had asked about only to have something to do with his hands. "Provost wants you in his office. Now, not after this." The boy delivered it the way runners delivered urgent things at Greyvane, which was quickly and without embellishment, and was gone again before Cael had fully stood up.

Prynn watched him go, not unkindly, and said only: "Whatever it is, the shelves will still be here." It was, Cael understood walking out, the closest thing to reassurance the archive had to offer, and it did more work than it should have.

The walk to Naveth's office took four minutes. He used all four of them counting things — the exact number of students on the yard, the color the sky had gone since morning, the exact sound his own footsteps made on the stone versus the softer sound they made on the packed earth path — because counting things was easier than sitting with the one thing he wasn't ready to count directly, which was how many years he'd actually spent believing this moment would eventually come and how little that belief had done to prepare him for it arriving.

The office door was open when he reached it. That, more than anything, told him how the room inside had already decided to treat this.

---

Naveth sat behind his desk with the stillness of a man who had rehearsed his own face in advance. Quenna stood near the window, arms folded, watching the door rather than the room. And the man from the gate sat in the chair angled toward the desk — unremarkable clothes, a document case now open on his knee, and a face that had aged the way Cael's own memory of it hadn't quite kept up with. Older around the eyes. The same patience underneath.

"Cael," Naveth said, and something in the single syllable carried the whole shape of the next several minutes. "This is Warden Coss. I understand you two have met."

"We have," Coss said, before Cael could answer for himself, and there was nothing performative in the correction — just the flat efficiency of a man who preferred precision to ceremony. "Two years ago. A market square, a certification office, and a great many questions I asked as gently as the questions could be asked."

Cael sat, because Quenna's small nod toward the empty chair made it clear he was meant to, and because standing would have made this a confrontation rather than what it actually was, which was a procedure. He had learned, somewhere in the last two years, to recognize the difference even when the two felt identical from the inside.

"You're a long way from a district record office," Cael said.

"I am." Coss opened the document case fully, laying its contents flat on his knee with the easy care of a man who had done this exact motion enough times that it required no thought at all. "Which is, I imagine, the first thing worth explaining. I want to be honest with you about what I am, because I suspect you've spent two years assuming something less accurate than the truth, and the truth will serve this conversation better than the assumption would."

He produced a card — small, stamped, plain in the way that official things were plain when the people who designed them had stopped needing to impress anyone. Naveth's expression didn't change looking at it, which told Cael the provost had already seen it, privately, before this meeting began.

"'Warden' was never a town title," Coss said. "It reads like one. It's meant to. A local district finds it easier to accept a Warden than a Compact field agent walking through its gates, and the Compact has never seen a reason to correct that impression when the impression does useful work. But the grade is real, and it's mine, and I've held it since before I ever set foot in your district." A pause, precise. "I was never a local official who happened to notice you, Cael. I was the Compact's hand on your file from the morning of your Kindling, patient at a distance because patience at a distance was what the assignment required." He set the card down. "Greyvane changed the assignment."

Cael sat with that. It landed the way true things sometimes landed — not as a surprise so much as a shape finally resolving out of something he'd been looking at sideways for two years without quite letting himself look at directly. He had known, in the careful way he knew things he'd never fully written down, that Coss's gentleness had never once been the whole story. He had simply never had a mechanism to attach the knowledge to.

"You're here to file something," Cael said.

"I am." No hesitation in it. Coss lifted a second document from the case — thicker than the first, official in the particular way of things drafted to survive scrutiny. "The demonstration-provision enrollment under which you're currently attending this academy is, on its face, a category violation. The provision was written to cover unkindled prospects — children who have not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment. You underwent formal Kindling assessment a year and a half ago. You hold a registered classification. The classification happens to be one the standard system has no good procedure for, but you are not, by any honest reading of the provision's own language, unkindled. The enrollment is invalid as categorized." He set the second document on Naveth's desk, precisely, corner aligned with corner. "And a Compact-chartered academy that knowingly harbors an invalid enrollment exposes its charter to review. I don't say that as a threat, Provost. I say it because it's the fact that makes what happens next unavoidable rather than optional."

Naveth's hands, folded on the desk, did not move. "You're filing a formal challenge."

"I am filing it now, in this room, on the record." Coss produced a third paper, already prepared, already signed at the bottom in a hand that had clearly practiced its own economy the same way everything else about him had. "Under charter procedure, a filed challenge of this kind compels a public adjudication hearing — Compact magistrate presiding, conducted on academy grounds, entered into the permanent registry. I'd ask you to understand that I take no satisfaction in that. I'd also ask you to understand that satisfaction was never the relevant question."

The room held still around the paper on the desk the way rooms held still around things that had already happened, whether or not everyone in them had finished absorbing it yet. Cael watched Quenna's face and found nothing there but the careful blankness of someone deciding, in real time, which of several responses would actually help.

"How long do we have," Naveth said, finally, "before the hearing."

"Three weeks. The magistrate's office sets the interval; I don't control it, and I wouldn't ask to." Coss closed the document case, the small metal clasp landing with a sound too soft to be as final as it felt. "I'd also say, for what it's worth, that I've seen this provision tested before, in other districts, under other circumstances. I have never seen it survive."

Nobody in the room asked him to elaborate. Nobody needed to.

---

The meeting ended the way meetings like this ended — with logistics, dates confirmed, a copy of the challenge left on Naveth's desk for the academy's own counsel to begin working against, Quenna already turning half her attention toward whatever preparation the next three weeks would demand of her. Cael sat through all of it with the particular stillness he'd learned from watching people who had to hold a room steady while something inside them was doing something else entirely.

Coss rose to leave before Cael did. At the door, he stopped, and turned, and for the first time since walking into the room, addressed Cael directly rather than the proceeding around him.

"You were fourteen," he said. "In that market square. I did my job as gently as the job allowed me to do it — gently enough, I hoped, that you'd remember the gentleness before you remembered anything else about it." He held Cael's gaze without softening it further. "This is also the job. I want you to understand that I mean that as plainly as I've ever meant anything. It doesn't make it easier. It also doesn't make it less true."

He meant it. That was, Cael understood, watching him go — understood with the same cold cataloguing clarity he brought to everything he was afraid to feel directly — the entire, unbearable problem with the whole machine. It wasn't staffed by cruelty. It was staffed by a man who did his job as gently as the job allowed, and the job itself had never once required gentleness to also require his expulsion, his classification, his careful two-year pursuit across half a continent. Cruelty would have been easier to be angry at. Competence, exercised kindly, in service of a system that simply didn't have room for what he was — that was harder to hate and harder to fight, both at once, and Cael sat with the shape of that discomfort long after the door had closed behind him.

---

The hearing notice went up in the main hall before the end of the day, posted on the board beside the assessment calendar with the same unceremonious efficiency Greyvane applied to every piece of its own administration. Cael read it standing in front of the board the way he'd read the assessment calendar on his first day here — his own name, in public, on a schedule, except this time the schedule wasn't an opportunity someone had built a door around. It was a date circled in ink by a system that had spent two years deciding he didn't belong on the other side of any door at all.

*Public Adjudication Hearing. Matter of enrollment status, unclassified observer track. Three weeks hence.*

He stood there long enough that a passing student gave him a second look before continuing on. Then he walked to the archive.

Prynn was where he'd left her, still among the same shelves, and looked up when he came in with the particular patience of someone who'd already guessed, from the runner's urgency earlier, that whatever had happened wasn't going to be small.

"They've filed," Cael said. There wasn't a version of the sentence that needed more explanation than that, not to her, not today.

"I heard." She set down the ledger she'd been working from. "Three weeks."

"Three weeks."

Prynn studied him for a moment with the same territorial precision she brought to everything, the look of a woman deciding whether a request was worth the trouble of the records it would disturb. Then she nodded, once, as though he'd already asked the question rather than merely arrived carrying it.

"Where do you keep the Compact's registry code?" Cael said. "The actual text. Not a summary. Not what people say it says. The code itself, as written."

Something shifted in Prynn's face — not surprise, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a question she'd been keeping shelf space for without knowing who would eventually ask it.

"Follow me," she said, and led him toward the back of the archive, past the shelves he'd already catalogued twice, into a section he hadn't yet had reason to look at closely. "Sixty years I've kept these records, Cael. You're about to become the first person who's ever asked me for that shelf because he actually intended to read it."

He didn't know, standing there, whether that was a compliment or a warning. He suspected, walking toward the shelf with her, that it was probably both, and that he was going to spend the next three weeks finding out which one mattered more.

# Chapter 2 — Minor-Tier

The question took him most of the morning to word properly, and once he had it worded he understood why it had taken that long: it wasn't really a question about Greyvane. It was a question about incentives, and Cael had learned, across every institution that had ever wanted things from him, that incentives were the only honest answer any place ever gave.

He'd tried several draftings of it in the notebook before he settled on the version he actually intended to ask out loud. *Why did you take me* was too small, and invited a small answer — a shrug, a kindness, something that wouldn't survive contact with a bad month. *What do you get out of this* was too blunt for a first conversation with a man who ran the place he'd be living in for the foreseeable future. He wanted the version that would actually get him the truth, not the version that would get him a story designed to make him feel welcome. He landed on it walking to breakfast: *why did this academy take the risk.* Not *me.* The risk. The distinction mattered to him more than he could fully explain, except that a man could lie about a boy in a way he couldn't easily lie about a business decision with sixty years of ledgers behind it.

*Why did this academy take the risk?*

He put the question to Provost Naveth directly, on the second morning, in an office that smelled of old paper and older stone and told him more about the man before he'd said a word than most people managed to say in an hour. Sixty-some years old, the weathering of someone who'd spent decades somewhere larger and grander before ending up somewhere smaller and, evidently, more his. The office was orderly in the way of a man who'd made peace with limited resources by refusing to waste any of them on disorder — a single desk, uncluttered; two chairs, one for him and one for whoever sat across from him, no third chair suggesting he rarely entertained groups; a window that looked out over the training yard rather than the approach road, which told Cael, before either of them had said a word, which direction this man actually paid attention to.

Naveth didn't answer right away. He studied Cael for a moment — not unkindly, but with the frank assessment of someone deciding how much of the real answer a fifteen-year-old could actually use. It was not the assessing look Cael had grown used to from Arbiters and assessors and Compact officials, the look that measured him against a category and found the fit wanting. It was closer to the look Hesk gave a bracket before deciding whether to trust it with real weight.

"You want to know if I'm a gambler," he said finally, "or an idiot, or something else. That's the real question underneath the one you asked."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. It's the correct question. I'd have asked it too, in your position." He leaned back, and the chair took the shift in weight with the worn, accustomed give of furniture that had been sat in this exact way for a very long time. "I ran a mid-tier academy for eleven years before I came here. Good school. Good students. Every year I watched two or three of them leave for institutions that could offer more — not because we'd failed them, but because we'd succeeded, and success at that level means someone bigger eventually makes a better offer. I got tired of building things I didn't get to keep."

"So you came here."

"Not immediately. There was a decade in between I won't bore you with, and a few years where I told myself I was between postings rather than admitting I didn't know what I actually wanted to build next." He said this plainly, without the practiced self-deprecation of a man who'd told the story enough times to have smoothed its edges. "But eventually, yes. I came here, and it took me most of a year to understand why it fit better than anywhere larger ever had. What I understood is something the mid-tier academies never have to learn, because they don't need to: Greyvane cannot compete for the students the system prices correctly. We will never out-offer a school with better facilities and better placement records for a Silver-tier prospect with a clean file. That competition is closed to us permanently." He said this without bitterness, the way a man states the boundaries of a problem he's already solved. "So we compete for the students the system prices *wrong*. The late Kindlers. The disputed classifications. The re-certification cases nobody else will touch because touching them is administratively inconvenient." He gestured, without turning, at a shelf behind him lined with thin bound ledgers. "That's our alumni record. Late Kindlers who reached Bronze on schedules nobody predicted. Disputed classifications that got overturned, eventually, because somebody here believed the paperwork was wrong before the registry admitted it. We build our reputation one clerical error at a time, and it turns out clerical errors are undervalued at a rate that's kept this place solvent for sixty years."

"And I'm the biggest one you've ever admitted."

"You are the largest clerical error this academy has ever taken a chance on, yes." He said it without softening it, and somehow the bluntness landed as more respectful than any careful phrasing would have. "Every student in this building is someone's clerical error, Cael. You're simply the largest one we've ever had the nerve to sign for."

It was not warmth. Cael understood, sitting there, that it would have been easy to want it to be warmth — an old man moved by a boy's hard circumstances, taking him in out of something like kindness. It wasn't that, and the lack of it was, unexpectedly, more reassuring than the alternative would have been. He had spent a year and a half learning to be careful of systems that claimed to want him out of kindness. A system that wanted him because the incentives worked was a system he could actually trust to keep wanting him, for exactly as long as the incentives held.

He thought, sitting across the desk from a man who had just described him, without cruelty, as the largest clerical error in sixty years of institutional history, about all the people who had tried to be kind to him instead. Hesk's kindness he trusted absolutely, because Hesk's kindness had never once asked anything of him in return. But the certification office in Denvash had been kind, in its way, the day of his Kindling — gentle-voiced, careful with the paperwork, apologetic about the classification even as they processed it — and that kindness had lasted exactly as long as the encounter itself and not one hour longer. Kindness, he was beginning to suspect, was a weather condition. Incentive was closer to a season. A season, at least, told you honestly how long it intended to last.

"One more question," he said, before he could talk himself out of it. "What happens if I stop being a good bet?"

Naveth considered this with the same slow, deliberate care he'd given every question so far — the care, Cael was learning to recognize, of a man who took his time because the question deserved it, not because he had nowhere better to be. "Then this academy makes a mistake it can survive, the way it's survived every mistake it's made in sixty years, and we adjust the ledger and keep going. That's the other half of the model, Cael. We don't just bet on the students the system prices wrong. We accept, going in, that some of those bets don't pay off. The ones that don't pay off don't sink us. Only the ones that do have to."

"And Quenna?" Cael asked. "Where does she sit in the ledger?"

Something shifted in Naveth's face — not quite a smile, but the acknowledgment of a question landing where questions were supposed to land. "Quenna is the one who priced you," he said. "She came to me with a file, an argument, and her professional reputation laid across the table as collateral, and she has been staking that reputation on irregular cases for as long as I've known her, which is a long time. She has never once been wrong on the merits." He paused, then gave Cael the rest of it, because the man apparently did not believe in withholding the rest of things. "She has occasionally been wrong about how much being right would cost. You should know that going in. If this goes badly, it goes badly for her before it goes badly for me, and it goes badly for you before either of us. That's the order of the ledger. I'd rather you heard it from me than worked it out on your own and wondered why nobody had said it."

Cael sat with that. Everything about it matched what he already knew — Quenna finding him at the Ironyard, the months of watching before she'd said a word, the offer built like an argument because an argument was what it would eventually have to survive. "Thank you," he said, and meant it more than the two words could carry.

"Don't thank me. Pass your assessments." Naveth stood, which was apparently how meetings ended in this office — no ceremony, just the practical conclusion of a man with other work waiting. "The door's open when you have questions worth both our time. You'll know the difference. You strike me as someone who generally does."

He wrote it down later, in the notebook, more or less verbatim: *Trust the ones who show you the ledger. Distrust the ones who only show you their heart.* Beneath it, smaller, after some thought: *Ask what happens if you're wrong. The honest ones will tell you. The rest change the subject.*

---

The rest of the day was a tour, conducted by a junior clerk with the brisk economy of someone who'd given this exact tour more times than she found interesting, and Cael filled pages as they walked — not idle notes but the dense, cross-referenced shorthand of someone building a map he intended to actually use.

The training hall came first. Smaller than the Ironyard by a comfortable margin — a single open floor rather than the sprawl he'd grown used to on the circuit, the whole space divided by chalk lines into supervised sections instead of left to the free-for-all churn a circuit yard ran on. The air held the same smell every training floor he'd ever stood on held, chalk and sweat and the faint scorch of somebody's Path discharging too close to the boards, and underneath it something the Ironyard had never had: quiet. Not silence — two of the sections were in use, and the sounds of drill work carried — but an ordered quiet, one voice at a time, instruction and response, nobody shouting odds from a rail. He counted the sections without meaning to: six, each large enough for a pair of practitioners and an instructor, none large enough for the crowd a marquee bout on the Ardenmere circuit used to draw. That told him something too. Whatever training happened here, it happened in front of an audience of one supervising adult and whoever else was scheduled into the same block, not in front of the district. He wasn't sure yet whether that was a loss or a relief. He wrote down that he'd need more days to know.

The residence wing sat between the training hall and the lecture wing, and it wore its retrofit honestly: corridors that bent where no architect would have bent them, because they followed the bones of whatever the waystation had needed the building for first; doors of six different vintages; a common room on the ground floor built into what had clearly once been a stable, the old hay-door bricked in but still legible in the wall like a scar that had healed well. His own room was narrow, whitewashed, furnished with a bed, a desk, a shelf, and a window that looked at the training yard's wall — which suited him, since a wall asked nothing and told nothing. Lira's was two doors down. Brom's was across the corridor. The clerk recited this arrangement as routine allocation. Cael, who had read the intake roster upside down on her clipboard, knew there had been rooms open in three other halls, and noted that someone — Quenna, almost certainly — had decided the three of them were a unit before any of them had thought to ask.

The lecture wing was the newest structure on the grounds by a wide margin, and it looked it — clean-cut stone rather than the weathered coursing the wall and the training hall shared, the only building on the whole complex that seemed to have been built with Greyvane itself in mind rather than inherited from whatever the waystation had needed centuries before anyone had thought to call this place an academy. Three floors of classrooms, and the clerk explained the organizing principle with the flat pride of someone reciting a fact she considered obviously correct: the rooms were sorted by Path family, not by tier. A Wind-adjacent lecture sat beside a Tide-adjacent one on the same floor, regardless of whether the students inside were Copper or Iron or, in the theoretical upper reaches nobody at Greyvane had much cause to teach, Silver. Cael worked at this as they climbed the stairwell. Most academies he'd read about — and he'd read about a great many, in the months before this offer had arrived — sorted by tier, because tier was the axis institutions actually cared about ranking students along. Sorting by Path family instead suggested an institution more interested in what a student could eventually understand than in where they currently stood, and he thought that told him something true about Naveth's clerical-error philosophy that the man himself hadn't quite said out loud.

The day's rhythm assembled itself around them as they went. The bells, the clerk explained, ran the whole grounds: first bell for the morning assembly and any scheduled assessments, second and third for lecture blocks, fourth for the midday meal, fifth and sixth for the supervised training floors, a seventh that meant nothing except that the kitchens were closing and anyone who hadn't eaten had made a decision. Students moved between the buildings in the loose, unhesitating patterns of people who no longer had to think about where they were going, and Cael watched them with something adjacent to envy — not of any one of them, but of the collective certainty, the being-expected-somewhere that structured every hour of their day. He had run his own schedule for a year and a half, built it himself out of training blocks and circuit dates and the discipline of nobody checking. It had worked. It had also been, he could admit now, watching a boy his own age jog toward the lecture wing because a bell told him to, a weight he hadn't known he was carrying: being one's own institution, every day, with no one to be late for.

The yard was last on the ground-floor circuit, walled separately from the training hall proper, open to the sky in a way the enclosed sections weren't. Formal ground, clearly — space built for scheduled matches with an audience rather than the constant, half-supervised churn of practice bouts that had filled every daylight hour of the Ironyard. He stood at its edge a moment, picturing what a demonstration assessment would actually look like here — himself, alone, in front of a panel, in a space this deliberately formal — and found the thought less frightening than he'd expected. A formal space, at least, had rules everyone could see. He'd learned to prefer visible rules to invisible ones, even when the visible ones were harder.

And the archive.

It was the oldest structure on the grounds by a wide margin — the waystation's original records room, never emptied when the conversion to an academy happened sixty years ago, simply absorbed into the new institution the way old buildings sometimes got absorbed rather than demolished. The door was original too, or looked it: iron-banded, darker with age than anything else on the grounds, and set into a doorway that predated the wall repairs he'd noted at the gate by what looked like a century at least.

The woman guarding it introduced herself as Prynn, seventies by the look of her, precise in a way that seemed to extend to everything about her — the set of her shoulders, the flat neatness of her grey hair, down to the exact angle at which she held the door open. Not fully open. Open enough to be polite to a touring student, not open enough to suggest anyone should feel invited to wander in unescorted. Cael noted the angle the way he noted most small deliberate choices people made without announcing them as deliberate.

"Waystation records," she said, when Cael asked, in the tone of someone answering a question she'd been asked exactly this way many times before, by many students who'd asked it exactly this way and then never once acted on the answer. "Pre-academy. Some of it predates the Compact's standardization entirely." Behind her, in the narrow slice of the room the door's angle permitted him to see, he caught the impression of shelving stretching back further than the doorway suggested the building should hold — the optical trick of a room organized with total efficiency, every inch accounted for, nothing wasted on aisles wider than a single person needed to pass through. The air that reached him through the gap was cooler than the corridor's, and carried paper and binding-glue and stone, a smell he recognized from Vell's archive in Ardenmere the way a person recognizes a voice before a face.

She looked at him then with an attention that felt, for a moment, less like a tour-guide's practiced patience and more like actual assessment — the same frank, measuring look Naveth had given him that morning, though where Naveth's had been asking *what kind of risk are you*, hers seemed to be asking something closer to *what kind of reader are you.* "You'll want to come back," she said. "Most students never do. The ones who matter, always do, eventually."

He didn't understand the comment fully in the moment. He wrote it down anyway, on the theory — increasingly reliable, the more he practiced it — that the things he didn't fully understand yet were usually the things worth keeping. He glanced back at the door once, on his way out of the tour's last stop, and found Prynn still watching him go, with an expression he couldn't quite name and didn't try very hard to.

---

The main hall carried the assessment calendar on a board by the entrance, public, unglassed, available to anyone who cared to read it. Cael found his own name on it without having to search — *Demonstration Assessment, Unclassified Observer Track, monthly, first bell* — dated days out, his first evaluation already scheduled before he'd finished his first full day on the grounds.

He stood in front of the board longer than the information required, because there was something in the plainness of it — his name, in public, on a schedule, exactly the way Lira's and Brom's names sat on the same board under their own tracks — that did something to the small anxious part of him that had spent a year and a half assuming institutions preferred him invisible. He read the line twice. *Demonstration Assessment, Unclassified Observer Track, monthly, first bell.* No asterisk beside it, no smaller print explaining what he was in language different from what everyone else's entries used. Just a name, a category, a date. He thought about the certification office in Denvash, and the Compact clerks who'd handled him after, and every institution in between that had processed him with the gloved carefulness people used for something they weren't sure was safe to touch plainly. Greyvane did not seem interested in making him invisible, and it did not seem interested in handling him carefully either. It seemed interested in making him *scheduled* — ordinary, procedural, one more line on a board anyone could read — which was, he was beginning to understand, its own kind of visibility, and possibly the safer kind.

He found Brom on the defensive-branch floor an hour later, and the scene he walked into told him everything about how the day had gone for at least one of them.

A crowd had gathered at the floor's edge — not large, a dozen students at most, but attentive in the way a crowd got when it had stumbled onto something worth actually watching rather than something it had come to watch on purpose. Instructor Wray stood at the center of it: a woman of perhaps fifty, Shield Path by the stance she held even at rest, the balanced economy of someone whose whole discipline was built around not wasting a single unnecessary motion, every shift of her weight doing exactly the work it needed to do and nothing more. She had Brom running drills against a training partner twice his size — a broad-shouldered Iron-tier student who looked, from where Cael stood, like he outweighed Brom by a third and had at least four years of formal instruction behind him that Brom had never had — and she had been, by the look of the crowd's settled patience, watching him do it for far longer than the standard evaluation required.

Cael watched the last exchanges before the verdict came. The bigger student worked in the orthodox pattern of formal defensive training — pressure, angle, pressure again, each strike arriving from the textbook's approved geometry — and Brom met it the way Brom met everything, which was to say from underneath, letting the weight come to him and finding the half-inch of give that turned force into a loan he intended to repay with interest. Twice Wray stopped the drill with a single raised finger, said something too quiet to carry, and set it running again with some small condition changed — the partner's angle, the starting distance — and each time Cael recognized what she was doing, because it was what he did with the notebook: isolating a variable. She wasn't watching Brom fight. She was testing which parts of him were structure and which were habit.

The last exchange answered something for her. Brom took a strike that would have folded most defensive practitioners at the knees and absorbed it instead, the same give-and-return economy Cael had watched him refine across a year and a half of circuit fights, redirecting the weight of it into a counter that put his much larger partner briefly, visibly off balance. The crowd made a small collective sound. Wray didn't.

Cael arrived in time to hear the verdict.

"Whoever trained you," Wray said, stepping back from the floor as Brom straightened out of his stance, "taught you to survive being underestimated. That's a real skill. It got you this far." She studied him a moment longer. "I'm going to teach you what to do when you're not underestimated anymore. That's a different skill, and you don't have it yet, and it's going to make everything you've learned so far look easy by comparison."

Brom, later, walking back toward the residence wing, was quiet in the weighted way he went quiet when something mattered more than he wanted to say out loud. He held it most of the distance. Then he offered the assessment in five words, with the care of a person handling an unfamiliar object.

"I think I have a teacher."

Cael looked at him. It was, he understood without needing it explained, the first time since he'd known him that he'd heard Brom say the word without a trace of irony attached to it — no old joke about Velmere's paid instructors, no wry distance from the idea of formal teaching. Just the word, plain, and something in Brom's face that looked almost like he didn't fully trust it yet either.

"That's good," Cael said.

"Yeah." Brom kept walking, eyes on the middle distance. "She watched me for five minutes and found the thing I've spent two years hoping nobody would find. Then instead of using it, she told me she was going to fix it." He shook his head, once, slowly. "Nobody's ever offered to fix me before. Plenty of offers to use me. First one to fix."

"Are you going to let her?"

"I already decided," Brom said. "Somewhere around the second time she stopped the drill. I just hadn't caught up to the decision yet." He glanced sideways at Cael. "That happen to you? Deciding something before you notice you've decided it?"

"Constantly," Cael said. "I keep a notebook mostly to find out what I've already decided."

Brom made the low sound that served him for a laugh. "Yeah," he said. "Yeah, I think it is good."

At dinner, in the converted-stable common room, Lira slid onto the bench across from them with a tray and the loose-shouldered ease of someone whose day had gone better than she'd braced for. She'd walked the town below the hill between her scheduled blocks — "smaller than it looks from the road, and the road makes it look small" — and had already located, with the unerring instinct of a circuit fighter, the one baker worth knowing and the two students on her re-certification track's shared roster. "There are others," she said, and something in how she said it made both of them look up. "Re-certification cases. Not many. But I'm not the only name on the list, which means somewhere there's a procedure that got used before me and will get used after me." She addressed her food for a moment. "You spend two years thinking you're the only person a thing ever happened to. Turns out there's a *track*."

"Good or bad?" Brom asked.

"Ask me in a month," Lira said, which from Lira was nearly optimism.

Cael wrote it down that night, in the log, under a heading he'd started keeping without quite deciding to: things that were changing that he wanted to remember had changed. Brom had a teacher. Lira had signed a paper she hated and meant it. He had a name on a schedule, in public, on a wall anyone could read. He added a fourth line, after some thought, because the tour had earned its own place in the record: an old woman who kept a room full of records older than the Compact, and had told him, without quite explaining why, that he'd be back.

Small things. He was old enough now, or had been through enough, to know that small things were usually where the real changes actually happened — quietly, in the margins, long before anyone thought to write the large version down. He closed the log, and lay awake a while longer than he meant to, listening to a building settle into sounds he didn't recognize yet, the way he'd once lain awake listening to a different house's sounds the night before his Kindling, in a life that already felt like it belonged to someone several versions of himself ago.

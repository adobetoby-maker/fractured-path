# Chapter 17 — Registry Law

The archive smelled the same at every hour of the day, which was one of the first things Cael catalogued about it and one of the few things about the next three weeks that stayed constant. Old paper, older stone, the particular dry mineral note of ink that had finished being ink a long time ago and become something closer to a permanent stain on the world. He came to know that smell the way he'd once come to know the smell of a training yard before a bout — as a signal that the real work was about to start.

Prynn set him up at the long table nearest the founding-era shelving, not because it was convenient but because, she told him on the first morning, "you'll want to be close to the older material once you understand what you're actually looking for, and you don't understand that yet." She was right, though it took him four days to prove her right, which was longer than he liked and exactly as long as the problem required.

He treated the registry code the way he treated an opponent he hadn't fought yet: read everything available before touching any single detail, build the shape of the whole before chasing any one detail, trust that patterns showed themselves to people patient enough to sit still for them. Four hundred years of charter law, amendment by amendment, the language shifting generation to generation the way a fighter's style shifted across a career — cautious in the old sections, confident in the middle ones, defensive again in the most recent amendments, as if whoever kept revising the code had learned, the hard way, exactly how much trouble a badly worded clause could cause a century later.

---

The architecture of it, once he could see the architecture of it, was almost elegant.

Charter law sat at the top — the founding document, the reason the Compact existed at all, broad and philosophical in the way founding documents always were. Beneath it, the classification schedule: the actual working list, revised and re-revised, of every Path, every tier, every provisional and disputed category the Compact had ever formally recognized. Beneath that, practitioner obligations — what a classified individual owed the system once the system had named them. And beneath all of it, at the bottom, where the real teeth lived: enforcement provisions, the mechanisms by which the Compact made any of the rest of it matter.

He drew it in the notebook as a structure, the way he'd once drawn Ember Path's lattice or Iron Skin's declaration timing, because a structure was a structure regardless of what it was built out of. Four layers, load-bearing, each one resting on the one beneath it. And a structure, like any structure, had a foundation. If the foundation didn't hold, nothing built on top of it held either, however solid it looked from a distance.

He spent two full days just reading the schedule. Every Path family, every tier designation, every provisional category the Compact had ever tentatively recognized before deciding whether to keep it — Contested-Standing, Interim-Assessed, half a dozen others that had come and gone across four centuries as the Compact's understanding of what a Path even was kept slowly, awkwardly revising itself. Each one named. Each one written down, in the schedule itself, in the flat administrative language that made a category real in the eyes of the law rather than merely real in the world.

He looked for [SHATTERED].

It was not there.

He read the schedule a second time, slower, in case he'd missed a subsection, an appendix, a cross-reference tucked into some amendment's margin. He had not missed it. He read it a third time on the fourth night, by lamplight, with Prynn silently refilling his tea at an hour when she had no professional obligation to still be in the building, and confirmed what the second read had already told him: the word did not appear anywhere in the four hundred years of accumulated schedule text. Not once. Not as a footnote, not as a deprecated entry, not even as something once proposed and later struck.

[SHATTERED] existed nowhere in the code. It existed only in the output an Arbiter's instrument produced at the moment of a Kindling — a flag the system itself generated, that four hundred years of enforcement practice had simply treated as though it were a classification, because nobody in four hundred years had apparently thought to check.

He sat with that for a long time before he wrote it down.

---

"You've stopped moving," Prynn said, from somewhere behind the reference desk, in the tone of a woman who'd been quietly monitoring his stillness for longer than she was admitting to.

"I found something."

"Most people who say that to me in this room have found a spelling variant they think is significant. Which is it."

He turned the notebook around so she could see the page — the four-layer structure, the schedule's full list copied out beside it, and the single word circled at the bottom with nothing connecting it to anything above. "The classification schedule is enumerated. Closed. Every category the Compact has ever asserted authority over is written down in it, by name, because that's how the code grants jurisdiction in the first place — you can't enforce a category the schedule doesn't list, because the schedule *is* what makes a category exist for enforcement purposes." He tapped the circled word. "This isn't in it. It's never been in it. It's Arbiter output and four hundred years of custom, and custom isn't code."

Prynn came around the desk and looked at the page for longer than the argument, on its face, should have required. When she looked up, something in her expression had shifted — not surprise, exactly. The quiet stillness of someone recalibrating how seriously to take a fifteen-year-old.

"Sixty years I've kept these shelves," she said, finally, dry as the dust she was famous for tolerating without complaint. "You're the first person to read the code the way it's written instead of the way it's enforced."

"Is that a compliment."

"It's an observation. Whether it's also a compliment depends entirely on what you do with it next." She set his tea down, precisely, in the small clear space he'd left for it at the corner of the table, the way she'd been doing every night for a week without either of them commenting on it. "Most people who find a seam this deep in the code go looking for a lawyer to argue it for them. You should think carefully about why that isn't what you're doing."

He had thought about it. He'd thought about very little else, in the gaps between reading, for two straight nights.

---

The outline took him the better part of the fifth day to draft, and most of the sixth to be honest with himself about what it actually said.

He had started, on the fifth day, trying to write an argument that his enrollment was legitimate — that the observer category, however awkward a fit, was defensible under a generous enough reading of the provision's language. He got most of a page into it before he stopped, crossed out the whole page in one long diagonal line, and sat with the notebook closed for the better part of an hour.

The enrollment was not defensible. He had known that from the first day, from the moment Quenna had walked him through the category and told him plainly that the words on the form didn't match the truth underneath them. Trying to argue otherwise would mean defending a lie in front of a magistrate whose entire professional function was catching people defending lies. It would lose, and it would deserve to lose, and worse than losing, it would waste the only real weapon he'd found in five days of reading.

He opened the notebook again and started the outline over, and this time he wrote the sentence he'd been avoiding since the moment he'd circled the word on the schedule two nights before.

*I am not going to argue that my enrollment is valid. I am going to argue that the Compact has never had authority over what I am, and that everything they have ever done to someone like me — the expulsion four instances back through history, the market interview, my own Kindling report, all four hundred years of it — was built on a word the law never actually wrote down.*

He read the sentence three times. Then he understood, sitting alone at the long table with the lamp burning low and the founding-era shelves at his back, exactly how large the thing he was about to do actually was.

If the argument won, it would not simply preserve one workaround enrollment at one minor academy. It would put a crack — public, on the record, with his name attached to it — directly into four hundred years of machinery that had been running, unquestioned, on custom instead of text. Every Compact office that had ever expelled, monitored, or flagged someone under that word would have to reckon, eventually, with the fact that a fifteen-year-old had read their own code more carefully than they had.

It was, he understood, the kind of argument that made enemies rather than merely losing gracefully. It was also, as far as three weeks of reading had shown him, the only argument that was actually true.

He sat with the outline a long time, the lamp burning down toward the hour Prynn usually made him leave. Then he picked the pen back up, and kept writing.

---

*The code is architecture,* he wrote, near the bottom of a page already crowded with the day's notes, in the careful hand he reserved for things that mattered. *Architecture has seams. I have spent two years learning how to find the seam in anything that has one — a fighter's stance, a Path's declaration timing, a system's own rules about what it's allowed to do to people. This is the same work. It just doesn't leave bruises.*

He closed the notebook. Outside, past the archive's single high window, the Greyvane grounds had gone fully dark, the kind of dark that meant Prynn would be along any minute now to remind him, without needing to say it twice, that even the most important argument of his life could survive being continued tomorrow.

Two weeks left. He had the seam. What remained was building an argument sturdy enough to stand in it — the same discipline he'd spent two years learning on a training floor, applied now to something with no third exchange, no tell to read, nothing but four hundred years of paper and the same patient work of proving it wrong.

He kept writing.

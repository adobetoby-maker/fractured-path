# Chapter 17 — Registry Law

The archive smelled the same at every hour of the day, which was one of the first things Cael catalogued about it and one of the few things about the next three weeks that stayed constant. Old paper, older stone, the dry mineral note of ink that had finished being ink a long time ago and become something closer to a permanent stain on the world. He came to know that smell the way he'd once come to know the smell of a training yard before a bout — as a signal that the real work was about to start.

Prynn set him up at the long table nearest the founding-era shelving, not because it was convenient but because, she told him on the first morning, "you'll want to be close to the older material once you understand what you're actually looking for, and you don't understand that yet." She was right, though it took him four days to prove her right, which was longer than he liked and exactly as long as the problem required.

She laid down the rules of the room before she laid down a single volume. Nothing left open overnight. Nothing stacked more than three high. Ink at the far end of the table, never near the bindings. A slip of blank paper marking every place he pulled from, so that a shelf sixty years in her keeping never spent an hour uncertain about what it was missing. He absorbed the rules the way he absorbed a new instructor's floor discipline — without argument, because a floor's discipline was information about the floor, and this floor had kept four hundred years of paper alive through fire seasons, damp winters, and the founding-era roof, which Prynn described, with visible resentment, as "an ongoing negotiation."

"Most students come in here wanting an answer," she said, setting the first volume of the Compact charter in front of him with both hands, the way other people handled newborns or unexploded things. "They want the one page that settles their question so they can leave. You will not find one page. If your question could be settled by one page, the Compact would have settled it two hundred years ago, because I promise you that you are not the first person to be angry at this code."

"I'm not angry at it."

She looked at him over the top of the volume, reassessing something. "No," she said, after a moment. "You're not, are you. What are you, then?"

He thought about how to say it accurately, because Prynn had already shown herself to be a person who noticed the difference between accurate and approximate. "I'm studying it," he said. "The way I'd study anyone I was going to fight."

Prynn considered that answer for longer than it seemed to require. Then she went back to her desk, and returned with a second volume, and a third, and did not stop until the stack beside him stood eleven high — three past her own rule, which she did not acknowledge and he did not point out.

"Then you'll want the amendment concordance as well," she said. "Fighters watch how an opponent moves before they watch where he's standing."

---

He treated the registry code the way he treated an opponent he hadn't fought yet: read everything available before touching any single question, build the shape of the whole before chasing any one detail, trust that patterns showed themselves to people patient enough to sit still for them.

The first two days produced almost nothing but the shape — and the shape, he came to understand, was itself the first real finding. Four hundred years of charter law, amendment by amendment, the language shifting generation to generation the way a fighter's style shifted across a career: cautious in the old sections, where the Compact was still a treaty among guilds that did not entirely trust each other; confident in the middle centuries, where its authority had stopped being argued about; defensive again in the most recent amendments, hedged and cross-referenced and qualified, as if whoever kept revising the code had learned, the hard way, exactly how much trouble a badly worded clause could cause a century later.

He filled nine pages of the notebook with nothing but the code's history of flinching. Every defensive amendment was a scar, and a scar was a record of a wound, and a wound meant that at some point, somewhere, somebody had found a gap in the text and driven something through it hard enough to make the Compact bleed. He catalogued the scars the way he'd catalogue an opponent's guard: not because any single one of them was his opening, but because the pattern of where a system protected itself told you where it had learned to be afraid — and, more usefully, where it had never learned to be.

The enforcement provisions were dense with scars. The classification schedule had almost none.

He noted that on the second evening and did not yet understand what it meant. Either nobody had ever attacked the schedule, or nobody had ever needed to. It would take him two more days to learn that there was a third possibility, and that the third possibility was the whole case.

He made one wrong turn first, and it cost him most of the third day. The obvious road — the road anyone would take — was the observer provision itself: its drafting history, its amendments, every prior enrollment that had leaned on its language. He walked that road for eleven hours, tracing the provision back through three revisions to its origin as a kindness written for the children of traveling guild families, and reached the end of it certain of exactly one thing. The road went nowhere. However generously the provision was read, it had been written for the unkindled, and he was not unkindled, and no amount of history changed what the words said. Coss would have walked the same road months ago. Coss would be waiting at the end of it.

He crossed out eleven hours of notes in one long diagonal line and wrote beneath them: *Stop studying my own position. Study his. Where does his authority actually come from?*

It was the same correction Lira had beaten into him a year and a half ago, on a training floor, in a different language: *stop watching your own feet.*

---

The architecture of it, once he could finally see the architecture of it, was almost elegant.

Charter law sat at the top — the founding document, the reason the Compact existed at all, broad and philosophical in the way founding documents always were, full of words like *stewardship* and *the ordered development of practice* that committed nobody to anything measurable. Beneath it, the classification schedule: the actual working list, revised and re-revised across four centuries, of every Path, every tier, every provisional and disputed category the Compact had ever formally recognized. Beneath that, practitioner obligations — what a classified individual owed the system once the system had named them: registration, assessment, conduct standards, the whole quiet apparatus of belonging to something whether or not you had asked to. And beneath all of it, at the bottom, where the real teeth lived: enforcement provisions, the mechanisms by which the Compact made any of the rest of it matter — voidance, sanction, removal, the machinery that had emptied a boarding-house room in Denvash inside a single morning two years of living ago.

He drew it in the notebook as a structure, the way he'd once drawn Ember Path's lattice or Iron Skin's declaration timing, because a structure was a structure regardless of what it was built out of. Four layers, load-bearing, each one resting on the one beneath it. Enforcement rested on obligations. Obligations attached to the classified. Classification was created by the schedule. The schedule drew its life from the charter.

And a structure, like any structure, had a foundation. If the foundation didn't hold, nothing built on top of it held either, however solid it looked from a distance, however many centuries it had stood without anyone thinking to lean on it and check.

He found the load-bearing sentence on the fourth morning, in the charter's definitions clause, in language so plain that he read past it twice before its weight registered. *Classification, for all purposes under this charter and its subordinate provisions, shall mean designation under the Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings, as amended.* Shall mean. Not *shall include*. Not *shall generally correspond to*. The charter did not describe classification. It defined it — closed the word, sealed it, made the schedule the single door through which any category had to pass before the Compact's authority could touch the person standing in it.

That was why the schedule had no scars. Nobody had ever attacked it, because the schedule was not a wall. It was the ground.

He spent the next two full days on the schedule itself, reading it the way he had never read anything, and he had spent a year and a half learning to read things that were trying not to be read. Every Path family, every tier designation. Every provisional category the Compact had ever tentatively recognized before deciding whether to keep it — Contested-Standing, Interim-Assessed, Dual-Registered, half a dozen others that had come and gone across four centuries as the Compact's understanding of what a Path even was kept slowly, awkwardly revising itself. He read the amendments in sequence and watched categories be born in one generation's confidence and struck in another's embarrassment, and noted that even the struck ones remained in the text, lined through but legible, because the code preserved its own mistakes as scrupulously as its rules. Each category named. Each one written down, in the schedule itself, in the flat administrative language that made a thing real in the eyes of the law rather than merely real in the world.

He looked for [SHATTERED].

It was not there.

He read the schedule a second time, slower, in case he'd missed a subsection, an appendix, a cross-reference tucked into some amendment's margin. He built a checklist of every place a category could legally live — the main enumeration, the provisional annex, the disputed-standings appendix, the amendment schedules, the definitions cross-index — and cleared them one by one, the way he'd clear an opponent's possible counters one by one until what remained was not a guess but a residue. He had not missed it. He read it a third time on the fourth night, by lamplight, with Prynn silently refilling his tea at an hour when she had no professional obligation to still be in the building, and confirmed what the second read had already told him: the word did not appear anywhere in four hundred years of accumulated schedule text. Not once. Not as a footnote, not as a deprecated entry, not as a struck line preserved in the record of its own repeal. Not even as something once proposed and voted down — and the schedule recorded its rejections, too; he had found three of them, categories argued for and refused, their refusals documented with the same administrative thoroughness as everything else.

[SHATTERED] existed nowhere in the code. It existed only in the output an Arbiter's instrument produced at the moment of a Kindling — a flag the system itself generated, that four hundred years of enforcement practice had simply treated as though it were a classification, because nobody in four hundred years had apparently thought to check.

He sat with that for a long time before he wrote it down.

He sat with it because it was too large to trust on a first reading, and because the fights that had cost him most were the ones where he had committed to a seam before confirming it was real. Somewhere in this building, or in some Compact records hall three districts away, there had to be the page he'd missed — the amendment, the annex, the enabling order that made the flag legal. Systems did not run for four centuries on nothing.

Except that they did. He knew that they did. He had watched a circuit run for generations on customs no charter anywhere had written down; he had watched a market interview conducted under an authority nobody present could have cited; he had watched his own life rerouted at fourteen by a morning's worth of procedure that everyone in the room had treated as law because everyone in the room had always treated it as law. Custom wearing law's coat was not the exception in the world he had grown up in. As far as he could tell, it was most of what the world was wearing.

He wrote it down.

---

"You've stopped moving," Prynn said, from somewhere behind the reference desk, in the tone of a woman who'd been quietly monitoring his stillness for longer than she was admitting to.

"I found something."

"Most people who say that to me in this room have found a spelling variant they think is significant. Which is it."

He turned the notebook around so she could see the page — the four-layer structure, the schedule's full enumeration copied out beside it, and the single word circled at the bottom with nothing connecting it to anything above. "The classification schedule is enumerated. Closed. Every category the Compact has ever asserted authority over is written down in it, by name, because that's how the code grants jurisdiction in the first place — the charter's definitions clause says *classification shall mean designation under the schedule*. Not resemble. Not imply. Mean. You can't enforce a category the schedule doesn't list, because the schedule is what makes a category exist for enforcement purposes at all." He tapped the circled word. "This isn't in it. It's never been in it. It's Arbiter output and four hundred years of custom, and custom isn't code."

Prynn came around the desk and looked at the page for longer than the argument, on its face, should have required. She read the enumeration he'd copied. She read the definitions clause, twice, her lips moving slightly on the second pass the way they did when she was checking a citation against her own memory of the shelf it lived on. When she looked up, something in her expression had shifted — not surprise, exactly. The quiet stillness of someone recalibrating how seriously to take a fifteen-year-old.

"Sixty years I've kept these shelves," she said, finally, dry as the dust she was famous for tolerating without complaint. "You're the first person to read the code the way it's written instead of the way it's enforced."

"Is that a compliment."

"It's an observation. Whether it's also a compliment depends entirely on what you do with it next." She set his tea down, precisely, in the small clear space he'd left for it at the corner of the table, the way she'd been doing every night for a week without either of them commenting on it. "Most people who find a seam this deep in the code go looking for a lawyer to argue it for them. You should think carefully about why that isn't what you're doing."

"There's no lawyer within three districts who'd take it," he said. "And if there were, it would become the lawyer's argument. The panel would weigh it as a professional's maneuver — something purchased. It has to come from me, or it reads as a trick instead of a reading."

"That's the strategic answer." Prynn folded her hands. "I asked for the real one."

He was quiet for a moment. The lamp ticked as it cooled somewhere in its brass.

"The system decided what I was without asking me," he said. "Every argument anyone has ever made about me was made over my head — assessors, wardens, a magistrate I never met signing an order I never saw. If someone else stands up in that room and argues this for me, then even the argument that the system never had the right to speak for me gets spoken for me." He looked at the circled word. "I've read the code now. I get to say it myself."

Prynn was silent long enough that he thought the conversation had ended and she had simply declined to leave. Then she picked up the empty tea tin, and said, on her way back to the desk, without turning around: "Fourth shelf, second case. Enforcement digests, the complete run. If you're going to say it yourself, you had better know every case they'll say back."

He had thought about it. He'd thought about very little else, in the gaps between reading, for two straight nights.

---

Lira came for him on the eighth day, as promised and without appointment, appearing at the archive door at an hour Prynn's posted schedule labeled *quiet study* with an expression that indicated she had read the posted schedule and made her decision anyway.

"Yard," she said. "Now. You have ink on your face and your shoulders are up around your ears."

"I'm in the middle of the enforcement digests."

"You're in the middle of becoming a piece of furniture." She held the door. "Ten days ago I told the table I'd keep you a fighter. That wasn't a threat about someday. Yard."

The dusk drills were short, and she kept them old — first-year patterns, footwork he could have run asleep, the evasion frames from before either of them had ever heard the word Greyvane. She wasn't training him for anything and they both knew it. She was doing maintenance, on something neither of them named, and by the third sequence he could feel exactly how much the week had taken and hidden the taking of: the stiffness across his back, the tenth-of-a-beat lag in his pivot, the way his eyes kept wanting to fix on a point instead of soften to the whole floor.

"There he is," she said, when the lag finally closed, somewhere in the fourth sequence. "You went missing around Tuesday. I thought I'd better check the archive hadn't kept you."

"The argument needs the archive."

"The argument needs the arguer." She stepped back, drills done, breathing easy. "You want to know what I think? You've been telling everyone this is a fight and you're studying it like a fight, and that's the right idea done half right. Because you don't study an opponent by giving him your whole body for three weeks. You study him, and you eat, and you sleep, and you stay yourself, so that the person who finally steps onto the floor is the one who trained all year — not the one who spent three weeks in a chair rehearsing." She tossed him a cloth for the ink on his face. "Same time in three days. Don't make me come get you."

He went back inside. But he noted, at the bottom of that day's page, in the interest of accuracy: *Correction logged. The opponent has no body, but I still have one. Maintain it.*

---

The outline took him the better part of the tenth day to draft, and most of the eleventh to be honest with himself about what it actually said.

He had started, that first drafting morning, trying to write an argument that his enrollment was legitimate anyway — a fallback line, a second position to retreat to, the instinct of a fighter who never entered a floor with only one exit. He got most of a page into it before he stopped, crossed out the whole page in one long diagonal line, and sat with the notebook closed for the better part of an hour.

The enrollment was not defensible. He had known that from the first day at Greyvane, from the moment Quenna had walked him through the category and told him plainly that the words on the form didn't match the truth underneath them. Trying to argue otherwise would mean defending a lie in front of a magistrate whose entire professional function was catching people defending lies. It would lose, and it would deserve to lose, and worse than losing, it would spend the panel's patience and poison everything he said after it. A fallback position that weakened the main position wasn't an exit. It was a hole in his own guard, cut by his own hand.

There could be no second argument. Which meant the first one had to be built the way Brom built a stance — nothing decorative, nothing he couldn't hold under full pressure, every joint load-bearing or gone.

He opened the notebook again and started the outline over, and this time he wrote the sentence he'd been avoiding since the night he'd circled the word on the schedule.

*I am not going to argue that my enrollment is valid. I am going to argue that the Compact has never had authority over what I am — and that everything it has ever done to someone like me was built on a word the law never actually wrote down.*

Then, because an argument was only as strong as the record it would be tested against, he went to Prynn's fourth shelf and pulled the enforcement digests, and spent two days finding out what the Compact had actually done to people like him.

Four instances. That was the entire recorded history — four practitioners across four centuries who had drawn the flag he carried, each one documented with the enforcement provisions' usual thoroughness. He read all four files in full, taking his time, because these were the only people in the history of the written record who had ever stood where he stood, and the least he owed them was a careful reading. Not one of them had been argued for. Not one had received a hearing of any kind — removal in every case had run on administrative order, unopposed, complete within days. Three of the four files simply ended, the way files ended when the system stopped finding their subjects worth the ink. The fourth ended with a single line of closure notation he read six times and copied out whole, because it was the entire life of a person, compressed by procedure into eleven words, and he did not intend to let himself forget it.

None of the four had ever gotten the chance to answer. He would be the first. He wrote that down too, and found his hand was not entirely steady doing it, and let the unsteadiness into the letters rather than waiting it out, because Vell had taught him that a record that hid what the recorder felt was only half a record.

Then he understood, sitting alone at the long table with the lamp burning low and the founding-era shelves at his back, exactly how large the thing he was preparing to do actually was.

If the argument won, it would not simply preserve one workaround enrollment at one minor academy. It would put a crack — public, on the record, with his name attached to it — directly into four hundred years of machinery that had been running, unquestioned, on custom instead of text. Every Compact office that had ever expelled, monitored, or flagged someone under that word would have to reckon, eventually, with the fact that a fifteen-year-old had read their own code more carefully than they had. And every official who reckoned with it would know his name, because the record that carried the argument would carry the arguer.

It was the kind of argument that made enemies rather than merely losing gracefully. It was also, as far as everything he had read could show him, the only argument that was actually true.

He sat with the outline a long time, the lamp burning down toward the hour Prynn usually made him leave. Then he picked the pen back up, and kept writing.

---

*The code is architecture,* he wrote, near the bottom of a page already crowded with the day's notes, in the hand he reserved for things that mattered. *Architecture has seams. I have spent a year and a half learning how to find the seam in anything that has one — a fighter's stance, a Path's declaration timing, a system's own rules about what it's allowed to do to people. This is the same work. It just doesn't leave bruises.*

He looked at the last line, and then, in the interest of accuracy, amended it:

*It leaves them later. On whoever loses.*

He closed the notebook. Outside, past the archive's single high window, the Greyvane grounds had gone fully dark, the settled dark that meant Prynn would be along any minute now to remind him, without needing to say it twice, that even the most important argument of his life could survive being continued tomorrow.

Two weeks left. He had the seam. He had the four files of the people who'd never been given the chance to stand in it. What remained was building an argument sturdy enough to hold a boy's whole weight in front of a Compact panel — the same discipline he'd spent his training years learning on a floor, applied now to an opponent made of paper: no tells, no third exchange, nothing to read but four hundred years of text, and the same patient work of proving it had been beatable all along.

He kept writing.

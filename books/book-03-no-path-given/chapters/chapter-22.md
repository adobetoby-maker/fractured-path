# Chapter 22 — No Path Given

The lecture hall was full again by first bell, and this time nobody was pretending it was for anything other than what it actually was.

Cael sat at the respondent's table alone. Quenna had offered, the night before, to sit beside him for the second sitting the way she had for the first, and he'd asked her not to — not out of any lack of gratitude, but because the argument he was about to make had to read, unmistakably, as his own. Not an academy's argument, wearing his name. His. He'd spent three weeks in an archive earning the right to say that honestly, and he intended to stand behind it the same way.

Three weeks of Prynn's shelves sat in a stack of tabbed pages on the table in front of him. He barely touched them the entire session. It was all resident by now — drilled past memory into something closer to reflex, the same way Lira's evasion patterns had become resident in his hands rather than his thoughts. He had studied the code the way he studied everything that mattered: until it stopped being information and started being something he simply knew how to move through.

"The floor is the respondent's," Yorlan said, and sat back.

Cael stood.

---

**First movement.**

"I'm not going to defend the enrollment," he said, and let that land before continuing. "I want to be clear about that from the start, because I think the panel deserves honesty more than it deserves a clever argument that avoids the point. Warden Coss's case yesterday was correct. The provision was written for the unkindled. I am not unkindled. As categorized, the enrollment is invalid. I concede all of it."

The gallery did not make a sound. Cael did not look back at it — he'd promised himself, going in, that he'd keep his eyes on the panel and nowhere else — but he felt the stillness anyway, the particular quality of a room that had just watched its expectations fall out from under it in a single sentence.

At the delegation's table, Coss went very still. Total concession was not a move his preparation had a prepared answer for, and Cael understood, watching him not react, that this was exactly why it was the correct opening. A man who had spent a day building an airtight case had nothing left to attack once the case had already been granted.

"I'm not here to argue the enrollment is valid," Cael said. "I'm here to argue the Compact never had the authority to void it in the first place."

---

**Second movement.**

"Voiding an enrollment," Cael said, "is an exercise of enforcement authority. That's not my characterization — it's the code's own structure. Enforcement authority under the charter attaches to classified practitioners. Section two, subsection four, and every provision built on top of it since. Classification, under the same architecture, is created by one thing and one thing only: the enumerated schedule."

He opened the code to the tabbed page he'd marked three days ago, and did not read from memory, though he could have. He wanted the panel watching him find it on the page, in front of them, in the Compact's own document.

"I'd like the panel to help me find [SHATTERED] in the schedule."

He read it. All of it. Every Path family, every tier designation, every provisional and disputed category the Compact had ever formally asserted authority over, name by name, in the flat administrative language of the code itself, working through amendment after amendment, decade after decade of additions and revisions and clarifications, until the reading took on its own weight in the room — a silence that was no longer surprised, now, but something closer to attentive, the gallery beginning to understand, sentence by sentence, exactly what he was building toward.

It took a long time. That was the point.

"[SHATTERED] is not there," he said, when he finished. "It is not in the schedule. It has never been in the schedule. It exists in Arbiter output. It exists in four hundred years of enforcement practice that has treated it as a classification. It has never once existed in the text the Compact's own charter says is the *only* source of classification authority." He closed the code. "The system that removed me from my home at fourteen, that has monitored me for two years, that filed the challenge against this enrollment — ran entirely on precedent and custom. It never once ran on the law it claims to be enforcing."

---

**Third movement.**

"I want to be precise about what I'm arguing," Cael said, "because precision is the only thing that's earned me the right to stand here. I am not arguing that [SHATTERED] is a mistake, or that it should be removed, or that the Compact was wrong to be concerned about whatever it thinks the flag means. I am arguing something much narrower, and much harder to dismiss: that a flag with no entry in the enumerated schedule confers no classification, and a practitioner with no classification cannot be subject to an enforcement authority that only attaches to classified practitioners. The Compact's own architecture makes that conclusion unavoidable. I didn't write the architecture. I only read it."

He let the silence hold a moment before finishing.

"The Compact's challenge says I have no path recognized under their system. I'm asking the panel to hold them to their own words. No path given — no authority attached. Their rules were never built to contain me. They can't use rules that don't contain me to remove me from somewhere I'm legitimately trying to be."

He sat down.

---

Coss's rebuttal came exactly where the rehearsal had predicted it would — custom, implication, the practical absurdity of a flag with no formal home nonetheless carrying four centuries of enforcement weight. He argued it well, because he argued everything well, laying out the case that the schedule's silence on [SHATTERED] was an oversight rather than an exclusion, that custom and Arbiter output together constituted a functional classification even absent formal enumeration, that no reasonable reading of the code could have intended to leave an entire category of practitioner entirely outside its authority.

It died on Ilsev.

She asked her questions the way she asked everything — surgical, aimed evenly at both sides, and devastating to exactly one of them, because her whole career, everything Cael remembered about her from a room two years behind him, had been built on the single unbending principle of following the text wherever the text actually went, rather than wherever anyone found it convenient to be.

"Warden Coss." Her voice cut cleanly through the room. "Cite the schedule entry."

Coss paused. "The custom is well-established, Assessor —"

"I didn't ask about the custom. I asked for the entry. Section, subsection, designation. Cite it."

The silence that followed was different from every silence that had come before it in the room — not surprise, not tension, but the particular quiet of a question with no answer, sitting in the open, in front of everyone.

"There isn't one," Coss said, finally, because he was, as he had always been, a man who told the truth even when it cost him. "I cannot cite an entry that does not exist."

---

Yorlan delivered the ruling with the same flat, colorless economy that had governed every word he'd spoken across two sittings, and the lack of drama in his voice was, somehow, the most devastating thing about it.

"The panel does not rule on the validity of the enrollment as categorized," he said. "That question is not properly before this tribunal, and remains, as it always has, an internal academic matter for Greyvane to resolve as it sees fit." He paused, the smallest pause, procedural rather than dramatic. "The panel finds that the Guilds Compact has not established authority, under the enumerated classification schedule, to void the enrollment through this challenge. The respondent's classification status is undefined under the current schedule. The challenge is dismissed for want of established jurisdiction."

Not a validation. Cael understood that immediately, cataloguing the ruling's precise edges the way he catalogued everything — the panel had not said his enrollment was legitimate. They had said only, narrowly, exactly, that the Compact could not prove it had the right to say otherwise. A crack, not a collapse. Contested, not resolved.

On the record. In public. Forever.

The gallery did not understand the distinction, or did not care about it, and either way the room went up around him in a single unbroken roar that seemed to physically move the air.

Cael sat very still at the respondent's table, hands flat on the wood, and let the sound wash past him without quite reaching the part of him that was still, for one more breath, entirely alone with what had just happened.

He saw Lira on her feet first, somewhere in the crowd's blur, already moving toward the table before the gavel had finished its second strike. He saw Brom, further back, nodding once, slowly — the particular nod of someone who had spent a year and more finding Cael's whole existence the most interesting problem he'd ever encountered, watching that problem win. He saw Karis, still seated, still writing, tears entirely unacknowledged on her face, her pen moving across the page as though the moment might slip away from her if she stopped documenting it even for a second. He saw Quenna, one row back, closing her eyes and releasing a breath that seemed to carry the entire semester's weight out of her at once. He saw Prynn, near the back wall, dust-dry and, for the first time since Cael had met her, entirely incandescent.

And he understood, before anyone reached the table, in the last breath of stillness he was going to get, exactly what he had just done. Four recorded instances of practitioners like him, across the whole history of the Compact's registry, and none of them — not one, in four hundred years — had ever gotten the chance to do what he had just done. He had not beaten the system. He had made the system say, in its own voice, on its own record, in front of everyone who would ever need to read it again — that it had never actually had the right.

# Chapter 22 — No Path Given

The lecture hall was full again by first bell, and this time nobody was pretending it was for anything other than what it actually was.

The gallery had grown overnight. Word of the first sitting's final minute had gone out of Greyvane's gate with the credentialed observers and come back before dawn with reinforcements — two more grey registry coats, a woman in adjudicator's black who sat apart from everyone and took no notes at all, a cluster of townsfolk from down the hill who could not have explained a word of charter law and had come anyway, because the town had adopted its academy's anomaly somewhere over the past season without ever holding a meeting about it. The benches were full an hour early. Prynn, who had appointed herself warden of the gallery's front row by right of seniority and glare, held four seats against all comers until Lira, Brom, and Karis filled three of them. The fourth she kept empty beside her, for reasons she declined to explain to anyone, and nobody had the courage to ask.

Cael sat at the respondent's table alone.

Quenna had offered, the night before, to sit beside him for the second sitting the way she had for the first, and he'd asked her not to — not out of any lack of gratitude, but because the argument he was about to make had to read, unmistakably, as his own. Not an academy's argument, wearing his name. Not an assessor's careful drafting with a boy attached. His. He'd spent three weeks in an archive earning the right to say that honestly, and he intended to stand behind it the same way. Quenna had heard him out, and studied him for a long moment, and then moved her chair to the row behind the table — close enough that he could feel her there, far enough that the record would show a respondent who answered for himself. It was, he understood, the last thing she could give him, and the hardest: the visible withdrawal of protection from a boy she had staked her career on protecting.

Three weeks of Prynn's shelves sat in a stack of tabbed pages on the table in front of him. He would barely touch them the entire session. It was all resident by now — drilled past memory into something closer to reflex, the way Lira's evasion patterns had become resident in his hands rather than his thoughts. He had studied the code the way he studied everything that mattered: until it stopped being information and started being something he simply knew how to move through.

The panel filed in at the bell. Yorlan. Ilsev, carrying — Cael marked it, and marked Coss marking it — her own copy of the registry code, which she had not brought to the first sitting. The grey-bearded third assessor. Havel at the recording desk below them, a fresh block of transcription paper squared in front of him like a fighter's taped hands.

Yorlan's opening took less than a minute. "Second sitting, same docket. The record stands as taken. The respondent stated at close of first sitting that he does not defend the enrollment and instead challenges the Compact's jurisdiction to void it. The panel has considered whether such a challenge is properly before it." A pause, flat as all his pauses. "It is. Jurisdiction may be raised at any time, by any party, because a tribunal that will not examine its own authority has none worth the name. The respondent may proceed. Under charter procedure his primary presentation may not be interrupted. The floor is the respondent's."

He sat back.

Cael stood.

The room's silence had a texture he knew — the indrawn stillness of a floor at the start of a bout, two hundred people declining to breathe at the same time. He had stood in this silence before, in smaller rooms, with more of his blood at stake and less of his life. He set his hands flat on the table for one heartbeat, the old grounding, Lira's yard at dusk in the muscles of it. *This is real. Start from that.*

Then he began.

---

**First movement.**

"I'm not going to defend the enrollment," he said, and let that land before continuing. "I want to be clear about that from the start, because I think the panel deserves honesty more than it deserves a clever argument that avoids the point. Warden Coss's case yesterday was correct. Every part of it. The provision was written for the unkindled — the drafting records he entered prove it, and I have no answer to them, and I'm not going to pretend I do. I am not unkindled. As categorized, the enrollment is invalid. I concede all of it."

The gallery did not make a sound. Cael did not look back at it — he'd promised himself, going in, that he'd keep his eyes on the panel and nowhere else — but he felt the stillness anyway, the quality of a room that had just watched its expectations fall out from under it in a single sentence. Somewhere behind him, he knew without looking, half of Greyvane's student body had just concluded he was surrendering.

Yorlan leaned forward slightly, and did the thing procedure required, which was also, though he could not have intended it, the thing that made the concession real in a way no sentence of Cael's could have. "The respondent understands that a concession entered on this record is not withdrawn. The panel will treat these points as established. Confirm."

"Confirmed, Magistrate. Established. All of them." Cael kept his voice level. "I'm not conceding them as a maneuver. I'm conceding them because they're true, and because my argument doesn't need them to be false. If my argument needed any true thing to be false, it would be the wrong argument."

At the delegation's table, Coss had gone very still. Not the stillness of composure — Cael had spent a year and a half learning the man's stillnesses, and this was the other one, the one from the yard's edge five days ago. Total concession was the single opening his preparation had no prepared answer for, and Cael understood, watching him not react, exactly why it was the correct opening. A man who had spent a full sitting building an airtight case had nothing left to attack once the case had been granted. Every stone Coss had laid yesterday was now the floor Cael was standing on.

"I'm not here to argue the enrollment is valid," Cael said. "I'm here to argue something before that question — underneath it. Voiding an enrollment is an act. Acts require authority. I'm here to ask where, in the Compact's own law, the authority for this one comes from. I'm here to argue that the Compact never had the authority to void it in the first place."

---

**Second movement.**

"Voiding an enrollment," Cael said, "is an exercise of enforcement authority. That's not my characterization — it's the code's own structure. The enforcement provisions are the fourth layer of a four-layer architecture, and each layer states what it rests on. Enforcement authority, under section two, subsection four, and every provision built on top of it since, attaches to *classified practitioners*. Not to people. Not to anomalies. To classified practitioners — the term is used forty-one times in the enforcement provisions, and it is never used loosely, because the drafters defined it and then trusted their own definition."

He lifted the charter volume, opened it to the first tab, and read the definitions clause into the record, slowly, the way Coss had read the provision the day before — twice, the second time at the same measured pace, so that the transcript and the room would hold every word of it.

"*Classification, for all purposes under this charter and its subordinate provisions, shall mean designation under the Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings, as amended.*" He set the volume down. "Shall mean. Not shall include. Not shall generally describe. The charter does not illustrate what classification is. It defines it, and closes the definition. Under the Compact's own founding text, there is exactly one way to be a classified practitioner, and it is to hold a designation that appears in the schedule. Whatever does not appear in the schedule is not a classification. Not informally. Not approximately. Not at all — because the charter reserved the word, and the charter is the only speaker with the standing to use it."

He paused, and looked at the panel — Yorlan expressionless, the third assessor writing, Ilsev entirely motionless with her own copy of the code open in front of her, one finger resting at what he was almost certain was the definitions clause.

"Which brings the panel to a factual question," Cael said. "Small, checkable, and, I'll grant, strange that it has apparently never been asked on any record before this one. My registry status is [SHATTERED]. Every document in the Compact's case says so. The challenge itself says so — it cites my status as the ground of the violation. So the question is this." He lifted the schedule volume. "I'd like the panel to help me find [SHATTERED] in the schedule."

He read it.

All of it.

He had planned this from the eleventh day in the archive, and rehearsed the decision more than the reading, because the reading needed no rehearsal and the decision had needed a great deal: it would have been faster to summarize, to assert the absence and invite the panel to verify it at leisure, and every instinct trained by every institution he had ever passed through said that a fifteen-year-old does not stand in front of a Compact tribunal and read a statutory schedule at it for the better part of an hour. Which was, in the end, the reason to do it. An assertion could be doubted. A summary could be argued with. But an absence — a real absence, in a closed list — could only be proven one way: by walking the whole boundary of the list in public, with every door and window of it in view, until the room itself had witnessed that nothing was inside.

"Path families, primary enumeration," he began. "Ash. Anchor. Bell." The old ordering, the schedule's own, four centuries of accreted taxonomy. He read the Path families with their tier ladders. He read the compound designations and the lineage variants. He read the provisional annex — Contested-Standing, Interim-Assessed, Dual-Registered, each with its enabling amendment and its review terms. He read the disputed-standings appendix, the categories the Compact had accepted under protest from member guilds and marked as accepted under protest, because the schedule recorded even its own reluctance. He read the struck entries, lined through but preserved, and the recorded rejections — the three categories argued for across four centuries and refused, their refusals documented, because the schedule was a ledger and a ledger that omitted its refusals would not have been worth the ink of its acceptances.

The reading took on its own weight in the room as it went. The first minutes had restlessness in them — a gallery expecting rhetoric, receiving inventory. Then the restlessness burned off, and what replaced it was attention of a kind Cael had felt exactly twice before, both times on a circuit floor: the attention of a crowd that has stopped waiting for the interesting part because it has begun to understand that the interesting part is happening, slowly, in front of it. Somewhere past the half-hour, he heard the gallery's silence change again, into something almost architectural — two hundred people collectively holding the growing shape of a list that contained everything, everything, four hundred years of everything, except the one word every document in the room said he was.

Coss rose once, early in the second quarter-hour. "Magistrate, the Compact stipulates that the schedule contains no entry under that designation. In the interest of the tribunal's time —"

"The respondent's primary presentation may not be interrupted," Yorlan said, without heat, and then added the sentence that Cael would copy into the log that night word for word: "And the panel declines the stipulation. A stipulated absence is an agreement between parties. The respondent appears to be offering the panel a demonstrated one. They are not the same object, and the panel prefers the better one. Proceed."

Coss sat. Cael proceeded. Ilsev, he saw, was no longer following in her own copy. She had closed it. She was listening with her eyes on the middle distance, the posture of an examiner who has stopped checking the work for errors and started checking it for the one thing rarer than correctness — completeness.

The light from the high windows had moved a full bench-length across the floor by the time he reached the final amendment schedule, read its last designation, and closed the volume.

"[SHATTERED] is not there," he said, into the stillness. "The panel has now heard the entire Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings, as amended — every entry it contains and every entry it ever contained. The word is not in the enumeration. It is not in the provisional annex. It is not in the disputed appendix. It is not among the struck entries or the recorded rejections. It has never been proposed, never been adopted, never been refused — the schedule does not even contain the record of anyone asking. It exists in Arbiter output. It exists in four hundred years of enforcement practice that has treated it as a classification. It has never once existed in the text that the Compact's own charter says is the *only* source of classification. The machinery that removed me from my home at fourteen, that has monitored me for two years, that filed the challenge before this tribunal — every part of it ran on precedent and custom. It never once ran on the law it claims to be enforcing."

---

**Third movement.**

"I want to be precise about what I'm arguing," Cael said, "because precision is the only thing that's earned me the right to stand here. I am not arguing that [SHATTERED] is a mistake, or that it should be removed, or that the Compact was wrong to be concerned about whatever it believes the flag means. I am not asking this panel to rule on what I am. Nobody in this room knows what I am — that is, in fact, the one finding four centuries of records genuinely support. I am arguing something much narrower, and much harder to dismiss: that a flag with no entry in the enumerated schedule confers no classification; that a practitioner with no classification cannot be reached by an enforcement authority which, by its own terms, attaches only to classified practitioners; and that this tribunal therefore cannot void this enrollment — not because the enrollment is sound, but because the hand reaching for it was never given the reach. The Compact's own architecture makes that conclusion unavoidable. I didn't write the architecture. I only read it."

He let the silence hold a moment. The next part was the part he had drafted eleven times in the archive and finally, on the twelfth draft, stopped drafting — because it had stopped being drafting and become the thing itself, the sentence the whole argument had been converging on since a morning in Denvash two years of living ago.

"The panel may be tempted to see this as a technicality," he said. "I'd ask it to consider what the technicality is standing in front of. Four practitioners before me carry this flag in the enforcement record. Four, across the whole history of the registry. Not one of them received a hearing. Not one was argued for. Every one of them was removed from their life by administrative order, inside days, under an authority no one ever checked — because the flag looked like a classification, and everyone extended it the courtesy of assuming somebody else had once confirmed that it was. I have read their files. Three of them simply end. I am the fifth instance, and the first one ever permitted to stand in a room like this and ask the question, and the question is not clever, and it is not new. It has been sitting in the text for four hundred years waiting for anyone to ask it: *where is it written?*"

He picked up the challenge document — Coss's own filing, the paper that had started the three weeks — and read from it, one line, the line every version of the argument had always been going to end on.

"The Compact's challenge describes my status in its own words: *no recognized Path designation given.* " He set it down. "I'm asking the panel to hold them to that. No path given — no classification held. No classification held — no authority attached. Their rules were never built to contain me. They said so themselves, in the first document of this proceeding, in their own hand. They cannot use rules that don't contain me to remove me from somewhere I am legitimately trying to be."

He sat down.

For three full seconds, the hall made no sound at all. Behind him, in the front row, he heard Karis's pen stop, and then fail to start again, which in Karis was the loudest reaction available to her.

---

Coss's rebuttal came exactly where the rehearsal had predicted it would — custom, implication, the practical absurdity of a flag with no formal home nonetheless carrying four centuries of enforcement weight. He argued it well, because he argued everything well, and he argued it standing in the wreckage of a case that had been conceded out from under him, building in the room for the first time in the entire proceeding, and Cael — watching him do it — felt the strange, doubled respect of a fighter watching an opponent improvise brilliantly on a floor that has already tilted.

"The respondent asks the panel to find that the schedule's silence is an exclusion," Coss said. "The Compact submits it is an oversight — and that the distinction matters less than he claims. Four hundred years of practice is not nothing, Magistrate. It is the accumulated judgment of every official who has ever handled these cases: that the flag denotes a real condition requiring real oversight. Arbiter output and enforcement custom together have functioned as classification for the entire life of the registry. Every amendment to the schedule was drafted by people who knew the flag existed and felt no need to add it — because its standing was understood. The respondent's reading asks the panel to conclude that the Compact's founders built a system with a category of practitioner deliberately placed beyond all authority — no obligations, no oversight, no recourse. No reasonable reading of the code intends that hole. And the panel should be clear-eyed about what ruling for the respondent creates: a precedent that the Compact's reach fails precisely where its concern is greatest."

It was the institutional argument, the one Brom had found in a common room with a borrowed stillness, and it was aimed, Cael knew, not at the text but at the panel's fears. It was also the best available move, made cleanly, by a man who had never once in two years made any other kind.

It died on Ilsev.

The deliberation questions began with the third assessor — practical, blunt, a man checking the floor before trusting it. "If the panel rules as the respondent asks, what is his status tomorrow morning? In plain terms."

"Undefined," Cael said. "The same as it was yesterday morning, Assessor, and every morning for two years. The only difference is that tomorrow the record would admit it."

Then Ilsev — and her first question went, as Karis had predicted eight days ago, not to Coss but to him, because her rigor had no side and never had.

"Respondent." Her voice was exactly as he remembered it from a room a year and a half behind him: dry, exact, without hostility and without comfort. "The warden argues implication. Address it directly. Four hundred years of uniform practice, known to every drafter of every amendment, none of whom saw a need to enumerate the flag. Why is that not the schedule speaking through its silence?"

"Because the schedule doesn't speak through silence, Assessor. It can't. It's an enumeration — speaking through entries is the only thing it does." He kept his eyes on her. "If custom were sufficient, the schedule wouldn't need to exist at all — the Compact could point at practice and call it law. It has never once done that. Every time practice and text diverged in four centuries, the Compact amended the text — forty-one times in the schedule's history; the amendments are the record of the Compact refusing, over and over, to let custom stand in for code. And the drafters' silence proves less than the warden needs it to. The drafting records entered yesterday show this tribunal exactly how the Compact behaves when it wants a category covered: it writes the category down, and when it wants a category excluded, it writes the exclusion down. Against that habit — their habit, in evidence, from their own files — four hundred years of not writing something down cannot be read as writing it down invisibly. If the panel rules on custom instead of text, it isn't applying the law. It's admitting it can't find it."

Ilsev regarded him for a moment with no expression he could name. Then she turned to the delegation's table, and asked the question the entire hearing had been narrowing toward since a boy in an archive had circled one word at the bottom of a page.

"Warden Coss." Her voice cut cleanly through the room. "Cite the schedule entry."

Coss paused. "The custom is well-established, Assessor —"

"I didn't ask about the custom. The custom is not in dispute; the respondent conceded it more thoroughly than you argued it. I asked for the entry. Section, subsection, designation. The panel has now heard the entire schedule read into its own record, so I will know the citation when I hear it. Cite it."

The silence that followed was different from every silence that had come before it in that room — not surprise, not tension, but the quiet of a question with no answer, sitting in the open, in front of everyone, being formally offered its last chance to have one.

Coss looked at the exhibits arrayed in front of him — the drafting records, the precedents, the two years of files, the entire flawless case that answered every question except this one. Then he straightened, and answered it, because he was, as he had always been, a man who told the truth even when it cost him.

"There isn't one," he said. "I cannot cite an entry that does not exist."

Ilsev nodded once — not in triumph; she was incapable of triumph; in receipt — and wrote a single line, and was done.

---

The panel recessed to deliberate. It was gone for less than an hour, which Quenna, leaning forward from the row behind, murmured was either very good or very bad and declined to guess which. Cael sat at the respondent's table through the whole of it, hands flat on the wood, and did not try to read the door. He had made the argument right. He had said every sentence the way it deserved to be said. What the panel did with it now belonged to the panel — and there was a version of him, two years younger, who could not have borne that, and the version sitting at the table found, examining himself with careful surprise, that he could.

The panel returned. The room stood, and sat, and stopped breathing.

Yorlan delivered the ruling with the same flat, colorless economy that had governed every word he'd spoken across two sittings, and the lack of drama in his voice was, somehow, the most devastating thing about it — the sound of the machine itself speaking, with nothing in the room left to blame it on.

"The panel rules as follows. On the enrollment: the panel does not rule. Its validity as categorized is not properly before this tribunal absent established jurisdiction, and standing on that question is remanded to the academy as an internal matter." A pause, procedural, exact. "On jurisdiction: the charter's definitions clause is closed. Classification, for all purposes under the charter, means designation under the enumerated schedule. The panel has heard the schedule in its entirety, on this record. The designation [SHATTERED] does not appear in it. The Compact has therefore not established that the respondent is a classified practitioner within the meaning of its own enforcement provisions, and has not established authority to void the enrollment through this challenge. The respondent's classification status is undefined under the current schedule." The last pause, smallest of all. "The challenge is dismissed for want of established jurisdiction. The record is public. This tribunal is concluded."

Not a validation. Cael understood that immediately, parsing the ruling's edges even as it was still landing on the room — the panel had not said his enrollment was legitimate. They had not said he was safe, or wronged, or anything at all about what he was. They had said only, narrowly, exactly, that the Compact could not prove it had the right to say otherwise. A crack, not a collapse. Contested, not resolved.

On the record. In public. Forever.

The gallery did not understand the distinction, or did not care about it, and either way the room went up around him in a single unbroken roar that seemed to physically move the air.

Cael sat very still at the respondent's table, hands flat on the wood, and let the sound wash past him without quite reaching the part of him that was still, for one more breath, entirely alone with what had just happened.

He saw Lira on her feet first, somewhere in the crowd's blur, already moving toward the table before the gavel had finished its second strike, over the rope line without breaking stride, because rope lines had never once in her life applied to her. He saw Brom, further back, nodding once, slowly — the nod of someone who had spent a year and more finding Cael's whole existence the most interesting problem he'd ever encountered, watching that problem win. He saw Karis, still seated, still writing, tears entirely unacknowledged on her face, her pen moving across the page as though the moment might slip away from her if she stopped documenting it even for a second. He saw Quenna, one row back, closing her eyes and releasing a breath that seemed to carry the entire semester's weight out of her at once. He saw Prynn, near the back wall, dust-dry and, for the first time since Cael had met her, entirely incandescent — and he saw, with the last clear sightline before the crowd closed in, that the empty seat she'd held all morning had a folded slip of paper resting on it, the kind she used to mark a place on a shelf where something that mattered was supposed to be.

Across the floor, Coss closed his document case, and did not look away when Cael's eyes found his, and inclined his head — a fighter's measure of a finished bout, nothing in it withheld — before turning toward the doors and whatever a clean loss cost a careful man.

And Cael understood, before anyone reached the table, in the last breath of stillness he was going to get, exactly what he had just done. Four recorded instances of practitioners like him, across the whole history of the Compact's registry, and none of them — not one, in four hundred years — had ever been given the chance to stand in a room like this. Three of their files simply ended. He had read them by lamplight and copied the eleven words that closed the last one, and he had carried all four of them into this room without ever saying so, and now the record they'd been denied existed, with his name on it, in the Compact's own voice.

He had not beaten the system. The system was still out there, four hundred years deep, already turning its attention toward the hole he had just made it acknowledge. What he had done was smaller, and larger: he had made it say, in its own voice, on its own record, in front of everyone who would ever need to read it again — that it had never actually had the right.

Then Lira reached the table, and the stillness ended, and the noise finally got in.

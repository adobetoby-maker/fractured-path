# Chapter 18 — The Compact's Case

The delegation arrived a week ahead of the hearing, and Greyvane's gate clerk, who had processed exactly one Compact field commission in living memory, processed three official arrivals inside a single afternoon and looked, by the end of it, like a woman recalculating her estimate of how interesting her job was allowed to get.

Cael watched them arrive from the archive window, cataloguing the way he catalogued everything that mattered.

Registry Magistrate Yorlan came first: a man in his sixties, moving with the flat, deliberate calm of someone who had presided over enough hearings that urgency had stopped being a useful emotion for him personally, dressed in the plain formal grey of a career adjudicator rather than anything ceremonial. He carried a single document case and shook Naveth's hand with the flat, correct courtesy of a man greeting a venue rather than a person. Cael read him immediately as neither ally nor enemy — the rarest and, he suspected, the most useful kind of official he had encountered since his Kindling. Yorlan was procedure embodied. Nothing more, nothing less. Exactly what the argument he'd spent a week building required him to be.

The second arrival stopped his notebook cold for a different reason entirely.

Assessor Ilsev.

He knew her immediately, the way he knew very few things immediately — the same sharp, procedurally exact bearing he remembered from the senior evaluation a year and a half and a district behind him, the assessor whose counter-procedure had shaped the shorthand of his whole Ardenmere circuit career without either of them fully realizing it at the time. She had been senior then, too. The seat she held now was the same kind of seat, simply attached to a different case — which told him something about how deliberately the Compact had chosen who to send.

The third was younger, and it took him a moment longer to place — Assessor Havel, promoted one grade since a market square he still occasionally thought about, assigned as recording officer, carrying the particular watchful stillness of someone who had learned, the hard way, that a Compact hearing recorded every word for reasons that outlasted the hearing itself.

Familiar faces. He wrote this down, because it mattered, because institutions were people wearing the same coat for years at a time, reused across a career the way a circuit reused its venues. The Compact was not a machine that generated new officials for every case. It was the same small set of people, redeployed, carrying every prior case with them into the next one whether the file admitted it or not.

---

The deposition happened two days later, in a room Naveth's office had converted for the purpose — a long table, Coss on one side with his document case open in front of him, Cael on the other with nothing in front of him at all, because Quenna had advised, correctly, that bringing notes to a deposition read as preparation for evasion rather than confidence in the truth.

Coss conducted it the way he did everything, formally correct and genuinely skilled, and Cael understood within the first three questions that this was not a man trying to trap him. This was a man trying to establish, cleanly and on the record, a set of facts that would make the *unclassified observer* category untenable in front of a panel that had not yet decided how to read it.

"You've participated in supervised exhibition matches," Coss said. Not a question, quite. A fact laid on the table for confirmation.

"Yes."

"Against ranked opponents."

"Yes."

"You won."

"Yes."

"Would you characterize your performance in those matches as consistent with someone who has not undergone Kindling assessment."

Cael considered the question the way he considered everything Coss asked him — slowly, honestly, giving nothing beyond what the question actually required. "I'd characterize it as consistent with what the panel observed and logged. I don't think my characterization of my own performance is the relevant fact here."

Coss almost smiled at that. Not quite — a flicker at the corner of his mouth, there and gone, the particular expression of a man recognizing a move he had used himself, more than once, against people who had not deserved it as much as this one apparently did.

The rest of the deposition ran the same way: cooperative, minimal, nothing volunteered that hadn't been directly asked for. Cael gave Coss exactly what the questions required and nothing that would help build the case beyond it, the same way he'd once given a market-square interview exactly what it required and no more, a lifetime and a district ago. Coss, who had two years of reports describing precisely this pattern of behavior, seemed to expect nothing else. They knew each other's game completely by now. It was, in its way, the most respectful hostility either of them had ever conducted.

---

Ilsev found the flag late that afternoon, while reviewing the case file's supporting documentation ahead of the formal proceedings — a routine review, the kind any senior evaluator would conduct before empaneling on a matter this unusual.

Cael was not in the room for it, and never would be. What happened there happened entirely without him, in the flat administrative light of a records office two doors down from where he was preparing for a deposition he had no idea was about to become, for one hour, about something other than him entirely.

Ilsev found the routing annotation the way she found everything — by reading past the point where most people would have stopped. A records request, processed before the challenge was formally filed, carrying a designation she didn't recognize: Suppression-Advisory. Watch Level 4. She did what she had always done with a designation she didn't recognize. She asked about it, on the record, because that was the whole of her method and had been for as long as anyone in this hearing chamber had known her.

The origin query came back with nothing in it worth the asking. *Systemic protocol, registry sub-layer.* No authorizing official. No creation record. A designation that predated the file it was attached to, sitting inside a system that otherwise prided itself on being able to explain everything it did.

Havel, recording, watched her read the answer twice. Watched the small, precise stillness that came over her — not alarm, nothing so dramatic as alarm, just the particular attention of someone filing a fact she couldn't yet do anything useful with. She noted it, formally, as an administrative irregularity, referred it up a chain that would very likely never send anything back down, and moved on to the next document in the stack. Procedure satisfied. That was what you did with an irregularity you couldn't explain and didn't have clearance to chase.

Havel said nothing. It was not his place to say anything, and he had learned, across two years of watching people with more authority than himself decide what could and couldn't be pursued, that silence was frequently the only professional option available to a recording officer who noticed more than his grade entitled him to act on.

That night, in the small private notebook he had kept since Ardenmere for exactly this purpose, he added a third entry beneath the two he already carried. He did not show it to anyone. He was not sure yet what he was building — only that he was building something, one careful, unexplained entry at a time, and that someday, not this year, it might matter that somebody had been keeping count.

Cael, elsewhere in the building, learned none of it, and would not for a long time.

---

He crossed paths with Coss again near the yard's edge as the light finished going, the deposition long since concluded, both of them apparently drawn outside by the same restless need to walk rather than sit with the day's work.

"Three weeks in the archive," Coss said, without preamble, falling into step a few paces off rather than directly beside him. "You're going to argue the code."

"You're guessing."

"I'm reading." Coss's voice was even, unbothered, the tone of a man stating a fact rather than scoring a point. "You've spent three weeks with Prynn instead of preparing character witnesses or building a sympathy case. That's not what someone does who's planning to argue the enrollment was reasonable under the circumstances. That's what someone does who's found something in the text itself." A pause, just long enough to register as deliberate. "It's the only argument you have that I'd actually worry about."

Cael said nothing, because there was nothing useful to say that wouldn't hand Coss more than the silence already had.

Coss stopped walking. Cael stopped with him, more out of the momentum of the conversation than any conscious choice.

"For what it's worth," Coss said, "I hope it's a good one. I've built a clean case. Clean cases deserve to be tested by good arguments, not sympathy or delay. If you've actually found something in the text, I'll respect it whether it wins or not." He nodded once, the particular economy of a man who had said everything he intended to say. "Five days."

He walked on. Cael stood at the yard's edge a while longer, watching the last color go out of the sky over Greyvane's wall, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he believed him.

He got out the Power Log that night and wrote, carefully, the entry he'd been circling for most of the walk back: *Five days. He's built a clean case and he wants a clean fight. I understand that better than I want to. I've spent a year and a half wanting the same thing from people who were trying to end me. Strange, to finally get it from the one person whose job it's always been.*

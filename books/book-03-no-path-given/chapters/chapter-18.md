# Chapter 18 — The Compact's Case

The delegation arrived a week ahead of the hearing, and Greyvane's gate clerk, who had processed exactly one Compact field commission in living memory, processed three official arrivals inside a single afternoon and looked, by the end of it, like a woman recalculating her estimate of how interesting her job was allowed to get.

Cael watched them arrive from the archive window, cataloguing the way he catalogued everything that mattered.

Registry Magistrate Yorlan came first: a man in his sixties, moving with the flat, deliberate calm of someone who had presided over enough hearings that urgency had stopped being a useful emotion for him personally, dressed in the plain formal grey of a career adjudicator rather than anything ceremonial. He carried a single document case and shook Naveth's hand with the correct, weightless courtesy of a man greeting a venue rather than a person. He did not look around the yard the way visitors looked around the yard. He looked at the yard the way an assessor looked at a floor before a bout — checking that it met the requirements, and then ceasing to see it. Cael read him immediately as neither ally nor enemy — the rarest and, he suspected, the most useful kind of official he had encountered since his Kindling. Yorlan was procedure embodied. Nothing more, nothing less. Exactly what the argument he'd spent a week building required him to be.

He noted, watching Naveth walk the magistrate toward the guest wing, that Yorlan asked precisely two questions on the way — Cael was too far to hear them, but he could see the shape of them, brief and administrative, answered in a sentence each — and that he asked nothing else. A man who had already read everything. A man who intended the hearing to contain no surprises, on the reasonable grounds that in his experience it never did.

*He's wrong about that,* Cael wrote in the notebook, and then, in fairness, added: *Probably. So am I, probably. That's what the hearing is for.*

The second arrival stopped his notebook cold for a different reason entirely.

Assessor Ilsev.

He knew her immediately, the way he knew very few things immediately — the same sharp, procedurally exact bearing he remembered from the senior evaluation a year and a half and a district behind him, the assessor whose counter-procedure had shaped the shorthand of his whole Ardenmere circuit career without either of them fully realizing it at the time. She stepped down from the coach, declined the porter's hand without discourtesy, and stood for a moment reading the notice board beside the gate — the public assessment calendar, his own demonstration dates posted on it — with the complete, unhidden attention of someone who considered public postings to be part of the record and the record to be her business. She had been senior then, too. The seat she held now was the same kind of seat, simply attached to a different case — which told him something about how deliberately the Compact had chosen who to send.

It told him something else, too, once he'd sat with it. The Compact could have sent anyone to fill the senior evaluation seat. It had sent the one assessor in the region famous — he had learned this from Prynn's enforcement digests, where Ilsev's name recurred like a signature — for ruling against her own side whenever her own side's paperwork failed to hold. Whoever had empaneled her either hadn't thought about it, or had wanted the hearing to be genuinely rigorous, or had been so certain of the case that rigor looked like a free virtue. All three possibilities were worth writing down. He wrote all three down.

The third arrival was younger, and it took him a moment longer to place — Assessor Havel, promoted one grade since a market square he still occasionally thought about, assigned as recording officer, carrying the watchful stillness of someone who had learned, the hard way, that a Compact hearing recorded every word for reasons that outlasted the hearing itself. Havel carried his own case, and a second, heavier one that would be the transcription apparatus, and he paused inside the gate — one beat, barely anything — to look across the yard at the academy that was about to become the most closely read institution in the district. Cael, watching from the high window, could not tell from this distance whether Havel was looking for him. He filed the question with all the other questions about Havel he had been accumulating for a year and a half, in the section of the notebook he had come to think of, privately, as *people who noticed more than they said.*

Familiar faces. He wrote this down, because it mattered: institutions were people wearing the same coat for years at a time, reused across a career the way a circuit reused its venues. The Compact was not a machine that generated new officials for every case. It was the same small set of people, redeployed, carrying every prior case with them into the next one whether the file admitted it or not. Everyone on that panel had met some version of him before — on paper, in a market square, across an assessment table. Whatever happened at the hearing would happen between people with history, which meant it would be legible, which meant it could be studied.

He was still writing when Quenna appeared at the archive door, and told him the deposition had been scheduled for two days out, and that they needed to talk about how not to lose the hearing before it started.

---

The preparation took one evening and consisted, in the end, of Quenna talking and Cael writing nothing at all — at her instruction.

"No notes," she said, first. "Not in the room. You walk in with empty hands and you sit with empty hands. Bringing notes to a deposition reads as preparation for evasion. Empty hands read as confidence in the truth. It's not fair and it's not rational, but I've sat the assessor's side of that table for twenty years, and I'm telling you how the room actually reads."

"That's the easy part."

"It is. Here's the hard part." She sat across from him, and the usual carefully maintained neutrality was gone from her face, which he had learned to treat as her highest form of respect. "Coss is not going to try to trap you. Traps are for weak cases, and his case isn't weak. He's going to ask you plain questions with plain answers, and every plain answer will be a stone in a wall, and the wall will be finished before you see its shape. Your instinct will be to fight each question as it comes. Don't. You can't win a deposition. Nobody has ever won a deposition. You can only lose one — by volunteering, by characterizing, by explaining. He asks what happened; you say what happened. He asks what it means; that's the panel's question, and you say so. Answer the question asked. Never the question implied."

"I've done this before," Cael said. "In a market square."

"I've read the transcript of that interview." A dry beat. "It's why I'm only giving you one evening of this instead of five. But understand the difference in scale. Havel was one junior assessor with a field docket. This is a deposition in support of a filed challenge, taken by a Compact field commission, and every word goes in front of a magistrate. The questions will be better. The silences will be better. Coss has read two years of reports about how you handle questioning, which means he has planned for exactly the version of you that handled Havel." She stood. "So be that version anyway. It's still the right version. Just don't expect it to surprise him."

---

The deposition happened two days later, in a room Naveth's office had converted for the purpose — a long table, Coss on one side with his document case open in front of him, Havel at the table's end with the transcription apparatus, Cael on the other side with nothing in front of him at all.

Coss began with the formalities, and even the formalities were information: name, standing, the date, the challenge's docket designation, the statement — read out evenly, for the record — that the deponent appeared voluntarily and was not compelled. He conducted all of it the way he did everything, formally correct and genuinely skilled, and Cael understood within the first three questions that Quenna had described the next hour exactly.

"You've participated in supervised exhibition matches," Coss said. Not a question, quite. A fact laid on the table for confirmation.

"Yes."

"Against ranked opponents."

"Yes."

"You won."

"Yes."

"How many such matches, in total, since your arrival at this academy?"

"One exhibition match. The others were demonstration assessments, closed sessions. The records distinguish them."

"They do." Coss made a small notation, and Cael understood the notation was not for Coss's memory — nothing in that document case required the man's memory — but for the record's sense of pace. "Then let me use the records' language. Including closed demonstration assessments, how many times have you performed before a faculty panel?"

"Every month since enrollment. The assessment calendar is posted publicly. I'd be guessing at the count; the calendar wouldn't be."

"Noted. The calendar will be entered." No irritation. If anything, a barely readable trace of approval, the craftsman's acknowledgment of clean joinery on the other side of the work. "Would you characterize your performance in those matches as consistent with someone who has not undergone Kindling assessment?"

Cael considered the question the way he considered everything Coss asked him — slowly, honestly, giving nothing beyond what the question actually required. "I'd characterize it as consistent with what the panel observed and logged. I don't think my characterization of my own performance is the relevant fact here."

Coss almost smiled at that. Not quite — a flicker at the corner of his mouth, there and gone, the expression of a man recognizing a move he had used himself, more than once, against people who had not deserved it as much as this one apparently did.

"The panel's logs describe, and I quote, 'unorthodox architecture, consistent execution, no safety concerns.'" Coss turned a page without looking at it. "In your own words: what is the architecture?"

"That's a classification question."

"It's a factual question."

"It's a factual question whose answer requires a classification vocabulary," Cael said. "I don't have one to offer you. No Arbiter assessment ever gave me one. If I invent terms for what I do, the record will treat my invented terms as admissions, and they'd be admissions about words rather than facts. What I do is in the assessment logs, described by qualified observers. Those descriptions are the facts."

Silence, for a moment. Havel's pen moved, stopped, waited.

"You understand," Coss said — and this was the closest the hour came to pressure, delivered without any change of register at all — "that declining to describe your own capabilities may itself be noted."

"I understand that everything may be noted," Cael said. "That's what a record is."

Coss looked at him across the table for a long moment, and something passed behind the man's eyes that was not hostility and had perhaps never been hostility — a kind of professional loneliness, Cael would think later, writing the day up in the log; the look of a man who had spent two years as the only person taking his file seriously, meeting the file, and finding it more careful than the reports could convey.

The rest of the deposition ran the same way: cooperative, minimal, nothing volunteered that hadn't been directly asked for. Coss walked him through the enrollment paperwork, and Cael confirmed every signature. He walked him through the training-floor privileges, and Cael confirmed every session. He asked — once, cleanly, without emphasis, the one question the whole hour had been built to deliver — whether Cael had, at the time of signing, understood the observer category to describe him accurately. And Cael, who had spent two days deciding how to meet exactly this question, told the truth, because the truth was coming out at the hearing regardless and a deposition was the worst possible place to be caught shading it: "I understood it was the category the provision made available. Whether it describes me accurately is a question about the provision. I expect the hearing to take that up."

"It will," Coss said, and closed the folder, and stated the time for the record, and thanked him — formally, correctly, and, as far as Cael could read, sincerely — for his cooperation.

They knew each other's game completely by now. It was, in its way, the most respectful hostility either of them had ever conducted.

---

Ilsev found the flag late that same afternoon, while reviewing the case file's supporting documentation ahead of the formal proceedings — a routine review, the kind any senior evaluator would conduct before empaneling on a matter this unusual.

Cael was not in the room for it, and never would be. What happened there happened entirely without him, in the flat administrative light of a records office two doors down from the hall where his deposition had concluded hours before — while he was elsewhere in the building, returning Prynn's digests, thinking about Coss, unaware that for one hour that afternoon the case file had briefly stopped being about him at all.

Ilsev worked through the supporting documentation the way she worked through everything — in sequence, completely, past the point where most reviewers would have stopped. The enrollment submissions. The provision citations. The monitoring summaries, two years of them, Coss's flat professional prose describing a subject who answered questions minimally and never volunteered. And then, in the routing history of a records request processed months before the challenge was formally filed, an annotation she did not recognize: a designation, sitting in the priority field where a monitoring level should have been, that matched no monitoring level she had ever been issued. *Suppression-Advisory. Watch Level 4.*

She read it twice. She checked it against the request's stated authority, and found the stated authority was two grades too low to have generated it. She checked it against the case file's own classification of the subject, and found the file's classification could not have generated it either, because the designation predated the file.

She did what she had always done with a designation she didn't recognize. She asked about it, on the record, because that was the whole of her method and had been for as long as anyone in this building had known her name.

The origin query came back before the end of the hour, with nothing in it worth the asking. *Systemic protocol, registry sub-layer.* No authorizing official. No creation record. No review date, no issuing office, no entry in the designation registry that was supposed to contain every designation the system was capable of producing. A flag that predated the file it was attached to, sitting inside a system that otherwise prided itself on being able to explain everything it did — a system whose entire authority, as a boy two doors away had recently discovered from the other direction, rested on the claim that everything it did was written down somewhere.

Havel, recording, watched her read the answer twice. Watched the small, precise stillness that came over her — not alarm, nothing so dramatic as alarm, just the attention of someone filing a fact she couldn't yet do anything useful with. She sat with the query result for a moment longer than procedure required. Then she did what procedure did require, because procedure was the whole of her and she had never once pretended otherwise: she noted it, formally, as *administrative irregularity, referred*, entered the referral into a chain that would very likely never send anything back down, and moved on to the next document in the stack. Procedure satisfied. That was what you did with an irregularity you couldn't explain and didn't have clearance to chase.

But Havel, whose job was to record and whose gift — curse, he sometimes thought — was to notice, saw the one thing the referral form had no field for: Ilsev, before turning the page, read the designation a third time. Not to check it. She had already checked it. She read it the way a person rereads a sentence in a language they are certain they speak, on the chance that the third reading will make it stop being wrong.

It didn't. She turned the page.

Havel said nothing. It was not his place to say anything, and he had learned, across two years of watching people with more authority than himself decide what could and couldn't be pursued, that silence was frequently the only professional option available to a recording officer who noticed more than his grade entitled him to act on.

That night, in the small private notebook he had kept since Ardenmere for exactly this purpose, he added a third entry beneath the two he already carried. The first two were his own — a market square, a records anomaly, questions he had asked and been moved off of. The third was different, and he understood the difference mattered even if he could not yet say why: this time, someone senior had seen it too. Someone whose entire reputation was built on the registry's text had looked at a designation the text could not account for, and had been unable to do anything about it either. He wrote the entry in the same dry, factual style as the first two, dated it, and closed the notebook. He did not show it to anyone. He was not sure yet what he was building — only that he was building something, one careful, unexplained entry at a time, and that someday, not this year, it might matter that somebody had been keeping count.

Cael, elsewhere in the building, learned none of it, and would not for a long time.

---

He crossed paths with Coss again near the yard's edge as the light finished going, the deposition long since concluded, both of them apparently drawn outside by the same restless need to walk rather than sit with the day's work.

"Three weeks in the archive," Coss said, without preamble, falling into step a few paces off rather than directly beside him. "You're going to argue the code."

"You're guessing."

"I'm reading." Coss's voice was even, unbothered, the tone of a man stating a fact rather than scoring a point. "You've spent three weeks with Prynn instead of preparing character witnesses or building a sympathy case. That's not what someone does who's planning to argue the enrollment was reasonable under the circumstances. That's what someone does who's found something in the text itself." A pause, just long enough to register as deliberate. "It's the only argument you have that I'd actually worry about."

Cael said nothing, because there was nothing useful to say that wouldn't hand Coss more than the silence already had.

"You won't confirm it. Good," Coss said. "You shouldn't. I'd note for your education, though — since apparently nobody in your life has ever been positioned to give you one in this field — that I could requisition Prynn's lending ledger tomorrow and read your last three weeks like a bill of particulars. I haven't. I won't. Do you know why?"

"Because you don't need to," Cael said. "You've already built your case. Knowing mine wouldn't change yours. It would only tempt you to argue against me instead of for the challenge, and you don't argue against people."

Coss stopped walking. Cael stopped with him, more out of the momentum of the conversation than any conscious choice, and for a moment the two of them stood at the yard's edge in the last grey light — the boy the system had flagged, and the man the system had sent, two careful readers of the same long file.

"Two years," Coss said, at last, "of writing reports about a subject who understood exactly what I was doing and never once made the mistake of thinking it was about him." He shook his head, once — not disagreement; something more like a man confirming a measurement. "For what it's worth: I hope it's a good one. The argument. I've built a clean case. Clean cases deserve to be tested by good arguments, not sympathy or delay. If you've actually found something in the text, I'll respect it whether it wins or not." He nodded once, the economy of a man who had said everything he intended to say. "Five days."

He walked on. Cael stood at the yard's edge a while longer, watching the last color go out of the sky over Greyvane's wall, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he believed him.

He got out the Power Log that night and wrote, carefully, the entry he'd been circling for most of the walk back: *Five days. He's built a clean case and he wants a clean fight. I understand that better than I want to. I've spent a year and a half wanting the same thing from people who were trying to end me. Strange, to finally get it from the one person whose job it's always been.*

He read the entry over, and then added, beneath it, because the day had contained one more true thing and the log existed for true things:

*Quenna says nobody wins a deposition. I think that's right, and I think today I didn't lose one. Against this opponent, on his floor, under his rules — I'll take it.*

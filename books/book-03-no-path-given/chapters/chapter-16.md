# Chapter 16 — Three Weeks

For two days after the challenge was filed, Greyvane ran a low fever.

Cael felt it everywhere: in the refectory, where conversations now came in two volumes — the ordinary one, and the dropped one that followed him between tables; in the lecture wing, where an instructor lost his own thread mid-sentence when Cael came in late and every head in the room performed not turning; in the yard, where the hearing notice on the main board had been read so many times the corners had gone soft. The academy had two hundred students, and by Cael's own conservative count, all two hundred of them now knew that in three weeks a Compact magistrate was going to sit in their lecture hall and decide whether the observer was allowed to exist here. Some of them looked at him with sympathy. Some with the fascination people brought to weather damage. A few — more than he would have guessed, and he was keeping honest count — had taken to nodding to him in passing with a small deliberate firmness, the way people nod at someone whose side they have quietly picked.

He spent both nights in the archive. That was where the thread came from — the one he carried into Naveth's office on the third morning — and it had arrived the way seams always arrived for him: not as inspiration but as accumulation, hour on hour of reading the enforcement provisions with Prynn's lamp hissing softly and the cold coming up through the reading room floor, filling the observation notebook with the code's structures as though the code were a fighter and its clauses were declarations, until somewhere after midnight on the second night the pattern had shown him its joint. Every power the Compact's enforcement chapters granted — every single one, he had checked until his eyes burned — was granted in the same grammatical shape. Authority *over classified practitioners*. Powers exercised *according to classification*. Procedures attaching *upon classification*. The word was load-bearing everywhere. And he had gone looking for the place where the code defined the word, and found the schedule, and read it, and then sat very still for a long time, listening to the lamp.

He said nothing to anyone that night. He wanted to be wrong about it first, thoroughly, before he let himself be right. He spent the second night failing to be wrong.

Naveth's office held six people uncomfortably, and on the morning the war council convened, it held six people who all understood, before anyone said a word, that comfort was not going to be available to any of them today.

Cael sat between Lira and Brom, on the side of the desk that made him a subject of discussion rather than a participant in one, until Quenna — standing at the window with her arms folded, watching the yard below as though the yard might offer an answer the room couldn't — turned and fixed that without asking anyone's permission.

"He stays for this," she said. "All of it. If we're deciding what happens to him, he hears every word of the deciding."

Naveth didn't argue. He had the look of a man who had already run every argument he could make against himself and lost each one before breakfast. He set the challenge notice on the desk, squared it with the edge the way a man squares a document he has read enough times to have stopped needing the words, and said the thing that had clearly been sitting in his chest since the notice arrived.

"We cannot win this on the merits."

Nobody in the room disagreed, which was itself an answer.

"I want to be precise about what I mean," he went on, "because I think it matters that we're all looking at the same shape of the problem instead of comfortable versions of it. The enrollment, as categorized, is a violation of the provision's plain language. Everyone at this table has known that since the day we signed him in. The provision was written for children who have not Kindled. Cael has Kindled. The category doesn't fit him and never did — we built a fit for him because the alternative was leaving him with nothing, and I stand by that decision completely. But standing by a decision and being able to defend it under formal review are not the same thing, and my counsel has spent three days confirming there is no version of defending the enrollment itself that survives a competent challenge. Warden Coss's filing is competent. It may be the most competent thing that has ever crossed my desk."

He let that stand a moment, and Cael watched the sentence cost him something. Naveth had spent sixty-odd years being right about people other institutions were wrong about; his entire professional theology rested on the premise that careful institutions could shelter the mispriced inside honest paperwork. The filing on his desk was a proof, written in his own system's language, that the shelter had a load limit — and that he had, knowingly, built past it.

"So what's the option," Lira said. Not hostile. Just wanting the shape of it stated plainly, the way she wanted everything stated.

Naveth was quiet a moment before he answered, and Cael understood, watching him, that the provost was choosing his words the way a man chooses them when he's about to say something he hated and needed said anyway.

"The least damaging option available to this academy," Naveth said, "is a negotiated withdrawal. We approach the Compact before the hearing, concede the category error, and quietly revise Cael's standing to something the provision's language actually covers — which, honestly, is nothing. He would not be enrolled here anymore, under any category. The charter survives. I keep the doors open for the other two hundred students whose enrollments are also, in various smaller ways, clerical errors this system was built to catch and price wrong. That is the option that protects the most people with the most certainty."

The silence that followed was not surprised silence. It was the silence of people who had already, privately, done the arithmetic themselves and arrived at the same ugly number. Cael had done it on the first night, between code volumes: two hundred students on one side of the scale, one on the other, and an honest man responsible for all of it. He had found, running the numbers, that he could not even resent the option's existence. That was the worst part of institutions, he was learning — the way their cruelest mathematics could be performed entirely by decent people showing their work.

"You're not recommending it," Quenna said. It wasn't a question.

"No." Naveth looked, for the first time since Cael had known him, genuinely tired. "I am not going to be the man who took that option off this table without saying it out loud, because it is a real option and pretending otherwise would be a kind of lie I don't tell in this office. But I am also not going to recommend surrendering a student to save an institution, and if that costs the institution eventually, I will have made my peace with it before it happens rather than after." He looked at Cael directly. "This is your enrollment, not mine to trade. What do you want to do."

Cael had been turning the question over since the notice went up two days before, in his slow deliberate manner with anything that mattered too much to answer quickly — walking every angle of it before he let himself commit to one, the same discipline he brought to a bout he hadn't fought yet. He had walked the running option: there was always running, there had been running available every day since Denvash, and it led where it had always led, which was to a smaller life with the same file following it. He had walked the surrender option Naveth had just laid out with such painful honesty. And he had walked the third thing, the one built out of two nights of lamplight and a grammatical pattern, and found that it was the only one of the three he could imagine writing in the log without flinching.

"I'm not leaving," he said, "and I'm not letting Greyvane fall because of me either. There's a third option. Nobody's said it yet because it sounds insane, and I want to say it anyway before I lose my nerve."

"Say it," Lira said.

"I'm not going to defend the enrollment. Naveth's right — there's nothing there to defend. I'm going to defend the hearing on different ground entirely." He'd read the phrase so many times in the last two nights it came out steadier than he expected. "The Compact's whole challenge assumes they have authority over me to begin with. That authority is written in the language of classification — every enforcement power the code grants is granted *to classify practitioners*, exercised *against classified practitioners*. I want three weeks to find out whether that's actually true, or whether it's just something everyone's assumed was true for four hundred years because nobody with a reason to check ever checked."

The room absorbed it in stages. Brom's eyebrows made their slow climb. Karis's pen, which had been still, came off the table. Naveth's counsel-trained caution visibly began drafting objections. But it was Quenna he watched, because Quenna was the one who had bet a career on him, and Quenna's expression didn't change, exactly — something in the set of her shoulders sharpened instead, the alteration he'd seen in fighters when a bout stopped being what they'd prepared for.

"You're going to argue registry law against a Compact field commission. In three weeks. At fifteen."

"I'm going to study it the way I study opponents," Cael said. "It's a system. Systems have architecture. Architecture has seams. I've found seams in things people spent years training to defend. I don't see why this should be different, except that the training I'd need is reading instead of watching."

"It's exactly different," Quenna said, "because if you're wrong, the consequence isn't losing a bout. It's losing everything the bouts were ever protecting."

"I know what it costs if I'm wrong." Cael held her look. "I've been counting that cost since the day I signed the enrollment. I'd rather lose trying to prove they never had the right than lose handing myself over quietly because trying felt too large."

Brom, who had said nothing yet, made a small sound that might have been a laugh under different circumstances. "That's the most Cael sentence I've ever heard, and I've heard a lot of them."

Naveth studied him for a long moment — the measuring look, Cael thought, of a man recalculating a bet he'd already made once and was now, under fire, being asked to make again. Whatever the recalculation returned, it returned the same answer as the first time, because that was the whole of who Naveth was: a man who had built an institution out of doubling the bets other people folded. "If this is the path," he said finally, "the academy will not obstruct it. I can't officially endorse an argument that challenges the Compact's jurisdiction outright — my charter puts limits on what an institution under it can say against it directly. But nothing stops a student from making his own case, on his own time, with access to this academy's resources like any other student preparing for any other assessment." A pause. "And nothing stops me from being extremely slow to notice how much archive access he's using."

"That's the second useful thing you've done for me today," Cael said, and meant it as gratitude even though it came out flatter than he intended.

"Don't thank me yet," Naveth said. "Thank me if it works."

---

The division of labor took shape over the following hour, the way the best plans in Cael's life had always taken shape — not handed down from above, but built by everyone who had a piece of the problem laying their piece on the table until the whole thing had a shape nobody could have built alone.

"I'll work the code with Prynn," Cael said. "She knows the archive better than anyone alive, and I've already found the first thread — I need someone who can tell me when I'm reading a real seam and when I'm just seeing what I want to see."

"She'll want that," Quenna said. "She's been waiting sixty years for a student who reads the shelves instead of asking her to summarize them."

"I want to work the historical registry documents," Karis said, and there was something in how quickly she said it — no hesitation at all — that told Cael she'd already been thinking about exactly this before anyone asked. "If the modern schedule's silent on his classification, the question of *why* it's silent has an answer somewhere, even if the answer's four hundred years old and buried in a records room nobody's opened in a generation. I want to find out what got written before anyone decided what to call him."

"That's not the argument," Cael said, careful. "The argument has to live inside their current text or it doesn't hold at a hearing that only cares about their current text."

"I know." Karis met his eyes, steady. "I'm not looking for the argument. I'm looking for the truth. They're not the same search, and I think you know that better than anyone I've ever met."

He did know it. He let her have the point without arguing it further, and watched her rule a fresh page into the columns of a search she would clearly have begun within the hour, and felt — under the morning's weight — the odd comfort of it: whatever the hearing decided about what he was, there was now a person on the continent whose whole method was aimed at finding out what he *actually* was, independent of any tribunal's needs. The two searches would run in parallel for three weeks. He suspected, without being able to justify it yet, that hers was the one that would still be running in three years.

"I'll run liaison with the student body," Brom said. "The hearing's public. Witnesses of fact matter — Edran already withdrew a petition on the record, which means his testimony about what he's actually seen you do carries weight nobody can dismiss as friendship. There'll be others who watched the assessment bout. I'll find out who's willing to say what they saw, plainly, without embellishing it into something it wasn't."

"And you'll keep them honest," Quenna said. "Not coached. Nobody wants testimony that reads as rehearsed."

"I know the difference between preparing someone and scripting them," Brom said, a little dry. "I spent two years being coached badly. I know exactly what I don't want to do to anyone else."

"Quenna and I hold the procedural line," Naveth said. "Document schedules, scope disputes, the magistrate's office. Every day of delay my counsel can lawfully generate is a day of archive time, and I am told" — the faintest ghost of humor crossed the provost's exhaustion — "that we are institutionally gifted at generating lawful delay."

Lira had been quiet through most of it, arms crossed, watching the plan assemble itself around a version of Cael she clearly had opinions about, and when the room's attention finally settled on her, she didn't wait to be asked.

"I'll take the part nobody's mentioned," she said. "You're still a fighter with a monthly assessment coming whether the hearing happens or not. You don't get to disappear into three weeks of archive dust and come out the other side someone who forgot how to move. I keep you sharp. Training floor, same as always, no excuses because you're busy being clever." She looked at him, and something under the flat practicality of it was fiercer than the words alone carried. "The person who walks into that hearing has to still be *you*, Cael. Not a lawyer wearing your face. If you lose that somewhere in three weeks of reading, I don't care how good the argument is. You'll have already lost the part that matters more."

Cael held her gaze, and understood — the way he'd understood a handful of things in his life all at once, arriving complete rather than assembled — that of everything anyone in the room had offered him that morning, this was the one he most needed and would have been least likely to ask for himself. Naveth was defending the enrollment. Karis was pursuing the truth. Brom was mustering the witnesses. Lira, alone of all of them, had assigned herself to defending *him* — the actual person, the boy under the case — against the argument itself, against the three weeks, against the version of survival that survived by becoming something else.

"Same time as always, then," he said. "Whatever's left of me after the archive."

"Whatever's left of you," Lira agreed, "is still going to be better on a floor than most people at their best. Don't forget that either."

---

The meeting broke apart the way war councils in every story he'd ever half-listened to on Hesk's radio broke apart — not with a resolution, exactly, but with everyone in the room understanding their piece of a problem too large for any one of them to solve alone, and turning, separately, to go start solving it. In the corridor outside, the six of them dispersed in five directions, and for a moment Cael stood alone at the junction watching them go — the provost toward his counsel, Quenna toward her files, Karis toward the residence wing at a research-launch stride, Brom toward the yard where Edran would be training at this hour, Lira toward the floor to book their evening slot — and had the strange doubled experience of seeing it from outside: five people, redeploying their entire lives around a fifteen-year-old's insane sentence, because he had said it out loud and meant it.

He went to the archive first, because that was where the actual work began, and found Prynn already waiting at the long table with three books stacked and open, as though she'd known before anyone told her which way the meeting would land.

"I heard," she said, without looking up. "This academy has thin walls and thinner discretion. Sit down. We have three weeks and four hundred years of code between us, and I intend to use every hour of both."

He sat.

She had a method waiting for him, because of course she did. "You'll read it in enactment order," she said, turning the first volume to face him. "Not shelf order. Shelf order is how it's stored. Enactment order is how it *grew* — and a thing's weaknesses live in how it grew, not in how it's stored. You want to know what four hundred years of amenders thought they were fixing, in the order they panicked about it." She set a folded card beside the volume: her own concordance of the amendment history, hand-ruled, older than his parents. "Every amendment is a confession. Somebody found a gap, and the patch tells you where the gap was. Read enough patches and you'll have a map of every place the wall was ever thin."

Cael looked at the card, and then at her, with the sensation of watching a door open onto a discipline he recognized. "That's how I study fighters," he said. "Their corrections are the map. Nobody drills what they've never been punished for."

"Then you already know how to read law," Prynn said, dry as her shelves. "Everyone else starts by believing the text is finished. You'll save us a week."

They worked until the second bell, and past it, because she had been known to forget. She took the concordance side; he took the text; and the first evening's labor was pure inventory — the code's architecture blocked out in the observation notebook in the same schema he used for Path work: *charter law* at the root, granting everything; the *classification schedule* as the first declaration, the commitment everything downstream depended on; *practitioner obligations* and *enforcement provisions* as the follow-through, each one drawing its force from the declaration above it. He drew it as a declaration tree because a declaration tree was the shape his mind knew, and when he turned the notebook around to show her, Prynn studied the diagram for a long moment and then made a sound he had never heard from her before, brief and dusty, which he realized only afterward had been a laugh.

"Sixty years," she said, "and the first accurate picture of the registry code I've ever seen drawn is a fight chart."

The registry code, laid open in front of him as something to actually be *studied* rather than merely obeyed, looked at first like the most opponent-shaped thing he had never fought — dense, procedural, built across centuries by hundreds of hands that had never coordinated with each other and had somehow, layer on layer, produced something that functioned anyway, the way a coral reef functions without any single architect. He had spent a year and a half learning to read a fighter's declaration timing for the half-second seam between commitment and recovery. He did not yet know what a seam looked like in four hundred years of amended charter law — the grammatical pattern from his two lamplit nights was a beginning, a first tell glimpsed at distance, not yet a strategy. He suspected, sitting down across from Prynn with the first volume between them, that he was about to find out the hard way, the only way he'd ever learned anything that mattered.

Karis intercepted him on the archive steps as he left, going in as he was coming out, a lamp in one hand and a satchel of empty notebooks over her shoulder like a soldier drawing ammunition. "Prynn's given me the founding-era shelves," she said. "The waystation records. Everything that predates the last standardization." There was a light in her he had seen exactly twice before — once in the archive on the day she introduced herself, once on the training hall floor with a wrecked lattice smoking behind her. "Four hundred years ago, somebody wrote things down about people the schedule couldn't hold, in whatever words they had before the modern words existed. I'm going to go find out what the words were."

"The hearing can't use any of it."

"You keep saying that as though it will discourage me." She adjusted the satchel. "The hearing gets your search. I get mine. Go train — Lira's been stalking the yard for a quarter hour and her expression is not patient."

Lira collected him at dusk, exactly as threatened. The evening session was short and entirely without mercy — footwork first, then the old Ardenmere patterns, then three rounds of the lattice-simulation drills she had apparently decided were now a permanent fixture of his life regardless of whether any lattice was scheduled.

"You read all day," she said, circling, when he was slow coming out of the second round. "Shoulders like a scribe. Fix that before it sets, or by hearing day you'll gesture at the magistrate like a man reaching for a shelf."

"The magistrate isn't going to care about my shoulders."

"Everyone cares about shoulders. Nobody knows they care about shoulders." She swept his lead foot, put him in the dirt with an economy that would have embarrassed him a year ago and now just made him laugh, and stood over him with her hand out. "That's the actual secret of every ranked fighter and every courtroom on the continent, and you got it from me for free."

Somewhere in the session's second half — moving through the old patterns with his body while the day's arguments drained out of his head — he noticed that the drills had become the only hour since the filing in which he had thought about nothing at all, and understood all over again precisely what she had assigned herself to, and why she was better at it than anyone alive.

That night, back in his room, with the fragment binder closed and the observation notebook untouched for once, he opened the Power Log instead and wrote the entry the day had been building toward since the moment he'd said the insane thing out loud in Naveth's office and discovered, to his own surprise, that he meant it.

*New opponent. Four hundred pages, no tells, no third exchange.*

He looked at the sentence a moment, and then, because it was true and because writing true things down was the only method he had ever fully trusted, added the rest.

*Three weeks. Same method as always: watch it until I can see how it moves.*

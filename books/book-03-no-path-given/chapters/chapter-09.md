# Chapter 9 — Method

Karis's sessions had a shape to them within a week, the way most things did once enough repetitions had passed through Cael's notice for a pattern to show itself. Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, the smaller training room off the archive wing — a converted storage chamber, really, low-ceilinged, one high window that threw a slanted rectangle of light across the floor and moved it, over the course of forty minutes, from the near wall almost to the door, so that Cael could track the session's progress without ever consulting the water clock in the corridor. Her research notebook open on the table beside his observation notebook. A chalk line she'd drawn across the floor on the first day, dividing the room into the space where he worked and the space where she watched, which neither of them had ever crossed and neither of them had ever mentioned, and the two of them working in a silence that had stopped feeling like an imposition sometime around the third session and started feeling, instead, like the quiet two people kept when they both understood exactly what the other one was doing.

The sessions themselves were plainer than anyone watching from outside would have guessed. He ran the Wind-adjacent evasion framework at half intensity while she timed his recovery intervals against a mark on her page. He held the Iron-adjacent surface read on a training post wrapped in three different grades of cloth and described what he could and couldn't feel through each, and she wrote down his descriptions word for word and then read them back to him to confirm she hadn't smoothed anything in the transcription — the first time she did that, he'd stared at her for a moment, because nobody had ever checked their record of him against him before. The Compact had assessed him. The circuit had rated him. Karis transcribed him, and then asked whether the transcription was accurate, as if the difference between what she'd heard and what he'd meant were a quantity worth chasing down to the last decimal. It was. He'd known it was his whole life. She was the first person with a notebook who'd agreed.

She never asked him to explain anything he hadn't offered first. That was the whole architecture of the terms, and she held to it with the same precision she brought to everything else — no questions about origins, no questions about acquisition, nothing that would put either of them on the wrong side of the paper all four of them had signed. Once, early, she'd started a sentence — "When you first—" — and stopped herself mid-word, closed her mouth, made a note in the margin of her own book, and moved on to the next timing run. He'd watched her do it and understood that the note was the question, filed rather than asked, and that she'd probably built a whole page of such notes by now, questions she'd caught herself almost asking, kept the way he kept his own unanswerables. He found that he minded it less than he'd expected to. Possibly he didn't mind it at all. A person who wrote her questions down instead of asking them was a person who understood that some doors opened from the inside only.

But holding to the terms didn't mean she wasn't building something underneath them, session by session, out of nothing but what she was allowed to see. He knew the look of accumulating structure. He'd worn it himself for a year and a half, in circuit galleries and training yards and the long watchful evenings at the Ironyard rail — the outward stillness of someone stacking observations one on top of another and waiting for the stack to become a shape. Karis had it now, had had it for at least a fortnight, and he had begun, in the privacy of his own notebook, keeping a small side-tally of the moments her pen paused. The pauses were the tell. She paused when something fit.

He found out how much she'd built on a Wednesday evening, in the common room, with the fire down to coals and the residence wing's usual traffic thinned to almost nothing. Lira was reading in the corner chair, boots off, one foot tucked under her, working through a defensive-theory text Wray had loaned her with the expression of someone auditing an opponent rather than studying a subject. Brom was halfway through a bowl of something Naveth's kitchen had produced in industrial quantity that week — a barley thing, dense, the third night running it had appeared, which Brom had declared on the second night to be "honest food" and defended since against all comers. Cael had his own notebook open and wasn't writing in it. And Karis, at the long table, closed her research notebook with a deliberate, settled finality — not the absent snap of someone finished for the night, but the flat, careful closing of someone who had been waiting for the right room to say something in, and had just decided this was the room.

"I want to share a hypothesis," she said. "Not a finding. A hypothesis. I'd like all three of you to hear it at once, because if I'm right, it changes how I think about the sessions, and you should all get to react to that together rather than separately."

Brom set down his spoon. Lira put a finger in her book to hold the page and did not look up from it right away, which Cael had learned to read as her version of full attention — the not-looking was a courtesy, a way of letting a speaker get all the way into a difficult sentence before anyone's face could react to it.

"Go on," Cael said.

Karis laid it out the way she laid out everything — clean, sequenced, no hedging where hedging wasn't warranted and no false confidence where it was. "I've been documenting your capabilities against the conditions under which they appeared. I don't have complete data — you haven't told me where any of them came from, and I haven't asked — but I have enough structural information to see a pattern in the shape of the acquisitions themselves, independent of their source. Every capability I've been able to characterize corresponds, in its signature, to a discipline you had extended, close-range exposure to. Not brief contact. Not a single encounter. Sustained observation, over time, of one practitioner working at genuine intensity." She paused, the way she did when she was about to say the part that mattered most. "Whatever your architecture does, observation appears to be upstream of it. And if exposure is the input — if that's actually the mechanism, and not just a correlation I'm imagining because I want to see one — then in principle, the process should be directable. You should be able to aim it. Not just receive it."

The room went still in a way that had nothing to do with the food going cold or the fire needing tending.

Cael sat with the sentence the way he sat with anything that reorganized something he'd thought was fixed. He was aware, distantly, of the coals ticking in the grate, of Brom's spoon resting against the rim of the bowl at an angle that would have slid it in and drowned it if Brom had been paying attention to anything except Karis, of Lira's finger still holding her page in a book she was no longer reading. Mostly he was aware of the size of what had just been said, and of the strange double vision of hearing it from outside.

Because Karis had never seen the Power Log. That was the thing he kept returning to, in the long seconds while nobody spoke. She didn't know about Lira's Wind, about Feryn in the Cinder House yard, over a year and a lifetime ago, about Brom's slow unfolding trust or Reydan's third exchange. She had none of the raw data he'd spent every notebook he owned accumulating in careful handwriting, page after page, entry after entry, the private ledger of everything he was that only two other people alive had ever read. She had only what she could see from the outside — the shape of the results, without any of the history that produced them — timing runs and recovery intervals and transcribed descriptions, the residue his capabilities left in observable space. And from that alone, working purely from observables, holding scrupulously to terms that walled off every fact that would have made it easy, she had reconstructed, in under two months, the outline of a question he and Lira and Brom had been circling in whispers and hedges for the better part of a year and a half.

It should have frightened him. Some part of it did — the demonstrated fact that what he was could be read from outside, by a mind sharp enough, meant it could someday be read by minds he hadn't consented to. But underneath the fright was something he had less practice naming, and it took him most of those long seconds to find the word for it. Relief. Someone else could see it. It was real enough to be seen. He had spent so long as the only instrument capable of measuring himself that he'd stopped noticing the loneliness of it, the way you stopped noticing a weight you never set down.

Brom said it first, softly, not quite a joke. "Told you she had a method."

"It's not proof," Karis said, before anyone could treat it as more than it was. "It's a hypothesis with reasonable support. I'd want controlled conditions to test it properly — and I understand that testing it might not be something you want to do, and that's your decision entirely, not mine. I just thought you should hear the shape of it from me directly, rather than piece it together from what I do and don't ask."

Lira looked up from her book then, at last, and studied Karis across the room for a moment with an expression Cael couldn't fully parse — some measurement being taken, some column being totted up. Whatever the total came to, she didn't say it aloud. She only nodded, once, and went back to her book, and Cael filed the nod away with everything else, because he had watched Lira decide things about people for a year and a half and he was fairly sure he had just watched a decision.

"Thank you," Cael said to Karis, and meant it — meant it more than the two flat words could carry, and suspected she knew that, because she inclined her head with the small exactness she used for received data and did not press for more.

He did not say anything else that evening. He sat with his notebook open and unwritten-in while the fire burned down and Brom finished the barley thing and eventually the three of them drifted off to their rooms in the usual order — Brom first, then Karis, then Lira with a light hand on his shoulder in passing that said *later, if you want* — because the thing the hypothesis had actually done to him wasn't a research question. It was a floor giving way somewhere under a room he'd thought was solid, and he could feel the whole house of himself resettling above the new gap, joist by joist, beam by beam, and he wanted to know the final shape of the damage before he showed it to anyone.

---

He turned it over for two days before he understood exactly what was destabilizing about it, and once he understood, he couldn't stop understanding it, the way certain kinds of clarity didn't allow for going back to not having them.

The fragments arrived. That was the fact he had built his entire relationship to his own ability around, from the first unnamed thing that had let him move like Lira in a training yard through every acquisition since: they came on their own schedule, unbidden, like weather. He didn't summon rain. He didn't choose which storms crossed which stretch of sky. The not-choosing had been, he realized now, load-bearing in a way he'd never examined — it was the thing that let him think of himself as someone things happened to rather than someone who did things, a distinction that mattered enormously to a boy who had spent a year and a half being handed classifications by people who'd never once asked his opinion about what he actually was. *Shattered* was a thing done to him. The fragments were things that happened to him. Everything in his file, everything in his history, everything in the Compact's patient distant ledger of his existence agreed on the grammar of it: Cael, object. The world, subject. He had hated that grammar for a year and a half and leaned on it anyway, because its alternative had never once occurred to him.

If it could be directed, the not-choosing was a story he'd told himself.

He tested the idea against the record, because testing ideas against the record was the only honest thing he knew how to do with them. He took the Power Log up to his room the first night and went through it entry by entry, all the way back, past the Greyvane pages and the circuit pages to the earliest entries with their too-careful lettering, the handwriting of a boy trying to make a notebook look official because officialdom had just spat him out. Lira's Wind — arrived after months of sparring, months of watching her move with his whole attention because his whole attention was the only advantage he had. Feryn — arrived after a bout he had studied for, obsessed over, mapped in advance. Brom — after weeks of sessions in which learning Brom's architecture had been, quite literally, the assignment. Reydan — at the crest of the hardest fight of his life, against the opponent he had watched more closely than any before. He read the pattern with the lamp burned low and the residence wing gone silent around him, and the pattern read back plainly, and had been reading back plainly the whole time to anyone with the wit to hold the entries at arm's length instead of living inside them.

And if it was never random, then what had happened with Lira's Wind-adjacent framework, the reflexive echo he'd carried out of those early months and never fully understood — what had happened with Feryn, with Brom's Iron-adjacent surface-read, with the strange desperate reach that had found Reydan's Compression at the exact worst moment of a fight he'd nearly lost — none of it had been weather at all. It had been something closer to appetite, operating below the threshold where he could see it working, taking from the people he loved most in the world without his conscious knowledge or their conscious consent, on a schedule he'd told himself, for a year and a half, that neither he nor anyone else had any say in.

The second day, he carried the idea around Greyvane like a stone in his boot. He sat through a lecture on declaration ethics — the irony of which was not lost on him — and filled half a page with notes he never afterward remembered taking. He watched the open training floor from the observer's rail at midday, eleven Paths in motion below him, and caught himself, for the first time in his life, monitoring his own attention: *am I looking at that Glass-work because it's interesting, or is something in me looking at it the way a hand looks at bread.* The question was unanswerable and unwearable and he wore it anyway, through the midday meal and past it, until Brom found him.

Brom found people the way weather systems found valleys — without apparent intention, reliably, and with a settling quality once arrived. He came off the defensive-branch floor still in training gear, took the stretch of rail next to Cael without asking, and watched the floor below in companionable silence for the length of two full bouts. That was Brom's method, insofar as Brom admitted to having one: he let the silence do the opening argument.

"You've been looking at that Glass-work for a quarter hour," he said eventually. "The morning after Karis says the thing might be aimable." He didn't look over. "I can count too."

"I don't know what I'm doing when I watch anymore," Cael said. "That's the honest answer. I don't know if I ever knew."

Brom considered that with the thoroughness he gave everything, turning it the way he'd turn an opponent's stance, looking for where the weight actually sat. "First month we trained," he said, "you watched me like a ledger. I knew it. Everyone who's ever stood across from you knows it — it's the least hidden thing about you. You think the watching changed its nature because she gave it a name?"

"I think it might have had a nature I didn't know about."

"Mm." Brom was quiet for another exchange's worth of the bout below. "When Wray corrects my footwork," he said, "he's not stealing my balance. He's paying the closest attention to it anyone ever has. Those are the same behavior from the outside. Intent's the whole difference, and intent is the one thing you get to decide." He straightened off the rail, taking his time rolling the stiffness out of one shoulder — then caught Cael's expression and shrugged. "I'm not saying it's simple. I'm saying you've been assuming the worst grammar available since last night, and the worst grammar isn't the only one that fits the facts. She said *directable*. She didn't say *hungry*. You supplied that part yourself."

It was, Cael reflected, watching Brom walk off toward the residence wing with his gear bag over one shoulder, pure Brom: arrive, say four sentences that reorganized a problem, and leave before anyone could argue with him. *You supplied that part yourself.* He took out the observation notebook and wrote the sentence down, because it was true and he hadn't seen it, and the gap between true and seen was the gap he kept the notebook for.

But it didn't close the question. It only narrowed it to its honest width — and the honest width was still wide enough to hold a year and a half of acquisitions from people who'd never been asked. He carried the narrowed thing through supper, up the stairs and back down them again, because there was one person the question belonged to before it belonged to anyone else, and he had learned across a year and a half of walking toward things that the longer he carried a thing alone, the heavier it got for everyone else when he finally set it down.

He was not a person things happened to. He was a person who did things. He had been, apparently, the whole time, without knowing it — and the not-knowing didn't make it kinder. It made it worse, in the way that an accident you couldn't have prevented was easier to live with than a habit you'd simply never noticed you had.

He took it to Lira the same way he'd taken the first disclosure, a year and a season ago, on a rooftop that felt very far away now: alone, at night, no audience, nothing dressed up to make it easier to hear.

---

He found her on the low wall outside the residence wing, the one that had become their spot without either of them deciding it should be. She was already there when he came out, which told him Brom had probably said something, or she'd simply counted the days since Karis's hypothesis and arrived at tonight on her own — she was better at reading him than anyone alive, and she'd had two days of watching him carry the stone around. The night was clear and cool, the hill town's lamps scattered downslope below the academy wall like coals shaken out of a grate, and the training yard was a dark rectangle beyond, holding the day's heat up into the air the way stone did.

He sat down beside her without preamble, because preamble had never been the honest way to do this with her.

"I might have been taking from you on purpose," he said. "Without knowing it was on purpose. Karis thinks the process might be directable. If she's right, then what happened with your Wind-adjacent framework wasn't something that just happened near me. It might have been something I did. To you. Without asking, because I didn't know I was capable of asking."

Lira didn't answer right away. She looked out at the dark shape of the training yard, the way she did when she was actually thinking rather than performing having thought, and Cael made himself wait for it, hands flat on the cold stone of the wall, because rushing her through this would have been its own small theft — one more thing taken without asking, and he was done with those, or wanted to be.

"You told me what you knew, when you knew it," she said finally, steady, no give in it at all. "You told me in Ardenmere, the moment you understood there was something to tell. You've told me every fragment since, the day it happened, in your own handwriting, in a log you handed me because you wanted me to have it, not because I made you. If it turns out the mechanism was aimed at me in some way you couldn't see and I couldn't feel — that doesn't change any of that. Keep doing what you've been doing. Tell me what you know when you know it. There's nothing to forgive in a thing you didn't know you were doing, Cael. There's only something to keep being honest about once you find out."

"That's generous."

"It's not generous. It's the actual arithmetic." She bumped her shoulder against his, the old familiar gesture, worn smooth by a year and a season of use. "You want to know what would actually cost you something with me? Finding out, and not telling me. That's the version where I stop trusting the log. You haven't done that version. You're not doing it now, sitting here telling me before you've even confirmed it's true." She looked at him properly then. "So stop apologizing for a thing you haven't done yet, and go figure out whether it's even real."

He let the sentence sit in the cold air between them for a while. Down the hill, a door opened somewhere in the town and closed again, a small far-off sound with a whole unknown life attached to it.

"You're not afraid of it," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I didn't say that." She was still looking at the yard. "I'm afraid of most of what's ahead of you, if you want the full ledger. I'm afraid of what the Compact does when it stops being patient. I'm afraid of what it costs you every time the thing in you turns out to be bigger than the last measurement said it was." A breath. "But I'm not afraid of *you*, and I'm not going to start being afraid of you because a girl with very good handwriting worked out that the thing might have a handle on it. If it has a handle, better your hand than nobody's. That's the whole of what I think."

"Better my hand than nobody's," he repeated, slowly, trying the weight of it.

"Somebody's steering," Lira said. "That's what her hypothesis actually means, if it's true. Something has been steering the whole time. The only question on the table is whether it gets to keep being the part of you that doesn't ask." She stood, unfolding off the wall with the easy economy that no academy had taught her, and looked down at him for a moment. "Go on and learn it, Cael. You were always going to. You've never once left a thing you didn't understand alone in your life — it's the most reliable fact about you, and I've been keeping records longer than she has."

It was the covenant from a year and a season ago, renewed under a heavier load than it had carried the first time, and it held anyway, the way things that were built correctly the first time tended to hold when the load increased. He sat on the wall a while after she'd gone in, letting the cold work through his coat, watching the last lamps go out down the hill one by one, each small extinguishing a household closing its ledger on the day. Somewhere behind him, in the residence wing, a girl from Ternhall was almost certainly still awake, filling margins with the questions she wasn't allowed to ask. Somewhere three days west, in a city he still dreamed about, a woman kept a records room where the truth about people was stored like grain against winter. The world was full of keepers of accounts. He'd been one his whole life without ever once auditing the account that mattered most, the one written in his own architecture, in a hand he was only now learning might be his.

He wrote it down that night, in the Power Log, in the careful hand he saved for the entries that had to outlast his own doubt about them.

*If it can be directed, I have to learn to direct it. Not to have more. To stop not-knowing what I'm doing. Not-knowing is the version that eventually gets someone killed.*

He read the sentence back once, and understood, closing the log, that the fear underneath it wasn't really about power at all. It was about the people standing close enough to him to be spent by it, whether or not he'd ever meant to spend them — Lira on the cold wall telling him the arithmetic came out clean, Brom defending barley soup two rooms away, Karis and her chalk line and her filed unasked questions. The account was theirs as much as his. It always had been. Learning to read it was the only honest payment he had.

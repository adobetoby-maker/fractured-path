# Chapter 9 — Method

Karis's sessions had a shape to them within a week, the way most things did once enough repetitions had passed through Cael's notice for a pattern to show itself. Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, the smaller training room off the archive wing, forty minutes, her research notebook open on the table beside his observation notebook, the two of them working in a silence that had stopped feeling like an imposition sometime around the third session and started feeling, instead, like the kind of quiet two people kept when they both understood exactly what the other one was doing.

She never asked him to explain anything he hadn't offered first. That was the whole architecture of the terms, and she held to it with the same precision she brought to everything else — but holding to the terms didn't mean she wasn't building something underneath them, session by session, out of nothing but what she was allowed to see.

He found out how much she'd built on a Wednesday evening, in the common room, with Lira reading in the corner chair and Brom halfway through a bowl of something Naveth's kitchen had produced in industrial quantity that week, when Karis closed her notebook with the particular finality of someone who had been waiting for the right room to say something in.

"I want to share a hypothesis," she said. "Not a finding. A hypothesis. I'd like all three of you to hear it at once, because if I'm right, it changes how I think about the sessions, and you should all get to react to that together rather than separately."

Brom set down his spoon. Lira put a finger in her book to hold the page and did not look up from it right away, which Cael had learned to read as her version of full attention.

"Go on," Cael said.

Karis laid it out the way she laid out everything — clean, sequenced, no hedging where hedging wasn't warranted and no false confidence where it was. "I've been documenting your capabilities against the conditions under which they appeared. I don't have complete data — you haven't told me where any of them came from, and I haven't asked — but I have enough structural information to see a pattern in the shape of the acquisitions themselves, independent of their source. Every capability I've been able to characterize corresponds, in its signature, to a discipline you had extended, close-range exposure to. Not brief contact. Not a single encounter. Sustained observation, over time, of one practitioner working at genuine intensity." She paused, the way she did when she was about to say the part that mattered most. "Whatever your architecture does, observation appears to be upstream of it. And if exposure is the input — if that's actually the mechanism, and not just a correlation I'm imagining because I want to see one — then in principle, the process should be directable. You should be able to aim it. Not just receive it."

The room went still in a way that had nothing to do with the food going cold or the fire needing tending.

Cael sat with the sentence the way he sat with anything that reorganized something he'd thought was fixed. Karis had never seen the Power Log. She didn't know about Lira's Wind, about Feryn in a Denvash alley two years and a lifetime ago, about Brom's slow unfolding trust or Reydan's third exchange. She had none of the raw data he'd spent two books accumulating in careful handwriting, page after page, entry after entry. She had only what she could see from the outside — the shape of the results, without any of the history that produced them — and from that alone, working purely from observables, she had reconstructed, in under two months, the outline of a question he and Lira and Brom had been circling in whispers and hedges for the better part of two years.

Brom said it first, softly, not quite a joke. "Told you she had a method."

"It's not proof," Karis said, before anyone could treat it as more than it was. "It's a hypothesis with reasonable support. I'd want controlled conditions to test it properly — and I understand that testing it might not be something you want to do, and that's your decision entirely, not mine. I just thought you should hear the shape of it from me directly, rather than piece it together from what I do and don't ask."

Cael thanked her, meant it, and did not say anything else that evening, because the thing the hypothesis had actually done to him wasn't a research question. It was a floor giving way somewhere under a room he'd thought was solid.

---

He turned it over for two days before he understood exactly what was destabilizing about it, and once he understood, he couldn't stop understanding it, the way certain kinds of clarity didn't allow for going back to not having them.

The fragments arrived. That was the fact he had built his entire relationship to his own ability around, from the first unnamed thing that had let him move like Feryn in a Denvash alley through every acquisition since: they came on their own schedule, unbidden, like weather. He didn't summon rain. He didn't choose which storms crossed which particular stretch of sky. The not-choosing had been, he realized now, load-bearing in a way he'd never examined — it was the thing that let him think of himself as someone things happened to rather than someone who did things, a distinction that mattered enormously to a boy who had spent two years being handed classifications by people who'd never once asked his opinion about what he actually was.

If it could be directed, the not-choosing was a story he'd told himself. And if it was a story, then what had happened with Lira's Wind-adjacent framework, the reflexive echo he'd carried out of that first bout and never fully understood — what had happened with Feryn, with Brom's Iron-adjacent surface-read, with the strange desperate reach that had found Reydan's Compression at the exact worst moment of a fight he'd nearly lost — none of it had been weather at all. It had been something closer to appetite, operating below the threshold where he could see it working, taking from the people he loved most in the world without his conscious knowledge or their conscious consent, on a schedule he'd told himself, for two years, that neither he nor anyone else had any say in.

He was not a person things happened to. He was a person who did things. He had been, apparently, the whole time, without knowing it — and the not-knowing didn't make it kinder. It made it worse, in the way that an accident you couldn't have prevented was easier to live with than a habit you'd simply never noticed you had.

He took it to Lira the same way he'd taken the first disclosure, a year and a season ago, on a rooftop that felt very far away now: alone, at night, no audience, nothing dressed up to make it easier to hear.

He found her on the low wall outside the residence wing, the one that had become their spot without either of them deciding it should be, and sat down beside her without preamble, because preamble had never been the honest way to do this with her.

"I might have been taking from you on purpose," he said. "Without knowing it was on purpose. Karis thinks the process might be directable. If she's right, then what happened with your Wind-adjacent framework wasn't something that just happened near me. It might have been something I did. To you. Without asking, because I didn't know I was capable of asking."

Lira didn't answer right away. She looked out at the dark shape of the training yard, the way she did when she was actually thinking rather than performing having thought, and Cael made himself wait for it, because rushing her through this would have been its own kind of taking.

"You told me what you knew, when you knew it," she said finally, steady, no give in it at all. "You told me in Ardenmere, the moment you understood there was something to tell. You've told me every fragment since, the day it happened, in your own handwriting, in a log you handed me because you wanted me to have it, not because I made you. If it turns out the mechanism was aimed at me in some way you couldn't see and I couldn't feel — that doesn't change any of that. Keep doing what you've been doing. Tell me what you know when you know it. There's nothing to forgive in a thing you didn't know you were doing, Cael. There's only something to keep being honest about once you find out."

"That's generous."

"It's not generous. It's the actual arithmetic." She bumped her shoulder against his, the old familiar gesture, worn smooth by a year and a season of use. "You want to know what would actually cost you something with me? Finding out, and not telling me. That's the version where I stop trusting the log. You haven't done that version. You're not doing it now, sitting here telling me before you've even confirmed it's true." She looked at him properly then. "So stop apologizing for a thing you haven't done yet, and go figure out whether it's even real."

It was the covenant from a year and a season ago, renewed under a heavier load than it had carried the first time, and it held anyway, the way things that were built correctly the first time tended to hold when the load increased.

He wrote it down that night, in the Power Log, in the careful hand he used for everything that actually mattered.

*If it can be directed, I have to learn to direct it. Not to have more. To stop not-knowing what I'm doing. Not-knowing is the version that eventually gets someone killed.*

He read the sentence back once, and understood, closing the log, that the fear underneath it wasn't really about power at all. It was about the people standing close enough to him to be spent by it, whether or not he'd ever meant to spend them.

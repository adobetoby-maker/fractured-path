# Chapter 1 — The Gate

The road did most of the work of arriving before Greyvane itself did.

Three days out of Ardenmere, walking the kind of quiet the three of them had gotten good at not needing to fill, the country changed by degrees — the flat market towns giving way to hill country, the road narrowing and steepening and then, on the third morning, doing the thing roads did before something waited at the top of them: straightening out, as if it had decided not to waste anyone's last half-mile on unnecessary turns.

The first day had been mostly farmland, the kind of open, tended country that made a person feel watched by nothing but crows, and they'd covered ground quickly on the strength of habit alone — a year and a half of circuit travel had taught Cael and Lira the same walking pace, and Brom had matched it inside a week of falling in with them, without any of them ever discussing it. The second day the farmland thinned into scrub and low woodland, and the road started climbing in earnest, gentle at first and then less so, and by evening they'd made camp in a stand of pine that smelled like every other stand of pine any of them had ever slept under, which was its own kind of comfort. Cael had lain awake a while that second night listening to Brom's breathing settle and Lira shift once, twice, into a position she'd hold until morning, and had thought, not for the first time, that the sound of two other people sleeping nearby had become something close to a requirement for his own sleep, without his ever quite noticing the day it started being true.

Cael had spent most of the walk doing what he always did with unstructured time, which was fail to leave it unstructured. He'd gone through the binder twice — four fragment entries, the deployment-range column current, the Tide-adjacent anomaly still sitting in its own section with nothing under *Note* but a single line he'd written and not crossed out: *Session nine. Could not reproduce. Still don't know what that was.* He'd read Hesk's last letter three times, which was one more than he usually allowed himself, on the theory that letters were meant to be received once and carried rather than worn down through repeated handling. He'd made it three times anyway.

The Tide-adjacent entry was the one his eyes kept returning to, though, more than the letter, more than anything else in the binder. Four confirmed fragments sat in their own tidy rows, deployment range noted, source noted, everything a practitioner could reasonably want to know about a thing he now understood well enough to use without thinking about it. The fifth entry had none of that. Just the date, the bare fact of what had happened in Brom's sparring circle that one afternoon, and the single unfinished line underneath it. He had turned it over so many times in the months since that turning it over no longer felt like investigation. It felt like checking that a scar was still there — a habit rather than an inquiry, the kind of thing a person did less because he expected to learn anything new and more because stopping felt like admitting he'd given up on it.

"You're doing the thing," Lira said, on the second afternoon, not looking up from her own pace, which had settled somewhere between purposeful and impatient since the moment Ardenmere's gate had shrunk out of sight behind them.

"What thing."

"The thing where you read something you've already memorized because reading it is easier than sitting with whatever you're actually thinking about."

"I don't have that thing."

"You have a whole notebook about that thing."

He didn't have a response to this that wasn't itself an example of the thing, so he put the binder away.

Brom, walking a half-step behind them both with the loose, ground-eating economy of someone who'd covered more ground on foot than either of them combined, said nothing for a while, and then: "First time leaving a place on purpose."

"You left Velmere."

"I left Velmere the way you leave a room that's on fire. This is different." He considered the road ahead, the hills doing their slow work of resolving into something with an actual shape. "This is walking toward something instead of away from something. Haven't done that before. Didn't know it felt different, but it does."

Cael turned this over. It was, he thought, an accurate description of what he was doing too, though it had taken Brom saying it plainly for him to recognize the shape of it in himself. He wrote it in the notebook before he could stop himself, which rather proved Lira's point, and did not mention that he'd done it.

"What do you think it'll be like," Lira asked, later, when the road had leveled out enough to walk three abreast instead of single file, "an actual academy. A real one, even if it's a small one."

"Structured," Cael said. "Predictable, in the ways that matter. Probably less predictable in the ways I haven't thought of yet."

"That's not an answer, that's a hedge."

"It's an honest hedge. I don't have enough data to answer the actual question." He considered it further, because she'd asked in the tone that meant she wanted the real attempt, not the deflection. "I think it'll be the first place that has rules about me instead of just reactions to me. I don't know yet whether that's better."

"It's better," Brom said, without hesitation, from behind them. "Reactions change every time you walk into a new room. Rules, you can learn. You're good at learning rules."

Cael didn't have an answer to that either, not right away, so he let it sit with the rest of the day's small, accumulating certainties, and kept walking.

---

Greyvane appeared the way minor institutions announced themselves, which was to say: not with the confident scale of somewhere that expected to be found impressive, but with the plain fact of being exactly as large as it needed to be and no larger. The wall came first — old stone, waystation-era by the look of the coursing, weathered into the kind of grey that took centuries rather than decades. The gate was newer, added at some point after the wall had stopped needing to keep anyone out and started needing to log who came in.

Cael catalogued it the way he catalogued everything, because cataloguing was easier than sitting with what he was actually feeling, an irony he was aware of and unable to do much about. The wall: old waystation stone, load-bearing, unornamented. The gate: two generations newer at most, iron-strapped oak, a single clerk's booth built into the near side of it rather than a full guardhouse, which told him something about how much traffic this place actually saw. Beyond the gate, what he could see of the grounds: a training yard, walled separately, smaller than the Ironyard by a comfortable margin; a residence wing with the particular window-spacing of a building that had been retrofitted for dormitory use rather than built for it; a lecture wing further back, newer construction, the only part of the complex that looked like anyone had built it with Greyvane itself in mind rather than inheriting it from whatever the waystation had needed.

Smaller than the word *academy* had promised him, in the three days he'd had to build an idea of the place out of nothing but Quenna's account of it. More real for the smallness. He'd learned enough about the gap between what things were called and what they were to find the honesty of a small place calling itself accurately more reassuring than a large place's marketing might have been.

The light was doing the thing afternoon light did in hill country, coming in low and sideways and catching the wall's old stone at an angle that made the whole surface look faintly gold, and Cael stood there a moment longer than the walk required just watching it, because it was the kind of thing he'd have missed entirely a year and a half ago, and hadn't missed since Ardenmere taught him to notice what a place looked like before he decided what it meant. Somewhere beyond the wall a bell rang once, slow and unbothered, marking some hour of the academy's day that had nothing to do with their arrival, and the plain fact of an institution that had gone on being itself, on its own schedule, for sixty years before they'd ever heard its name, did something to settle the last of the road's nervous energy out of his shoulders.

The gate clerk had a list.

This, more than the wall or the gate or the modest scale of the grounds, was the thing that actually landed. The clerk — a woman perhaps forty, ink-stained at the first two fingers of her right hand in the manner of someone who spent her days transcribing rather than composing — ran a finger down a page, found what she was looking for, and said, without any particular ceremony: "Three of you. Cael, unclassified observer track. Lira, re-certification candidate. Brom, standard enrollment." She looked up. "You're expected. Go on through — intake's the first door past the yard, someone will meet you."

Cael stood there a half-second longer than the moment required.

It was, he understood, a small thing. An administrative fact, the kind of logistics that made any institution function, no different in principle from the certification appointment the city of Denvash had scheduled for him a year and a half before he'd ever asked for it. But there was a difference between a door opening because the system required it to open on schedule, and a door opening because someone in particular had looked at his name in advance and decided it belonged on the other side. He had spent most of his life being processed. This was the first time, he thought, walking through the gate behind Lira and Brom with a lightness in his chest he didn't have a category for yet, that he'd arrived somewhere and found his name already waiting for him rather than something he had to prove into existence on-site.

He wrote it down, later, in different words. For now he just noticed it, and let it sit there, unfiled, which for Cael was its own kind of significant.

---

Intake ran three lines, which made the asymmetry visible before anyone said a word about it.

The intake building itself was small and old — waystation stock, like the wall, with the low ceilings and thick-set doorframes of construction that predated anyone's current notion of how much headroom a person needed. Three desks, three clerks, a bench along the far wall where a handful of other arrivals sat waiting their own turn, and a window at the back that let in enough afternoon light to make the whole room feel less like a bureaucratic chamber and more like someone's well-organized study. Cael catalogued the room out of habit while they waited to be called forward, and found himself, not for the first time, grateful that habit gave him something to do with his hands and his attention while the actual waiting happened underneath it.

Brom's line moved fastest. Application, record, stamp — the clerk handling his paperwork glanced at his Copper-tier certification, his clean transfer record from two seasons of documented circuit work, and processed him with the brisk efficiency of someone handling the most straightforward case she'd see all week. Eleven minutes, start to finish. Brom accepted his enrollment tag — a small stamped disc, the kind that would get him through most doors on the grounds without a second look — turned it over once in his fingers, and allowed himself an expression that on anyone else might have been called smug, and on Brom registered as something closer to quiet astonishment that a process involving him could be this uncomplicated.

"That's it?" he said.

"That's it," the clerk said. "Orientation's at the fourth bell. Residence wing, east hall, your name's on a door."

Brom looked at the tag again, and then at Cael, with the look of a person who had spent two years being the complicated case in every room he'd entered and had genuinely not anticipated what it would feel like to be the simple one. "I don't know what to do with this," he said, meaning the tag, and possibly meaning something larger than the tag.

"Put it in your pocket," Lira said, from the next line over, not quite masking the strain in her own voice. "That's generally what people do with the uncomplicated ones."

Brom put it in his pocket, and then, a moment later, took it back out and looked at it once more, as though checking it hadn't quietly turned into something more complicated while he wasn't watching. It hadn't. He put it away again, properly this time, and went to go find the residence wing on his own, because there was, for once, nothing left in his day that required anyone's supervision.

Her line took the better part of an hour.

The re-certification provision, it turned out, required more than a stamp. It required her prior record — her Fenmark file, which had evidently traveled ahead of her by whatever channel academies used to communicate about students they'd expelled — cross-referenced against the provision's exact language, verified, and then, at the end of it, a single sheet placed in front of her that she had to sign. Cael, standing close enough to see without meaning to intrude, caught the heading: *Acknowledgment of Prior Standing.* Beneath it, in the flat administrative language institutions used for things that mattered enormously to the people signing them and not at all to the people who'd drafted the form: a formal acknowledgment that her enrollment at Fenmark Academy had been terminated, that the termination stood as recorded, and that her candidacy here was contingent on accepting that record as accurate rather than disputed.

Lira read it twice. Her jaw did most of the talking during the second read — a muscle working at the hinge of it, the taut control of someone holding a much larger reaction down to a size that fit the room.

She'd spent two years refusing to say the word *expelled* about herself without immediately following it with an explanation of why the assessment had been wrong. She signed the sheet without a word, and the silence afterward said more than the explanation would have.

"That's the whole of it?" Cael asked, once she'd stepped back from the desk.

"That's the whole of it." She was looking somewhere past him, at nothing in particular. "Turns out being angry about a thing for two years doesn't actually change what you have to sign to move past it. Who knew."

"You could have told them the assessment was wrong."

"I could have. There's a box on the form for a formal dispute filing, if I want to pursue one later, separately, through channels that have nothing to do with getting enrolled here today." She exhaled, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch. "I decided I wanted to be enrolled here today more than I wanted to be right on paper about something that happened two years ago. That's new, for me. I'm choosing to think it's growth."

"It might be growth."

"Don't sound so surprised."

Cael did not, in fact, sound surprised, but he understood the comment was doing a different kind of work than a literal correction would address, so he let it stand.

She stood there a moment longer, the signed sheet already handed back and filed away behind the desk, out of her hands now in every sense that mattered. "Two years," she said, mostly to herself. "I thought I'd feel different, signing it. I thought it would feel like losing again."

"Does it?"

"No." She sounded almost surprised by her own answer. "It feels like putting something down I've been carrying since before I met you. Heavier than I let myself notice, until it wasn't there anymore." She shook her head once, like she was shaking off the thought before it could turn into something she'd have to examine standing in a hallway. "Go find out what they've decided you are. I'll be on the wall."

---

Cael's line took the rest of the day.

There was no queue for it, in the end, because there was no established process it fit cleanly into — which was, he understood by the second hour, the entire point of the category he'd been enrolled under. Quenna met him at the intake desk herself rather than delegating him to a clerk, and walked him through it personally, which he registered as either a courtesy or a liability depending on how he chose to read her attention.

The enrollment document, when it finally reached the table in front of him, classified him as an *unclassified observer.*

He read the category line three times. It was a designation, the accompanying notes explained in the same flat administrative register that had governed Lira's acknowledgment sheet, reserved for prospective practitioners who had not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment — children, typically, whose families had petitioned for early academic exposure ahead of their certification appointment. It granted training-floor access, lecture attendance, and academic standing, without conferring any Path classification, tier ranking, or Arbiter-issued record. It was, on its face, a category built for someone who did not yet exist as a practitioner in any way the Compact's registry recognized.

Cael, fifteen years old, Iron-equivalent by the honest ledger of a year and a half of circuit work, four fragment entries filed in the binder currently sitting in his pack, read the words *has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment* and did not say anything for a long moment.

"I underwent it," he said finally. "A year and a half ago. There's a record."

"There's a record with a classification in it that the Compact's own schedule doesn't define," Quenna said, evenly, the same even precision she'd brought to every conversation he'd had with her since the night she'd first sat down across from him at the Ironyard. "The provision's language covers 'any practitioner without a standard Arbiter-issued classification.' Yours isn't standard. It isn't, strictly, anything the schedule accounts for at all. That's not a loophole I invented, Cael. It's a gap that was already there. I'm walking you through the door it happens to open."

"It's still a lie. On the form. In writing."

"It's an accurate description of a category the Compact never built for someone like you, filled out using the closest available language, because the alternative was leaving you with no category at all." She held his gaze without softening it. "The provision's language is what protects you. What it says is defensible — the words on this page will hold up to scrutiny exactly as written. The provision's *intent*, though, is not the same thing as its language, and that's the part you'll be asked about, someday, by someone who's read this form more carefully than the clerk who processed Brom's did."

"Someone will look closely."

"Yes." No hesitation in it, no attempt to make the answer smaller than it was. "Our job, between now and then, is to be established fact by the time they do. A student with a monthly assessment record. A student whose demonstration performance speaks for itself, publicly, on a schedule anyone can verify. The longer you're simply *here*, doing well, on the record, the harder it becomes for anyone to argue you shouldn't be."

"And if someone argues it anyway."

"Then we'll have had months, at least, of established fact standing between you and whatever they're arguing." Quenna folded her hands on the table, an economical gesture that somehow closed the conversation without ending it. "I've made bets with worse odds than this one, Cael. I generally don't make them without believing they'll pay."

Cael looked at the form again. The category above the signature line was, by any honest accounting, not what he was. He thought, briefly, about the certification appointment in Denvash, a year and a half and a lifetime ago now, the four lines about [SHATTERED] classification that had sat in his memory ever since with the stubborn weight of a thing he'd never managed to write down properly. Four instances, in the whole recorded history of the registry. He was about to become, on paper, something the registry had never once had to account for at all — not a fifth instance of a known problem, but a category error the system's own language couldn't hold. It was a strange kind of progress, becoming a problem too new to have a file yet. He wasn't certain it was better than the alternative. He was fairly certain it was the only door currently open.

He was going to sign it anyway, because Quenna's math was better than his fear, and because he had already walked three days toward this door with two people who'd walked it with him rather than around him, and turning back now would have cost more than the lie on the page was asking him to carry.

He signed it. His handwriting was careful — the same careful hand he used for the fragment log, the hand that had learned somewhere along the way that permanence deserved precision even when precision couldn't fix what was underneath it.

The ink dried. Nobody at the table said anything about the gap between the words and the truth, because everybody at the table already knew it was there, and naming it again wouldn't have made the form any more honest.

"Welcome to Greyvane," Quenna said, and there was something almost gentle in it, underneath the procedural flatness. "Orientation's at the fourth bell. I'd get settled before then, if I were you."

---

He found Lira and Brom outside the intake building, sitting on a low wall in the last of the afternoon light, the way the three of them had found low walls to sit on in more towns than he could easily count anymore.

"Well?" Lira said.

"I'm an unclassified observer," Cael said. "Officially. In writing. With a signature."

Brom considered this with the quiet interest of someone turning over an unfamiliar mechanism to see how it worked. "That's not what you are."

"No."

"Does that bother you?"

Cael thought about it honestly, which was the only way he knew how to think about anything that mattered. "Less than I expected. Quenna's right that the words hold up on their own, whatever they're covering for. I think I've spent enough of the last year and a half being something the paperwork didn't have a name for that one more form saying the wrong thing about me doesn't move the number much." He paused. "I'll probably still write about it tonight."

"You'll definitely still write about it tonight," Lira said, not unkindly.

"For what it's worth," Brom said, "I don't think the form is the interesting part."

"What's the interesting part."

"That they built a door for you at all. Most places just build walls and let you figure out you're not getting through them." He said it the way he said most of the things that turned out, on reflection, to be the most useful thing anyone had said all day — plainly, without dressing it up, like he was reporting a fact rather than offering comfort. "A wrong-shaped door's still a door. You've walked through worse."

Lira nudged his shoulder with hers, not quite a laugh but adjacent to one. "Look at you. Philosophizing before dinner."

"Don't get used to it," Brom said, and didn't sound entirely unhappy about the possibility that she might have to anyway.

They sat a while longer, watching the last of the light go gold and then grey over walls that had stood through more than sixty years of students walking exactly this kind of uncertain first evening, in one shape or another. Somewhere past the training yard a bell began to sound — not the fourth bell yet, Cael judged, something earlier, a signal for a different rhythm of the day he hadn't learned to read yet.

He got out the notebook anyway, before the bell finished ringing, and wrote it the way he wrote everything that mattered: plainly, in careful handwriting, without ornament.

*Enrolled. The word is doing a lot of work.*

He looked at the sentence for a moment. Then, underneath it, smaller: *So am I. That's the deal.*

He closed the notebook, and went to find out what a fourth bell sounded like.

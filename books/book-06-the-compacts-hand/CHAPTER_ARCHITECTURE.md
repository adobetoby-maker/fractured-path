## Book Promise

Make the reader feel the moment the institution drops the pretense — and make the companions' choice to walk out feel earned.

## Protagonist Arc Statement

*I can work within the system* → *the system was never designed to include me.*

The arc statement is staged honestly, in the log, at both poles: the first sentence is written — confident, earned across three books of hearings and clauses and stamps, and wrong at a depth Cael cannot yet see — in Chapter 2. The second is written at a campfire beyond the Registry Line in Chapter 24. The whole book is the distance between the two entries, and the book's cruelest discipline is that the first sentence keeps *winning its arguments* the entire way down. Cael does not lose within the system. He learns that winning within it was never the variable being measured.

## The Challenge Reference (established in Part 1, referenced throughout)

**The Compact's Hand (the title's referent — an object and an argument, per the Copper Crown / Silver Standard precedent):** Two things wearing one phrase. First, the formula: every binding instrument the registry issues closes with the attestation line *given under the Compact's hand* — the signature of institutional authority itself, the mark that converts text into force. Karis has read ten thousand instances of it across four books of research; it is the most boring phrase in registry law. Second, the colloquialism the edge of the continent has always used and the center pretends not to know: *the Compact's hand* is what people call the reach that doesn't file paperwork — the pressure that arrives without a seal. The book's argument in one phrase: the same hand does both, and has for two hundred years — and the revelation at the book's center is that the hand has been forging the documents it signs since before anyone living was born. The final entry in Cael's Compact file — *active non-compliance, observation priority escalated* — is given under the Compact's hand. Both meanings. That is the point.

**The classification challenge (the machinery, laid out in Chapter 2 and load-bearing throughout):**
- *The instrument:* a formal challenge to enrollment basis, filed at registry level against Halcenvane's assay-provision enrollment — the output of the threat-classification proceedings Book 5's convening opened. It does not allege fraud (Book 3 closed that door) and does not contest the clause's text (Vastin's B5 advisory closed that one). It asserts instead that an unclassifiable practitioner presents an *evaluation-integrity question* that the demonstration provision cannot answer, and petitions for compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol.
- *The trap, anatomized:* the re-evaluation is unpassable by design — not rigged, which would require someone to rig it, but *structurally incapable*: the Arbiter protocol has no category for [SHATTERED] architecture (the series' founding fact), and the procedure for an evaluation that returns no valid classification is codified: enrollment nullification, practitioner-status suspension, and **asset-restriction** — the registry's mechanism for freezing an unclassified person's standing, records, sponsorships, travel endorsements, and institutional relationships. Expulsion, industrialized.
- *The legal cruelty the book is built on:* the defense wins its arguments. The assay clause is sound law; Karis's documentation is filing-cabinet perfect; the Greyvane precedent holds. None of it matters, because the challenge is not an argument — it is an administrative act wearing an argument's clothes, and compelled re-evaluation is within the registry's discretion regardless of the enrollment's validity. Book 3 taught Cael that text beats custom. Book 6 teaches him what text cannot beat: the body that owns the text.
- *The stratum cross-check (from Book 4's checkpoint, honored):* the assay clause bypasses Arbiter records entirely — evaluation by demonstration, no Arbiter in the loop. The falsification machinery lives inside Arbiter evaluation. The challenge's whole function, understood only after Chapter 16, is to move Cael from the stratum the machinery cannot touch into the stratum it owns.

**The proceedings:** convened at the registry seat at Ostrand (established geography — no new venue), on a term-length schedule. **Procurator Jent** appears for the Compact: 50s, colorless, exact — a career instrument who has argued four hundred enrollment matters and won the ones he was sent to win; the book keeps him deliberately small, competent, and unhateable, because hating Jent would let the system off the hook. The presiding registrar is identified in the record by seal alone — unnamed by design (the old-yard-owner convention, deployed structurally: the face the proceedings show is nobody's). **Ilsev** holds the evaluation seat — empaneled precisely because her scruples are unimpeachable, which the faceless layer above her understands as camouflage. **Havel** serves on records rotation. **Vastin** is NOT called — the Compact's best evaluator, uncalled, because his findings are already on record and already inconvenient; his petition for standing is denied in Chapter 5.

**Anchor Path (new Path, defined this book — PROVISIONAL):** containment architecture — anchored force lattices, perimeter binding, suppression fields laid as fixed points rather than projected effects. Distinct from Shield Path (personal defensive declaration) in kind, not degree: Shield protects the self; Anchor cages the other. Rare, unglamorous, and disproportionately represented in Compact enforcement service, for the obvious institutional reason the book does not belabor: it is the Path the system uses to hold people. Its appearance in the off-channel team (Chapters 11, 13) is itself evidence of who sent them. Naming note: "Ward Path" was drafted and REJECTED for root-collision with the Warden rank (Coss's title) — see the naming checkpoint.

**The Registry Line (coined this book — descriptive term, PROVISIONAL):** the boundary of the Arbiter station network — the practical edge of the Compact's jurisdiction, east of Ostrand's province, beyond which no station operates, no tier is verified, no enrollment exists, and no asset-restriction can be executed because there is no registry to execute it in. Beyond it: the edge territories — the continent's unadministered margin, where Books 7–9 live. The series has pointed at this line since Book 1 (expulsion was always the threat of being pushed across it); Book 6 ends with the companions crossing it in the other direction: not pushed. Walking.

**The Halcenvane cast this book:** Withrow (whose academy is now the named respondent in the continent's most-watched proceedings, and whose registry-trained honesty is the institutional conscience of Part 3), Bracken (documentary defense, and the counter where Karis's forfeiture is signed), Rooke (whose cohort loses its captain's bout partner and who gets one unadorned goodbye), Gault (evaluator of record for a provision about to be revoked; his last panel note is the driest grief in the book), Ephram (cohort captain now — his arc completes in Chapter 20), and Seln (assessment office, at his desk, until the morning he isn't).

**Chapter 2 — The Procedure**
~4,600 words

The trap anatomized — the chapter that does for the classification challenge what Book 3's early chapters did for registry law and Book 5's did for tournament scoring: teaches the reader the machine before the machine moves. Karis, Bracken, and Withrow's counsel take the instrument apart clause by clause across a long night in the records hall, and the chapter walks the findings in order of arrival. It does not allege fraud — Greyvane closed that. It does not contest the assay clause — Vastin's own advisory closed that, and the challenge *cites his advisory approvingly*, which Karis flags as the first genuinely frightening thing in the document: they've stopped arguing with the text. It asserts an evaluation-integrity question and petitions for compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol. Bracken supplies the procedure for evaluations that return no valid classification, reading from the registry code in his flattest voice: nullification, suspension, asset-restriction. Withrow supplies what asset-restriction means, from twenty years inside: "Everything with his name on it stops. Records, sponsorship, endorsements, travel. It's expulsion that follows you."

Karis's structural finding is the chapter's spine, delivered with the formality she reserves for findings she doesn't like: the challenge is unpassable *by design without a designer* — the Arbiter protocol simply has no bin for [SHATTERED], and the procedure for no-bin is codified, so the outcome is determined before the first session sits. "It's not a question. It's a corridor. Every door in it is real law, and every door opens one way." And her second finding, said almost in passing, which Chapter 16 will detonate: the assay clause worked for three books because it bypasses Arbiter evaluation entirely — and this instrument's one non-negotiable demand is that Cael stand in front of an Arbiter. "They're not trying to beat the provision. They're trying to move you back inside the instrument. I don't know why that distinction feels important. Noting it."

*Key beat (the first pole, on the page):* the Power Log, end of chapter: "I can work within the system. I have the receipts: a hearing, a provision, a stamp renewed four times, a tournament their own clause put me in. The system has text, and text has held me every time the people failed. This is more text. We'll read it better than they do. We always have." He is wrong, and the book knows exactly how long it will take him to find out.

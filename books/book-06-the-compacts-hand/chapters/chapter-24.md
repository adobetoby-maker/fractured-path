# Chapter 24 — Nine

The first true edge-territories camp was a switchback shelf above an unnamed valley, and Cael counted it in the way he had counted in every room of his life, because the habit had outlived its reason and he had decided to keep it.

They had come down off the ridge at the sixth day's noon and found the track turning back on itself four times across a slope of loose grey scree, and on the third turn the shelf: forty feet by fifteen of level ground, dry, with the rock face at its back and a lip of thorn and rowan at its edge, a spring coming out of the face at the north end and running off the shelf's lip in a thread. The valley lay below it and had no name. It had a river in it, and a floor of birch scrub going gold, and on the far side a scree slope with a track cut across it by something that was not people, and nothing else anywhere: no field, no wall, no smoke, no cut wood, nothing at all that said a person had ever had an intention here.

Seln had walked it first, as he walked everything first, and came back and said, "One approach, and it comes up in the open for two hundred yards. Water at the north end. If it rains hard the shelf sheds east, so nothing sleeps at the east end." That was the whole of his report and it was the reason they camped there.

They got in at the eighth hour and had the camp made by the tenth, and the making of it was the first work any of them had ever done that no institution had a form for.

There was a discussion about the watch, and it was the evening's only argument, and it was short. Lira wanted three watches of three hours. Seln said two of four, and said why: three watches meant three men sleeping badly and one of them badly enough to be useless at noon, and a party of five with one cart and one road did not need three sets of eyes, it needed two sets that worked. Lira looked at that for a moment and said, "Fine — but not you and not Brom on the same one, because you'll both take the bad half and neither of you will admit it," which was so exactly correct that Seln declined to answer it and Brom laughed, once, quietly, into the lashings.

Cael took the second watch and got, for the first time in four years, a genuinely useless one. There was nothing to watch. He sat at the shelf's lip with the fire behind him and the two hundred yards of open approach in front and ran the old instrument over a country that returned nothing: no light, no movement, no grade, no coat, no seal, no glass. Somewhere down the valley something barked twice and stopped. He caught himself, at about the second hour, charting the stars into columns out of sheer professional habit, and stopped, and laughed at himself, and then went back to doing it, because the alternative was sitting in the dark not counting anything and he had never once managed that.

The found family ran its old grammar on new terrain, which was the first thing Cael noticed and the thing he wrote down first, because it was not obvious and had not been guaranteed.

Brom's quiet competence, two years aimed at acquiring dinner twice over, was aimed now at load-balancing a cart for a road with no waystations. He had the whole of it off and spread on the shelf's stone before anybody else had unrolled anything: the boxes, the kits, the tools, the grain, the case; and he went through it once with his hands and once with his eyes and then rebuilt the load with the heavy weight low and forward and the water where a man could reach it walking, and he moved four things from the cart to the panniers, and he did not explain any of it. When Lira asked him what the rule was he said, "Nothing you'll want tomorrow goes under anything you won't," and went back to the lashings. Cael entered that as a sentence worth keeping and noted that Brom, who had never in four years volunteered a general principle about anything, had produced one within a day of the road having no rules on it.

Karis indexed her three boxes into a working field-archive by firelight, which took her the whole of the evening and outlasted two rebuildings of the fire. The system was a good one because she had been designing it in a coach for five days: three boxes, four categories, a running index in the notebook's back matter, and every bundle re-tied with a cloth tag she inked herself. She was building the room she had lost — the annex's north room, the lock, the warrant — into an architecture that needed no lock, because it needed no institution, because a system carried on a cart cannot be revoked at a counter by a person who has never read it.

"It's worse," she said, when Cael asked her how it compared. "It's slower to search and it will be damp within a month and I will lose things. It is also the first archive I have ever kept that nobody can close." She tied a tag. "I have been thinking about which of those facts is larger. I have concluded that the question is badly posed and that I should stop asking it and go on working, which is the finding I reach about most questions and is why I am tolerable to travel with."

Lira mapped tomorrow's route the way she mapped a bracket sheet, flat on a stone with a stub of charcoal: the valley's exits, three of them; the ridge's approaches; the water; the two lines a party with a cart could take and the one line a party without a cart could take if it had to leave the cart. She annotated the difficult stretch with the same shorthand she used for a difficult opponent, which Cael could read and nobody else could, and when she finished she looked at the whole sheet for a while and said, "It's the same work. Nobody told me it was going to be the same work."

Seln was gone for an hour after they made camp and came back with the lay of every approach for a mile, unasked, and reported it in four sentences and did not sit down until he had finished. A man who had spent fifteen years protecting people who did not know it was not going to stop now that they did; the only difference was that he now said the findings out loud, which was, in its way, the largest change any of them had undergone.

The practical question got asked over the meal, by Lira, in the way Lira asked practical questions, which was without warm-up: "What do we do for money."

It was a good question and it had four answers and none of them was frightening, which surprised all of them a little. Karis had the sum they had left, to the copper, and read it out, and it was more than any of them had expected because five people had spent a term being fed and housed by an institution and had not been spending. Brom said that a big man with a cart and a horse is employable anywhere there is anything to move, and said it without any of the self-deprecation the sentence invited, because it was simply true and he had done it before. Lira observed that unadministered country has fights in it and that fights in unadministered country get paid in a currency nobody has to verify, and got a look from Karis and said, "I said *observed*." And Seln, who had been silent, said, "Nine hamlets in four days, going by the keeper's account of the road, and not one of them has had a person come through who can read a contract. There is your first six months," and went back to his food, and Karis wrote it down, which meant it was decided.

Nobody said the other thing, which was that four of the five of them had never in their lives been unemployed and that the fifth had been unemployed in every way that mattered for fifteen years while drawing a salary.

And Ephram's absence at the fire was noted once, without grief. He had stayed. Staying was right for him, and had been right for him since the trial, and would go on being right for him; and the family's arithmetic held him anyway, the way it held everyone it had decided to keep, present or not. Lira said his name once during the evening, in a sentence about a bout two years old, and nobody made anything of it, and that was the correct handling and everybody at the fire knew it.

---

The ledger of the year went into Hesk's satchel by firelight, complete, because every year's had gone in complete and this was the ninth entry and the arc's number.

The volume itself had been a birthday gift in a midwinter two years back, and had held the log through a tournament, a proceedings, and a resignation, and had about a third of its leaves left. He had carried it in Hesk's satchel since Ardenmere. Both the satchel and the volume were, as of six days ago, among the very few objects in his possession whose ownership no registry anywhere recorded, and he noticed that on the way to opening it and decided it was the least important thing he had noticed all week and wrote it down anyway.

He did it in the established forms, which mattered more out here and not less. The volume out; the fire at his back for light and his knee for a desk; the margin ruled with the straight edge of Lira's route sheet for want of a rule; the head of the page dated. He set the pen down for a moment before using it, which he did once a year and had still never written about.

Then the inventory, in the order they had come and not in the order they mattered, because importance was a judgment and the log did not keep judgments in the inventory column:

*Nine confirmed fragments.*

*Wind-adjacent — Lira. Pressure-adjacent — Feryn. Iron-adjacent — Brom. Compression-adjacent — Reydan. Ember-adjacent — Karis. Shadow-adjacent — source protected, sealed still, the logic updated and the discipline held: a defector's tradecraft in my hands would confirm the surveillance-era acquisition, so the seal stays, and Seln knows now that it does, and knows why, which changes nothing about the seal and everything about the debt. Storm-adjacent — Daeva. Anchor-adjacent — unnamed; the hand with no face; the piece of the machine itself. Blade-adjacent — Ephram; the only "non-hostile" in the log; taken in earnest from a friend who asked to be shown straight.*

*Nine integrated abilities. Each with a name beside it, one of them protected and one of them missing — and the missing line I have left blank on purpose, because the blank is the truest thing the entry says.*

*One anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent. Turned over once, per the custom at intervals that matter — checked against the cache, checked against everything the cache explained, and touching none of it. Still open. Still real. Patience.*

He read the page back before he sanded it, which was also the custom, and found the two things the page did not say and would not say, and let it not say them.

The first was that the nine had cost. Not in the entry's ledger sense — the costs were charted elsewhere, hourly, in the notebook: the pulls, the four weeks of an architecture reseating around Anchor-adjacent's fixed-point grammar and then, on top of it and before it had finished, the edge-lines and the line-reading of a friend's Blade doctrine arriving to be housed. The forearm from the Crown yard was a week old and had gone the colour of a bad plum and would be gone in another week. The bill for eleven minutes at a rate the managed version had never had to hold had come due on the second day of the road, all at once, as a headache and an inability to eat, and had been paid, and the log had said so at the time.

The second was that the entry could be read, by a stranger, as a list of nine victories. It was a list of nine people. Six of them had given him something without knowing, at the time, that they had; one had asked to be shown straight and had been, and had given it anyway; one had wanted to end him and now wanted an answer instead; and one had no name, and would never have one, and had been sent to hold him by an office that had not troubled to record which of its instruments it was spending.

The anomaly he turned over the way he turned it over at every interval that mattered, which was: he opened the session-nine page, read his own fourteen-year-old handwriting, checked it against everything that had been added to the world since he last checked it, and closed it again.

It was a shorter procedure this year than any year before it, and the shortness was itself the finding. He had spent five months in the company of an archive that explained two hundred years of the machine's private business — a modification, a corrections function, a contingency that fired when the corrections function could not — and he had laid the anomaly beside all of it in an annex, deliberately, expecting a fit, and had found none. Not a partial fit. Not a suggestive one. The cache explained a great many things and did not touch this, and he had written the sentence at the time and would not improve on it now.

So the entry stood as it had stood for four years, in the same three words, and he did not embellish them, because a man who embellishes an unsolved entry is a man beginning to pretend he has solved it.

He wrote none of that in the inventory column, because the inventory column was for the inventory. He wrote one line for the old promises instead:

*Reydan will get his answer. Daeva will get her rematch. The road east is long, and it runs the right direction. Good.*

And then, because he was eighteen and had a fire and no schedule and nobody within a day's walk who cared what he wrote, he added, under the rule, the only thing the whole year had actually taught him and the pole of an entry he had written in confidence in a records hall five months and a lifetime ago:

*I wrote in the autumn that I could work within the system. I want the sentence left standing in the volume where I wrote it, uncorrected, because it wasn't a lie and I did the work and the work won. Every argument. All term. And the arguments were never what was being measured. The system was never the room; the room was the part they let us into. Write the new sentence under the old one and leave both:*

*I can work within the system. The system was never designed to include me.*

*Both true. Four years apart. The second one didn't cancel the first — it just told me what the first one was worth, which was a term of perfect filings and one afternoon of a hand closing on air.*

He sanded the page, and let it stand a minute before turning the leaf, because ink laid over wet ink is a lie about when a thing was written.

---

The letters were written after, and held for the next westbound courier whenever one existed east of the Line, which might be never, and the might-be-never was itself a fact about where they now lived.

To Hesk, four pages of the tournament's own straightness — the ruling, the council, the counter, the boxes, the bout, the ford — because his grandfather read strain gauges and not polish and would want the whole run of it including the parts that did not flatter anybody. And at the end of it, the paragraph that would go in the pouch even if the four pages did not:

*"Eighteen, unenrolled, and past the Line. The shape you said to mind — I minded it, all the way to the edge of the map. The tools fit the road. Mind your own shape; two polite men know your shop, and I'm not there to count them anymore. — C."*

He wrote that last sentence three times before he kept it, because the first two versions were warnings dressed as news and the man deserved news dressed as news, and because Hesk had spent a lifetime declining to be protected by people who loved him and would decline it again by return of post, in about eleven words, and would be right.

And to Vell, because her ledger held his first honest record and the resignation belonged in it: the season told straight and the ending told straighter, the four files closed complete and the fifth man who filed nothing, and the request that she enter it in the book at her own discretion and in her own hand — filed, he had no doubt, cross-referenced, beside a circuit rating from a smaller and more honest book, so that the one true ledger of his life ran unbroken from a border-town circuit to the edge of the administered world.

It took him most of the fire's good hour, and Karis lent him the flat of a box lid to write on without being asked and took it back without comment when he was done, and Lira, passing behind him at some point, read exactly four words over his shoulder and moved on, which was as much intrusion as anyone in that family had ever permitted themselves.

He addressed neither letter to anywhere he could be answered at, because there was nowhere. That was the strangest sentence of the evening and he made himself write it out plainly in the log so as not to be soft about it: *both letters go west with no return address, because I don't have one. Everyone who has ever written to me has written to a room in an institution. There isn't a room. There's a shelf above a valley, and tomorrow there'll be a different one.*

He did not write to Withrow, and thought about why, and decided it was because she had said the last thing and would be irritated to have it answered; and he did not write to Ephram, because Ephram had said his last thing at a board with a towel over his shoulder and would consider a letter a demotion.

---

The closing window: a registry office, altitude unstated, faceless by design.

The file that had been growing since a Kindling four years ago, updated by a hand no one ever attaches to a person — a clerk of some grade, in some room, working through the afternoon's amendments in the order the tray gave them, who had never seen the subject of this file and never would, and who processed it between an academy charter renewal and a lapsed sponsorship without altering the rhythm of the work.

The prior designations were retired one by one, because the machine kept true records about its own lies: *enrollment: closed by resignation.* *Proceedings: moot.* *Re-evaluation: not executed.* *Asset-restriction: pending jurisdiction.* Four lines, each one struck through neatly and re-entered with its closing status, each one the end of an instrument that had cost somebody a year of their life to build or to survive.

And the new line entered beneath them, the last institutional sentence written about him inside the world that wrote such sentences, given under the Compact's hand — both meanings, the formula and the fist, the seal on the instrument and the reach that files no paperwork — because the whole of it had been the argument that the two were one hand and had been for two hundred years:

*Active non-compliance. Observation priority escalated.*

The amendment took perhaps ninety seconds. The clerk blotted it, entered the date, and set the folder in the tray on the left, which in that office meant complete, and reached for the next one.

Nobody in the building had ever met him. Nobody in the building had decided anything about him. Every person who had touched the file across four years had been doing their job correctly, at their grade, in order, on the record — and the file had arrived, by the accumulated work of all that correctness, at a sentence that said the registry no longer had a procedure for him and would therefore watch him instead.

The window holds four seconds. It closes.

The machine does not stop. It re-files. Not a machine defeated, not a secret detonated, not a system brought down — a file, updated, by nobody, same as always, and a hand that has closed on air and written down that it is waiting.

---

He closed the volume and put it in the satchel, and put the satchel where he always put it, and did not put anything away after that because there was nothing else that needed putting away, and the not-having-anything-else was the last new fact of the day.

The fire burned down to the good stage, the one where nobody adds anything and everybody stays anyway, and the valley below the shelf went from blue to black in about a quarter of an hour, and the first of the stars came out over a country with no name and no measurements in it.

He had no tier. That was, out here, an absolutely meaningless fact about him, and he turned it over at the fire's edge for a while and found that it had changed its shape one final time. At fourteen it had been a wound; at fifteen, a problem to be managed; last winter, the whole architecture of a legal defense and the thing a continent argued about for a term. Tonight it was a piece of information with nowhere to be filed and nobody to file it, in a valley where no instrument had ever asked anybody anything, and it sat in him like a stone in a pocket that had stopped being heavy at some point in the walking without his noticing when.

Karis worked. Brom checked the lashings a second time and did not pretend he was doing anything else. Lira fed the fire as though it were an opponent with a tempo, which was how she met most things. Seln sat where he could see the two hundred yards of open approach and said nothing for an hour, which from him was contentment.

Cael catalogued them, because that was what he did at the end of anything that mattered, and because he had learned in five months that the only records that survive a machine are the ones kept by hand. Four people who had signed away, at a counter, in one morning, a ranking, a ladder, a certification and a career. One man who had signed away nothing because he had never signed anything, and who had left a set of keys in a row on a blotter instead. A cart. Three boxes and a case under one canvas. A horse. A valley with no name in it.

It was, by every instrument the continent owned, less than any of them had possessed a week ago. He had run that arithmetic four separate times on the road east and it came out the same way every time, and he had stopped running it, because there was a column the instruments did not have and every single one of the assets was in it.

"I have nine things that aren't a Path," Cael said, eventually, to nobody in particular, "no tier, no rank, no enrollment, no protection — and four people who signed out of the world that was supposed to provide them, and one of the nine taken from a hand the world sent to hold me, and one taken in earnest from a friend, and the machine's own maintenance records in a case under that canvas, which we can't file and can't publish and are carrying east anyway because the truth is worth carrying even when it can't be spent."

He turned it over once and let it stand.

"And somewhere behind us, a file with a new sentence in it, written by nobody, same as always."

Lira, feeding the fire: "Let them escalate. They'll need better maps."

Brom: "And better roads."

Karis, writing — the field-archive open on her knee, the first entry of a new record in a place that had none: "Noted."

Down the fire, the man who used to file reports about him watched the dark east horizon with evident professional approval and said nothing, which — from him — was home.

The hand had signed its last instrument with his name on it, and the name was still wrong, and the wrongness was still the only true thing in the file. He wasn't inside its jurisdiction anymore. The fire burned in a territory no Arbiter had ever measured, above a valley with no name, on a road that ran the right direction — and for the first time in four years, nothing anywhere was deciding what he was, except him.

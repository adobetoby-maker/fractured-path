# Chapter 2 — The Procedure

The instrument reached Karis's room at the fourth bell of the afternoon, in Bracken's own hand, and she read it standing in the doorway the way Withrow had read it standing at the counter — the first page, then the whole, then the first page again — and said, when she had finished, the only sentence she would spend on feeling before the work began: "They've been reading us." Then she sent her assistant for the registry code, all three volumes, and the Greyvane transcript, and the four prior assay-challenge rulings Bracken kept in the records hall's locked press, and told the assistant to bring tea and not to expect to be sent home before the second bell, which was a lie by three bells and the assistant would tell it as a war story for years.

The delegation learned the news the way the delegation learned everything, by the configuration adjusting: Lira came off the Silver floor mid-drill because Rooke had heard from Bracken and told her, in four words, that she was needed; Brom arrived from nowhere with bread nobody had asked for; Seln, at the counter, filed an evaluation-linked floor-access request on Cael's behalf for the evening — a records-hall slot, all night, under the standing's own privileges, in the office's plainest form — before anyone had thought to need one. By the second bell the whole bluff knew a sealed instrument had come up the road under escort, and by the third the broadsides' correspondents in Ostrand had it too, and Withrow's office had drafted its one-line public answer — *the academy has been served and will respond within the schedule* — and released it, because silence was a document too and she preferred to write her own.

The records hall kept its lamps lit past the fourth bell that night for the first time in Bracken's tenure, and the registrar noted the fact on the fire-watch's ledger in his own hand — *lamps: records hall, late, by my authority* — because a man who kept true records kept them even about the night he spent learning what a true record was worth.

Four of them at the long table: Bracken with the registry code in three volumes at his elbow; Withrow's counsel — a grey, precise woman who had made it a condition of eleven years' retainer that her name appear on no filing she advised, only the academy's, and who was therefore known on the bluff simply as counsel; who had verified the assay architecture's provenance from a distance two years ago and had now, for the first time, come up the bluff road in person; Karis, with the instrument's copy open in front of her and the notebook open beside it and her assistant sent to bed at the second bell over protest; and Cael, at the table's end, with the observation notebook, because a document about him was being taken apart and four years had taught him to be in the room for his own anatomy.

Withrow came and went — her office, the table, her office — the way she had come and gone through the inspection year, and each time she came she brought one more thing the table needed: the inspection year's finding, the tournament's referred exhibit, tea. On the third trip she brought her own registry-years copy of the code's chapter fourteen, annotated in a younger hand, and set it beside Bracken's without comment, and Cael read the annotations upside down across the table and found them to be the notes of an officer twenty years gone who had administered the procedure twice and had underlined, both times, the same clause.

He counted the underlinings before he read them, because counting came first: two, in two different inks, in the same three words, with a bracket beside the second and no note in the margin at all.

"You may ask," Withrow said, from the table's head, without turning around.

"What did you underline?"

"*At its discretion.*" She came back to the table and put one finger on the line, and the finger was steady, which he filed. "Part two. *Suspension of practitioner status pending review, such review to be initiated by the registry at its discretion.* I underlined it the first time because I was twenty-nine and I thought it meant the registry would choose when to be merciful." She took the finger away. "I underlined it the second time, six years later, because I had learned what it actually means, which is that nothing in the sequence obliges anybody to ever begin the review. A restriction with no scheduled end is not a punishment. It is a condition." She looked at Bracken. "Registrar. How many words in that chapter set a deadline?"

Bracken went through it at his counter's own pace, the way he did everything, and came back with the number, and the number was none.

"None," he said.

"None," Withrow agreed, and went back to her office for the tea.

Cael counted the instrument before anyone opened it, because counting was the only work available to him at that table and because a document was a room like any other. Twenty-three leaves, written one side. Forty-one numbered paragraphs. Two schedules annexed. The word *discretion* eleven times, which he tallied twice to be sure of, and the word *evidence* four, and the word *fraud* once — in the negative, in the second paragraph, disclaiming it. Nine references to provisions of the registry code and two to the Compact's own convening minute, and, on the last leaf, above the seal, the attestation line every binding instrument on the continent closed with, in the same type as the rest, no larger: *given under the Compact's hand.*

He wrote the tallies in the observation notebook and looked at them for a while, and the thing the tallies said, before anyone had read a clause aloud, was that the document had been written by a party that expected to spend no time at all on proof.

Karis ran the night the way she ran everything, in order of arrival, and the findings arrived in the order the instrument had been built to deliver them, which was itself — she would say this at the third bell, and it would be the night's coldest sentence — the first finding.

---

**First finding: it alleges no fraud.**

She read the operative clause twice, aloud, and then laid the Greyvane hearing's ruling beside it — the adjudication that had made Cael, at fifteen, a person the law was forced to describe accurately — and traced with one finger the exact perimeter the instrument had drawn around it. "They don't touch the execution. Not the enrollment's filing, not the assay's conduct, not the semester renewals. Every fraud theory the broadsides have printed for a year — the concealed classification, the staged provision — they've declined all of it." She looked up. "Greyvane closed that door and they've read the closing. This is a document written by people who lose no arguments they can avoid having."

**Second finding: it does not contest the clause.**

The counsel worked it in the direction she worked everything, which Cael charted from the table's end and entered under her heading that night as *backwards, on purpose*. She did not begin at the instrument's first page. She began at its last operative paragraph — the remedy — and read up: what is asked for, then what is asserted to justify asking, then what is recited to justify asserting, and only then the opening courtesies, which she skipped entirely.

"Recitals are where a nervous drafter puts his conscience," she said, when he asked, because he asked her once and she answered as though the question had been billable. "Twenty-six years. Every filing I have ever beaten, I beat because the recitals conceded something the remedy needed and the drafter didn't notice he'd given it away." She turned the leaf. "This one has four sentences of recital. Four. It is the shortest front end I have ever seen on an instrument of this weight, and I want the table to understand what that means: whoever drafted it knew that reading up is how the other side reads, and gave the other side nothing to read up into."

That took longer, because the counsel wanted it checked against every prior challenge to an assay-provision enrollment in the registry's history — there were four; Bracken had them in the locked press, bound, because Bracken kept things — and the table read all four before midnight. A coastal academy's enrollment of a late-Kindled fisherman's son, challenged on procedure, sustained on the text, sixty years gone. A guild-house apprentice, challenged on sponsorship, sustained. Two more, older, thinner, both sustained — the provision's whole litigated history a short shelf of institutions losing to a clause they had failed to read. "Four challenges," the counsel said, closing the last. "Four losses. Every one of them attacked the text, and the text held. Your Greyvane hearing was the fifth, and it held the same way." She turned back to the instrument. "And this — this has been drafted by someone who read the same four and declined to be the sixth."

The finding, when it landed, landed as procedure. "The clause's validity is conceded," the counsel said, at the end of an hour, in the voice of an advocate who had expected a fight and found the ground already ceded. "Explicitly. Paragraph six. *The petitioner does not contest the text of the provision under which the enrollment was executed, nor its lawful survival of the standardization directive.* They've handed you your whole Greyvane case as a stipulation." She set the page down. "I have been doing this for twenty-six years and I have never had an opponent concede my strongest ground in their opening instrument. It is either very generous or very frightening."

"It's the second," said Karis, and turned to paragraph seven, and read it, and went still — the archive stillness, the finger stopped on the line — and Cael, who had been waiting all night for the stillness, counted. Twelve seconds.

**Third finding: it cites Vastin.**

"Paragraph seven," she said, in her flattest register. "*The petitioner acknowledges and adopts the evaluator's advisory of the prior year concerning the tournament fielding clause, to wit: that the clause says what it says, that the enrollment is lawful on its face and adjudicated at public record, and that if the Compact wishes practitioners of this kind not to compete, it should write different rules rather than misread the ones it has.*" She turned the page around so the table could read it. "That's his advisory. The one that put you on the roster. Quoted whole, with approval." The stillness broke into something Cael had never charted on her before, and entered that night with care: *the first genuinely frightened expression I have seen on Karis's face in three years — not at a threat; at a citation.* "They've stopped arguing with the text. They're *agreeing* with it. They've taken the best evaluator's best sentence — the one that said write different rules — and they've written the different rules, and they're citing him as the authority for doing it." She set the page down very precisely. "That is the first genuinely frightening thing in this document, and I want it minuted that I said so before I'd read the rest."

The counsel, quietly: "Minuted."

Cael wrote it in the observation notebook in the exact words, and beneath it the reading two years of the man had produced: *they've made Vastin's honesty load-bearing for the thing he warned me about. He told me the channels would change. He didn't tell me they'd change by quoting him.*

---

Then the instrument's own machinery, and the night's second hour, and the table learned the thing the instrument had actually been built to do.

"Evaluation-integrity question," Karis read. "Paragraph nine. *An unclassifiable practitioner's presence within a chartered institution's evaluation architecture presents an evaluation-integrity question which the demonstration provision was not drafted to answer and cannot answer.*" She looked at the counsel. "Is that a category?"

"It is now." The counsel had the registry code's second volume open. "It's a phrase from the registry's own integrity provisions — the ones that govern whether a station's evaluations can be trusted. Ordinarily invoked when an Arbiter station's frame is suspected of malfunction, or an assessor of corruption. It has never, as far as I can find tonight, been invoked against a *person*. The petition takes a provision written to audit instruments and points it at the enrollee, on the theory that an enrollee no instrument can read is himself an integrity question." She turned the page. "It's not an argument. It's a reclassification of the whole matter — from a question about your enrollment, which they conceded, to a question about the system's ability to evaluate you, which nobody has ever been asked to answer, because nobody ever could."

"And the remedy," said Karis.

"Compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol. Paragraph eleven. The enrollment to stand or fall upon the result."

The counsel turned to Cael before the procedure, because the procedure would be about him and she had twenty-six years of never letting a client hear a remedy before he understood the question. "The petition will ask, in effect, whether the demonstration provision can *vouch* for you — whether a floor and a panel and a record are an evaluation the registry can trust, or merely a description of a person it can't. They'll say the second. I'll say the first, and I'll say it well, and the seat will have discretion to prefer the second regardless of how well I say it." She looked at him with the flat kindness of a professional pricing the truth. "You should know that before you hear chapter fourteen. I'd rather you were angry at the right thing."

"I'm not angry," Cael said. "I'm counting."

"Good," said the counsel. "Keep the count. It'll be the only honest record in the building."

Bracken had the third volume open, and the table turned to him, because the third volume was procedure, and procedure was his instrument.

He read it in the flattest voice the hall had ever held — the voice, Cael thought, that he had used to certify enrollment records forty times a year for thirty years, the boilerplate voice, the voice of law nobody had needed yet. "Registry code, evaluation procedures, chapter fourteen. *Where an evaluation conducted under standard Arbiter protocol returns no valid classification, the following shall apply in sequence.* One. *Nullification of any enrollment, sponsorship, or standing predicated upon the evaluand's practitioner status.* Two. *Suspension of practitioner status pending review, such review to be initiated by the registry at its discretion.* Three." He paused, and the pause was not for effect; it was a registrar reaching for a term he had never once had to say aloud. "*Asset-restriction.* Section fourteen, part nine. *The registry may restrict, freeze, or hold in abeyance any standing, record, sponsorship, endorsement, travel credential, or institutional relationship attaching to an unclassified person, pending resolution of practitioner status.*" He closed the volume. "That is the procedure for an evaluation that returns no valid classification. It's codified. It has been codified since before I was born. I have never once seen it executed, because in thirty years I have never once seen an evaluation return nothing."

"You've seen one," Cael said.

"I've seen one," Bracken agreed. "It's why the procedure exists."

Withrow, at the table's head, had not sat down. She had come back from her office with the tea and had stayed, standing, through the whole of chapter fourteen, and when Bracken closed the volume she supplied the thing the code did not, from twenty years inside the registry, in the voice she kept for the things that mattered.

"Asset-restriction is the registry's word for it. I'll give you the working one." She set the tea down. "Everything with his name on it stops. Records — sealed, not destroyed; you can't consult them and neither can anyone acting for you. Sponsorship — every institution that has ever put its name beside his is notified that the association is *under restriction*, which is registry language for *decide now whether you'd like to be next*. Endorsements, travel credentials, the ferry pass, the guild letters, the courier privileges: frozen. Halcenvane's sponsorship file — this academy's — restricted, which means the academy answers questions about him to an office that has already decided the answers." She looked at Cael. "It's expulsion that follows you. Denvash put you out of a gate. This puts you out of every door on the continent at once, by paperwork, and the paperwork never expires." A breath. "I administered two of them, in my registry years. One of them deserved worse. I think about the other one. I'd like it noted that I remember what it did to their families anyway."

"Noted," said the counsel, who had begun, at some point in the hour, to keep the minute herself — a page headed in her small exact hand *Halcenvane v. the Compact's petition: working minute, privileged*, which she would keep for the whole term and which Cael would ask to read, once, at its end, and be shown.

---

Bracken, who had not spoken since he closed the volume, put the code's third volume aside and drew the instrument's last leaf toward him instead, and did the piece of work a registrar did when the substance had become unbearable, which was arithmetic.

"Fourteen days to answer," he said. "The instrument is dated the twenty-first. Service was the twenty-eighth — seven days on the road under escort, which is the escort's pace, not the post's; the post would have done it in four." He wrote the figures out. "So the fourteen runs from service, per the code, which leaves us the twelfth. Fourteen days from tonight." He set the pen down. "I raise it because I have watched institutions lose these on the calendar and not on the law. A filing that arrives on the fifteenth day is not a late filing. It is no filing, and the record reads *no answer entered*, and there is no provision anywhere in three volumes that lets a seat forgive it."

"We'll be inside it," said Karis.

"I know. I am saying it out loud so that it has been said out loud at a table where five people heard it." He put his spectacles back on and took them off again. "That is most of what a registrar is for."

Karis's structural finding came at the third bell, and she delivered it standing, with the formality she reserved for findings she did not like, and the hall's lamps had burned low enough by then that Bracken got up and trimmed them before she began, because a finding of that grade deserved to be read by good light.

"I want to state the whole architecture once, in order, for the minute, and then I want to say what it is." She had the instrument's pages fanned across the table in the order the night had read them, and she touched each as she went. "One. The enrollment's execution is conceded. Two. The provision's text is conceded — with the best evaluator's own advisory cited in support. Three. An evaluation-integrity question is asserted, under a provision written for instruments, against a person. Four. The remedy petitioned is compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol. Five. The Arbiter protocol has no category for [SHATTERED] architecture — that is not our claim; it's the founding fact of this whole file, the eleven seconds of silence in a border town, and it is the one thing in four years no office has ever disputed. Six. An evaluation returning no valid classification triggers chapter fourteen: nullification, suspension, asset-restriction. Codified. Sequential. Automatic."

She set her hands flat on the table.

"So here is what it is. It is unpassable — and I want to be precise about the word, because precision is the only defense we have left tonight. Not *rigged*. Rigging requires a rigger — an assessor bought, a frame tampered with, a finding falsified — and this instrument requires none of that and contains none of it. Every clause in it is real law. Every step is a real procedure. It is unpassable *by design without a designer*: the protocol simply has no bin for him, and the procedure for no-bin is written down, so the outcome was determined the moment the petition was filed, before the first session sits, and every honest official who touches it from here to the ruling can act with total integrity and the result will not change by a hair." She looked around the table. "It's not a question. It's a corridor. Every door in it is real law, and every door opens one way."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The counsel wrote the sentence down. Bracken, at the volumes, took his spectacles off and did not put them back on.

"We answer it anyway," Withrow said, from the head of the table. It was not a question either.

"We answer it anyway. Completely. Perfectly." Karis sat down. "Because the alternative is not answering, and the record of not answering reads *fled*, and this academy is the named respondent, and I have never in my life let an instrument go unanswered on the text." She pulled the notebook toward her. "I'll have the response architecture drafted in four days. It will be the best-documented enrollment defense the registry has ever received. I want that on the record too, because I intend to be right about the corridor and I would rather be right with a perfect filing on the table than a poor one."

And then — almost in passing; she was already writing the response's first heading, and the sentence came out of her sideways, the way her findings sometimes came when they were not yet findings — the other thing.

"There's something else in it I can't place." She didn't look up. "The assay clause worked for three years because it bypasses Arbiter evaluation entirely. Evaluation by demonstration — a floor, a panel, witnesses, a record. No Arbiter in the loop. That's *why* it could hold him: it never asked the instrument the question the instrument can't answer." Her pen stopped. "And this petition's one non-negotiable demand is that he stand in front of an Arbiter. They've conceded the provision, conceded the record, conceded everything — and the one thing they will not concede is the *stratum*. They're not trying to beat the provision. They're trying to move him back inside the instrument." She looked at the page for a moment longer. "I don't know why that distinction feels important. Noting it."

She wrote it in the notebook's front matter, where a certain family of citations lived — Cael saw the page; he had been reading that page upside down for two years — beside the fielding clause and the squad clause and the stratum note from a tournament charter, and closed the book on it, and went back to the response.

Cael entered it in the observation notebook with a question mark beside it, the way he entered everything she started and didn't finish. The question mark would stand until midwinter, and past it.

---

The residence was awake when he came up at the fifth bell, because the residence had decided, without anyone deciding, to be awake.

Lira was on the common-room hearth with her boots off and the fire banked to embers and the Silver program's drill-book open on her knee and not being read. Brom was in the room's one good chair, awake — not asleep upright; awake, the specific stillness of a man keeping a vigil he would never call one. Seln was not there; Seln was at his desk, at the fifth bell, in an office with nobody in it, which Cael had learned to read as the man's own way of being present: the office would have the instrument's copy cross-indexed by morning, and the cross-index would be a better map of the document than the counsel's.

Karis had come up behind him — silent, the response's first heading already drafted in her head, her hands ink-stained to the second knuckle — and she stood in the common room's doorway a moment before anyone saw her, and Cael, who saw her, charted the thing she was doing, which was counting the room. Three people awake for her. One at his desk. A configuration adjusting for a night's work before the work had been announced.

"Four days," she said, to the room, before Lira could ask. "The response will be perfect. I'll need Brom to carry volumes from the press twice a day and Lira to keep the intake's rota away from the records hall door and both of you to not ask me how it's going, because it will be going perfectly and I won't want to say so until it's done." She looked at Cael. "You'll read every draft. Not for approval. Because it's about you and I don't write documents about you that you haven't read. That's the arrangement."

"That's the arrangement," Cael agreed.

"Good." She went up. The room let her.

"Well," said Lira.

He told them. Not the whole night — the architecture, the six steps, the corridor, Withrow's working definition of what asset-restriction did to a person's name, Karis's sentence about the stratum with its question mark. He told it in the order it had arrived, because the order had been the first finding, and when he was done Brom was quiet for the length of time Brom was quiet before the load-bearing question.

"Every door's real law," Brom said. "And every door opens one way." He turned it once, like a plaque. "Then what's the argument for?"

"For the record. For the academy. For being right in writing when the thing that's wrong has a seal on it." Cael sat down on the hearth's other end. "Karis says the filing will be perfect. I believe her. I've watched her be perfect for three years and the perfection has held every time."

"Every time the *people* failed," Lira said. She said it carefully, the drill-book still open on her knee, a woman testing a floor's footing before committing weight. "That's your sentence. You've said it since Greyvane. The text holds when the people fail." She looked at the embers. "What if the people didn't fail this time? What if they read the text better than we did and wrote a new one?"

"Then we read that one better," Cael said. "We always have."

She didn't argue. She looked at him the way she had looked at Zerin's sheet — three reads, unhurried — and closed the drill-book, and said, "Bed. Karis has four days of filing and she'll want us dumb and rested," and went up. Brom went up after her, at his own pace, and stopped at the stair's turn, and said, to the room in general, "Corridor," once, the way he said things he intended to keep thinking about, and was gone.

Cael wrote the log at the window, with the copy of the instrument on the sill where he had left it, and the entry ran to four lines and cost him no crossings-out, and every clause of it was earned — a hearing, a provision, four renewals, a tournament — and it was wrong at a depth he could not yet see, and the season would take exactly as long as it took to show him the depth.

*I can work within the system. I have the receipts: a hearing, a provision, a stamp renewed four times, a tournament their own clause put me in. The system has text, and text has held me every time the people failed. This is more text. We'll read it better than they do. We always have.*

Below him the records hall was still lit, and while he watched, its windows went dark in pairs along the wall as Bracken worked back down the room trimming the lamps out, one by one, in the order the fire-watch's ledger required. The last of them was the long table's. When it went, the shape it left on the eye was the shape the night had actually been: three volumes stacked at the table's head, four chairs pushed square to the edge, and the instrument lying where the reading had stopped, first page up, waiting for better light.

# Chapter 23 — The Registry Line

The east road out of Ostrand's province ran four days, and the country thinned as it went, and the infrastructure thinned with it — and Cael catalogued the thinning, because cataloguing was how he loved things, and because the thinning was the last argument the road had to make and it made it in geography.

He kept the count in the observation notebook, under a heading he ruled on the first morning and did not explain to anybody: what the road still had.

The first day it had everything. Milestones with the province's mark cut in them at the half-league. A toll at the second bridge with a bored man in it and a printed schedule of rates nailed to the post. Four inns, of which three were chartered. Carters going both ways at a rate of one every quarter-hour, and among them, twice, the district post. Two grey coats, at the toll and at the third crossroads, standing where grey coats stand. And a boy at a hamlet's edge who asked Brom, without preamble, what tier he was, because a big man on a cart is a question a boy in a province asks, and Brom said "Copper" and the boy said "oh" and lost interest entirely, which made Lira laugh for a mile.

The second day it had less. Milestones at the league instead of the half. One inn. No toll. Carters at perhaps one an hour, and the composition of them changed: fewer goods, more people, and the people carrying their own goods. The prices went up, which Karis noted with professional interest and entered — a loaf at the second day's inn cost a third more than a loaf at the first day's, in a country with more grain in it, because the cost of a thing on a road is not the cost of making it but the cost of the last man who was willing to bring it.

And on the second afternoon, the last chartered waystation.

It was a coaching inn of the ordinary kind, stone below and timber above, a yard, a trough, six stalls of which four were empty, and the registry's own furniture still on its wall by the door: the posting-board, with the small sigil at its head and everything the registry required a chartered house to display, current, kept, and completely useless.

Cael read the board the way he read all boards, standing in the yard with the horses being watered behind him.

*By charter of the registry, this house being chartered: TIER VERIFICATION available at the district station, Ostrand, four days west. No station lies east of the river crossing, and the station at the ford is unstaffed. TRAVEL ENDORSEMENTS may be lodged at this house for forward transmission; allow twenty days. ENROLLMENT INQUIRIES by the same. The chartered rates of this house are posted within. Complaints of a chartered house lie to the district seat.*

Twenty days for a travel endorsement to be transmitted from a house four days from the station that would receive it. He worked the arithmetic out of habit and got the answer the road had been giving him all afternoon, which was that everything on the board was true and nothing on it was usable, and that the registry's presence out here was a thing kept in repair rather than a thing in use — the way a household keeps a clock in a room nobody sits in.

The keeper was a woman of sixty who had run the house for thirty years and had views. She told them, unasked, what the road did ahead: the hamlet on the third morning; the provincial marker after it; the ford on the fourth afternoon and the old station on the far bank; and nothing after that except road. She told them that the post-rider came out from the district twice a week, ran his eastern leg to the ford, watered, and turned; that anything going west was handed to him at the ford or at her counter and nowhere else; and that in thirty years she had watched about two dozen parties go east and had seen five of them come back, and that she said so to everybody and it stopped nobody, and she had stopped expecting it to.

"Most go east because something's behind them," she said, filling Brom's flask. "Yours is a strange one. You've boxes. Nobody with something behind them stops to rope boxes."

"We're not being chased," Karis said. "We resigned."

The keeper considered that for a moment and evidently found it the most interesting sentence anybody had said in her yard that season. "Then you'll do better than most," she said, and went in.

Cael made two entries about the house before they left it. The first was that it took the registry's charter money and displayed the registry's board and had done for thirty years, and that in the yard the woman priced her rooms by what a traveler looked like he could pay, which is not a chartered rate, and that nobody within four days could have told her otherwise. The second was that when Lira asked her, at the door, what she would do if the district ever stopped sending the charter money, she said, "Same as now, only with a nail-hole where the board was," and laughed, and that the laugh was the most accurate assessment of the Compact's reach anyone had offered him all year, and had come from a woman who filled flasks.

---

The third morning gave up the last posted tier-verification notice.

It was nailed to a boundary marker at a hamlet's edge, weathered soft, a year old at least, the standard notice in the standard type — *a registry station lies within four days' travel west; no station lies east; practitioners are reminded that tier certification is verifiable only at station* — and somebody had, at some point, used the bottom third of it to work out a sum in charcoal about hay.

Past the hamlet the markers stopped carrying registry sigils. They went on carrying distances for a while, and then stopped carrying those too, and after that they were simply stones by a road that somebody long ago had troubled to stand upright, and Cael entered the transition at the exact stone where it happened, because the exact stone was the kind of thing his notebook existed for.

The third afternoon gave up the last watcher.

He was at the provincial boundary marker: one man, in a grey coat, on a good horse, holding his position at the marker the way a man holds a position at the end of a leash — because that was what he was; the registry's reach ended where the stations ended, and a watcher four days east of the last staffed station was a watcher watching the edge of his own jurisdiction, with orders that ran to the marker and no further, and nothing east of him but a job description that stopped.

He had them in his glass for a long while before the cart drew level. Cael, who had spent four years being looked at through glasses, ran the man's procedure and found it correct and unhurried: the glass, the small book, the entry, the glass again. Five travelers, one cart, one direction. Five names if he had them, which he did, because the resignations had been filed at a counter three days ago and the counter's ledger went where such ledgers went.

And as the cart drew level, he did the one thing his procedure did not require.

He put the glass down, and inclined his head to Brom. Not to the cart. Not to the party. To the man on the driving bench with the continental Copper title on a plaque in a kit at his feet, from a career surveillance officer at the end of his leash, professional to professional, in a country with nobody in it to see him do it.

Brom returned it with the same economy, and neither man said anything, and the watcher turned his horse back west, because there was nowhere further east for the leash to reach and both of them knew it.

They went half a mile before anybody spoke.

"That was a nod," Lira said.

"That was a nod," Brom agreed.

"He's been logging us for a year and a half."

"He has." Brom shook the reins. "He also watched me win a continental final in the rain and had to write down what time I left the ground. Man's entitled to a nod."

Seln, from the cart's tail, delivered the professional footnote without opening his eyes. "He'll have entered the courtesy. They enter everything." A pause. "He'll have entered it as *no incident*, which is the correct entry, and which is also — from that grade, in that coat, at that marker — the kindest sentence available to him. Take it as meant."

---

The fourth afternoon gave up the last Arbiter station.

It stood at the river crossing: a small, old, well-kept post on the near bank, one room and a slate roof and a stone apron in front of it worn hollow by generations of feet that had stood in exactly one place. The last of the network. The practical eastern terminus of the whole apparatus that had measured every person on the continent for four hundred years, and it was the size of a granary.

Cael went over it the way he went over instruments.

The door was not locked and had not been locked in years; there was no lock. The room held a bench, a swept floor, a shelf with nothing on it, and the frame. The frame was the standard frame — the same architecture as the one at Denvash, the same as the district station at Ostrand, four uprights and a lintel of the dressed grey stone the network had always used, with the sigil set into the lintel's underside in its housing. The stone was clean. The apron outside had been swept, this season, by somebody. The roof's slates had been repaired at the north corner with slates that did not match, which meant a person had climbed onto the roof of an unstaffed station at the edge of the administered world and mended it with what was to hand, and had done it well.

The sigil was dark in its housing. No evaluand, no evaluator; a frame kept in repair by a keeper never seen, against a traffic that had dwindled to nothing.

Karis found the ledger on the shelf's underside, because Karis found ledgers. It was a station book of the ordinary kind, half full, and the entries in it ran back eleven years and stopped four years ago: Kindling evaluations, two and three a year, children brought down out of the hills by parents who had walked them two and three days to be measured, each entry a date, a name, and a classification in a station officer's hand. The last dozen were in a different hand from the first hundred. The last one of all had a date and a name and, in the classification column, nothing — the line left open, as though the officer had intended to come back to it.

"Somebody stopped coming," Karis said. She turned the leaf and found no more. "The station wasn't closed. Nobody closed it. The officer stopped being sent, and the book stayed on the shelf, and the roof got mended by whoever mends the roof, and the frame sat here dark for four years while children in those hills went unmeasured." She put the book back exactly where it had been, which was her one superstition. "There are people out there of eleven and twelve years old who have never been classified as anything. I would like it entered that I find that the most hopeful fact I have encountered in four years of studying this apparatus, and that I am aware of what it says about me."

The five of them passed it one at a time, at the ford, because the road went past the apron and the apron was the only good footing to the water, and each of them had a different account to settle with the instrument.

Lira, who had been measured wrong by one of these at fourteen, and had spent six years proving the measurement wrong, crossed it without a glance, which was its own kind of glance.

Brom, who had been priced by one — Copper, forever, the number his family's ledger demanded and his architecture exceeded — crossed it and touched the housing once, briefly, in passing, the way a man touches a doorframe on his way out of a house he is done with.

Karis, who had studied these instruments until she found the maintenance records of their oldest stratum, crossed it and looked at it the longest, and said nothing, because she had said it all in an annex, and what was left was the look a researcher gives the primary source when she has finally understood it and is walking away from it anyway.

Seln, who had serviced the machine these frames fed, crossed it and did not look at it at all, which from Seln was the loudest crossing of the three that had gone before.

And Cael, last, stopped.

The others took the cart down to the water. He stood on the swept apron in front of a dark frame at the end of the world's paperwork, alone, for about a minute.

He had thought, on the road, that he might have something prepared, and had caught himself drafting it on the third evening and had stopped, because a speech to a machine is a speech to whoever you imagine is listening, and there was nobody. He had learned, across four years, the difference between a gesture aimed at a witness and a thing said to a thing.

"You couldn't read me," he told it, conversationally, the way you tell a fact to an old machine. "Four years I thought that was the system failing. Turns out it was the most honest thing the system ever did — the one time the layer under you couldn't finish its job, because there was no bin beneath me to file the lie in." He looked at the dark frame a moment longer. "You're the only part of the whole apparatus that never lied about me. You just went quiet. Everything above you did the lying." He shouldered the satchel. "Thanks for the eleven seconds. They were the truest measurement I ever got."

He walked on across the ford.

The water came to the axle. On the far bank the road went up through gravel and out of the trees, and behind them the little station stood on its apron with its swept floor and its mended roof and its dark stone, going on being kept, by somebody, for nobody, at the edge of everything.

---

The Registry Line passed under the cart's wheels at mid-afternoon, unmarked.

There was no marker, because the thing it bounded had no interest in being bounded. It was not a border; nobody had drawn it; no instrument on the continent recorded where it fell. It was simply the practical edge of the Arbiter network — the meter of road beyond which no station operated, no tier was verified, no enrollment existed, and no asset-restriction could execute, because there was no registry east of it to execute in.

The cart's wheels crossed it. The country did not change. The road ran on into the same thinning hills it had run through all afternoon, past the same gorse, under the same sky, and the five of them crossed the most consequential line any of them would ever cross without a single one of them knowing, in the moment, which meter of road it had been.

Cael tried, afterward, to fix it. He worked backward from the ford and the pace and the light, and got it to within perhaps two hundred yards, and then stopped, and wrote down that he had stopped and why: that the whole point of the thing was that it was not a place, and that a man who insisted on marking it would only have been building one more station.

Lira was the one who said it, an hour later, realizing — reining the lead horse a moment, looking back west and then east and then at the others, with the specific stillness of a person checking a fact she cannot quite believe is a fact.

"Nobody's watching."

Four years of rotations, watchers, windows, files, escorts, seals, glasses logging them through — and an hour of road, past a line no one had marked, where the sentence was simply true. No grey coat at any distance. No glass. No paper coming up any road under any seal. The country held them the way country holds anything, indifferently, enormously, and did not report them to anyone, because there was nobody east of the Line for the country to report to.

"Nobody's watching," Cael agreed, and charted the absence the way he had charted the presence for four years, and found the chart had no entries in it, and left the page blank, because a blank page was the honest record of the thing.

What he did not write, and turned over for the rest of the afternoon, was that he had been watched for so long that he had stopped being able to tell which of his habits were his. The counting of exits. The reading of a room's grades before its faces. The four-year practice of never letting a true sentence out of his mouth in a corridor. He had built an entire competence out of being observed, had been praised for it, had beaten a hearing and a tournament and a term of proceedings with it — and somewhere on this stretch of road the thing it was built against had simply stopped, and the competence was still running, with nothing to do, like a mill wheel turning in a dry race.

He decided, provisionally, to keep it. Karis was right that assuming the reach ended with the stations was bad method. But he entered the other half honestly, because the log did not take summaries: *the habits stay. The reason for them just went away, and I noticed how much of me was the reason.*

The others took it in their own currencies, which he collected, because he collected.

Karis said it was, technically, incorrect — that there was no reason to assume the faceless layer's reach ended with its stations, and that they should behave for a year as though it did not — and then said, in a different voice entirely, "And I am aware that I have just declined to enjoy the first hour of this in my life, and I would like that noted as a defect of character rather than of method."

Brom said nothing at all for two miles and then said, "Nobody's counting the horse either," which was the most cheerful sentence anybody had heard out of him since Norhold.

And Seln said, "It ends where the stations end. That is a fact about them and not about us," and then, after a while, in the same flat administrative register, "It took me eleven years to be certain of that. You have it on your first afternoon. Do not waste it by being careless with it," which was, from that man, an entire benediction.

---

They made the fourth night's camp two hours past the Line, in a fold of ground with the road above it, and it was the first night any of them had ever slept outside a jurisdiction.

Nobody said that out loud. What happened instead was that the ordinary business of a camp got done, and every one of them was audibly and visibly not saying it, and the not-saying gave the evening a strange, light, unmoored quality that Cael tried three times to name in the notebook and gave up on. Brom cooked. Lira wanted to know what the watch arrangement was and Seln said there was no watch arrangement out here that was worth losing sleep over yet and would be within a week, and set one anyway, and took the worst of it, and denied that he had. Karis worked by firelight with the boxes open, which she had not been able to do in a coach and had evidently been suffering about for four days.

And at some point in it Lira said, to the fire, "I keep waiting to be nervous," and nobody answered her, and about a minute later Brom said, "Give it a week," and that was the whole conversation and it was, Cael thought, entirely sufficient.

The Daeva deferral was paid at the fourth night's camp, in the last of the light, on a knee, with the fire behind him.

Her letter had come in the term's second month, direct and without comfort, and had ended with an instruction rather than a wish, and he had carried it in the satchel through a ruling and a resignation and four days of road without answering it, because the honest answer had not existed yet and he did not send unfinished ones.

Now it existed. He wrote it short, in his own register, and did not draft it twice:

*They ruled. We left — resigned, before the date, in good standing, four files closed complete and one man who filed nothing. You said keep myself unclassified. Done, permanently, on my own paper this time: I'm past the Registry Line, where the word has no meaning, which turns out to be the only place the word is safe.*

*The rematch stands. Not postponed — relocated. It happens on a floor nobody owns, and I think you'd prefer that anyway; a Gold-tier fact and an unclassified one, fighting where no seal can score either of us, is the only honest version of the bout, and we both knew it at Norhold. Find me when you're ready to fight something no ladder built. I'll be east.*

*— C.*

He sealed it with nothing, because he had no seal any more and had not had one that meant anything at any point in his life, and addressed it to her academy's hall in the plain hand a stranger uses.

In the morning it went back west with the waystation's post-rider, met at the ford at the eastern end of his run — a coaching house's man in a leather coat, watering his horse at the last Arbiter station's apron, who took the letter, glanced at the direction, glanced at it again, and said, "That's the champion," and then, after a moment's thought, decided against asking anything, and put it in the pouch, and rode west. He had no idea he was carrying the youngest Gold-tier in history's correspondence out of the administered world and into it, and Cael watched him go for a while, because that letter was the last piece of mail the registry's world would ever carry for him and it seemed worth watching leave.

The log banked the thread beside its lineage:

*Reydan wanted the answer when I have it. Daeva wants the rematch with whatever I am. Both promises ride east with me. Both get kept. Not yet. The road's long and it runs the right direction.*

---

The fifth day was the first day of the rest of it, and it was mostly uphill.

The road stopped being a road at about the third hour — not dramatically; it simply stopped being maintained, and then stopped being surfaced, and then became the track that two carts a season make, and Brom got down and walked at the horse's head for the last of it. There were no milestones. There was no post. Twice they passed cut wood stacked by the track, which meant somebody within a day's walk was in the business of cutting it, and once, at a distance, a fenced enclosure with sheep in it and nobody visible.

Cael ran the count in the notebook and found the heading he had ruled on the first morning had run out of entries. What the road still had: a track, weather, water at intervals, and the five of them. He drew a line under it and wrote the date and started a fresh page with a new heading, and the new heading was one word, and the word was *east*.

The first ridge beyond the Line came at dusk on the fifth day, and they came up it in the last of the light with the horse blowing and nobody talking.

The edge territories opened below.

Unadministered, unmeasured, enormous: the continent's whole unmapped margin falling away east in ridge after ridge, blue and then bluer, into a dark that no station's light reached, no tier verified, no seal governed. There was a river down there somewhere, showing twice. There was smoke, once, a long way off, which meant people, and no way of knowing anything else about them. There were hills behind hills behind hills, and no road visible past the first eight miles of the one they were standing on.

They looked at it in five different registers. Lira looked at it the way she looked at a floor she meant to fight on. Brom looked at it the way he looked at a long road, which was as a series of solvable problems in order. Karis had the notebook open before the cart stopped, beginning a field-archive of a place no archive existed for, and asked Seln twice for distances and got them. Seln read its approaches out of pure habit and said nothing about what he found. Ephram's absence at the ridge was noted once, without grief, and let go.

And Cael, last, stood at the ridge's edge and catalogued the one thing the whole vista made undeniable, and entered it in the log by the last of the light:

*First look at the edge territories. Everyone warns you the world out here isn't governed. Nobody mentions that it's big.*

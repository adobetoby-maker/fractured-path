# Chapter 16 — Two Hundred Years

Karis had the cache for the second night, and she mapped it the way she had spent her whole working life preparing to map the primary source of it — against everything she carried, in her formal register, findings entered one at a time — and each finding, as it landed, landed on a thread somebody at that table had been carrying for years.

She had done the work in the intervening day, and the intervening day was the part nobody watched. Nine hours in the locked annex with the door wedged and a jug of water and no lunch. Three hundred and eighty documents laid out in dated courses on a floor because the table was not long enough, walked between in stockinged feet with a slate in one hand. Her own four-year working archive brought down in three crates and opened beside them: the Greyvane hearing's compiled record, the copied Ardenmere passages, the registry-law commonplace books, the standardization directive's own founding text in the form she had transcribed it at Prynn, and the founding-era compilation with the Greyvane signatures that had arrived from Prynn's archive the week before on a request the delegation's budget had funded and Withrow had signed without asking why.

She had built an index. That was the whole of the intervening day, and she described it to them at the start of the second night in one sentence, because she wanted them to understand what she had and had not done: "I have not interpreted anything yet. I have made it possible to interpret. Everything I am about to say is a comparison between two documents that are both on this table, and if I say a thing you cannot see, stop me."

She worked from the oldest stratum up, because provenance ran downward and she trusted the floor of a case more than its lid. The lamp. The memoranda in dated courses. The maintenance leaf in its wax-paper sleeve, apart, handled with the specific care she gave documents that could not be replaced. Cael at the table's end with the observation notebook and the anomaly's question, because the night would have a check in it only he could run. Seln at the wall, because it was his case and a keeper attends his archive. Lira and Brom present and quiet, the way the family sat with things too large to help with.

---

**Finding one — the mechanism and the flag.**

The maintenance leaf's *advisory corrections function* lived, its own text said, in the evaluation sub-layer — the stratum of the Arbiter protocol below the reading, below the classification, the maintenance level a practitioner never perceived and an evaluator never touched. The corrections fired at Kindling: after the architecture was read and before the classification reached a record a human saw, the function rewrote inconvenient architecture into a lesser Path, so that the falsification never occurred in a document, never crossed a desk, never required a corrupt official — because by the time any official saw the classification, the classification was already the lie, clean, native, indistinguishable from a true reading to every instrument that ran on the same sub-layer.

She built it for them in four steps, on the slate, in chalk, because Brom had asked her to say it in a way he could hold.

One: a child stands in a station and the protocol reads the architecture. Two: the reading is true, and exists, for an interval nobody has ever measured, inside a layer nobody reads. Three: the function consults something — a schedule, a rule, a list; the leaf does not say which, and she wrote *unknown* on the slate in the same chalk and underlined it — and where the reading is inconvenient, writes a lesser one in its place. Four: the classification emerges. An assessor reads it aloud. A clerk enters it. A certificate issues. A family goes home.

"That's why no living official knows," Karis said. "There's nothing to know. No one falsifies anything. The falsification happened before the record existed, in a layer no one reads, on a schedule no one runs. Every official in the chain handles a true document. The document is just true about a lie." She set the leaf down. "It's the most elegant corruption I have ever seen described, and I want it on the record that elegant is the correct word and that I hate using it."

"The station clerk in the river town," Brom said.

"The station clerk in the river town was telling the truth. So was the form. So was the certificate." She did not soften it. "That's the design. There is nobody to be angry at anywhere in that file, and a girl in a river town is thirty-three years old and living somebody else's life."

Lira asked the question that had been sitting under the table since the night before, and she asked it the way she asked everything, which was without a cushion. "Inconvenient to who?"

"That's the second finding I'd have made if the leaf said more, and it doesn't." Karis pulled two courses of the reclassification stack toward her. "But the orders do. Forty pairs, and I sorted them last night by what the *true* reading described rather than by date, which nobody has ever had the material to do." She turned the sorted courses so the table could see the shape. "Twenty-six of the forty are corrections downward from architectures with one property in common: reach. Perception at distance, effect at distance, influence over a medium somebody else is standing in. Not power. Reach. The rest are miscellaneous, and eight of those I'd argue about."

"So it isn't about strength," Cael said.

"It has never once been about strength. There's a filed Iron in this stack whose true reading would have put her three grades up, and the machinery let a very strong Bronze in the same district go through untouched, because the strong one's architecture couldn't do anything at a distance except be strong." She squared the courses. "Whatever wrote that function wasn't worried about people who could hit hard. It was worried about people who could see, or reach, or hold something they weren't standing next to. Which is a preference. Which means somebody had a *policy*, two hundred years ago, about what kind of practitioner the continent was allowed to have — and every Kindling since has been quietly enforcing it, one fourteen-year-old at a time, in a layer nobody reads."

"How many fourteen-year-olds?" said Brom.

"Take the forty as a sample of nothing, because it is a sample of nothing — it's what one man happened to touch across nineteen postings." Karis put her pen down, which she did when she wanted a sentence heard as an estimate and not a figure. "But there are roughly nine hundred district stations. Two hundred years. If it fired on one child in five hundred, it would still be more people than have died in every war on this continent since the founding, and they'd all be alive, and none of them would know."

Seln, at the wall: "Which is why I stopped estimating."

And then the case the function could not handle: an architecture with no lesser bin.

She laid the proceedings transcript beside the leaf — the returns Ilsev's referrals had produced, entered in the record over years, their exact text: *no originating authority of record; designation predates file creation* — *referral closed at registry level; no further inquiry authorized* — and she laid beside those the Suppression-Advisory Watch Level 4 flag's own history, all of it, the whole accumulated dossier of a thing that had followed a boy since he was fourteen: Havel's carried note, Ilsev's paper trail, the designation nobody could trace, the two referrals, the two returns, the flag no analyst had been able to read, the citation the Norhold convening had used.

"Look at what the returns actually say," she said, "and stop hearing them as bureaucracy for one minute. *No originating authority of record.* That is not a stonewall. A stonewall says *the requesting officer lacks standing* or *the matter is above your grade*. This says there is no author. *Designation predates file creation* — the flag is older than the file it sits in, which is administratively impossible unless the flag came from somewhere other than the file's own chain. And *referral closed at registry level; no further inquiry authorized* — closed by nobody, at a level, with an authorization that names no one. Three returns, four years, two offices, and not one human name anywhere in any of them."

She let that stand for a moment and then delivered the sentence the whole night had been walking toward, the transcript in one hand and the leaf in the other:

"When the Arbiter meets an architecture it cannot reclassify into a lesser Path — because [SHATTERED] has no lesser bin; there is nothing beneath it to correct it into — the corrections function *cannot fire*. It has no output for the input. And what fires instead is the contingency: the suppression-advisory flag. The machinery's fallback for the one case it cannot falsify. The flag isn't surveillance. It's the corrections function failing safe — flagging the anomaly it couldn't correct, so the layer's protection behavior can handle by exception what the layer couldn't handle by rule." She set both documents down. "And the protection behavior is what Ilsev has been photographing for three years. The returns — *no authority of record*, *closed, no further inquiry* — are the sub-layer defending itself. Auto-closing inquiries no living official authorized, because no living official runs that layer. She's been interrogating the mechanism for three years and filing its answers." A breath. "She just never knew what was answering."

Nobody spoke.

Seln, at the wall, said one thing, level and unhurried, and it was the corroboration the finding needed and the only one available anywhere. "I have submitted four inquiries in my career that returned in that grammar. Three were about classification anomalies. One was about a Level 4 designation on a subject in a coastal posting nineteen years ago." A pause. "I did not connect them. I filed them as institutional obstruction, which is what obstruction looks like from inside, and I was wrong for nineteen years about what had answered me."

Cael entered the finding whole, and beside it the thing the finding did to four years of his life: *the flag on my file since the day I Kindled — the thing Havel found and was told to stand down about, the thing Coss couldn't read, the thing that came back "no authority" and then "no inquiry" — was never a person deciding to watch me. It was the machine failing to correct me and flagging that it couldn't. I've been carrying a surveillance order my whole life that no one ever signed because no one ever gave it. The layer gave it. To itself.*

He sat with the pen down for a while after writing it. What he was doing, he decided afterward, was the small and stupid work of dismantling a face — because there had been a face on it, for four years, without his ever having built one on purpose: someone, somewhere, in an office, who had decided he was worth watching. He had hated that person in a low steady way since he was fifteen. There was no person. There had never been a person. And the thing that was actually there was worse in a way he could not immediately price, because a person can be argued with, and a person retires.

Neither Ilsev nor Havel would ever be told. That was settled in eleven words at the table — Karis proposed it, nobody dissented, Seln said "correct" — and the reasons were three and all of them were about the carriers: telling Ilsev destroyed a woman's entire understanding of the instrument she served in exchange for nothing she could act on; telling Havel put a note six entries deep into the hands of a man who would then have to decide whether to file it; and both disclosures widened a circle that could not afford one more chair. The two of them would go on being what they had been all along, which was honest, and unknowing, and load-bearing.

---

**Finding two — the strata, kept honest.**

She dated the cache's oldest documents against her own research, and she did it physically, in front of them, because a dating is only as good as the room's ability to check it.

Paper stock, first: the oldest memorandum's laid sheet against the founding-era compilation's, held up together to the lamp, the same chain-line spacing and the same faint watermark neither of them could identify. Type-face, second: three sorts in the memorandum with the same broken foot as three sorts in the standardization directive's transcribed source, which meant one press, or two presses supplied from one foundry, within a working generation. Seal format, third: the pre-reform registry seal with the outer ring, discontinued in a year Karis named without checking. Vocabulary, fourth, and the one she trusted most, because vocabulary was where an institution's hand showed: a cluster of eleven administrative constructions shared between the memorandum and the directive that appeared nowhere in the registry's language forty years later.

Then she set down the third document, and touched it once before she let it go.

"Same era. Same administrative hand." She had all three fanned under the lamp: the oldest memorandum's *corrective classification at Kindling*, the standardization directive's own founding text, and the copied Greyvane passage. "The falsification modification, the standardization directive that discouraged the assay clause into disuse, and the retirement of the old word — [UNBOUND] — are the same era, the same authors. The scheme's founders retired the word when they buried what it described. They didn't need it anymore. Once the corrections function was rewriting the architecture at Kindling, there were no more of them left to name, because the machinery unmade them at the door. The word became a description of a thing that no longer occurred, so they let it fall out of the vocabulary, and wrote the directive that made the whole older stratum unfashionable, so that no one would go looking in it."

She did not say the word again, that night or afterward.

"Three acts," she said. "Close the door. Remove the exit. Delete the name of what used to come through. That's not three policies. That's one policy with three instruments, drafted by people sitting in the same room, and I can show you the room's paper."

And then the boundary, stated as method, because keeping mysteries distinct was what she was for.

"Here is the limit, and I am entering it as a limit and not a finding, because the difference is the whole of my discipline." She squared her hands on the table. "The falsification is two hundred years old. The cache proves it — the modification, its direction, its function, its contingency. But the sub-layer the modification is *written into* is older. Demonstrably older: the maintenance leaf maintains a layer it did not build. Read its own grammar. It is a service instruction. It says *the function shall be appended to* — appended, to a thing already there. It refers to the layer the way a millwright refers to a river. Nowhere in it does anyone claim to have made anything. The cache documents the modification. It does not document the layer."

"Then who did make it?" Lira asked.

"That is exactly the question, and my answer is that I do not know, and that I can prove I cannot know from these documents." Karis's voice did not change at all, which was how they knew she meant it as method and not as caution. "Who built the sub-layer, and when, and why the old word's danger was a danger at all — none of that is in these files, and nothing in an operative's two-century paper trail can reach it, because Seln's files are operational, and the layer beneath the operation is not a thing operations touch. If I put a guess in the register tonight, the guess gets read as a finding by whoever opens the register in ten years, and then we have done to ourselves in miniature exactly what was done to that girl in the river town: written a plausible thing over a true unknown and let the record carry it forward."

She squared the three documents.

"We've explained the middle of the story. Not the beginning. Entered as a limit."

She wrote it into the register in those words, and drew the line under it, and dated it, and the two questions — the layer's floor, and the word — went into the annex's record as open, which was where they stayed.

---

Brom looked at the three fanned documents for a while and then said, "You could have said all that with less."

"I could."

"Say it with less."

Karis considered him, and gave him the courtesy of doing it. "Two hundred years ago, somebody appended a correction to a machine they didn't build. We can prove the correction. We can't reach the machine, and we can't reach whatever the machine was for before they got to it."

Brom nodded once, slowly, and Lira, who had been turning something over since the paper-stock demonstration, said, "That's the part that keeps snagging on me. They *found* it. They didn't invent a way to falsify a Kindling — they found a layer already sitting under every station on the continent and worked out that they could write in it." She looked at the leaf in its sleeve without going near it. "Two hundred years is a long time. It isn't a long time compared to whatever's under it."

"No," Karis said. "It is not."

"And nobody's asking who put it there."

"Everyone in this room is asking," said Karis. "Nobody in this room can answer, and I am not going to let us spend the truth we do have on a question we can't reach. That's the limit. It stays entered as a limit, and if it's still open in ten years I will still be pleased that we wrote it down honestly tonight."

---

**Finding three — the anomaly, checked and released.**

Cael ran the check the night demanded, because someone in the room would demand it later and it should be him.

Session nine, held against everything the cache explained.

The falsification operated at Kindling, on other people's classifications, rewriting architecture into lesser Paths. The anomaly was an unexplained event inside his own architecture, two years old, unreproduced, Tide-adjacent, that had happened once under conditions the log said should have been impossible and had never happened again. He laid the two against each other with the whole instrument, in front of the circle, and did it the slow way and out loud, because Karis made him: era against era, mechanism against mechanism, direction against direction, what the corrections function did to a reading against what had happened in a training room to a boy who was already unclassifiable and therefore already outside the function's reach.

Nothing touched. The cache explained the machine's correction of others. It explained the flag on his file. It explained the corridor, the challenge, the two-hundred-year direction. It did not explain the anomaly, was not connected to the anomaly, and did not so much as brush it.

"Good," said Karis, and meant it, and wrote *checked; unrelated; both remain open* in her own hand.

The log got one line, per the standing discipline:

*Not this either. Still open.*

---

**Finding four — the retroactive chill.**

And then the sentence from the opening night, paid in full, and Karis paid it because she had banked it — the line she could not place, the question mark that had stood since a records hall on the term's eighth day.

"I said, the night they served us, that the assay clause worked for three years because it bypasses Arbiter evaluation entirely, and that the one thing the petition would not concede was the *stratum* — that they weren't trying to beat the provision, they were trying to move you back inside the instrument, and I didn't know why it felt important." She had the challenge's copy under her hand now, beside the cache. "It felt important because it was the whole design. The assay clause is the one legal shelf the corrections function cannot reach — evaluation by demonstration, no Arbiter in the loop, a floor and a panel and human eyes. For three years you have lived in the single stratum of the entire registry where the machinery that unmakes people like you *cannot operate.* And the challenge — conceding the clause, conceding the record, conceding everything — has exactly one non-negotiable demand: that you stand in front of an Arbiter." She looked up. "It was never about your enrollment. The corridor's whole function is to herd you out of the demonstration stratum, the one shelf the corrections function can't touch, and back inside the instrument that was built, before your name, to correct you at the door. Compelled re-evaluation isn't the remedy. It's the *delivery mechanism*. They're not trying to expel you. They're trying to get you back under the layer that couldn't finish the job the day you Kindled."

"Who is?" said Brom.

It was the right question and it was the one nobody could answer, and Karis, to her credit, said so in four words and did not decorate it. "That, I can't reach."

Because the design was two hundred years old and the petition was four months old, and between those two facts lay a gap that the cache did not cross: whether the faceless office that had filed the challenge understood what it was delivering him to, or whether it was simply the latest hand to reach for the instrument nearest to it and find that the instrument fitted. Seln, at the wall, gave them the only professional opinion available.

"Assume the smaller thing," he said. "It is nearly always the smaller thing. Somebody wants a boy stopped, and reached for the standard instrument, because the standard instrument is what one reaches for." A pause. "That is worse, not better. A conspiracy can be exposed. A convenience cannot."

The corridor finally had a *why*, and the why was two hundred years old, and it had been waiting for a boy at the far end of it since before the boy's name.

---

The night broke up at the third bell, and it broke up badly, because nobody could say the next sentence.

Karis said it eventually, standing over the courses on the floor with her repaired hand held out from her body, in a voice that had gone flat with fatigue. "We can't file it. I want to be the one to say that, because I'm the one who believes in filing." She looked down at three hundred and eighty documents. "Every instrument that could verify this runs on the thing it accuses. There is no office, no seat, no panel, and no court on this continent that can authenticate that leaf without asking an Arbiter, and an Arbiter answers through the layer. And the moment it's filed, it stops being evidence and becomes Seln — a defected operative, fifteen years of unauthorized duplicates, in a proceeding about whether the respondent's people are dangerous." She put her hand down. "So it can't be filed, and it can't be published, and it's true, and I have never in my life been in this position and I do not like the shape of it."

What it could be instead she would not name at that hour. There was one man on the continent who could tell them whether any part of it could be verified from the inside, and knowing would destroy him, and she said that much and no more, and then she said the rest of it flatly, gathering the floor's courses oldest first in stockinged feet. "Which is the next decision, and I am not taking it at the third bell on nine hours' work. Tomorrow. All of us, with the door shut, and everybody saying the worst version of it out loud before anybody says the best."

They put three hundred and eighty documents back into a scuffed traveling case in the order they had come out of it, which took twenty minutes and was done in near silence, and Seln locked it and put the small key back into whatever pocket of his person it lived in, and went out into the corridor first to look at it before the rest of them used it.

---

The other thing arrived that night, in writing, long before it would arrive in the body — and Cael wrote it slowly, in the new satchel, because it was the sentence the whole term had been walking toward and it deserved the pen's full attention:

*I spent three years proving I could work within the system. Tonight I read the system's maintenance records. It isn't broken. It's maintained. The system was never designed to include me — it was designed, by name, before my name, to correct me at the door, and I'm only here at all because I'm the case the corrector couldn't parse. Every session I've won inside the instrument, I won by accident, in the one stratum the machinery can't reach, and the whole proceedings exist to walk me out of it.*

*So the question was never how to win inside. There is no inside to win. The question is what the five of us do with one locked case of the truth — which can't be filed, because the only instrument that could verify it is the instrument it indicts, and can't be published, because publishing it burns the one honest keeper the machine ever produced for nothing.*

*Karis says there's exactly one honest man left inside the machine, at thirty years' altitude, who has spent his whole life certifying the layer we just read the maintenance records of. Tomorrow we find out what the truth does to him. I don't know which is the greater risk — that it destroys him, or that it doesn't.*

---

Lira was on the stair when he came out with the satchel over his shoulder, sitting three steps up with her strapped foot straight out in front of her, giving no indication whatever that she was waiting.

She had been turning the case over since the third bell and had got it down to one sentence, and she gave him the sentence instead of a greeting, because it was the only part of the night that was any use to anybody.

"Stop calling it a case." She came up onto the good foot. "It's not evidence. It's a map."

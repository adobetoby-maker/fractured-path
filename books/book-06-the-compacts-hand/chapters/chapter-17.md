# Chapter 17 — The Instrument

The decision to bring it to Vastin was taken in the annex on the third night, and it was taken Karis's way, which meant that the worst version of it was said out loud first, by each of them in turn, before anyone was allowed to argue for it.

The worst version was this. They were proposing to carry evidence of the falsification to a serving Archmarshal of the Compact, and the outcome was one of two things and they could not know which until it was done. Either they had found the one honest man inside the machine and were about to spend, in a single hour, everything the last two years had built in small increments — the advisory that put Cael on a roster, the attendance at a hearing nobody required him to attend, the warning delivered off the record on a cold street. Or they were about to hand the Compact's senior enforcement officer the location of a cache that ended all five of them, and find out that the honest man had a limit, and that the limit was treason.

They went round the table.

Brom said the worst version in eleven words and then sat with his hands flat: "If he files it, Seln hangs and the rest of us are witnesses." Lira said hers at more length and it was the operational one — that Vastin had not been in a room with any of them unsupervised since Norhold, that a man does not have to intend a betrayal to be followed to one, and that she wanted to know who else would know he had gone out that evening and could not be told. Karis's was documentary and the coldest: that the exhibits, once seen, could not be unseen, and that an Archmarshal's memory was itself a copy of the cache walking around inside the Compact, permanently, for the rest of his life, in a head they could not lock.

Cael's was the one he had been carrying since the second night and had not said. "The worst version is that he does exactly what we hope. He refuses to report it, he refuses to sign, he takes himself out of the machine — and we've done that to him. He's fifty-one years old. Thirty of them are in the thing we're about to show him the maintenance records of. We're not asking him to help us. We're asking him to find out that his whole life was an instrument's, and then we're going to walk away east and leave him standing in it."

"That is not the worst version," Seln said, from the wall. "That is the price. They are different words and it matters that we use the right one."

He said his own last, because he was last, and he said it flat, with nothing in it.

"He is the best instrument the Compact ever made. Best instruments do not break where you expect. I have watched three men I would have sworn were incorruptible sign the thing that damned them, at the last, because the institution was the only self they had. Not one of them thought of himself as breaking. Each of them thought of himself as *holding*." He let the sentence finish in the room before he added to it. "I cannot tell you which he is. I can tell you that the only way to learn is the one that cannot be taken back."

Then the arguments for, which took less time than the arguments against, because there was really only one and Karis made it. Every road out of the term ran through a man who could refuse a signature, and there was exactly one such man alive, and he had already been benched for measuring true.

The vote was five hands. Cael's, then Karis's, then Brom's without hesitation, then Lira's with a shrug that was not carelessness but the particular impatience of someone who had made her decision an hour before the discussion started. And Seln's raised last, and meaning most — because Seln was the one who died if it went wrong, a defected operative in possession of the cache, delivered by his own choice into the presence of the man whose office existed to find exactly that — and he raised it anyway, unhurried, and looked at nobody while he did it, and the raising was the last argument the annex needed.

One thing was decided in the same hour and decided without a vote, because nobody proposed the alternative.

"He gets the cache," Karis said. "He does not get anything about Cael."

"Agreed," said Seln.

"I want it said in full and not by implication, because we're tired and tired people take shortcuts." She looked round the table. "He will be shown two hundred years of falsified classifications, and he will be told nothing whatever about the mechanism. Not tonight, not ever, not if it would help, not if he asks, not if the room goes badly and one of us is tempted to buy him with it. He is receiving evidence about the *system*. That is all he is receiving, and if we blur that line for a single sentence we have handed a serving Archmarshal the one piece of information that makes the five of us worth destroying regardless of what he thinks of the Compact."

"And if he works it out?" Lira said.

"Then he works it out, and that is his and not ours, and nobody in this room confirms it." Karis wrote the decision into the private register and read it back aloud and made them all hear it. "Say the sentence, all of you."

They said it. Even Brom, who thought the exercise was absurd, said it, because Karis had been right about worse things.

---

The mechanics were Seln's.

Neutral ground in Ostrand: a records administrator's office on the third floor of a leasehold building four streets back from the registry seat, a room he had, decades ago and in another life, had reason to know the sightlines of. Two ways out. A stair and a service stair. A window that gave onto a courtyard with one gate. The office's own occupant away for the term, the lease held by a name that was a name and nothing else, the key produced from a pocket without comment.

The request had reached Vastin through Withrow's counsel in a form so oblique that it had taken him a day to be certain it was a request at all — a line in a routine communication about the delegation's supervised-hours schedule, containing a date, a street, and an hour, and no invitation, and no signature that meant anything, and nothing that could be read as a solicitation by anyone who did not already suspect one. Seln had drafted it. Bracken had transmitted it without being told what it was, which Bracken had understood perfectly and had done anyway.

And no record. The meeting entered in no calendar, on no schedule, in no minute. Nothing carried into the room that would have to be accounted for on the way out.

And the cache's core exhibits only, not the cache itself. Nine documents in a plain folder: four reclassification orders clipped to their Kindling records, three memoranda spanning the whole lineage from the oldest stratum to the newest, the river-town pair with the father's letter, and the maintenance leaf in its sleeve. Tradecraft to the last, because a keeper who carried his whole archive into a serving Archmarshal's presence was a keeper who had stopped being one. What went into that room was enough to prove it and not enough to lose it.

Cael catalogued the office while they waited, because there was nothing else to do with the forty minutes and because he had never in his life been in a room he did not itemize. It was somebody's working room and had been left mid-year: a desk with a blotter worn through at the left elbow, which meant a left-handed occupant; two chairs that did not match; a shelf of lease abstracts in a district binding he recognized from Karis's crates; a stove, cold, with last winter's ash still in it; a wall calendar four months out of date with three days ringed in ink and no note against any of them. The window's shutter had a splintered slat that let a bar of the street's lamp in across the floorboards. There was a water jug and no cup.

It was a room with no version of any of them in it, which was exactly why Seln had chosen it, and Cael understood that he was going to remember it for the rest of his life and that this was a poor argument against it.

They took positions at the seventh bell. Lira on the street, because Lira on a street was worth two of anyone else. Brom on the stair-head, sitting on a crate with a periodical, enormous and entirely unconvincing and perfectly adequate. Karis and Cael and Seln in the room with the folder, and Seln in the chair that faced the door.

---

Vastin came alone, in the plain greys, eleven minutes early.

He came up the main stair and not the service stair, which Seln noted aloud afterward as the most informative thing the man did all evening, and he knocked, and waited to be admitted to a room whose door was standing open. He looked at Brom on the crate on his way past and said, "Good evening," and Brom said, "Sir," and neither of them made anything of it.

That he had come at all was the first datum, and Cael filed it before the door closed. A serving Archmarshal did not attend unrecorded meetings with a respondent's principals in a proceeding he had been benched from. He knew that better than anyone in the room. He had come.

He took the chair they offered and put his hands on the table and said, "I have an hour and I have told no one where I am, which is the last time in my life that sentence will be true. Show me what you have."

They set the exhibits before him. Seln laid them, in order, naming each in the same level tone, without argument — because argument would have been an insult to a man who read for a living — and then sat down and stopped talking.

Vastin read.

He read the way he had evaluated a boy in a records hall two years before: completely, without comment, at his own speed, one hand turning leaves, the other flat on the table. He read the four reclassification orders against their Kindling records first, and he read them the way an evaluator reads, which is to say he read the *assessors'* language rather than the orders', because the assessors were the ones who had been in the room with the children. At the third pair he stopped and went back to the first and read it again. At the river-town pair he read the father's letter twice and the district clerk's four-line answer three times, and then set the whole pair down and squared it and put one finger on the clerk's answer for perhaps four seconds before he moved on, and nobody in the room breathed while he did it.

The memoranda, next, oldest to newest, the type-styles aging under his hand, the phrase receding. He read those quickly, because he had read ten thousand memoranda and knew what he was looking at inside a line and a half.

And the maintenance leaf, last, in its sleeve.

Here the reading slowed, and Cael watched it slow, and understood what he was watching. A man who had certified Arbiter frames for thirty years was reading the maintenance record of the layer beneath the frame — finding, in a mechanic's two-century-old grammar, on a sheet older than the Compact's current charter, the function that had been running underneath every evaluation he had ever conducted. He read it three times. The third time his lips moved slightly on one clause, the way a man's do when he is checking a construction against the language he learned it in.

He finished. He set the leaf down and squared it to the stack, which was what his hands did with paper when the rest of him had stopped.

And then the silence.

Nobody filled it. Lira was on the street and would have filled it; there was, Cael thought afterward, some possibility that Seln had put her there partly for that reason. Karis did not move her hands. Seln sat with the folder's flap turned back and looked at the middle of the table. Cael counted, because counting was what he did with the unbearable, and stopped counting at four hundred because the count had begun to feel like an intrusion, and started again later out of pure inability to stop.

The lamp did its small work. In the courtyard below, a gate was opened and shut, and a man crossed it whistling four bars of something and was gone. Somewhere in the building a door closed, far off, and then, some minutes later, closed again. The building's fourth-floor tenant walked the length of his own floor twice. A cart went down the street and the sound of it took a long time to arrive and a long time to leave.

Vastin sat with the leaf square in front of him and his hands still on the table and his eyes on the middle distance where evaluators put their eyes when the evaluation is of themselves, and the silence ran for as long as it needed to run, which was longer than any of them had ever sat in one.

Cael catalogued him instead, because the instrument was the only thing he had that was any use in that room and because a boy who had been read by this man across a records-hall table had a debt in the ledger about being read back.

The hands did not move for the first eleven minutes. The breathing changed twice — once early, shallow and then corrected, the correction visible as a man deciding to breathe properly; once late, a long one let out through the nose that he did not appear to have chosen. He looked at the leaf four times and at the river-town pair five, and never once at any of the three of them, which Cael understood was not avoidance but the ordinary courtesy of an evaluator who did not want anybody in the room reading his face while it was still deciding what it was. Twice he began a motion toward his breast pocket, where thirty years of habit kept a pen, and twice the motion died before the hand had cleared the table.

Once, at what Cael judged to be the middle of it, he reached out and moved the river-town pair a half-inch to align it with the memoranda, and then put his hand back where it had been.

Then he said it, in the register of a man reading his own finding aloud after a lifetime of reading other people's:

"I knew the system was imperfect. I didn't know it was designed to be."

---

What he did next was the thing they had come for and the thing none of them had dared predict, and he did not once let it become an alliance.

He did not offer one. He did not touch the exhibits again — a small thing, and Cael charted it: a man declining to handle evidence a second time, because handling it twice was a way of making it his, and he was not making it his. He did not ask where the cache lived, which was the question the Compact's enforcement officer existed to ask, and the not-asking was the loudest thing he did all evening.

And he did not reach for a pen.

He told them, in the register of a man reading his own evaluation aloud, exactly what he could and could not do, and he built it the way Karis built findings, each course bearing the next.

**He could not verify it.** "Every instrument I have to test this with runs on the thing it indicts. I could take the maintenance leaf to the frames I have certified for thirty years and ask them whether the sub-layer contains an advisory corrections function, and they would answer through the sub-layer, and I would be asking the accused to testify through the mechanism of the accusation." He let that stand a moment. "There is a version of me, twenty years younger, who does it anyway, on the theory that an honest question is never wasted. That man would have got a clean return in four days and believed it, and filed his belief, and been the instrument's best witness against the truth. There is no verification available to me that would not be theater, and theater, once performed, would destroy you, and him" — a nod, not looked at, toward Seln — "and me, and change nothing. I will not perform it."

**He would not report it.** He said the sentence flatly and it landed in the room the way a stone lands in a well, and Cael understood while it was still in the air that he was watching a man cross a line he could not come back over: an Archmarshal, in knowing possession of evidence of the falsification, filing nothing. It was the same crossing a Shadow Path clerk had made at a coastal posting nineteen years earlier and had gone on making every morning for a decade in true empty reports, and now it had been made twice, in two registers, at two altitudes, by the only two men in the machine who had ever had the chance.

"I am required, by every oath I have taken, to report what I have seen tonight," Vastin said. "I have taken those oaths to an institution that has, I now know, been forging the documents it swore me to. An oath to a forger is not an oath." He said the last of it without any weight at all. "I will not report it. That is not a favor to you. It is the first honest thing my office has permitted me in thirty years, and I am keeping it for myself."

He stopped there for a moment, and what came next was the only unbuilt thing he said all evening.

"I have conducted, or supervised, or certified the frames for, somewhere above nine thousand evaluations." He said the number the way Karis said numbers, which was without any weight on it at all. "I do not know how many of those readings were corrected under me. Neither do you. Neither does anybody. That is not a rhetorical flourish; it is the actual condition, and I would like it stated plainly once tonight and then not returned to, because I am going to be living in it. I have been the most careful evaluator on this continent for thirty years, and carefulness was the *qualification*. A careless man would have produced noise. I produced clean readings, at a high rate, with a reputation that made them unquestionable, into a layer that rewrote the ones it did not like before anybody saw them." A pause. "If I had been worse at my work, I would have been less useful to it."

Nobody offered him anything. Cael had a sentence ready about how that was not his fault and had the sense, at the last, to recognize it as a thing he wanted to say for his own comfort, and swallowed it, and later entered the swallowing in the log as the only decision he was proud of all evening.

**And he would no longer lend the machine his hands.** "The proceedings have asked me to certify the re-evaluation protocol's applicability to the enrollee. It is the last signature the corridor wants — the best evaluator's name, on the record, saying the standard protocol is the correct instrument for this subject, so that what follows looks like an evaluation instead of a delivery. I received the request the week my petition for standing was refused." Something moved at the corner of his mouth and was not a smile. "The same office that would not hear my finding would very much like my signature. They will not have it. They will have to proceed without my name on it, and every registry-literate person on the continent will read the silence where the name should be."

He was quiet a moment.

"It is the only sentence I have left that they still need. I intend to make them do without it."

Seln said one thing during the refusals and only one, and he said it after the second, without moving from his chair. "You will want to write it down somewhere. Everybody does, the first year. Do not."

Vastin looked at him for a moment — the two of them the only people in the room who had ever done this — and said, "No. I know what that costs."

Karis asked the one procedural question, because somebody had to and it was hers. "Will they proceed?"

"Yes."

"Then what does the refusal buy?"

"Nothing you can spend." He looked at her with something that was almost respect and was certainly recognition. "It buys a line in a record that will be read in thirty years by somebody like you. I have spent my career learning that this is not nothing, and I have also spent it learning that it is not much. Both of those are true and I decline to choose between them."

Lira had come up from the street at the half-hour, per the arrangement, and she asked it, because it was hers to ask.

"Are you with us?"

"No. I am not with anyone." He looked at her, and then at the whole table, without unkindness — an evaluator delivering a finding about himself. "For thirty years I was an instrument. I have just learned what I was an instrument *of*. A man should sit with that before he chooses what he becomes next, and I have not sat with it yet, because I read it an hour ago. You are asking me to convert the largest thing I have ever learned into a side in a fight, tonight, before I have finished learning it. I will not do that either. It would be another man lending his hands to a cause because a cause was in front of him, and I have just finished being that man."

He stood.

"I will do the things I told you. I will do them because they are what an honest evaluator does, not because they are what your friends need — and if you cannot tell the difference, you have not understood the only thing I came here to be."

He thanked the room. He did it the way he had once thanked a records hall after an evaluation and a tournament chamber after a testimony — unperformed, exact, addressed to the room rather than to anyone in it, courtesy kept as bookkeeping and debts entered where they occurred. Then he took his coat and went out past Brom on the stair-head, and down the main stair, and out into the Ostrand street, alone: an Archmarshal returning to a machine he now understood better than any living officer and could no longer account for, having spent one unrecorded hour learning that his whole life had been an instrument's, and choosing, in the hour, not to become a weapon's instead.

Nobody in the room said anything for a while.

"Well," Lira said at last, "he didn't take it."

"No," said Karis, gathering the nine documents back into the folder with hands that were not entirely steady and would have denied it. "He didn't take anything at all. That's the part I'm going to be thinking about."

Seln checked the courtyard, and then the stair, and then the street, and came back and said the only assessment that mattered, in a tone that made it sound already filed.

"He came up the front stair. He knocked at an open door. He told us he had told no one, before he was asked." He picked up the folder. "A man arranging a betrayal does none of those things. He was not managing us. In nineteen postings I have met four people who were not managing me, and I did not like any of them either."

---

The log, that night:

*He didn't join us. I'd have trusted him less if he had.*

*The Compact's best instrument just took itself out of the machine's hand — quietly, completely, without switching sides, because the truth didn't give him a side. It gave him a mirror. He read the maintenance records of his own life for an hour and then told us that turning what he'd learned into our cause tonight would be the same failure that made him an instrument in the first place, and he's right, and it's the most honest thing anyone has said to me all term, and it came from the man the machine benched for measuring true.*

*He won't verify it — it would be theater. He won't report it — an oath to a forger isn't an oath. And he won't sign the certification the corridor needs — the one sentence they still require of him, withheld, so the silence reads. Three refusals. No alliance. He's still inside the Compact tonight, and he'll be inside it when this term ends, and he is more dangerous to them than any ally of mine could be, because an ally can be discredited and a serving Archmarshal quietly declining to lend his name cannot.*

*Write the date. Somewhere above him, the machine has no idea it just lost the only part of itself I ever respected — not to us. To a mirror.*

# Chapter 18 — The Ruling

The proceedings concluded across two sittings in the term's last month, and both of them ran as procedure from the first bell to the last, because that was the only way this particular corridor had ever known how to close. Nobody at Ostrand did anything but their job, correctly, in order, on the record. The clerk called the sittings on time. The seal ruled on motions with the even courtesy of a person who had done it four thousand times. Jent was punctual and unfailingly civil and made no argument he could not support. Ilsev wrote. Havel logged. And the corridor closed anyway, in the flat type of officials being exactly what they said they were, without one moment at which anyone in the chamber could have pointed and said: *there. That is where it went wrong.*

That was the thing Cael could not stop cataloguing across those two sittings, and he catalogued it the way he catalogued everything, which was in numbers. Two hundred and six people had touched the matter since the first service of the challenge. He had counted them out of the record over three evenings: clerks, seat officers, copyists, messengers, the four correspondents with standing accreditation, the seat's two ushers, the nine-man registry staff, the delegation's own counsel and their assistants. He could not find one of them who had done anything he would have called dishonest. He had looked hard, because looking hard was the only work left to him in that chamber.

The room at Ostrand was the registry seat's large hall, borrowed from the trading floor above and dressed down for the term: a raised desk under the seal's arms; the petitioner's table at the left, the respondent's at the right, twelve feet between them; the evaluation seat set at an angle on the desk's right hand so that its occupant faced both tables and neither; the records rotation's long bench beneath the windows; and the gallery, ninety-one seats, filled to the walls for the closings after having held a dozen people for most of the term's documentary work. The stove reached the third row. Cael had sat in the fifth all term and had stopped noticing the cold in the second month.

---

Jent closed first.

He had conceded everything worth conceding at the start — the enrollment's lawfulness, the institution's compliance, the record's completeness, the absence of any allegation of fraud — and so he closed on the only ground the petition had ever stood on, and he closed it in ten minutes, courteously, without a wasted syllable, standing at the correct height with four documents in front of him that he did not consult.

Discretion, first: the registry's plenary authority, in an integrity matter, to compel re-evaluation regardless of an enrollment's validity. He read the provision. He read the two cases in which it had been exercised in the last forty years, both unglamorous, both uncontested, both entirely on point. He did not overstate them.

The nature of the demonstration record, second, and this was the argument he had been building since the term's fourth week, laid out now in its finished form: that the record described the practitioner exhaustively, and that description was not evaluation within the meaning of the integrity provisions, because evaluation was a determination of *classification* and the demonstration provision had never purported to make one. "The respondent's record tells the seat, at extraordinary length, what this practitioner *does*. The integrity provisions do not ask what a practitioner does. They ask what he *is*, and by what instrument that was determined, and the honest answer on this record is: by none."

And the referred tournament exhibit, deployed once, at the close, with the efficiency he had shown all term.

"The adjudication office's own instrument found the practitioner's capability unscorable under its standard. The registry does not ask the seat to improve on that finding. It asks the seat to act on it — to acknowledge that a practitioner outside evaluation is a practitioner the registry must evaluate, and to order the one instrument that can."

He sat down at the correct height. He had made no accusation, raised his voice at no point, and taken ten minutes. Karis, at the respondent's table, wrote three words on her pad and turned it so Cael could read them: *He is very good.*

---

Then Karis rose, and gave the best legal argument anyone in that room would hear in their working lives, and it changed nothing, and every person at the respondent's table knew both of those things before she opened her mouth.

She spoke for fifty-one minutes. She had a folder in front of her and she opened it once. She argued the text one last time, whole, from the keel up — the assay clause's own language, the enabling instrument behind it, the four hundred years of practice that had made it what it was — and she did the thing she had done since the Greyvane hearing, which was to build every proposition on a document already in the seat's hands, so that not one sentence of her closing asked the seat to take her word for anything.

She argued the integrity provisions next, and she did the work nobody else had bothered to do: she had read every recorded invocation of them in the registry's archived history, ninety-one instances, and she had them tabulated, and she put the table into the record. Eighty-four were institutional — a station's records, a panel's composition, a certification chain. Seven concerned individuals, and all seven concerned an individual's *conduct*: a falsified endorsement, a bought certificate, a practitioner sitting an evaluation for another man. "In four hundred years," she said, "the integrity provisions have never once been turned on a person for what he *is*. They have been turned on people for what they *did*. There is no instance. I have looked at all of them. The petition is asking this seat to make the first one, and it is asking without saying so."

She argued the demonstration record third, and here she slowed down. Four years of it. Eleven pounds of paper. Panels, seals, human witnesses, evaluators of record, wing assessments, a hundred and ninety-one supervised bouts, a continental adjudication office, and a Gold-tier practitioner's signed public statement. "The petitioner says description is not evaluation. Then let us be exact about what evaluation means, because the word is doing all the work in this petition and it has not once been defined. If evaluation means *a determination by an instrument*, the petitioner is correct and the seat should ask itself why that is the definition it prefers. If evaluation means *a reliable determination of what a practitioner can do, by competent persons, on the record, capable of being checked* — then this respondent has been evaluated more thoroughly than any Iron-tier practitioner on this continent, by human beings, under seal, for four years."

And she closed on the sentence she had been building the whole term toward, the one Cael would carry east.

"The registry has, in its possession, the personal findings of its senior-most evaluator, the finding of the continent's adjudication office, the statement of the continent's highest-ranked practitioner, and eleven pounds of documentary record, all saying the same thing in four grammars: the instruments failed, not the man. The petition asks the seat to prefer the silence of the one instrument that has not yet been tried to the testimony of every instrument that has. There is no evaluation in that. There is only the instrument the registry has decided, in advance, will return the answer it wants. The seat should decline to call that an evaluation, because the record — the whole record, four years of it — already is one."

She sat.

The gallery made its low sound, the one a room makes when it has understood something and has nowhere to put it. One of the four correspondents was writing so fast his pen tore the sheet, and he took a fresh one and started again from her third sentence rather than lose it. Bracken, in the second row behind the respondent's table, was crying without any expression on his face at all and did not appear to know it. Withrow sat entirely still with her hands in her lap and looked at the seal, not at Karis, which Cael understood an hour later: she had been watching for the one thing that would have mattered, which was any sign that the argument had landed somewhere it could do work, and she had been watching the only face in the room where that sign could have appeared.

Jent rose to reply and said, in full: "The petitioner has nothing to add. The submission the seat has just heard is the best statement of the respondent's case that could be made, and the registry does not dispute a syllable of it." Then he sat down, and that was the most damaging thing anyone said all day, and he had not meant it as damage. He had meant it as respect. Both things were true and the second did not soften the first.

Ilsev wrote for a long time after that. She wrote through the seal's procedural directions and through the clerk's reading of the exhibit schedule and for some minutes after the room had gone quiet, and nobody, including the seal, interrupted her.

And the refused signature landed in the record the same session, as one procedural line the clerk read without emphasis in a list of eleven administrative items, between a filing extension and a correction to an exhibit number:

*Evaluation-protocol certification: declined by the evaluator of record; proceeding on the presiding seat's authority.*

The Archmarshal's withheld name, entered as an absence.

Cael watched the gallery hear it. He watched the correspondents' pens stop — all four, within a second and a half of one another, which was the tell that mattered, because a thing that stops four professional pens at once has been understood by all four. He watched two guild officers in the fourth row exchange a look and then, a moment later, one of them lean and say something to the other behind his hand. He watched the ordinary curious in the back rows go on being ordinarily curious, because they had heard an administrative line about a certification and it had meant nothing to them at all. And he watched Jent, who did not react, and who had known it was coming for a week, and whose hand went to the second of his four documents and moved it a quarter of an inch and came back.

A certification declined by the evaluator of record was a thing the code permitted and the registry never saw. Its permanent presence in the ruling's foundation was a silence louder than any testimony these proceedings had set aside — and it would be there in thirty years, when somebody like Karis pulled the file.

---

Between the two sittings there were four days, and the four days were their own kind of instruction.

The delegation stayed at Ostrand in the guesting-house on the cathedral street, because going back to the bluff and returning would have cost two days of coach. They ate together in the evenings. Karis worked; she had a supplemental submission to draft on a point the seal had raised, and she drafted it, and it was good, and she knew while she was drafting it that the seal had raised the point out of courtesy and would rule the other way. Brom found a yard behind the guesting-house and used it, one-handed at first and then with both, and Lira came down and worked opposite him on the second morning, on an ankle that had come out of its strapping two weeks before, and neither of them said anything about the four days or the ruling or the road.

Cael walked the city. He had done it every sitting since the term's opening and had a route by now — the registry seat, the trading floor's colonnade, the coppersmiths' street, the river stair, the old wall — and on the third day he found himself standing outside the district Arbiter station on the north road, four miles out, having walked there without any decision he could identify.

It was a small building. He catalogued it because he could not help it: a stone hall with a slate roof, forty feet by sixty, one door, a bench outside it with three people on it. A woman with a child of about fourteen, waiting. A man of perhaps thirty with a district endorsement folder on his knees. The queue at a district station on an ordinary afternoon.

He stood across the road for a quarter of an hour and watched the woman take her daughter in through the door.

He wrote it down that night, and it was the shortest entry of the term:

*A girl went into the district station on the north road this afternoon at about the fourth bell. Fourteen, at a guess. Her mother had done her hair for it. In five weeks I go through the same door for the opposite reason, and whatever she came out with, neither of them will ever know whether it was hers. I stood across the road and did nothing, because there is nothing to do, and I have never in my life felt so exactly like the wrong instrument for a thing.*

---

Ilsev's turn came at the last session, and it was six minutes long, and it was the most important six minutes of the term to exactly five people in the building.

Jent moved, near the close, to enter the file's suppression-advisory designation as precedent for elevated handling of the enrollee — the same Level 4 citation the convening at Norhold had used. It was a routine motion, made without emphasis, on the last page of his last submission: the machinery reaching, one final time, for the flag its own contingency had raised.

And the seal, before ruling on the motion, asked the evaluation seat, on the record, to characterize the designation's provenance. That was routine too — a presiding seat confirming the standing of a cited authority before admitting it, a question asked perhaps four hundred times a year across the continent's registry seats and answered in one sentence every time.

Ilsev answered it the way she had answered every question ever put to her from a seat, which was by reading what the record said and declining to say anything the record did not.

She read into the record, verbatim, the exact text of the returns her own referrals had produced across four years — *no originating authority of record; designation predates file creation* — *referral closed at registry level; no further inquiry authorized* — reading them in her flat precise voice, at dictation speed, so that the clerk could take them down whole. She was entering into the permanent public record of the proceedings the exact language of the sub-layer defending itself, and she did not know that, and would never know it, and read it perfectly.

And she appended, in her precise, catastrophic honesty:

"The seat can characterize the designation no further. The record of inquiry into its origin is closed, and it was not closed by this seat, nor by any seat this seat can identify. The designation is cited as authority; its own authority is of record as unestablished. The evaluation seat notes the contradiction and can resolve it no further than the record permits, which is not at all."

The room moved on, because institutions move on. The seal noted the characterization, admitted the designation for the limited purpose, and proceeded to the next item, and the clerk called it, and the term's business went forward at the pace of a term's business.

Four people at the respondent's table did not move on.

Cael had his hands flat on the table and was looking at the grain of it. Brom had gone very still. Lira had turned her head perhaps ten degrees toward the evaluation seat and stopped. And Karis was writing, fast, in a hand that had gone entirely square, the transcript request already half-drafted in the margin — because she had just watched a woman who did not know what she was holding read it aloud into a public record that could not be sealed afterward, and because she had understood inside four words what the next hour of her life was for.

She obtained the transcript within the week, certified, at her own cost, and entered it in the private register beneath the maintenance leaf's provenance line, and wrote one sentence under it in the small hand: *Two hundred years of it, and the only piece that will ever be public got there because one woman refused to paraphrase.*

Ilsev learned nothing. She had read the machine's answers into the record because reading the record's exact text was her office and her faith, and she went home from the proceedings not knowing she had testified to anything but a bureaucratic dead end. The carrier stayed intact. The evidence stood.

Havel logged the session. He logged it in the records rotation's ordinary hand, in the ordinary book, and he was the officer who carried the day's minute down to the copyists at the fourth bell, and he did that correctly too.

That evening he submitted a transfer request out of monitoring rotation. It cited career development and a preference for provincial posting and it was four sentences long and entirely true in every particular that could be checked. In his kit, in the flat case he had carried since Ardenmere, the private note was six entries deep and would not receive a seventh. He was done watching, and he did not say so anywhere that could be read, and the request would be granted inside the month, and he would carry the note to a posting with a view of nothing that had happened in this room, unknowing, preserved.

---

The ruling came before term's end.

It came in the flat type, on the seat's ordinary stock, read aloud by the clerk in a chamber that had gone quiet in a way the whole term had not managed. It took two minutes and forty seconds. It did both things, and the doing was the corridor's last door, and it closed on schedule.

*The seat, having heard the petition and the responses in full, and having found the enrollment lawful in its execution and the respondent institution compliant with its charter throughout — which findings stand undisturbed — nonetheless finds the evaluation-integrity question sustained on the merits. The demonstration provision describes the practitioner; it does not evaluate him within the meaning of the integrity provisions. The petition for compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol is granted. The re-evaluation shall be conducted at the district Arbiter station within five weeks. Should the re-evaluation return no valid classification, the procedure of the code's chapter fourteen shall apply: nullification, suspension, asset-restriction. The demonstration-provision enrollment is revoked effective term's end.*

*Given under the Compact's hand.*

Lawful in execution. Compliant throughout. Revoked. Both things, in one paragraph, forever.

The gallery emptied in nine minutes. The correspondents went out first and at speed, because a ruling is a dispatch and a dispatch has a coach to catch. The guild officers went out unhurried and talking. The ordinary curious went out saying the word *revoked* to each other in the tone of people who had come to see something happen and had seen it. Jent gathered his four documents, put them in his case, bowed to the seat, and crossed the twelve feet between the tables to say — to Withrow, not to the respondent, correctly — "Headmistress." Withrow said, "Procurator." And he went, and the chamber's arithmetic was finished, and the whole thing had taken a term and eleven sittings and had never once raised its voice.

---

The respondent's table: the five of them and Withrow, the ruling's paper between them, and nobody performing surprise — because nothing about the term had been a surprise, and so the room did not have to pretend.

Karis had the transcript-copy request already written. She closed the privileged minute — *ruling: enrollment revoked; re-evaluation ordered, five weeks; ch.14 on no-classification; both* — and set down her pen after a term of never setting it down, and looked at the page a moment.

"Twenty-six years," she said, to no one. "The best argument I have ever made. Entered in full. Changed nothing." She looked up. "I would make it again. I want that on my own record, which is the only one I keep for myself."

"It's on mine as well," said Withrow.

She had not spoken since the reading. She sat at the table's end with the ruling in front of her — the named respondent institution's head, in a chamber where her academy's charter had just been found compliant and her enrollee's place in it revoked in the same paragraph — and she said the rest of it in the registry-trained voice she had never once dropped in four years.

"I have run this academy for twenty-two years and I have been in front of seats fourteen times, and I have lost four of those and thought the seat was wrong about two." She squared the paper. "This is the first time I have sat in a room where the seat was not wrong. Every finding in that paragraph is correct on the material before it. That is what I want the five of you to take out of here, because you will spend the next month being told you lost badly, and you did not lose badly. You lost to a question that was answered before it was asked, by an instrument that was never in this room." She stood, and gathered her copy. "I have four letters to write tonight and none of them will do any good. I shall write them anyway. It is what the office is."

Lira had not said anything since the clerk finished reading, and she said it now, and she said it to Cael.

"Five weeks." She had her elbows on the table. "That's not a sentence, that's a schedule. I want to be clear about what I'm looking at, because I've been wrong about this room since the first week and I'd rather be right about it late than not at all." She turned the ruling's paper round so it faced her. "They didn't beat us. Karis just made an argument nobody in this building could answer and the seat granted the motion anyway, and the seat wasn't lying and Jent wasn't cheating. Which means the thing that beat us isn't in this room and never was, and everything we do from here is either aimed at where it actually is, or it's theater."

"That's the council," said Cael.

"That's the council," she agreed. "Tomorrow. And I'm not going to argue it, because I argued it in the first week and lost, and I'd rather you got there without me pushing."

Seln had said nothing at all in the chamber, from the first sitting to the last, which was correct, because Seln was a clerk from an assessment office who had no business at a respondent's table and had been sitting in the fifth row of the gallery all term with a periodical. He came down to them when the chamber had emptied, unhurried, and looked at the ruling's paper for the length of time it takes a professional to read six lines.

"*Given under the Compact's hand,*" he said. "They put it on everything. Endorsements, transfers, appointment letters, the note that tells a man his posting has moved." He set the paper down. "I have had it written on me nine times. This is the first occasion I have read it and known what the hand was."

Brom asked the only practical question at that table, and there was nothing in it but the arithmetic.

"Five weeks from when?"

"From the ruling's date. Thirty-five days." Karis had it already. "The station's the district one, four miles out on the north road. The date will be served inside the week."

"Right," said Brom, and did not say anything else, and did not put his copy of the ruling down either.

---

Cael's log, that night, at the window, the ruling's copy on the sill:

*We won every argument for a term and lost the only sentence that was ever being written. The re-evaluation is in five weeks. Everyone keeps saying it like there's a decision left to analyze — the seat's discretion, the protocol's conduct, whether Ilsev's seat will certify the procedure. There isn't a decision left to analyze. There's a decision left to make. Different thing.*

*Karis drew the corridor in the first week and she was right in every particular and being right did not open a single door. The annex told us why: the corridor isn't in the code. It's under it, two hundred years down, in a layer no argument reaches, and it has been waiting for me since before my name, and the ruling is just the last door of it, and it's open, and it only opens one way, and the way it opens is back into the instrument that was built to correct me.*

*So. Five weeks. Council tomorrow. Not to analyze the ruling. To make the decision the ruling was built to prevent us from making — the one Lira asked about in the first week, and Brom priced in three sentences, and Seln has been holding a locked case toward for fifteen years, and I have been refusing since Greyvane because I believed the text was ground.*

*The text was never ground. Time to find out what is.*

---

He capped the pen and went down for the ink, because the guesting-house kept it in the front room, and the front room still had a lamp burning in it at that hour.

Brom was at the table with the ruling's copy-sets laid out along it — the seat's, the institution's, the delegation's, the four spares the clerk had run off at the close — and he was putting them into an order. Not reading them. Not filing them. Squaring each set to the one beneath it, heaviest to the bottom, the flat case's depth taken once between a thumb and a forefinger and not taken again: a farrier's son doing arithmetic with his hands about what a man can carry and how far.

Lira was on the stair behind Cael with her boot off, and had been for some time, and had understood it before either of them.

Neither of them said anything. The lamp went on doing its small work, and the ruling's paper went into a carrying order on a guesting-house table at the second bell of a morning that did not have a name yet.

# Chapter 4 — Standing

The term's second month braided itself the way the tournament autumn had braided, five tracks at once, and Cael ran the braid by schedule and gallery and the reports of people whose eyes he trusted in their own domains — with one difference from every braid before it, which he entered on the month's first night and would reread on its last: *this time every track but mine is a thing the system gave them honestly. Chart what that costs. Chart it before anyone has to pay.*

---

Lira's Silver program ran its first internal trial on the second floor at the month's opening — not a meet; a program bout, sanctioned, rated, Rooke's clerk at the table with the continental drill-book, the Silver Rank 5 from the river academy at the rating table as assessor, and across the flags the program's senior third-year, an Iron Rank 6 Force Path who was the best conventional product Rooke's cohort had built before Ephram — and the campus came to watch, because the Silver program's first rated bout under the institution's own seal was a thing the bluff had never staged.

The floor was the rebuilt second floor, the painted drill-lines still on it, forty feet under the high windows with the striking frames racked along the east wall and the rating table at the north head — a floor built for the drills she had been running for two months, which meant she knew its every line and the third-year knew its every line too, because he had been running them beside her. Cael took the rail with Brom and Karis and ran the instrument.

**First exchange:** the third-year fought her as the cohort fought — construction, pressure built in courses, the Force doctrine the river academy's Rank 7 had run against her at the confluence, at cohort speed — and the bout's whole premise was whether two months of continental sequences had changed what she did with it. She lost the exchange on flags, cleanly, the way the Zerin file had taught her to lose first exchanges: buying facts. The facts she bought were charted from the rail as she bought them — *he anchors left; his second course sets on a four-count; the structure is real and it has a floor* — and at the break she stood at her mark with the drill-lines under her feet and did the thing that made her Lira, which was not tighten.

**Second exchange:** she did not run the corner-game. That was the datum, charted from the rail before the exchange was a minute old — the taxation economy that had beaten the confluence and taken an exchange off Zerin, left in the kit. She ran, instead, the program: the sequenced-burst intervals the continental drill-book prescribed, cold at the confluence, right at the wool town, and now, on a painted floor, at cost — and she took the whole exchange forward of the drill-lines' second mark, closing at a stride and a half where the cohort was built to fight at three, so that the third-year's second course never set, because she was through its four-count on the third and already inside the distance the course needed. Flags hers. The gallery's sound was the sound of a story changing shape, and Rooke at the floor's head wrote one word.

**Third exchange:** the third-year adapted — the cohort adapted; that was its whole doctrine — and built faster, and the exchange was the bout's honest contest: the built grammar against a cohort at its own speed, and the grammar held. She ran Zerin's intervals as a *language* now, not a drill, and the language had tenses the third-year's construction could not price in time, and the flags came to her one and then two — both of them taken on her back foot at the floor's east third, with the racked frames a pace behind her heel and no ground left to give, because the grammar did not need ground — and Cael's chart recorded the datum the whole program existed to produce: *she can run the built version's grammar at Iron tempo against the cohort's best. Two months. The Rank 5 at the rating table has stopped writing.*

**Fourth exchange:** she closed it, on points, at range, the way the confluence had taught her to close a bout against a builder — no felling blow, the structure declined until it stood on empty floor. The flags went up. The trial went to the program's entrant, three exchanges to one, and Lira walked to the flags breathing hard and shook the third-year's hand, and the Rank 5 came down from the rating table and said something short that the delegation extracted at dinner: "He said, 'Two months. Zerin took two years to run that sequence at Iron rate. Rooke's file is right.'"

The bill came at the rail ten minutes later, because Rooke collected what he invoiced and had never once let a fighter walk off a floor without the price entered. He ran her through it standing, in the coaching file's own order: the burst-shoulder, tight — she rolled it twice and he watched the second roll and wrote; the landing-leg, honest; the fourth exchange's last two closes taken at range because the intervals had cost her the reach to take them any other way. "Sequenced bursts at Iron tempo, nine of them," he said. "Nine is two more than the drill-book budgets in a rated bout. You spent Silver-program coin to beat a cohort third-year and you'll pay for it Thirdsday." The ruling went in the file: two days at half work, the shoulder iced, no frames until the shoulder reported clean. "Cheap," Rooke said. "It's cheap now. It stops being cheap on a road with three bouts in four days. Learn the difference before the meet and not at it."

She paid it properly, and the file noted that too, and Cael entered the nine in his own chart beside the drill-book's seven, because a number a coach called expensive was a number worth carrying.

The third-year came to the rail on his way out with his kit over his shoulder and the flat courtesy of a man who had been beaten by something he intended to understand. "The four-count," he said. "How long have you had it?"

"Since the second week. I couldn't run it at speed until the sixth."

"So you've had it four weeks and you didn't use it on me until the second exchange." He shifted the kit. "Why?"

"Because I wanted to know whether your second course sets on the four-count every time or only when you're ahead." Lira said it without any weight at all. "It's every time."

The third-year took that away with him, which was the correct thing to do with it, and Cael charted the exchange as the evening's second finding: *she bought the first exchange on purpose in front of a rating table, which costs a flag on the record, and she did it anyway, and Rooke wrote one word.*

Rooke's review came at the flags, two words, Rooke-length, delivered to the coaching file and the fighter in the same breath: "Two months." He wrote nothing else. The Rank 5 said, "Two months," back, as a datum, and the two coaches looked at each other with the specific satisfaction of men whose instrument had just returned a number they could stand on — and neither of them said the word the file had carried since the confluence, because the file's word was for the road, and the road was six weeks out.

She came to the rail afterward with her collar dark and her hands taped and the look Cael had catalogued for two months, and this time — because the program had a bout in it now, and a flag, and a Silver's word — she gave it to him in words.

"Three years ago I'd have told you I didn't need any of this." She nodded at the painted lines, the frames, the clerk with the drill-book, the whole apparatus built toward her. "Scrapyard pride. I was very good at it. It got me a ledger and a circuit and a crown and a station with a door." She rolled the taped hands, watching them. "I'm better now. I can admit I want it."

Karis, at his other shoulder, said nothing, and wrote nothing, and afterward, on the walk back, said only: "She used the word *admit*. I've been waiting three years for that verb. Enter it under her name in the notebook's permanent section, not the working one."

"Noted," Cael said, and wrote it down this time, in the observation notebook, at the rail, where she could see him do it, and she watched the pen and did not stop it, which was the whole transaction.

*She wants it,* the log got that night. *She said so, on a floor, after a Silver stopped writing. In three years I have never once heard her say she wanted a thing the system could give her — she called them wrong, or late, or owed. Wanted is new. File the date beside the corridor's. Whatever this term costs, it now has a price on it that isn't mine, and the price has her name.*

---

Brom's paperwork arrived in two envelopes in the same week, and he opened them in different rooms.

The first was the district seat's: the advancement review's formal docket confirmation, panel constituted, the last Fifth-day of term, the season record entered as evidence, a line at the foot Bracken read aloud at the counter with the satisfaction of a registrar adding one to a tally he had kept for thirty years — *outcome anticipated: advancement, per title.* "They've written *anticipated* on a docket," Bracken said. "The registry does not write *anticipated* on dockets. Somebody at the district seat has decided the ink is cheaper than the pretense." He handed it over. "Iron-tier, by the last Fifth-day. The plaque's ribbon can go on your collar now. Nobody will think it's presumption."

Brom read it once and folded it and put it in the kit, and the ribbon stayed in the kit, and the table noted where the ribbon stayed.

The second envelope was Velmere-sealed.

It came up with the evening post — the estate's mark in the wax, the hand the delegation had seen twice a year for two years and once, at Norhold, on a single line — and he did not take it to the courtyard. He took it upstairs, and it lay on the small table in his room — Lira reported this, because Lira reported things — for two days, unopened, beside the docket, the two envelopes squared together with the care he gave to useful things and neither of them touched. Lira reported it the way Lira reported things, which was as logistics, at the residence table, to nobody in particular, with the drill-book open in front of her and her eyes on the page. "Second envelope's still on his table. Squared with the docket. Two days." She turned a leaf she was not reading. "He moved the lamp so it isn't in the light."

"You went into his room," said Karis.

"I went past his door. Twice a day, because the stair goes past his door." She turned another leaf. "I'd like it noted that I didn't go in, which for me is growth, and that I wanted to, which for me is honesty."

"Noted," said Cael.

"Don't note it on paper. Note it in your head." She closed the drill-book. "And when he opens it, nobody asks. Not one of us. Not even the researcher." She looked at Karis when she said it, without heat, the way you name the risk in a room to the person carrying it.

"I wasn't going to ask," Karis said. "I was going to sit where he could tell me if he wanted to. There's a difference and I have learned it entirely from watching him do it to me."

Brom came down the stair at that point, and the table's conversation changed direction without anybody steering it — the term's schedule, the second session's date, whether the intake's rota had been disbanded — and he ate, and said four words about the schedule, and went back up, and the envelope stayed on the table by the lamp for one more day.

On the third evening he opened it alone, and nobody at the table ever learned its contents, because he showed nothing of them; what the table got was one fact about his face when he came down to dinner afterward, catalogued from across the room at the instrument's full attention:

It was the face of a man being offered his whole ledger back.

Not joy. Not grief. The specific stillness of a person handed a document that contains everything he had ever been priced out of — the household, the standard, the Gold-tier arithmetic that had made a big careful boy a rounding error at fourteen — with a line at the bottom inviting his signature, and a date. Cael had seen the face once before, on Lira, at a registry station's anteroom, holding a folded certification that said *Iron*. He had not seen it on Brom. He filed it, and did not ask, and wrote the only margin the arrangement permitted: *the docket says Iron by term's end. The letter says something that arrived in the same week and got two days on a table before he'd touch it. Two envelopes. One ledger. He's being offered the number and the family in the same fortnight, and he has said "Noted" about neither, which from Brom means the account is open and the arithmetic is running. Wait for the Fifth-day. Don't renegotiate.*

---

Karis's standing spent its budget for the first time that month, and Cael was present for the spending because she made him present for it.

The budget line was, in the academy's arithmetic, small — a research allocation for copying and courier and archive requests, the kind of sum a faculty member spent without noticing — and she spent the first of it on a request to Greyvane's archive for a certified copy of the founding-era compilation's oldest signatures, the ones Prynn had once said would be there when she was forty. The request went down the bluff road under the academy's seal, with a budget code on it, and Karis stood at the counter watching Bracken enter the code with the expression of a woman who had spent three institutions asking for things on her own account and had just, for the first time, asked on an institution's.

"They'll fill it in three weeks," Bracken said. "Prynn's people are slow and exact. You'll have your signatures before midwinter."

"Before midwinter," Karis repeated, and wrote the date in the notebook's front matter, where the citations lived, and Cael saw the page and saw the space she had left beneath the date, and did not ask what it was for.

The assistant — a second-year named on no roster the delegation kept, because Karis kept her the way Vell had kept apprentices, by role — learned her fourth error of the month that afternoon, at the annex table, and Cael charted the teaching from the window because the teaching was itself a datum: Karis correcting a reading of the integrity provisions by declining to explain the correction, handing over the volume, and saying "find it," and the girl finding it in twenty minutes with her jaw set, and Karis saying "yes" and nothing else, and the girl going faintly pink with the specific pleasure of having been treated as competent. *She's building someone,* the log noted. *The institution gave her a room and a budget and a person, and she's using the person the way she'd use the room — as a thing to be made better than it was. Three years of watching Vell and Rooke and Quenna do it to us. It transferred. Note that everything the system gave her honestly, she is spending honestly, which is the only kind of debt that can be called in.*

---

Ephram trained beside Brom that month, at the striking frames, and Cael watched the two of them from the second floor's rail on four separate evenings before he understood what he was watching.

Not sparring. Not the cohort captain drilling the continental Copper champion, which would have been the natural reading and was wrong. The two of them worked the frames side by side in the same rhythm, a hair apart — Brom running the defensive structure top to bottom the way he ran it before every long road, Ephram running Blade doctrine at the cohort's speed beside him — and they did not speak, and the not-speaking had a shape. Ephram would stop, and watch Brom's reset, and resume. Brom would stop, and watch Ephram's entry line, and resume. Two men taking each other's measure with total thoroughness, night after night, the way you learn a floor you expect to fight on.

"He's deciding something," Cael said to Lira, at the rail.

"He decided it at Norhold," Lira said. "He's waiting for the right term to say it. Ephram doesn't say things early; he catches it from Rooke." She watched them. "Whatever it is, it's about the two of you, and it's about a floor, and he's going to ask for it properly, and you're going to say yes, and I'm going to be there."

"You've read this."

"I've read Brom's face while he watches Ephram's entry line. It's the face he had on the road from Ardenmere the week before he told you the story about Velmere." She pushed off the rail. "People who don't say things have a tell for when they're about to. It's that they get very good at their work."

---

Karis convened the annex the night before the first session, inner circle only, one lamp, and laid the session's procedure out on the table the way she had once laid out a tournament's scoring architecture: who sat where, what each seat was permitted to do, which of the code's provisions governed the entering of a petition and the entering of a response and the silence between them. "The seat will receive both," she said. "It will not rule on either tomorrow. It will confirm the schedule. The whole session is a form being filled in by four people in a room, and I want everyone at this table to have seen the form before they see the room, so that nobody mistakes the room for the thing that matters."

"What's the thing that matters?" Lira asked.

"The seal," said Karis. "Watch the seal. Watch what it does when the counsel enters eleven pounds of the truth. If it does anything at all, I'll revise the corridor."

---

The proceedings' first session convened at Ostrand on the fourth Fifth-day of the month, and it was procedural, cold, and brief, and it was the coldest hour of Cael's institutional life, because nothing in it was aimed at him.

The morning began at the side door, which was the day's first instruction in what he had become.

The delegation coach put them down at the foot of the market street at the half-bell, and the four steps of the wool exchange had thirty-one people on them — Cael counted them from the coach window before the door opened, because counting was cheaper than deciding how to feel — of whom nine were correspondents with folding boards and the rest were Ostrand's registry-literate come early for a bench. The side entrance had a registry officer and a list. The officer read the list, and read Withrow's name off it, and the counsel's, and Karis's as *documentary support*, and Brom's and Lira's as *institutional attendance*, and Seln's as *records support, Halcenvane*, and then came to the last line and read it aloud, which he did not have to do and did because the code said parties were identified on entry.

"The enrollee of record."

"Present," said Cael.

"Name for the door log?"

"There isn't one on the instrument," the counsel said, before Cael could answer. "The instrument says *the enrollee of record, unclassified.* Enter what the instrument says."

The officer entered what the instrument said. It took eleven seconds. Cael watched the pen do it and filed them where the other eleven seconds lived, and went in through a door that had been opened for the term because the ordinary one was not built for the people who had come to watch.

The registry seat's proceedings chamber was the exchange's old selling floor, cleared: a long room with the lot positions still cut into the boards, the registry's sigil on the end wall, the presiding seat on a low platform beneath it, the evaluation seat west of the seal, the records table behind the platform and below it, the two parties' tables facing — petitioner to the northwest, respondent to the northeast — and a public gallery of forty benches that the correspondents filled to the rails. Cael counted the room the way he counted every room, and the count came back with the same absence in it three times.

The presiding seat was occupied. A registrar in the registry's senior grey with the seat's hood forward, so that there was no face in it to read at all — and Cael pointed the whole instrument at the posture and the hands for an hour and got a reading he entered that night with the flat care he kept for failures: *the hands rest where the code puts them and move when the procedure moves them: the minute, the schedule, the receipt. No adjusting, no waiting, no weight shifted on the bench in forty minutes. Four years of reading men who were managing their faces, and it turns out the face was a courtesy. This one isn't managing anything, because there is nothing being managed. A posture, doing a seat's work.* The record identified the seat by seal. The clerk read the seal's designation into the minute at the session's opening and the registrar did not add a name, because the code did not require one, and the whole long room proceeded to conduct the most consequential proceedings of the enrollee's life in front of a chair whose occupant was, by procedure, nobody.

The gallery held the correspondents at the rail and, behind them, the ordinary curious — Ostrand's registry-literate, clerks and factors and two guild officers, come to watch a chair with no name conduct the continent's most-read case — and among them, in a plain coat, at the gallery's end where the sightlines to every table and door converged, the delegation's assessment clerk, who had come down on the coach as records support and had taken, without appearing to choose, the seat a man takes when he intends to read a room and not be read by it. Cael marked him from the respondent's table and did not look again. Withrow sat at the table's head in her registry grey with the response in its two cases beside her, and did not touch them, because a chancellor at a proceeding did not fidget with her own filing. The counsel sat with the minute-book open. Karis sat with the notebook closed, which was its own announcement.

Jent entered the petition. It took nine minutes. He read the instrument's operative paragraphs into the record in the voice of a man reading a schedule, tendered the exhibit list — Umber's referred tournament file among the exhibits, the *unscorable under standard* entry now evidence — and sat down at the correct height. The counsel entered the response, which took twenty-two minutes because two hundred and six items took twenty-two minutes to summarize even at a registry pace, and the seal received both without expression, and confirmed the schedule, and adjourned.

The evaluation seat was empty; its empanelment would be confirmed at a later session, the notice said, when the district's senior evaluators had been canvassed. The records table was staffed — a senior records officer and, behind him, on rotation, a junior with a private carefulness Cael knew from three postings and two academies. Havel did not look at the respondent's table once in forty minutes. From Havel, four years and five note-entries deep, the not-looking had the weight of a sentence.

Withrow, at the session's close, did the one thing the code permitted a respondent chancellor and nothing more: she rose when the seal rose, and inclined her head to the seat at the depth the registry taught its officers to incline to a seat, and Cael, watching, charted the gesture's exactness — twenty years of registry courtesy, performed at a chair whose occupant she would never be able to name — and understood that she was addressing the seal on purpose, as a seal, to enter into her own ledger that she had given the institution its due and would expect the same from it. She said nothing on the stairs. In the coach she said: "I have bowed to that seat three hundred times from the other side of it. Today I learned what it looks like from this one. Note that I bowed anyway."

The coach's council on the river road ran the session the way the delegation ran everything, in order of arrival. The counsel first: "Procedurally immaculate. He entered nothing he didn't need. I entered everything we had. The seat received both as if they weighed the same." Karis: "They do weigh the same. To the seat." Brom, after the length of time Brom took: "Havel didn't look at you." Cael: "No." Brom: "He looked at you at Greyvane. Twice, from the delegation gallery. I counted." Lira, to the window: "So the junior's stopped learning too." And Seln, from the corner, eyes closed, delivering the session's only finding in the register the table weighed at full freight: "The records officer at the senior chair is district grade. The junior behind him is monitoring grade, on rotation. The seat is registry-seat grade — the seal's grade, not a person's. Three grades of officer in one room and not one of them is the grade that decides anything. The office notes it." He did not open his eyes. "The office has sat in rooms like that. From the other chair."

Cael filed the session's observation on the coach back up the river road, and it was the observation the whole term would keep returning to, and the log held it whole:

*The hearing at Greyvane had Coss's face on it — you could read the whole case off him from across the room. The inspection had Vastin's; the evaluation was the man. This has a seal. A registrar of no age and no face reading a designation off a clerk's card, and a procurator with nothing on him, and a room conducting itself. I keep looking for the person and finding the hand.*

---

He wrote the month's close at the window, with the docket dates on the sill — Lira's meet, Brom's review, the second session, the blank — and the entry came the way honest entries came, not planned, just true enough to write down:

*Everyone I came here with is holding something the system finally gave them honestly. Lira's ladder. Brom's number. Karis's shelf. Write it down now, while it's simple: whatever this challenge costs me, it should not cost them.*

Karis read the entry over his shoulder — she had come to the window with a draft, and had stopped, and had read, which she did not do, and he let her, which he did not do — and said, in her flattest register: "That is not how any of us are built."

He wrote that down too, underneath, in the log's own parenthesis:

*(Karis read this entry over my shoulder and said "that is not how any of us are built." Noted. Filed. Afraid of it.)*

Then he read the parenthesis back to her aloud, because she had stood at his shoulder long enough to have earned the hearing.

She listened to it with her arms folded and the draft still in her hand, and did not soften a word of it.

"Afraid of it," she said. "Good. Be afraid of it accurately — with dates, and a column, and a total at the foot — and on the day it starts costing them you will know it was that day and not some other. That is what a notebook is for."

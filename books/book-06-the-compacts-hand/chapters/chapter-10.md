# Chapter 10 — Silver Season

The Silver program's first road meet fell at the term's midpoint, at the river academy's own hall two days north — the confluence town, the season's best floor, the place a regional Iron title had been decided a year before — and the delegation went to it as a delegation for the first time since Norhold, because Withrow had ruled that Halcenvane's Silver entrant traveled under the academy's colors with the academy's people, proceedings or no proceedings, and the ruling had the whole of her tenure in it.

She had made it at the delegation table, in one sentence, in front of Bracken, who had raised the question because raising questions was his office: whether the academy's colors ought to travel to a public meet in a term when the academy was a named respondent.

"The academy has an entrant in a Silver bracket for the first time in sixteen years," Withrow said. "We will be at the rail in our own colors, and if that is inconvenient to anyone at Ostrand they may put it in a filing, and I will read it."

Cael went because Lira's road had been his to chart since a boarding-house table in Ardenmere, and because a bout at Silver was a thing he intended to have catalogued from its first flag. Brom went because Brom went. Karis went with the response's fourth-session draft in a document wallet, because the proceedings did not pause for a meet and she did not pause for anything. Seln went as records support and took, at the confluence hall, the gallery seat where the sightlines to the floor and the doors converged. Rooke coached. The Silver Rank 5 from the river academy — her instructor, her assessor, her first Silver — came home to his own hall to watch his student fight up a tier on the floor where he had lost to her at Iron.

The Silver bracket at a regional meet was four fighters, and her draw was the deepest of them: a Silver Rank 8, Force Path, a career practitioner of the confluence's own guild who had held the region's Silver standing for six years and had, the file said, never once fought an Iron in a rated bout, because Irons did not enter Silver brackets. The file had a line for the enrollee's tier. It read *Iron Rank 1, Silver program, Halcenvane — entered under the program provision*. The guild veteran had read it, and had said, at the draw, with a professional's plainness that the delegation extracted from a steward, "Six years at Silver and I finally get a bout somebody will watch."

Rooke's pre-bout brief ran to four minutes at the residence table the night before, and Cael wrote all of it down.

"He builds in courses," Rooke said, with the delegation's charting of the veteran's last eleven rated bouts spread in front of him and the commercial file underneath it, unopened, because Rooke did not pay for pages he could produce himself. "Not like the ones you've fought. An Iron Force builder puts up a structure and holds it. This one puts up a structure and then puts a second one *under* it while the first is still standing, and the second is what's actually carrying, and by the time you've taxed the first he's three courses down and the load's somewhere you haven't been." He tapped the third sheet. "Anchoring runs on a four-count. That's the only number in the file that matters. Everything he builds is four counts from being load-bearing."

"So I'm inside four," Lira said.

"You're inside four *from the third exchange onward*, which is the part you won't like." Rooke sat back. "First exchange you'll lose. I want you to lose it. I want you to spend it measuring, because there is no honest way to learn a Silver's depth except to stand in it, and the two months of drill did not give you that and could not have." He looked at her over the sheets. "Everything I've taught you since Zerin's letter is built for the second exchange. That's where you find out whether the program is a program or a decoration. And the third is his, and the fourth is his, and if you're still on the floor in the fourth you've had the best night of your career and I'll say so out loud, which you know I don't."

"And if I'm not?"

"Then you'll have learned it anyway, and cheaper," Rooke said. "Silver doesn't have a discount rate. It has a tuition."

---

The floor was the confluence hall's: new boards, true and fast, a full rope square with gallery on all four sides and the judges' dais breaking the east rail, the floor where she had taken a Force builder apart a year ago by taxing his structures at their unfinished edges. She knew its every property. So did he. He had trained on it for six years.

Cael charted the geometry from the north gallery before the flags, because a floor was a document and this one had four things in it worth entering.

The square was thirty-two feet, roped at three heights, and the ropes had been re-tensioned that autumn and had almost no give, which meant the boundary was a wall rather than a cushion and a fighter driven onto it did not get a rebound. The boards were new and fast and had been laid the previous summer over the old floor, and they ran north to south, and at the square's south-west quarter the run changed direction where a repair had gone in — eight feet of boards laid crossways, level with the rest, visually identical from the gallery, different underfoot. The dais broke the east rail, which meant the east side had no gallery pressure and no noise, and fighters drifted toward silence without knowing it; Cael had watched that happen in three of the afternoon's Iron bouts and had timed the drift at about nine seconds. And the hall's high lamps were rigged on the crane-line above the square's centre and threw nothing usefully directional, which meant that unlike a mill town's loading doors, this floor had no light to spend.

*No light, no rebound, a false quarter in the south-west, and a quiet side that pulls,* the entry ran. *She has one of those four. He has had six years of all four.*

**First exchange.** The gulf, staged honestly, because Lira had spent two months being told it would be and had asked to feel it rather than be shielded from it.

The Rank 8 opened at what the file called his working rate, and the working rate was a different climate. Force construction with Silver *depth* — courses built under courses, each anchoring position sustained by a load the tier below could not hold, the structure advancing on her with the patience of a doctrine that had solved Iron-tier taxation years ago. He did not come forward. That was the first thing that was wrong with him, measured against every Force fighter she had ever met: he built where he stood, and the building took the floor, and the floor arrived at her without him ever having crossed it.

She ran the survey. Two bursts, short, at the structure's shoulder and its base, the way she had taken the last builder apart on these boards, and both came back with the same answer, which was that there was nothing unfinished to tax. She went to the second course and found the third under it. She went low and found the anchoring already set. The survey came back complete and useless: no seam in the depth; a four-count on the anchoring, exactly as Rooke's file had said; and a floor she could not yet find, because he had taken the centre in the first thirty seconds and had built her toward the false quarter, and she did not know it was false and had put her back foot on the crossways boards twice and had felt the difference both times and had not had a spare half-second to ask what it was.

She lost the exchange going away. Three flags, clean, no contact worth the name against him, and a hard set-down on the ropes at the finish that the ropes did not give back — the north-west corner post, her shoulder into it at rate, and the hall making the sound a hall makes when a home Silver puts a visiting Iron into the boundary in under two minutes.

She got up before the referee reached her, walked to her mark, and did not tighten.

Rooke at the rail wrote one word.

**Second exchange.** The program, deployed.

Not the corner-game — the corner-game was Iron economics and she had left it in the kit on purpose. The continental sequences, Zerin's intervals, run as language against a Silver structure for the first time in her life: the entry-and-reset that did not reset, the doubled half-beat, the four-count answer that was not an answer to the four count but a way of arriving before it began.

And the language *held*.

She took the centre first, which nobody had expected including her, by refusing the third exchange's geometry a whole exchange early — she went at his building hand rather than his structure, in the first eight seconds, and made him choose between finishing a course and covering, and he covered, and covering cost him the step he had been going to build from. The Rank 8's second course never set, because she was through its four-count on the third. She was inside his construction, at contact, on a fast floor, against a man who had not had an Iron inside his construction in six years, and the confluence gallery — which had come to see a Silver stand on an Iron and had instead been given a bout — made the sound galleries made when a story changed shape.

She took a flag on a scoring contact at the ribs, off a burst she paid for immediately in the shoulder.

She took the exchange's second on a re-entry angle the built version of the sequence had run at Norhold and she had run on a painted floor forty times, out of the quiet east side — the side that pulled, which she had used deliberately and Cael entered as such — and the Rank 8 went back a full stride for the first time in the bout.

He stepped back further. He looked at her across the boards with six years of Silver in his face. And he recalibrated upward, courteously, the way Silvers did, which meant he stopped fighting the file and started fighting the woman, and which cost her the next two exchanges and was the highest compliment available in the building.

**Third exchange.** His actual game.

Silver depth at full expression — the courses built faster than her language had tenses for, the load under each anchoring position enough that even the interval-work arrived at structures already finished. He gave up the centre voluntarily, which she read half a second late as generosity and which was not: giving up the centre put her on the boards' false quarter with the crossways run under her push foot, and the sequence she launched from there went out at three-quarter purchase, and she knew it as it left and could not recall it, and he was waiting at the end of it because he had built the end of it.

She got out. That was the exchange's real content, and only four people in the hall could see it: she got out of a finished structure at contact range, spent two bursts doing it, and came out the south side with her guard intact and her rate down.

He built again. She was through the four-count twice more and the third time she was not, because the fatigue had arrived — five bursts now, and the strapped shoulder reporting on all of them — and the exchange was the first of her life in which she fought at Silver tempo from inside it and was *outbuilt* rather than outrun.

She lost it on flags, honestly, close, extending him further than any Iron had extended a Silver on that floor.

Cael's chart recorded the line the whole program had been built to produce: *she is fighting a Silver's bout. Not surviving one. Fighting it. The margin is his. The bout is hers.*

**Fourth exchange.** He closed it, on points, at the working rate, the way a Silver ends an Iron who has cost him three exchanges of real work.

He did it professionally, at range, and he did it by declining to be interesting: no depth, no invention, nothing she could get a purchase on — a career practitioner who had learned three exchanges ago exactly what she could do to him inside four counts, and who spent the fourth exchange making certain that no count of any length happened within her reach. He built at distance and let the distance be the argument. She spent what she had left going after it twice, and the second time she nearly had it, and *nearly* was the exchange's whole difference: she came up half a stride short of a structure's building edge because the shoulder would not give her the burst at the rate the half-stride needed, and the flags went up while she was still arriving.

Silver Rank 8, three exchanges to one.

She walked to the flags breathing like a woman who had spent everything and had it accounted, and shook his hand, and the Rank 8 held the grip a second longer than courtesy and said something short, and she said something shorter, and the confluence gallery gave the bout the craft-reader's ovation, long and low.

Her instructor — the river academy's Silver Rank 5, the man she had beaten at Iron on these boards a year ago — was on his feet at the east rail with the dais between him and everyone, applauding with his hands over his head where his own students could see him do it, which cost him something at his own hall and which he plainly did not care about.

She walked off the floor incandescent.

That was the word the log used, and the log did not use words like that, and it used it because there was no other: she came to the rail with her collar dark and her hands taped and a light on her that Cael had seen once before, at a registry station's anteroom, and had not seen since — because the loss was the *right kind*. The kind with six more years of doors in it. And for the first time in her life, every door was open, and someone was holding each one, and the someone had an institution's seal on his file.

Rooke's post-bout note went into the coaching file at the rail, four words, Rooke-length, and he read them aloud as he wrote them, which he did not do:

"Again. Faster. It's there."

The bill came out with the file, because Rooke invoiced everything. Seven bursts across four exchanges, three of them over rate. The strapped shoulder, which had been a heat-wrap matter since Norhold and was now a five-days-at-half-work matter. A boundary contact at the north-west post in the first exchange, which would be a bruise the width of a hand by the following night and was already stiffening. And one line that was not an injury and went in the file anyway, because Rooke had never once separated craft from cost: *three-quarter purchase, SW quarter, ex. 3. She didn't know the boards changed. Nobody told her. That's mine.*

The lesson was one sentence and she said it herself, at the rail, before Rooke could, which he noted too.

"It isn't the craft. The craft got in twice. It's the rate — I was half a stride short at the end and the half-stride was the shoulder." She flexed the hand. "I don't need a different bout. I need to be faster inside the same one."

"Yes," said Rooke.

She slept that night with the bout sheet on the residence table where she could see it from the bed and the shoulder strapped for the five days Rooke had priced, and Cael knew this because she told him so at breakfast, unprompted, with the sheet in her hand, and did not seem embarrassed, which was itself the year's whole distance.

---

The counterweight came braided through the meet in two movements, and the first was timed for the gallery.

Mid-meet, between the Silver bracket's second and third bouts, with the confluence hall at its fullest and Halcenvane's colors on the floor for a second bout — a third-year in the Iron bracket, the program's own supporting entrant — a registry records officer came through the hall's main door with a document case and a clerk.

He did not send the clerk. That was the first thing Cael charted, from the north gallery, with the whole hall laid out beneath him like a floor before a bout. The officer came in at the main door, which was at the hall's south end and was the door the paying gallery used, and he walked the length of the hall on the gallery's inside rail, past three hundred seated people, in registry grey, with a document case held at the front of his body in both hands where it could be seen — and it took him, Cael timed it, fifty-one seconds to reach the delegation's block, and by the twentieth second the bout on the floor had stopped being what the hall was watching.

He served Halcenvane's delegation, in front of the whole confluence, with a documentary demand from the proceedings: the respondent institution's complete evaluation-linked floor records for the enrollee, term to date, to be produced at the fourth session.

Bracken took it. Bracken was the officer of the academy present who was entitled to take it, and he stood to do so, and he read the whole instrument standing, front and back, at reading pace, which took ninety seconds during which nobody in the delegation's block sat down either. Then he signed the acknowledgment, and asked the officer for the second copy, and received it, and thanked him, and sat.

Withrow had not moved. She thanked the officer as he went past her, by rank, in a voice pitched for the four rows around her, and the four rows heard a chancellor thank a registry officer for the correct service of a lawful instrument, and did not have anywhere to put it.

The demand was lawful. Bracken would confirm it by letter within the week — a routine production order, the code's ordinary instrument for building a record, served on a respondent's delegation because the delegation was where the respondent's officers were. It could have been served at the bluff. It could have been posted. It was served at a venue, at the meet's fullest hour, at the gallery rail, by an officer who walked the whole length of the hall to do it, and the timing was the message, and every registry-literate person in the confluence read the message as it was delivered.

Lira watched it from the floor's edge, taping for the third bout, and Cael, at the rail, charted what she watched: her academy's colors getting quieter in the hall, in real time — the confluence's regard for Halcenvane's Silver entrant, sixty seconds earlier the warmest thing in the building, adjusting itself around the sight of a registry officer serving the delegation paper. Not hostility. *Caution.* The hall recalculating what it cost to be seen cheering for the respondent's people.

He could count it. That was the part he wrote down and disliked writing. The east gallery's second and third rows had come to their feet for her second exchange; at the third bout's call they applauded the Halcenvane third-year seated, at half length, in the manner of a room being correct. A steward who had spent the morning finding the delegation chairs found them nothing in the afternoon. Two of the four correspondents' boards left the hall behind the officer.

"They served it *here*," she said, at the rail, afterward, in the voice that had once discovered an institution was small and now discovered one was patient. "Not at the bluff. Not at Ostrand. Here, in the middle of my bracket, in front of the river."

"It's a production order," Cael said. "It's lawful."

"I know what it is." She finished the tape. "I know what it was *for*, too. So does everyone in this hall. They just don't have Karis's word for it."

"Corridor," Cael said.

"That's the one." She bit the tape off. "It's a good word. I'd like a better one by spring."

---

The second movement was Karis's, and it happened at Ostrand the same week, at the fourth session, and it closed the term's legal question for good.

She was not counsel. She could not argue. But the code permitted the respondent's advocate to put questions to the petitioner's counsel on the record in an institutional matter, through the counsel, for the minute — a fossil provision from the era when proceedings were conversations rather than schedules — and Karis had found it in the third week of term and had been waiting four sessions for the question worth spending it on.

She had found it, she told the table afterward, in the code's own index, under a heading that had been left in place when the practice under it had died: *interrogatories, of counsel, at the seat*. Nine words of enabling text and a hundred and forty years since the last recorded use, which she had traced. The counsel had read the nine words twice and said that she would put one question and not two, because a provision nobody has invoked in a century survives being used once and does not survive being used twice, and Karis had agreed, and had then spent four sessions declining to spend it — through the empanelment, through the preliminary ruling, through the exhibits session and two scheduling sittings — because the whole value of one question is what you do not ask with it.

The counsel put it, at the fourth session, in the exact words Karis had written on a card: *What evidence, if any, would defeat the petition?*

The room understood the question before Jent answered it. Cael, at the respondent's table, watched forty benches of registry-literate people arrive at it in a wave that reached the back of the hall about two seconds after it reached the front, and watched the clerk's pen stop, and watched Ilsev, in the evaluation seat, look up from her file for the first time that session and keep looking up.

Jent rose. He did not consult his clerk. He did not ask the seat for leave to take the question in writing, which he was entitled to do and which the counsel had told the table he would probably do.

He answered it courteously, precisely, without a pause, in the voice of a man stating the weather, and the clerk entered it in the minute, and the minute was a public record, and the sentence entered the registry's archive forever:

"None, Advocate. The petition doesn't turn on evidence. It turns on discretion."

He sat down.

That was the whole of it. No qualification, no softening, no procedural cushion of the kind available to him in six different forms — a man asked a direct question about the instrument he was holding, answering it accurately, at the correct height, because lying to a seat was not something he did and because the true answer cost him nothing at all.

The winning-doesn't-matter sentence — the one Karis had drawn as a corridor, the one the seal had confirmed in a paragraph, the one Vastin had been told in two lines and Seln had audited from the other chair for fifteen years — finally said out loud, in a room with a transcript, by the man sent to conduct it, as a courtesy, because he had been asked.

Karis wrote it down. She did not need to; the clerk had it; but she wrote it in her own hand in the notebook's front matter, beside the fielding clause and the squad clause and the stratum note and the line she could not place, and she dated it, and she closed the book on it with the expression Cael had learned to read as *finding entered*.

He asked her about it on the steps, afterward, because he wanted the researcher's reading and not his own.

"Was that worth the provision?"

"It was worth four sessions and a hundred and forty years," Karis said. "Whether it's worth anything else depends entirely on who reads the minute in ten years' time, and I've stopped pretending I know." She put the notebook away. "He didn't have to say it. That's the part I'll be sitting with. He could have said 'the petitioner declines to speculate' and been correct and been finished. He answered because he was asked and because he thought the answer was obvious." She went down two steps. "It *is* obvious. To him. He's been in this machine for thirty years and it has never once occurred to him that the sentence he just said is the most frightening one on the continent."

---

The venue rail, the confluence, the last evening of the meet, the hall emptying below.

Lira had the bout sheet in her hand — Rooke's four words at its foot, the Rank 8's flags, the three-to-one that was the best loss of her life. Cael had the served demand in his, the production order, the paper an officer had walked a hall to deliver.

She looked at both papers, one in each of their hands, for a while.

"One of these is the best night of my life," she said.

"Noted," Cael said.

Neither of them said the other half. The hall went on emptying. The river went on outside, wide and slow and the color of weak tea, past a town where a regional Iron title had been decided and a Silver bout had been fought and a registry officer had walked a gallery's length to serve paper at the right hour.

She folded the bout sheet. Carefully. Along its creases, squared, with more attention than the folding needed — the care she gave to things she intended to keep, and the care, Cael charted without being able to help it, of a woman who was already, somewhere below decision, beginning to say goodbye to a page she had waited three years to be handed.

# Chapter 3 — Grounds

The response went down the bluff road on the eleventh day of the fourteen, three days inside it, in a document wallet with the academy's seal on the flap, and it weighed — Bracken had it weighed at the counter, because a registrar who had spent thirty years processing enrollment defenses wanted the number for his own private ledger — eleven pounds and four ounces.

Cael watched the courier take the switchbacks the way he had watched a courier take them a year and a half ago with a roster in his saddlebag, and catalogued the difference: not one rider; three, because a filing of that weight traveled in two cases, and the academy had hired the escort itself. The registry's ordinary post would have carried it. Withrow had declined it. "When we answer under seal," she had said, at the counter, "we answer under escort. Let them see the weight arrive."

The weight was Karis's, and it was the whole documentary architecture of four years deployed as a standing army: the Greyvane hearing's ruling and transcript; the assay clause's provenance, traced through the standardization directive's repeal schedules item by item, the finding she had first stated at Greyvane in her flattest register — *the clause was not repealed; it was orphaned* — now buttressed by Halcenvane's counsel's independent reconstruction and Prynn's fifty-page index and two years of renewals; the semester evaluations, all four, with Gault's panel minutes; the inspection year's finding, whole; the tournament's fielding-clause advisory, the same paragraph the instrument had quoted, quoted back; and, at the response's spine, forty pages in her own hand on the evaluation-integrity provisions, their history, their every prior invocation, and the plain textual fact that of the ninety-one instances the registry had ever recorded, none had been invoked against what a practitioner is — the seven that touched individuals touched conduct.

Bracken verified every page — every page; he read eleven pounds of filing in two nights at the counter with the fire-watch's lamps lit by his own authority a second time, and initialed each leaf, because a registrar's initial on a leaf was a promise about the leaf. The counsel signed the cover sheet beneath Withrow's name. Karis's name appeared nowhere on the filing, at her own insistence — "I'm not the respondent; I'm the archive" — and Cael, reading the finished thing at the records hall table the night before it went, had entered in the log the only review it needed: *it is the best document anyone has ever written about me. It is perfect. Karis says it is perfect and she has never once been wrong about a document's quality. I believe her. I believe the corridor too. Both things fit in the same hand tonight, and I don't know how long they will.*

---

The registry's answering notice came back in nine days, and it set the term's geometry in the flat type the season would learn to read like weather.

*Proceedings convened at the registry seat at Ostrand. Sessions on a term-length schedule, first session the fourth Fifth-day of the month; ruling before term's end. Evaluation seat empaneled from the district's senior evaluators. Procurator of record for the petitioner: Jent. Presiding seat: per seal.*

"Per seal," said Karis, reading it at the table.

"It means the presiding registrar is identified in the record by the seat's seal, not by name." Bracken had the notice in his hands and was looking at the last line with the expression he had worn at the joint-service form — a registrar meeting a protocol he knew existed and had never seen used. "It's permitted. It's in the code, for proceedings the registry designates as institutional rather than personal — the theory being that the seat speaks for the registry, not for the person sitting in it." He set the notice down. "I've never seen it invoked. In thirty years I have never once seen a proceeding conducted by a chair with no name."

"So the person who rules on my enrollment," Cael said, "is a seal."

"The person who rules on your enrollment is whoever holds the seal that day," said Bracken. "And the record will show the seal."

*They've filed the face out of it,* the log got that night. *Vastin had a face. They all had faces. The presiding seat of the proceedings that decide whether I have a name has declined, by procedure, to have one. Note the grammar. The Compact's hand — the closing line on every instrument — has always been a metaphor. They're making it a person. Or unmaking one.*

---

Procurator Jent appeared exactly once before the first session, and the season gave him that one appearance and no more, because the whole architecture of the man was that he did not require more.

The coach took the bluff road at first light with all five of them in it, and the ride down was the term's first council conducted in the register the delegation had perfected on a season of wagon benches: nobody saying the thing, everybody circling it. Lira had the Silver drill-book and did not open it. Brom sat with his forearms on his knees, which was how he sat toward things. Karis had the response's summary in her lap and reread it the way a fighter rereads a scouting sheet, and at the ferry she said, to the river, "He'll concede it. Whoever he is. He'll concede the whole filing in the first minute and I want everyone braced for what that feels like, because it will feel like winning and it isn't." Seln, at the coach's end, had the office's cross-index of the instrument on his knee — forty pages, every clause mapped to every provision it invoked and every provision to its history, produced in three nights, the best document about the instrument that existed — and he said nothing at all, and Cael, who had watched him say nothing for two years, read the nothing as a man who had cross-indexed instruments like this one before, from the other side, and knew what a conceded case was for.

Ostrand's registry seat was the wool exchange at the top of the market street — four steps up to a stone plinth, the lot positions still cut into the selling floor, lanolin in the cold — and the building had acquired, for the proceedings, a second door: a side entrance with a registry officer at it and a list, because the continent's most-watched enrollee was attending his own proceedings and the ordinary front step was not, the officer explained without apology, built for the correspondents.

The procedural conference was the code's ordinary preliminary — counsel of record on both sides, the schedule confirmed, the exhibit lists exchanged — and it convened in a borrowed room at the registry seat in Ostrand on a grey morning, the five of them having come down the bluff road in the delegation coach because Withrow had decided, and stated, that the enrollee would attend every session of his own proceedings and the academy's principal witnesses with him. The room was a district office's meeting chamber: a table, chairs, the registry's plain sigil on the wall, a window that looked down the market street to the wharfs, where three streets back the registry station's door — the door Lira had once preferred to a hall — stood four buildings from nothing.

Jent was already seated when they entered, and rose, and the rising was the first datum: a courtesy exact to the quarter-inch, neither warm nor cold, the gesture of a man who had risen for a thousand opposing parties and had long since found the correct height.

Fifties. Colorless in the way of good clerks — grey coat, grey hair, a face the instrument could find no purchase on because there was nothing on it being managed; the face simply *was* what it did. He greeted the counsel by name and Withrow by title and Karis — this the table noted — as "Advocate," which she was not, and which he had plainly chosen because it was the most accurate available word for the person who had written the filing.

"The response," he said, when the schedule was confirmed and the exhibit lists exchanged, and he touched the eleven pounds on the table beside him with two fingers, the way a man touches a well-made thing. "I've read it twice. The best-documented enrollment I've reviewed in thirty years. Genuinely." He said the last word to Karis, directly, without flattery, a professional's plain acknowledgment of another's craft. "The provenance work on the standardization directive's repeal schedules alone would have settled any of the four prior challenges in an afternoon."

"Thank you," said Karis, who did not thank people for compliments, and who Cael saw had thanked him because she had understood, half a second before the rest of the table, what came next.

The exhibit lists went across the table before that, and the exchange took four minutes and told Cael more about the petitioner than the twenty that followed. Jent's list ran to nine items. The academy's ran to two hundred and six. He accepted the two hundred and six without reading past the first page, initialed the receipt, and slid his nine across, and his nine were: the instrument; the convening minute; the registry's certified copy of the assay clause; the standardization directive; Vastin's advisory of the prior year; the adjudication office's referred tournament file; and three certified extracts of the registry code — chapter fourteen, the integrity provisions, and the schedule of the registry's discretionary powers.

Cael read the list upside down and did the arithmetic that had become his first reflex in a room with paper in it. Six of the nine were the Compact's own documents. Two were the academy's. One was Umber's. Not a single item on the petitioner's list had been produced to prove anything. Every item was there to be *cited*.

"Nine exhibits," the counsel said, in the tone of a woman confirming a delivery.

"Nine," Jent agreed. "I may add the response itself, if the seat prefers the whole of it in the record rather than the summary. I'd rather it were whole. It's better law than the summary makes it look."

Cael put the whole instrument on him for the rest of the conference, and ran it the way he had run it on every official who had ever held a decision about him: the hands first, then the breath, then the eyes at the moment of the sentence that ought to cost something, then the half-beat before an answer that a man spends deciding how much to give. Four measures, four years of practice, and the readings came back in order — hands still, breath level, eyes on the document not the person, no half-beat at all — and the fourth null was the one that mattered. There was no deciding. The answers arrived at the speed of the questions because nothing in the man was choosing between versions of them.

"It isn't in dispute." Jent said it without malice, without emphasis, without anything at all — a man stating the weather. "The documentation is conceded. The provision is conceded. The petition doesn't require either to be defective, and I'd be wasting the seat's time and yours to pretend otherwise." He squared the exhibit list. "The petition turns on the integrity question and the re-evaluation remedy. Those are matters of the registry's discretion. I'll argue them on the schedule, and you'll answer, and the seat will rule." He stood, at the correct height. "I look forward to the sessions. Chancellor. Counsel. Advocate." A pause, and the only sentence he addressed to the enrollee: "Sir." And he was gone, at a clerk's unhurried pace, leaving the conference exactly as long as the code required and not a minute longer.

The counsel's own read came in the coach on the way back, unprompted, delivered to the window in the voice of an advocate revising twenty-six years of assumptions in real time. "He's not there to argue. I've faced men sent to argue — they have a tell, a place they lean, a clause they love. He has none. He's there to *conduct* — to move the petition through the schedule at the code's pace and let the seat do what the seat was constituted to do." She turned from the window. "I have never lost a case to a man who didn't want to win it. I'm not certain I've ever faced one."

Seln, from the coach's end, without opening his eyes: "You've faced hundreds. They're called clerks. You've just never been on the other side of the counter."

Cael had read him across the table the whole time — the instrument at full attention, the way it had read Vastin across a records-hall room and Umber across a conference chamber — and the reading came back, that night, in the log, as the finding the season would spend itself confirming.

*Coss wanted to win. You could read the wanting on him from across a street; it was the whole man. Vastin wanted to evaluate, and the wanting was his spine. Umber wanted the mark to be true. Every official I have ever read had an appetite, and the appetite was the seam.*

*This one's been sent to win, and the difference is worse. There's no appetite to read. He complimented Karis honestly and conceded our whole case without a flicker and it cost him nothing, because none of it was ever his. He's a hand. Somebody else is the arm. And the arm has learned, somewhere in the last four years, that the way to beat a boy who reads people is to send someone with nothing on him to read.*

The response's drafting had its own scene, and the scene was the annex — Karis's room with the lock, the four days, the door shut and the assistant at the outer table filtering the intake's curiosity, and Cael reading every draft at the room's one window because the arrangement required it.

He watched her build it the way he had watched Hesk build a fiddle: in an order that looked like inspiration and was procedure. The Greyvane ruling first, because it was the keel. Then the provenance work, laid in — her own two-year finding, the counsel's reconstruction, Prynn's index, three routes to one conclusion, fastened together so that no one of them carried the load alone. She let him find one error, on the third day, and he understood, about an hour after finding it, that the letting had been deliberate.

It was in the renewals table — the four semester evaluations set out in a grid, panel, date, finding, seal — and the third row's date was the date of the panel's *convening* rather than the date its minute was sealed, which were nine days apart in the spring of the inspection year because Gault had held the minute open for the visiting officer's signature. Cael found it because he had been present at both, and had logged both, and the log was the only place on the continent where the nine days existed.

"Nine days," he said.

"Show me." She did not look up from the provenance pages. He showed her. She read the row, and the log entry he had brought up from the residence to prove it, and then she took the whole table out of the filing and rebuilt it in front of him with the sealing dates and a footnote explaining the nine days, which cost her an hour she did not have.

"You could have footnoted it without rebuilding it," he said.

"I could." Karis squared the new leaf. "And then the petitioner's clerk finds the nine days, and files a single line noting that the respondent's own table is inconsistent with its own exhibits, and the seat believes nothing else in two hundred and six items for the rest of the term." She capped the pen. "There is no such thing as a small error in a document about a person. Also — " and this was the part he thought about afterward — "I gave you the renewals table to read first on the second morning. Out of order. Ahead of the keel. Because you are the only person in this building who was in both rooms, and I have never yet built anything that didn't get better for being read by the one witness who can't be replaced."

Then the evaluation-integrity section, forty pages, which she wrote in a single night and reread in the morning with the expression she gave hostile documents, hunting her own errors the way she hunted Jent's. "There are none," she said, at noon, closing it. "I've looked twice. I'll look once more with the counsel and then it goes." She rubbed ink off her second knuckle. "It's the best thing I've ever written. I'd like that on the record, and I'd like it on the record that I know exactly what it's worth, which is the record."

"Both minuted," said Cael.

"You've picked that up from the counsel."

"I've picked up that somebody should be keeping the minute. She's keeping the registry's. I'm keeping ours."

Karis looked at the observation notebook in his hands — the Halcenvane section, two years of small facts, her own pages thicker than anyone's — and did the thing she had started doing on the road home from Norhold and had not once remarked on: she handed him the draft's last page to read first, unasked, and went back to the window while he read.

---

Lira found him at the north wall on the evening the response went, and she came with cargo, because her hospitality always traveled with it — two mugs and a question.

"Brom said a word on the stair the night of the records hall," she said. "*Corridor.* He's said it four times since, to himself, at the striking frame. Brom doesn't repeat things." She handed him the mug. "So I want the word explained by the person Karis explained it to. In small pieces. The way you do."

He gave it to her in small pieces, at the rail, with the valley going grey: the six steps, the doors that opened one way, the procedure for no-bin, the working definition of asset-restriction — Withrow's version, the one about every door on the continent closing at once by paperwork. Lira listened the way she listened to scouting sheets, three reads, and when he was done she looked at the river a while.

"At Fenmark they had a room for it," she said. "The reading-out. Two assessors, a form, my name on the form, and a door. I've told you about the door." She turned the mug. "I used to think the door was the cruelty. Being put out of it. It wasn't. The cruelty was that the form was *correct* — every line filled in properly, every procedure followed, my Copper certification true on its face — and I stood there fourteen years old knowing the form was right and the room was wrong and having no word for how both could be true." She looked at him. "Karis has the word now. Corridor. I've been looking for it for six years."

"And?"

"And I got out of mine by walking into a scrapyard and building a ledger. Nobody put me on a road to it. I found it." She drank. "So when your friend the researcher says every door opens one way — I hear her. I've been in the building. I just also know there's usually a yard out the back, and nobody puts it on the form."

She left him the mug and the sentence, and the log got both that night, and beneath them the reading: *she's already looking for the yard. She won't say so at the table until it's a finding. But she's looked at the corridor once and started counting exits, because that is what a scrapyard teaches you, and the rest of us learned in libraries.*

---

Withrow's stake was the part of the term nobody had written down yet, and she wrote it herself, in her office, on the evening after the conference, with the door shut and the enrollee across the desk and the charter framed above the file cases where it had hung for two years to be pointed at.

"Sit. This will take longer than four minutes and you will want to have been here for all of it."

She did not reassure him. She had never once reassured him, and he had catalogued the never-once across two years as the most reliable thing about her. What she did was the thing she had done in this office at the start of the inspection year: she showed him the ledger, both columns, in the registry-official's flat register, and let the columns speak.

"The academy is the named respondent. Understand what that is in registry grammar. It is not a courtesy of address — it is the institution's standing placed under the same seal as your enrollment. If the proceedings find against the enrollment, they find against Halcenvane's judgment in executing it, and the finding enters our charter file, and a chartered institution with an adverse finding in its file is an institution whose next inspection is not eleven days of declining to find anything." She laid it out with her hands flat. "That's one column. Here is what fills it. Eighteen years off the qualifying lists. Three years to third on the continent. Enrollment up a third. A Silver program chartered. A research standing the council voted its first budget for. Every one of those is a thing I built my tenure toward and every one of them is now on the same table as your name, because I put it there, in the autumn, on a roster, by declining to commit fraud." A pause. "I would do it again. I am telling you the price so that you never have to wonder whether I know it."

She got up while she said the next of it, and went to the file cases under the charter, and came back with the academy's charter file itself — the buff folder every chartered institution kept and no enrollee had ever seen — and opened it on the desk between them, facing him, which was not a courtesy she was obliged to extend and which he catalogued as one of the four or five things she had ever done that he could not have predicted.

"Read it. You're in it."

He read it. Twenty-two years of an institution's life in forty leaves: the charter's grant, three inspections, two adverse notes from before her tenure — a fire-watch deficiency, a records lapse — each with its remedy and its clearance stamped beside it, the qualifying-list entries, the tournament placement. And, at the file's current leaf, in the registry's flat type, an entry dated eight days ago: *proceedings pending; institution named respondent; enrollment basis under challenge.* No remedy beside it. No clearance. A blank column, waiting.

"That entry stays," Withrow said. "Whatever the ruling says. A cleared adverse note is still a note; every inspector for the next twenty years reads the file from the front. The best outcome available to this academy is that the blank column eventually says *sustained*, and the entry itself never leaves." She closed the file. "That is the cost, stated exactly, and I want you to have seen the actual leaf rather than my description of it, because you are a person who does not believe descriptions."

"And the other column."

"The other column is the reason I'm in this chair." She sat back. "I read the Greyvane hearing transcript four times before I ever met you. I read it as a registry officer, which is what I was for twenty years before I was anything else, and I read it looking for the seam — the place where a clever advocate had made a bad clause look good. There wasn't one. The law was on your side then. I have read the instrument they served us eleven times now and I have read the corridor Karis drew, and I will tell you what the registry officer in me sees, which is that the law is on your side *now*, on every point that can be argued, and that Karis is right about the doors." She looked at him across the desk with the whole load-bearing calm of her tenure. "I am choosing to keep believing that matters. Make a note of the word *choosing*."

He made a note of the word choosing. He made it in the observation notebook, in her section, under two years of small facts that had assembled into the portrait of a woman he had once catalogued as ambition and now catalogued as something he did not have a single word for, and beside the word he wrote the finding the office had produced: *she knows it's a corridor. She's staying in it anyway, on purpose, with the institution's whole standing, and she told me why in one word so I'd have it when the doors start closing. Choosing. That's not a legal position. It's a person deciding what she'll have been.*

"Go and train," Withrow said. "Rooke tells me the Silver floor has begun to interest you. Good. Whatever this term costs, spend it standing up."

---

The term's geometry laid itself out across the following week the way a season's always did — in postings, schedules, and the small print of other people's calendars — and Cael charted it from the covered walk's board and the residence table and the rail, because a term was a machine and machines had to be read before they moved.

Sessions at Ostrand: Fifth-days as the seat requires, the calendar the seat's own, the ruling before term's end. Bracken counted the docket and made it eleven sittings if the term ran honest. Lira's Silver bracket: the program's first meet in six weeks, on the road, Rooke's continental drills to be tested against actual Silver opposition for the first time in her life. Brom's review: the last Fifth-day of term, district seat, standard panel — the same day, Karis noted, reading the two dockets side by side, as the ruling. "They could sit in the same building," she said. "The review that gives him his tier and the ruling that decides whether you have one." She wrote both dates in the notebook, one above the other, and looked at them for a while. "The registry doesn't know how to be ironic. It's just efficient. Somehow that's worse."

And the re-evaluation date — the compelled evaluation the whole instrument existed to produce — a blank. The proceedings would fill it. The notice said so, in the flat type: *date to be set upon the seat's ruling.* A blank on a schedule, waiting, the way a blank had once waited on an exhibition board in Norhold's outer court for someone to volunteer — except that nobody would be volunteering for this one. The seat would fill it. The blank was the corridor's last door, and it already had a hinge.

He counted the watchers at dusk that week, from the north wall, and this time he placed them.

Two at the bluff road. The same two, or the same grade: grey coats, good boots, the whole patient economy of men paid by the year. Older than last year's. He had been carrying the observation since the first day of term without its conclusion, and the conclusion arrived on the wall with the valley going grey below, the way conclusions arrived when the instrument had enough data: they were more senior because the junior work was done. Four years of rotation men learning his habits, his hours, his floor schedules, the faces he ate with — all of that had been the gathering, and gathering was junior work, and it had ended. These men did not need to learn anything. They knew where he slept and when he trained and who came up the bluff road under seal. They were the grade that got sent when the file was complete and the office was waiting for the next entry.

*They're not gathering information anymore,* he wrote that night. *They're waiting for a status change.*

*The proceedings will make one. A term of sittings and a ruling. The men at the road already know what the ruling says. Everyone does, except the paper.*

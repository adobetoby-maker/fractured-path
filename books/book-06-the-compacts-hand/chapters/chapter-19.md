# Chapter 19 — What Leaving Costs

The council convened in the records hall the night after the ruling, in the room the delegation used when it wanted a table and no witnesses, and the room had been made ready by people who did not know what they were making it ready for: the long table wiped down, five chairs and a sixth carried in from the corridor, one lamp trimmed and set at the table's center, where a lamp goes when the business of an evening is reading.

Cael counted the room in on arrival, because he counted every room in and had never once found the habit wasted. Four file cases along the north wall with the term's whole defense inside them, eleven pounds of true record, closed. The shuttered window on the quadrangle, and past the shutter a campus with nine days of term left going about its evening in complete ignorance. Bracken's spare inkstand on the sideboard, where Bracken kept it and where nobody else was permitted to move it. And in front of his own chair, the ruling's copy: four paragraphs, a seal, and the attestation line at the foot that every binding instrument on the continent closed with, which he had read ten thousand times in Karis's citations without ever once hearing it as a sentence about a hand.

It was the council the whole term had been earning, and Cael set the frame himself, opening it, because the arithmetic had to be laid flat before it could be priced.

"Three roads. I'll say all three plainly and then we price them." He had no papers; the papers were spent; he had the ruling's copy and nothing else. "One: submit. Stand in front of the Arbiter in five weeks, be found unclassifiable — because I am, that's the founding fact — and hand the machinery the architecture it has waited two hundred years to file. The re-evaluation isn't a test I might pass. It's a delivery. I'd be walking myself back under the layer that couldn't finish the job the day I Kindled, and this time it has five weeks to prepare and no assay clause in the way." He let it sit. "Two: refuse inside the walls. Decline the re-evaluation, stay enrolled until they revoke it, be expelled on the machine's verb, the machine's record — *respondent refused compelled evaluation* — asset-restriction executing on schedule, Halcenvane bleeding for having harbored me, everyone at this table's file marked for association. That's the machine getting its expulsion and its confession in one. Three." He looked around the table. "Leave. Resign — before the re-evaluation date, before the revocation, before any of it executes. Convert their expulsion into our refusal. Cross the one line the registry cannot follow, because past it there's no registry to execute in."

Nobody spoke for the length of time it took the lamp to gutter once and steady.

"Price one," Lira said. "Properly. Not the shape of it — the bill."

So he priced it, and Karis priced it beside him, because half of it was in her hand and not his. The re-evaluation would be conducted at the district station under standard protocol, which meant a frame, an evaluator of record, and a procedure with no discretion in it anywhere: the instrument reads, the instrument returns, the seat files what the instrument returned. Karis had the chapter open and read the failure branch aloud in her flat citation voice, the way she had read every provision of the term — nullification of enrollment; suspension of practitioner status; and then the third clause, which was the one that took the room's air, because the first two only described a boy losing a school. *Asset-restriction.* Standing frozen. Records sealed to inquiry. Sponsorships voided at the seal. Travel endorsements withdrawn — and the pre-restriction inventory already compiled and sitting in a district file, five offices' worth of it, because the machine did its mapping before it did its closing, always.

"They've inventoried Denvash," Cael said. "Hesk's shop is on it. The ninth category — instrument-maker's stock, held against an unclassified associate's obligations. Two polite men have visited that shop twice this term and asked nothing they didn't already know the answer to." He turned the ruling's copy a quarter turn on the table, which was the only idle motion he made all evening. "Road one ends with me standing in a frame handing the layer its answer, and Hesk's forty years of gauges listed on a form as security for it. That's the whole price of submitting. It isn't a bad outcome for me. It's a bad outcome for everyone whose name is written next to mine on any paper anywhere."

"And two," said Brom.

"Two is the same bill with an extra line." Karis did not need the chapter for that one. "Refusal inside the walls is a status the code has a word for, and the word attaches to the institution as well as the person. Halcenvane becomes an academy that harbored a respondent through a refused compelled evaluation. Withrow's charter goes to review. Bracken's counter gets audited by people who will find nothing and take eight months to find it. Every enrollee at this table picks up a file entry reading *association, active non-compliance proceedings*, which is a sentence that follows a certification around for thirty years and does its work quietly at every counter they ever stand at." She set the volume down. "The machine would prefer road two. It gets the expulsion, the record, the verb, and four files marked, and it gets all of it without having to write a single dishonest word."

"So the choice is one or three," Lira said.

"The choice was always one or three," Cael said. "Two is the road you take if you want to lose while looking like you fought. I understand the appeal. I've had four years of practice at wanting it."

"And the cache," said Karis, because someone had to put it away and it should be her. "We don't fight with it. I want that argued and closed tonight so nobody spends the next five weeks imagining we might. Evidence against the Arbiter protocol cannot be verified by any process that runs on Arbiters — Vastin said it, I've confirmed it, it's structural. File it and we prove nothing and burn Seln for nothing. It's not a weapon. It's a reason. It tells us the fight can't be won inside; it doesn't give us a way to win inside."

"Argue it anyway," said Lira. "I want to hear it lose out loud. If nobody argues for it tonight, somebody argues for it in three weeks on a bad night, and by then we'll have moved."

So Karis argued for it, against herself, for four minutes, at full strength, the way she had argued the Greyvane hearing's weakest branch to its end before discarding it. The cache went to the proceedings; the proceedings were compelled to receive it; the record entered two hundred years of reclassification orders against their own Kindling files. And then the room asked the only question that mattered, which was who verifies it — and the answer was an evaluation seat, and an evaluation seat runs on an Arbiter, and the Arbiter runs the protocol the documents accuse. "You would be asking the machine to certify a finding against a modification inside its own certifying function," she said. "There is no seat on this continent that can receive that finding and no instrument that can test it. It is not that we would lose the argument. It is that the argument has no venue. The only thing entering the cache accomplishes is telling the Compact exactly what an assessment clerk of fifteen years kept in a locked case, at which point there is one man alive who can be found and corrected, and they will find him, because finding him is the one part of the whole apparatus that works perfectly."

Seln said, from the corner, without opening his eyes: "The office would have me inside a fortnight. Less, if they wanted it done tidily." A pause. "I would like it entered that this is not the reason. I have been past caring about that for eleven years. The reason is that the files are the only true record of the thing and there is exactly one copy, and burning the copy to make a gesture in a room is not what a keeper does with a record."

"You don't file a grievance with the instrument that forged the record," Cael said. "You leave its jurisdiction."

Nobody argued the frame. The debate had been had, across the term, in pieces — Lira's first-week question, Brom's three sentences, the road cut, the annex. What remained was the pricing. Lira had said months ago that she needed no different bout, only to be faster inside the same one; the term had found the floor under it, which was that being faster inside the same one worked up until the room decided what you were, and after that the only move left was a different room. The pricing was what the council was for, because a resignation that cost nothing earned nothing, and every stake the term had built came back to the table now to be paid in its owner's own voice.

---

Brom's turn came first, because the district seat, with the registry's flawless indifference to timing, delivered it mid-council.

The knock came at the records hall's door at the ninth bell and it was Bracken, who did not attend councils and would not have entered one, standing in the corridor with his coat still on and an envelope held level in both hands the way he held everything that had come off a counter. A district officer had ridden up the bluff at dusk with it. The advancement review had been docketed for the last Fifth-day of term, four days out — four days after the ruling, a different seat, the registry giving one man his tier in the same week it decided his friend had none and never noticing it had written a joke. But the review's *finding* had come early and hand-delivered, because a finding this clean did not wait for its own hearing, and the district seat had wanted it in the practitioner's hands before the term's other news reached the district seat.

"I'd have brought it in the morning," Bracken said, to the room and to nobody. "It seemed to me that a man should get the best paper of his life on the day it was written and not the day after." He put it on the table at Brom's place and went out and shut the door, and the shutting was as much as Bracken had ever said about anything.

Brom read it.

Advancement to Iron-tier. Found. Recommended. The season record entered, the continental title cited, the panel's language the driest possible affirmation of the least surprising outcome the district had processed in years. His family's whole ledger, settled in one paragraph — the number the Velmere household had priced a big careful boy out of at fourteen, awarded, on a form, with a line for his signature and a date.

And appended, in flawless registry prose, a guidance note:

*The seat notes, for the practitioner's consideration, that advancement suitability weighs continued association with enrollees under active non-compliance proceedings.*

Bureaucratic. Deniable. Unmistakable. Everything he had ever wanted, priced at Cael.

Cael read the note upside down across the table, because he read everything upside down across tables and had for four years, and ran the thing it actually was. It threatened nothing. It alleged nothing. It did not withhold the advancement; the advancement was found, and *found* was a word the registry did not take back. It simply observed, for the practitioner's consideration, that suitability was a thing that got weighed, and that association was among the things that got weighed in it — an accurate statement of registry practice, entered by an officer who could have told a sympathetic peer, without lying, that he had done the young man a courtesy by being frank with him. There was nobody to be angry at in the whole paragraph. That was the paragraph's craft.

Brom read the finding once, completely, in the manner he reserved for costly things. He read the guidance note. Then he set the whole thing on the table, face-up, and turned it a half-turn so that it was the right way up for the table and not for himself, and pushed it out to the middle beside the lamp, so that everyone could price it too — the number and the invoice, in the same paragraph, in the same flawless hand.

Nobody reached for it. The lamp put its light across the seal and the signature line and the blank date beside it, and the room read it in silence at its own speeds, and Cael watched the reading go round: Lira's mouth going flat; Karis's hand not moving toward a pen, which from Karis was a page of commentary; Seln's eyes opening once and closing again.

"Say it," Lira said at last. "Whatever it is. Say it before somebody at this table says something kind, because if anybody says something kind you'll agree with them to end it."

Brom looked at the paper for the length of time he looked at things he intended to remember.

Then he asked Karis one question.

"How many boxes is your research?"

Karis, who had understood the question before he finished it: "Three."

Brom stood up. "Then I'm carrying three boxes."

That was all of it. Nobody offered him the argument — not Cael, who had spent a month rehearsing a version of it at windows, and who understood, watching the big careful hands square the finding's edges to the table before letting go of it, that offering it now would be a way of asking Brom to say the rest out loud, and that Brom had chosen a sentence about boxes precisely so that the rest would not have to be said out loud by anybody.

The advancement was never answered. No signature, no refusal filed, no covering letter to the district seat; the form stayed on the table face-up as its own reply and was still there in the morning when the hall was opened, and somebody on the staff, not knowing what it was, squared it with the other papers and left it. The second Velmere envelope, unopened four weeks on the small table in his room, was answered the same way and in the same act, and it stayed unopened, because he did not open it, and the three boxes were the letter back.

Cael made one note that night and made it in the log rather than aloud: that the family had offered the ledger back in one week and the registry had offered the number in another, and that Brom had let both offers stand on tables until they went quiet, and that a man who does not argue with what he is refusing is refusing it at a depth arguments cannot reach.

---

The rest of the pricing ran in order, each stake named precisely and paid in its owner's voice, and the naming was deliberate — Cael made each of them say the thing whole, because a price nobody says out loud is a price somebody re-opens later on a bad road.

Lira: the Silver program, named for what it was — the first ladder ever built toward her, the drills the built version got, the bout sheet she had slept beside, the trajectory a Silver Rank 8 had told her was *there* — and declined in the sentence that completed the arc Zerin had opened at a Norhold board. "I said I wanted to become something they haven't seen yet." She said it evenly, a woman reading a bout's honest arithmetic. "Turns out it doesn't live here. It never was going to live anywhere with a seal on the door. The scrapyard was closer." She folded the thought the way she had folded the bout sheet at the confluence — carefully, kept. "I've said goodbye to a page before. I know how it's done."

"Say the whole bill," Cael said. "You made me say mine."

"Fine." She counted it on the table with one finger, item by item, in the register she used for flags. "A rated program under an institution's seal, which no scrapyard fighter on this continent gets offered. A drill-book I'd have paid four years for. A clerk at a table writing my numbers down where a district can read them. Rooke's file, which is the only file about me anybody has ever kept that was trying to make me better instead of make me fit. A Silver assessor who stopped writing in the third exchange." She sat back. "And a bracket in two years that I would have been in, and might have won, and would have wanted to win in front of people, which is the part I'd rather not have said out loud." She looked at Cael. "There. Priced. Now stop making me do arithmetic and let me pack."

Karis: the forfeiture named precisely — the ranking, the standing, the budget, the room with the lock, the assistant she was building, the first institution that had ever resourced her properly — and her answer was the researcher's, delivered without a flicker of the drama the moment invited. "I began packing the compiled materials two days ago. The decision, for me, was a finding, and I entered it when I made it. I don't re-litigate findings." She looked at the boxes she had already, it emerged, roped. "Three boxes. Brom's counted them. The room was a good room. I'll build another one somewhere without a registry to lock it."

Cael made her price it anyway, and she permitted it, and did it in the order a bibliography goes in rather than the order a heart does. The Iron-tier Rank 3, which was a certification and would survive; the *research standing*, which was not a certification and would not; the archive privileges; the annex allocation with the lock on the door, the first door in her life that had locked on her work and not against it; the budget code Bracken had entered under her name at a counter while she watched. And the request to Greyvane's archive for the founding-era compilation's oldest signatures, sent under that code, filling in three weeks, due before midwinter, addressed to an academy she would not be at.

"That one I mind," she said, in exactly the voice she used to say a citation was incomplete. "Prynn's people are slow and exact. Someone will fill it correctly and carefully and put it on a shelf at Halcenvane under a researcher's name that no longer exists on any roster. Four generations of signatures, certified, copied by hand, and nobody to read them." She wrote something in the notebook, briefly. "I'll write and redirect it to Denvash. Hesk can hold it. It will get read eventually. Everything true gets read eventually — that's the whole reason to keep records at all, and if I stopped believing it tonight I'd have wasted my life."

The assistant she did not price, because she declined to price a person. She said only that she would spend two of the nine remaining days finishing what the girl needed finished, and would write her a letter of competence in her own hand under no institution's seal, which was worth less at a counter and more everywhere else, and that she would say nothing to her about the reason, because a second-year's file did not need the word *association* in it on Karis's account.

Seln, asked nothing, volunteered one sentence, in the counter voice, and it was the truest thing said at the table: "I resigned fifteen years ago. I just never filed it."

Lira nearly asked the follow-up. Cael watched her decide not to, which took her about a second and a half, and afterward he entered the second and a half in the log as the evening's most encouraging datum, because a year ago she would have asked it, and Seln a year ago would have answered it with something that was true and told nobody anything.

---

Ephram was brought in at the council's end, alone, by arrangement, and was told the truth of the departure and nothing of the cache — which was the line the table had drawn before he came up the stair and which Cael drew again in his own head, deliberately, on the threshold, because lines you do not re-draw at the door are lines you cross by accident.

He listened to it standing, and did not sit when Brom moved a chair for him, and when it was finished he asked the practical questions in the cohort's order: when, whether Withrow knew, whether Rooke would be told before or after the boards read it, whether the five of them were going to the same place and whether that place was east. He did not ask why. Later, on the stair, he told Cael that he had stopped needing to be told why about six months into a year of watching a building make up its mind, and that anyone who had watched that year and still needed the why had not been watching.

Then he asked for one thing before they went.

"You're going to walk out of this academy in a week," he said, "and I have watched you hold back inside its walls for a year while it decided what you are, and before you go I want the thing I've been waiting for since the semester evaluation I made myself the fool about. One sanctioned bout. Full-earnest. Show me straight." He looked at Cael. "You owe the Crown yard one honest fight before you leave it. I'm collecting."

"Rooke has to sanction it."

"Rooke will sanction it." Ephram said it without any particular weight, the way a man states a thing he has already tested. "He's been waiting for somebody to ask him for a piece of paper he can post. He can't say goodbye and he can't say nothing, so he'll do the third thing, which is a schedule."

The vote came after, and it took the time a vote takes when every stake in it has already been paid on the page in the owner's own voice, which is very little time. Five hands. Cael counted them, because he counted things, and because he wanted the number entered accurately in a log that would be read on a road with no institutions in it: five, and Seln's last, and last because the man had spent nineteen postings never raising his hand first at anything.

---

Withrow was informed alone, in her office, by Cael, and she received it standing.

The office was as it had been for two years — the file cases, the framed charter above them, the desk with nothing on it that was not in use — except that the term's correspondence had colonized the long side table in stacks that had a system Cael could read at a glance and would not have been able to read two years ago: petitions, responses, guild inquiries, and one stack, the smallest, of letters from other chancellors, which he understood without being told were the ones that had cost her the most to answer.

She heard the whole shape out without interrupting, standing at the desk's end with her hands at her sides, the respondent chancellor learning her academy's defense would end by resignation.

Her registry-trained arithmetic ran it in front of him, because she owed him the arithmetic and it was the only comfort her register could offer, which was to be exact. "The resignation protects Halcenvane. The proceedings become moot — there is no enrollment to re-evaluate, no respondent institution to find against, no charter file entry, because you cannot revoke an enrollment that has been surrendered. The asset-restriction cannot execute against a person outside the registry's jurisdiction. You forfeit nothing that was not already forfeit under the ruling, and you take the academy off the same seal as your name, which is the thing I could not ask you to do and the thing you are doing anyway." She set the ruling's copy down. "As registry arithmetic, it is the correct answer. I have checked it four times, the way I check everything about you, and it is correct."

"You checked it four times before I came up."

"I checked it four times the night the ruling landed," she said. "It is my office to know the respondent's best remaining move before the respondent does. It is not my office to recommend it, because a chancellor who recommends resignation to an enrollee under proceedings has done the petitioner's work for him at no charge, and I would have had to enter that in my own ledger, and I keep that ledger the way Bracken keeps his." She squared the copy to the desk's edge. "So I have known the answer for four days and said nothing, and waited to see whether you would arrive at it, and you have arrived at it, and I am now permitted to tell you that it is correct. That is the whole of what the office allows me, and I want you to understand that I have used every inch of it."

Then the other column, unperformed, because she did not perform things.

"I spent my tenure building an institution worth measuring. You were the best case it ever held — not the most decorated; Brom's your decorated one, and Lira's your reclaimed one, and they're both worth a chancellor's whole career. You were the *hardest*, and an institution is only ever as good as the hardest thing it can hold correctly, and we held you correctly, on the text, for two years, until an office two hundred years older than my charter decided the text wasn't the point." She looked at the framed charter above the file cases. "*Left* is the machine's verb and you are taking it out of the machine's mouth, and I approve, as a registry officer, of the maneuver."

"There's a version where you fight it," Cael said. "Charter review, guild allies, four years of it. You'd lose. But you'd be on record losing."

"There is," she agreed. "I have costed it. It ends with this academy's charter in review for four years, three hundred enrollees taught by a faculty answering audits, and the same outcome for you in the first six months of it." She did not soften the next part, which was her particular courtesy. "I would have done it anyway if it bought you anything. It buys you nothing, and it spends three hundred people to buy me a feeling. I have been a registry officer for twenty years and I know exactly what that transaction is called, and I am not going to make it and then describe it to myself as courage."

A pause, and the line delivered in the flattest register she owned:

"The record will say you left. I'll spend the rest of my tenure making the record say why."

He believed her, and entered the believing rather than the sentence, because the sentence was the kind a person says at a door and the believing was the part that would still be true in ten years. She did not offer her hand and he did not expect it. She walked him to the office door and opened it herself, which she did for visiting officers of grade and had never once done for an enrollee, and Cael, going down the stair, understood that he had just been promoted out of the institution by its chancellor four days before he resigned from it.

The log, that night:

*Five weeks became five days. We leave before the date. All of us. Nobody gets corrected — not by the Arbiter, not on the record, not on their verb. We take the leaving into our own hands, the way Seln took his resignation into his fifteen years ago and never filed it. Karis has already roped the boxes. Brom's carrying three. Lira knows how to say goodbye to a page. Withrow's going to spend a career on a footnote nobody asked her for.*

*And Ephram wants a fight first. Of course he does. It's the only currency this place ever spoke that I still owe in. He'll get it. Then we're gone.*

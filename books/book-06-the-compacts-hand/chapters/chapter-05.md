# Chapter 5 — The Evaluator

The courtesy copy reached the corner office on the third floor of the Compact's western administration in the ordinary morning file, between a stations-office staffing note and a certification dispute from the coastal district, and Vastin knew what it was before he opened it, because he had been expecting it since a chamber with no windows had entered a question for formal proceedings and set his testimony aside with courtesy.

He had not expected it as a courtesy copy.

That was the first thing he noted, and he noted it the way he noted everything, without decoration: for three years, every document touching the enrollee's matter had crossed his desk as a *query* — an advisory requested, an evaluation invited, an assessment sought. This one crossed it as information. *For the evaluator's file, per standing distribution.* Not asked. Told. The instrument that had grown from his own advisory — he found the paragraph on the second page and read his own sentence back in the petitioner's type, *adopted*, approvingly, the way a man finds his signature on a document he did not sign — had been filed at Ostrand a fortnight ago, and the Compact's senior evaluator, the only officer of the Compact who had personally assessed the evaluand, was being informed of it in the same envelope as a staffing note.

The corner office on the third floor had been his for fourteen years and had, in that time, acquired exactly four things: a desk, a map cabinet with the western district's station network on its top sheet, a locked press behind the cabinet, and a chair for a visitor that was neither comfortable nor uncomfortable, having been chosen for a room where visits were expected to be brief and useful. There were no plaques on the walls. He had been offered plaques. The morning file came up at the half-bell in a leather wallet and went back down at the fourth, and in fourteen years it had never once been late, because the outer office understood that lateness in this room was a finding about the outer office.

He worked the file the way he had worked every file since he was a district evaluator with a bad coat: left-hand stack, things requiring judgment; right-hand stack, things requiring only a signature; and, in the middle, whatever had arrived that did not yet know which stack it belonged in. He put the courtesy copy in the middle and did the staffing note and the coastal certification first, because a man who read the interesting item first was a man whose judgment on the dull ones could be predicted, and predictable judgment was the beginning of bad judgment.

Then he read the whole instrument once, at his own pace, with the office quiet enough to hear paper.

It took him forty minutes to reach the finding a researcher at Halcenvane had taken a night to reach, and he reached it by the same road: the conceded execution, the conceded text, the integrity provision pointed at a person, the re-evaluation remedy, the protocol with no bin, chapter fourteen. The corridor. He had spent thirty years inside instruments and he recognized the architecture of this one the way a mason recognizes a wall built by a colleague — every course true, every joint tight, and the whole thing standing on a foundation the mason had been asked not to inspect. He did not need chapter fourteen read to him. He had written evaluations that triggered it. Twice. Both findings correct on the evidence, both protocols run to the letter, both files closed inside the schedule. What the sequence did afterward was entered under a different heading, in an office he had never worked in.

*Compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol.* He read the phrase a third time. He had sat in a records hall a year and a half ago and asked a boy eleven questions and declined to ask the twelfth, and had written afterward, in his own hand, the only finding the evidence supported: *Classification error is unlikely. Continue observation.* The instruments could not read the evaluand. He had said so. Now the petition proposed to stand the evaluand in front of the instruments again, formally, on the record, and to treat the instruments' silence as the evaluand's failure — and the proposal cited, as its authority for writing new rules, the evaluator who had said the old ones were being misread.

He got the enrollee's file out of the left-hand stack before he did anything else, because a finding was not a memory and he did not permit himself to work from memory about a subject.

It was thicker than it had been at the inspection and most of what had thickened it was other people's paper. His own portion was nine leaves. The observation schedule. The floor notes, in his hand, from a records hall on a bluff: the evasion frames run at three tempos, the surface read, the declared and the undeclared, the three-hour session and the two-hour, the boy's answers set down verbatim because an evaluator who paraphrased a subject was writing about himself. Eleven questions asked. He had numbered them. He read them now in order, which took four minutes, and found — as he had found each of the six times he had read them since — that they were good questions, correctly ordered, and that the twelfth, which was written on no leaf anywhere and had been declined in the room, was the only one that would have been of any use to a proceeding.

He had declined it because asking it would have been an interrogation and not an evaluation, and because an evaluator who asks a subject to explain what the instrument cannot read has stopped trusting his instrument and started trusting his suspicion. He had been right to decline it. He was still right. It occurred to him, holding the nine leaves, that being right had now cost the file everything it was worth, since a finding built on honest questions could be set aside in two sentences and a finding built on the twelfth could not have been written at all.

He put the leaves back in order and squared them and returned the file to the stack.

He set the instrument down and looked at the window for the length of time he permitted himself for such things, which was not long — long enough to see the river, and the stations-office roof across it, where four Arbiter frames he had personally certified sat in their housings reading practitioners every working day, and to remember, without wanting to, the two evaluations of his career that had triggered chapter fourteen. A guild-house officer who had falsified his own sponsorship records for a decade: finding entered, standing nullified, the sequence run out in nine days. A district assessor who had taken money to alter a Kindling entry: the same finding, the same nine days — and, four months after the file closed, one letter from the man's daughter, asking whether her father's name would ever come off the restricted list. He had answered the letter. He had told her the truth, which was no. He had not thought about the letter in six years and he thought about it now for the length of the window, and then he stopped, because the window's time was up.

Then he did what he did, because doing was his language. He drew a sheet of the office's own stock and drafted a petition for standing: the Compact's senior-most evaluator, the only official of the Compact who had personally assessed the enrollee under any protocol, requesting leave to submit an evaluator's finding to the proceedings, under his own name and seal, for the evaluation seat's consideration. He drafted it in the register the registry used for such things — plain, exact, citing the provisions that permitted supplementary findings in institutional proceedings, of which there were two, both old — the older of them pre-standardization text, drafted in the era when evaluators' findings were the proceedings' whole substance rather than their ornament, surviving in the modern code the way old clauses survived, unrepealed, unfashionable, and waiting; he read both twice and cited both, because a petition that stood on one leg deserved to be refused — and he read it back, and it was textually unimpeachable, and he filed it by the noon dispatch.

His clerk — nine years in the outer office; a man who had learned to enter the Archmarshal's directions without comment and to spend the remainder of each morning not asking about them — entered the petition into the dispatch ledger, and paused with the pen above the routing column, and Vastin, who missed nothing that happened within forty feet of his desk, said, "Registry seat, Ostrand. Proceedings. Institutional," and the clerk wrote it, and did not say that in nine years he had never routed a petition from that desk *to* a proceeding rather than from one. He did not need to say it. Vastin had noted the pause, and filed it, and the filing read: *even the outer office knows which direction this desk sends paper. Today it sent it the other way.*

He had submitted supplementary findings four times in thirty years and he set the four out on the desk from memory, in order, because a man predicting an outcome should first assemble his sample. A chartered institution's sponsorship dispute, sixteen years ago: standing granted in six days, finding received, finding decisive. A stations-office proceeding on a frame's calibration: granted in nine, received, and the frame retired on his page alone. A guild-house evaluation matter: granted in four. And a district's enrollment challenge, twelve years back, the nearest of the four to this one, in which he had submitted a finding against the district's own petition and the district had received it, entered it, and lost on it, and had written to him afterward to say so without complaint, because that was how the thing had worked for three hundred years.

Four for four. It was not a large sample. It was every instance there was.

He set an expectation, because he always set an expectation. Standing granted within the week, as a formality; a request from the evaluator of record for the file, to be scheduled; his finding entered at the second session. He had submitted supplementary findings to four institutional proceedings in thirty years and every one had been received with the courtesy the grade commanded.

The four days ran on the office's ordinary clock. The certification dispute from the coastal district was resolved by a two-line advisory. The stations-office staffing note was annotated and returned. On the third day a junior evaluator from the northern district came up to the corner office with a difficult file — a Kindling record that did not match its practitioner's demonstrated architecture by a margin the junior found troubling — and Vastin reviewed it with him for an hour, patiently, the way he had reviewed such files for thirty years: the architecture was within tolerance; the record was sound; the margin was the ordinary variance of a young practitioner developing faster than his evaluators expected; continue observation. The junior thanked him and went down, reassured. Vastin sat afterward with the file's copy for longer than the file warranted, and could not have said why, and entered the not-saying in no ledger, because it was not yet a thing with a shape.

On the fourth morning the outer office's step on the stair was wrong.

He knew the step. Nine years of it: two treads at a time to the landing, then single, then the pause at the door that was the clerk deciding whether the Archmarshal was reading. This one came up single from the bottom and did not pause at all, which meant the man had decided the thing in his hand outranked the reading, and Vastin had time, in the four seconds between the stair and the door, to know what it was and to compose his face, and did the second and resented, briefly and privately, having needed to.

"From the registry seat, sir. By seat courier, not the post."

"Thank you. Leave the door."

The denial arrived in four days.

---

He read the two old provisions again on the fourth morning, before the file came, the way a man rechecks a sum he has already proved: the pre-standardization text first, in the compilation's own type, three sentences that had once been the whole substance of how a proceeding learned what an evaluator knew. *The evaluator of record, or any evaluator of senior grade having personally assessed the subject, may submit findings to the proceedings, and the proceedings shall receive them.* Shall receive. The old text's favorite word, and his. He had cited it correctly. It had been invoked a bare handful of times in three hundred years and honored on every one of them, because a proceeding that declined to receive an evaluator's findings was a proceeding that had decided evaluation was not its business, and no proceeding in three hundred years had been willing to say so in writing.

It came in the morning file, in the registry's flawless prose, on registry-seat stock with the seal impressed rather than inked — which was itself a grade of reply, and he catalogued the grade before he read the words. Two sentences.

*The proceedings' evaluation seat is fully constituted. Supplementary findings are not contemplated by the schedule.*

He read it standing, which he did not do, and then he sat down, which he did not need to, and the office was very quiet, and he held the sheet at the reading distance his eyes now required and read it a second time, and a third, and the third reading was the one that mattered, because on the third reading he stopped reading it as a reply and started reading it as a *sentence*.

*Not contemplated by the schedule.*

He had written that sentence. Not that one — its sisters. A hundred of them, across thirty years, in the correct register, to the correct offices, declining unhelpful input: a field assessor's supplementary opinion not contemplated by the review's terms; a station's petition to reopen a closed finding not contemplated by the schedule; a junior officer's escalation not contemplated by the reporting structure. He had written them as a craftsman writes — cleanly, without cruelty, in the exact language that closed a door while leaving the person on the other side of it nothing to grip. It was good technique. It was among the best he had. And he recognized it now, at the reading distance his eyes required, from the receiving end, with the specific vertigo of a man who has spent his life fitting instruments and has just been handed one of his own, calibrated, pointed at him.

The proceedings did not want the best evaluation available. He turned the sentence over, and it did not change. The proceedings had constituted an evaluation seat, and the seat was *full*, and the Compact's senior evaluator — the one officer who had put the instrument to the evaluand and written down what it returned — was not contemplated by the schedule, because the schedule had been written by people who already knew what the evaluation would find, and an evaluator who might find something else was not an asset to a schedule. He was an irregularity. The proceedings wanted the evaluation they had been built to produce. His grade, his thirty years, his personal assessment of the subject: not contemplated.

He noted, with the evaluator's habit of keeping the instrument honest even while it recorded its own reading, that the sentence had cost the sender nothing. Two lines. Flawless. Deniable at every altitude. Exactly as his own had been.

He put the denial in the enrollee's file, in the left-hand stack, the one he read first.

---

And then, because an evaluator evaluates, he began writing the finding anyway.

He began it that afternoon, after the day's other business, when the office was quiet enough to hear paper, on the office's own stock, in his own hand — not the office's; the hand he used for the things that were his — and he headed it the way he headed every finding of his career, with the subject, the protocol, and the date, and beneath them, in the line the form provided for the addressee, he wrote nothing. Unsolicited. Uncommissioned. Addressed to no one.

*Evaluator's finding. Subject: the enrollee of record, Halcenvane, unclassified. Basis: personal assessment under the demonstration provision's evaluation-linked observation, the inspection year; the continental tournament record entire, personally attended at three sessions and reviewed at all; the assay-provision panel minutes, four terms; the enrollee's public season record, two years.*

He wrote for three hours. He wrote it as he would have written it for the second session — completely, at his own speed, in the register of a man correcting a category error by demonstration — and it ran to nine pages, and its finding was the one the evidence had supported since a records-hall room: that the standard Arbiter protocol was not an instrument capable of evaluating the subject; that its silence was a limit of the instrument and not a finding about the evaluand; that compelled re-evaluation under that protocol would therefore return, with certainty, no valid classification, and that an evaluation whose result was certain before it was conducted was not an evaluation but a procedure wearing one; and that the evaluation seat, if it wished to evaluate rather than to proceed, had one honest instrument available to it, which was the demonstration record, and should use it.

The finding's ninth page carried its last paragraph, and he wrote it slower than the rest, because it was the paragraph an evaluator was not supposed to write and he wrote it anyway:

*The evaluator notes, for the file, that this finding was offered to the proceedings and declined as not contemplated by the schedule. The evaluator records no objection to the schedule. The evaluator records that a schedule which does not contemplate the only personal assessment of the subject in the Compact's possession has been drafted with a purpose the evaluator was not consulted on and cannot, from this desk, identify — and that an evaluation which begins from its conclusion is a report about the evaluator, and that the evaluator, in this instance, was not the one evaluating.*

The clerk came up at the fourth bell to take the day's file down and found the Archmarshal writing, which he had seen perhaps a dozen times in nine years, because the Archmarshal's writing was ordinarily done in the mornings and ordinarily on the office's forms.

"The file, sir."

"Take it. Nothing of mine in it tonight."

The clerk took it, and went to the door, and stopped there for the length of time a careful man permits himself, and Vastin, who missed nothing within forty feet of his desk, allowed the stop.

"Sir. The lamps go out at the sixth bell on this floor. The building's rule, not mine."

"Then bring me a candle from the outer office and enter it on the office's consumables, under my name."

"Yes, sir." A pause, and then the only sentence the man had spoken in nine years that was not about the work: "Is it a finding, sir?"

Vastin considered the question, which was the correct question, and found that he could answer it accurately.

"It is a finding. It has no addressee."

"Very good, sir," said the clerk, who had no idea what to do with that, and brought the candle, and entered it on the consumables under the Archmarshal's name, where it would sit for the next forty years as the only irregularity in fourteen years of that office's accounts.

He wrote past the sixth bell by the candle. The third page cost him the most, because it was the page on which he had to set down, in the register a finding required, that the instruments were not at fault: that the Arbiter protocol was performing exactly as designed, that its silence in this instance was not a malfunction of any frame he had ever certified, and that a limit correctly reported by an instrument is the most honest output an instrument can produce. He had spent thirty years defending that sentence to people who wanted the frames blamed. He wrote it now in defense of a boy, and noted, in the margin of his own mind and nowhere on the leaf, that it was the same sentence.

He read it back once. It was the best finding he had written in a decade. It was addressed to no one, and there was no one to address it to, and he knew both things with the exactness that had made him what he was.

He filed it in his own records — not the office's; the locked press behind the map cabinet where he kept the things that were his — under a designation he had never used in thirty years, because he had never needed one, because every finding he had ever written had somewhere to go. He wrote the designation on the folder's tab in his own hand, and looked at it, and left it.

*Pending a proceeding worth sending it to.*

---

Word reached Halcenvane through Withrow's counsel eight days later, in the way word reached anyone from that altitude: sideways, secondhand, as a thing the counsel's district contacts had heard the registry seat's clerks discussing at the long inn by the wharfs. She brought it up the bluff road in person because it was not a thing to put in a note, and delivered it at the residence table in the voice she used for findings that changed the weather. The Archmarshal had petitioned for standing to submit a finding to the proceedings. The petition had been refused. Two sentences. Not contemplated by the schedule.

Withrow received it first, in her office, from the counsel, and her response was entered in the log that night because Cael was present for it: she listened to the two sentences, and asked to hear them a second time, and then said, in the registry-official's register, "Not contemplated by the schedule. I have written that sentence. Every registry officer of my grade has written that sentence." She looked at the charter framed above the file cases. "I used to think it was the most honest thing the registry did — declining input it didn't need, in plain words, without pretending to have considered it. I'm now going to have to think about what it means that I taught it to junior officers as a kindness." To Cael: "Go and tell the table. And tell them I said the word again. *Choosing.*"

Karis had the two provisions on the annex table by nightfall — the ones the Archmarshal must have cited, because there were only two, both old, one of them pre-standardization stratum — and she read them aloud to the circle before the counsel's news was discussed, because she wanted the table to know exactly how sound the refused petition had been. "He cited the right law. Both provisions. I counted them tonight. Eleven. All granted." She set the volume down. "They refused it in two sentences without citing a provision at all. That's not a ruling. That's a schedule declining to have a ruling in it."

The counsel gave the table one more thing before she went back down the road, and she gave it standing, with her gloves already on, which was how she delivered the items she had decided were not advice.

"I want to be precise about what I have and have not told you. I have not told you that the Archmarshal is on your side. He is not. He is an officer of the Compact who petitioned an office of the Compact under the Compact's own provisions and was refused by it, and every one of those words is the same institution." She drew the glove tight. "What I have told you is that the refusal was two sentences with no provision cited. In twenty-six years I have been refused perhaps two hundred times and I have been refused *without a citation* four times, and all four were offices that had already decided and did not wish to write down the ground they had decided on." She took up the case. "Write the citation down when you get one. Its absence is a finding."

Cael had done one piece of arithmetic while the counsel was still speaking, because the route was the only part of the news he could check himself: the long inn by the wharfs was where the delegation's coach settled its small accounts on an Ostrand run, and the delegation's small accounts were settled by one man, at a counter, for the quarter-hour it took, in a room of registry-seat clerks who would have taken him for furniture. He did not enter it as a finding. He entered it in the notebook under the heading the man's whole file lived under: *the office was in the room first.*

The inner circle sat with it at the residence table, and nobody spoke for a while, because there was nothing to say to it that the sentence had not already said.

Seln said it anyway, from the table's end, in the dry administrative register — the professional reading the tea leaves for people who had not been trained to, and had learned, by now, to weigh him at full freight.

"They benched their best instrument because he measures true." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "Whatever this is, it stopped being an evaluation before it started."

Nobody answered. Lira looked at the table. Brom looked at Seln, for a moment longer than usual, the way he looked at things he was pricing, and Cael charted the look and got the reading on the stair afterward, from Brom himself, unprompted, which happened perhaps four times a year: "He said *their best instrument*. He was talking about the Archmarshal." Brom stopped at the stair's turn. "He was also talking about himself. Fifteen years the best instrument they had. Benched by his own reports." He went up. Karis wrote it down.

The log, that night, at the window, with the counsel's note of the refusal on the sill beside the docket dates:

*Vastin warned me the channels would change. He didn't say they'd change on him too.*

*He's the Compact's best evaluator. He put the instrument to me himself, in a room, for a day, and wrote down what it returned. He asked to tell the proceedings what he found and the proceedings told him, in his own grammar, that he wasn't on the schedule. Note the shape: they didn't dispute his finding. They declined to contemplate it. That's not an argument lost. That's an instrument put back in the case unread.*

*Whatever is deciding what I am, it has now declined the only two credentialed opinions the system ever produced about me — his at the convening, his at the proceedings. Somebody decided, before the first session sat, that the best evaluation available was not an asset. I've spent four years learning to read the people the machine sends. I am beginning to understand that the machine has learned to send people I can't read, and to bench the ones I can.*

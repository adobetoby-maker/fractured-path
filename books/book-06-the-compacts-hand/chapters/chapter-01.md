# Chapter 1 — Served

He closed out the interval the way he closed out every interval, at the window, with the pen, on the night before the term began — and the window was the same window, which was the first entry.

*Road home from Norhold: seventeen days, closed in the old volume. Two months since, closed here, in Hesk's. Open the interval. Enter what happened. Enter what it cost. Close it.*

*The campus: an academy that placed third on the continent walks differently, and I have spent two months cataloguing the walk. The covered walk's board keeps the qualifying circular in its crooked frame and has added the finals standings beside it, and the new intake stops at both the way I stopped at the seating on the bluff road two years ago — taking the thing apart before deciding how to feel about it. Enrollment applications up by a third, Bracken says, in the voice of a man who will have to process them. The delegation is treated like returning weather: expected, remarked on, walked around. Ephram is cohort captain and captains the way he learned the harbor's locks, by understanding the whole mechanism before touching a lever.*

*Lira: the Silver-bracket program opened on the first day of the recess, under Rooke, with the institution's seal on the door — the first ladder anyone ever built toward her. She trains at dawn on the continental-grade drills and comes to the table at breakfast with the specific quiet of a person who has been given the thing she asked for and is discovering it is heavier than the asking. She has not said so. She has said "it's there" twice, quoting Rooke, which is how I know.*

*Brom: the mandatory advancement review — the continental title's machinery — docketed for term's end, at the district seat. Everyone says the word formality quietly and nobody says it to him. He carries the plaque's ribbon in his kit, not on his collar, and has stopped reading the Velmere post at the courtyard lantern and started reading it at the table, folded, without comment, which is a change I have logged and do not yet understand.*

*Karis: research standing renewed, with a budget line attached — the academy's first ever for an enrollee's private work — and archive privileges Prynn would have wept at, and a second-year assistant assigned to her whom she is training the way Vell trained me, by declining to explain anything twice. The founding-era shelf is on her desk in three languages of copy. She has not opened the tournament charter's provisions section since the road. She is, I think, making herself wait for winter, and winter is here.*

*Seln: at his desk. At the table, when the table assembles — every time now, the middle of the bench, since a waystation on the road home — which nobody remarks on and everybody counts. The office runs on his cross-indexing; the assessment counter posts its floor schedules a day earlier than any other office on the bluff; the man is, by every measurable output, the best clerk this academy has ever employed, and I have watched him be that for a year and a half and have never once seen the effort it costs.*

*The watchers: back at the bluff road since the second week of the recess. Thinner than Norhold — that arithmetic never reverses; a crossroads city watching everyone at once was a holiday, and holidays end. Two at the road, three on market days, the second-waystation change holding its schedule. But the faces are different, and the difference is the entry that matters: older men. Not more of them — more senior. I have logged four years of rotation faces and I know the grades by their coats and their patience, and the junior grades are gone. Whoever is paying has stopped sending people to learn. I don't yet know what that means. Enter it and watch it.*

*Inventory at term's open, for the record, because the record is going to be asked: seven confirmed fragments. Wind-adjacent, Pressure-adjacent, Iron-adjacent, Compression-adjacent, Ember-adjacent, Shadow-adjacent — and Storm-adjacent, whose stability flag cleared over the winter. Write that plainly, because it was not plain when it was written the first time: the flag stood eleven weeks. The frameworks resettled slower than after any acquisition in the log; the Wind economy returned to rate in the fourth week and the surface read in the sixth; the new mass seated itself into the whole architecture the way masonry seats, a hair a day, until one morning in the recess I ran the full suite on the second floor before dawn and nothing pulled at anything, and I stood there in the cold and let it. Settled. Logged with relief, and I am leaving the word relief on the page because Hesk's volume keeps the whole account or none of it.*

*One anomaly. Session nine. Unreproduced, uncounted, its own three-word room.*

*One match record the continent is still arguing about — the broadsides reached the bluff a week after we did and have not stopped; the interpretations have multiplied, and every one of them is still wrong, and the word that matters has still appeared in none of them.*

*I am seventeen years old, enrolled on a lawful basis at a chartered academy that is third on the continent, and the honest last line of the interval is the same as last year's and different: I do not know what my part in the term is. Last year everyone else had a bracket. This year everyone else has a ladder, a docket, a budget, a desk — the system, for once, handing my people things honestly. And me a stamp, renewed, and a room above them full of careful people who entered a question for formal proceedings and have not yet answered it. Open the term and find out.*

He read it back once, capped the pen, and went to bed with the shutters open, because the fountain had earned it again.

---

Convocation ran on the first morning as convocation ran — the lecture complex, all faculty and the whole enrollment, Withrow first — and Cael took the seat he had taken for two years, four rows up on the north aisle, and counted the hall as it filled.

Enrollment up by a third. The new intake sat in its block near the back, and the block was larger than any Halcenvane had held, and it did the thing new intakes did — watching the returning years to learn which distances mattered — with one variation the hall had never carried before: the block watched the delegation. Not the way the wool town had watched, or the Concourse; the way apprentices watch journeymen. Lira, arriving with her collar damp from the Silver floor, was tracked to her seat by forty pairs of eyes that had read the broadsides. Brom, arriving with bread, was tracked by the ones who had read the Copper bracket. Karis, arriving on the minute, by the few who had read the transcripts. And Cael, arriving early, had been tracked since the door, by all of them, in the specific silence of an intake that had paid its six marks for the assay transcript and its copper-and-a-half for the woodcut and had come up the bluff road to find out which was accurate.

"You've got a following," Ephram observed, sliding into the row behind with the cohort captain's file under his arm. "The second-years have organized a rota to sit where they can see you at meals. I've seen the rota. It's competently drawn up."

"Who drew it?"

"The one Karis is training. She's applied your notebook method to you." Ephram opened the file. "I told her it was a violation of something. She asked me to cite the provision."

Withrow came to the lectern with one page and did not look at it, and the year opened on her register, which had acquired, since Norhold, a quality Cael had spent two months naming and had finally entered as *load-bearing calm* — the composure of a woman whose institution had been measured against the whole continent and had returned a number she could stand on.

"Welcome to the winter term. Ordinary business in seven minutes. Then the year."

The ordinary business ran seven minutes: term dates, hall assignments, the intake's induction schedule, three staff appointments — one of them Rooke's Silver-bracket program formally chartered as a standing track, which drew a sound from the enrollment that Withrow allowed to run its full length before continuing, the first time Cael had ever seen her budget for applause.

"Last year I told you the tournament was a schedule and not an ambition. The schedule ran, and the continent counted us, and I have said everything I intend to say about the counting." She put the page down. "This year's business is smaller and harder. An institution that has been measured is an institution with a number to defend. We will defend it the way we earned it: by being exactly what we say we are, in writing, in front of whoever is watching — and I am given to understand that the watching has not decreased. Selection standards for the next cycle post on the covered walk on the first of the month. The research standing schedule posts today. Go to your assignments."

The hall emptied the way halls emptied when they had been given a year to carry, and Cael stayed a moment watching the stage, where Withrow was saying something brief to Bracken, and Bracken was nodding, and then Bracken looked up — not at the hall; at the lecture complex's tall east windows, and the bluff road beyond them, where a courier's escort would be visible from that angle a full minute before it reached the gate.

There was nothing on the road. Cael filed the look anyway, because Bracken looked at nothing without a reason, and went to his assignment.

---

Rooke's Silver floor ran at dawn and Cael went to watch it the second morning, because a program built toward one of his people was a thing he intended to have catalogued from its first week.

The second training floor had been rebuilt for it over the recess — the drills' geometry laid out in painted lines, the striking frames re-rigged for Silver load, a rating table at the floor's head where a clerk sat with the continental drill-book open — and Lira was on it with two third-years and one of Rooke's imported instructors, a lean Silver Rank 5 from the river academy who had fought her at the confluence and lost and had, apparently, asked for the posting.

Cael took the rail and ran the instrument.

The drills were the continental program's — the ones Zerin had run since she was twelve; he recognized their grammar from four bouts' charting — and Lira was running them cold, the way she had run the registry station's evasion sequence cold: wrong twice in the first pass, then right, then right at speed. The Silver Rank 5 corrected her with the flat economy of a man who had been corrected the same way and had learned that the flatness was respect. Rooke stood at the floor's head with the coaching file and wrote continuously, and every entry — Cael could not read it and did not need to — began with the same word the file had used since the confluence: *there*.

She came to the rail at the water break with her collar dark and her hands taped and the look he had catalogued at breakfast for two months, and did not give it to him in words, because it was not ready for words yet; she looked at the painted lines and the frames and the clerk with the drill-book and the whole apparatus built toward her, and rolled the taped hands, and said only: "It's heavier than it looks from the rail. Don't write that down."

"Noted," Cael said, and did not write it down, which she saw, and which was the point.

Rooke's voice from the floor's head: "Wind. Back on the line. It's *there*. Again." And she went, and Cael watched her go into the first ladder anyone had ever built toward her, and entered in the log that night the only margin the morning required: *she hasn't said she wants it. She's said it's heavy. In three years I have never once heard her call a thing the system could give her heavy — she called them wrong, or late, or owed. Heavy is new. File the date. Whatever this term is for, it has a price on it that isn't mine.*

---

Brom's docket arrived on the fourth day, and it arrived the way the registry's kindnesses arrived, as a form.

The district seat's advancement office posted the term's review schedule to Bracken's counter, and Bracken walked the relevant sheet up to the residence himself — which he did not do for forms; he did it for things — and laid it on the common table where the delegation was eating, and pointed at the line without comment, because the line did its own work. *Advancement Review, mandatory, per continental title: Brom, Halcenvane, Iron Skin Path, Copper-tier formal. Docketed: the last Fifth-day of term, district seat, Ostrand. Panel: standard.*

"Standard panel," Bracken said. "Which means two assessors and a sigil frame, the same room your friend stood in." He nodded at Lira. "The title triggers the review inside ninety days of the award; the academy applied to move it, and the district re-docketed it to the last Fifth-day of term, so you sit it after a term of work instead of in the middle of one. The season record supplies the evidence. I have processed eleven of these in thirty years and every one of them found for advancement, because a continental title is not a thing the registry can price below the tier it was won at without the registry looking foolish, and the registry does not look foolish. Everybody in this office is saying the word *formality* quietly." He looked at Brom, who was looking at the sheet. "I'll say it to your face, because you are a man who keeps records: it is a formality. Iron-tier by the last Fifth-day of term. Your family's ledger closes."

Brom read the sheet the way he read everything that cost him, once and completely, and folded it, and put it in the kit beside the plaque's ribbon and a one-line letter, and said, "Noted," and went back to his bread — and Cael, who had spent two years learning the man's grammar, filed the whole exchange under the heading it belonged to, which was not *formality*. It was *the ledger*. The Velmere ledger, the Gold-tier household's arithmetic, the standard that had priced a big careful boy out of his own family at fourteen — about to close, on a form, at a district seat, in a room the size of hall three. Brom carried the sheet the way he carried the sister's letter. Beside nothing else like it.

"You're not going to say anything about it," Lira observed, later, at the rail.

"He hasn't," Cael said. "So I won't. That's the arrangement."

"That's *his* arrangement."

"It's the only one he's ever offered. I'm not renegotiating it in his last term of Copper." He watched the yard. "Ask me after the Fifth-day."

---

Karis's standing came with a room.

Not a carrel; not an assignment in the upper reading room. A *room* — a former records annex off the library's north range, with a door that locked and her name on a card in its frame and a budget line the academy's own council had voted, the first it had ever voted for an enrollee's private research — and she stood in the doorway of it on the first afternoon of term with the founding-era compilations in her arms and the expression Cael had catalogued at Greyvane's shelf and Norhold's charter and had not, until now, seen pointed at a *door*.

"They gave you a room," he said.

"They gave me a room, a budget, archive privileges above faculty grade, and a second-year assistant who reads registry law the way I read it at sixteen — which is to say obsessively and wrong — and whom I am correcting at a rate of one error per morning so she'll learn to find the others herself." Karis set the compilations down on the room's one table and squared them, and the squaring had something in it he had never seen in her hands before, which he entered that evening as *ownership*. "Ternhall gave me a doctrine. Greyvane gave me a shelf with a gap in it. This place gave me a room with a lock and asked what I'd do with it." She looked at the door. "I have been transferring out of institutions since I was sixteen, Cael. I'd like it noted that this is the first one I've ever wanted to stay in, and that I noticed the wanting, and that I'm suspicious of it, because I'm me."

"Noted."

"The provisions section," she added, without looking at him, because she knew he'd been waiting two months to ask, "is on the shelf. Unopened since the road. It stays unopened until I've dated the older strata against — until I've read the older material properly, in order. Winter is for the strata. I promised myself at a rest-day table. Don't ask before midwinter."

He didn't ask. He filed the slip — *until I've dated the older strata against* — and did not know what she had meant to set them against, and entered it in the observation notebook under her name with a question mark, the way he entered everything she started and didn't finish, because she finished everything eventually and the notebook was where he waited.

---

The assessment counter opened at the third bell, and Cael went down on the fifth day with the term's floor-allocation form, because enrollees requesting evaluation-linked floor time filed at the counter, and because the counter was where Seln was.

The queue was longer than last year's — the intake had discovered the counter — and Cael used it the way he used every queue. Two clerks working. Seven form types in the rack, one new: a green sheet, *Research-Standing Floor Access*, which had not existed before Karis's room did and which meant the office had built the form the week the council voted the budget, before anyone requested it. He knew who. The knowledge went nowhere near his face, per an arrangement two years old.

Seln processed the queue at the speed of a man who had never once been asked to hurry. When Cael reached the counter he took the form, read it entirely, stamped the received line, and delivered the season's first counter sentence in the register the season had learned to weigh.

"Evaluation-linked hours are countersigned by the evaluator of record. Magister Gault countersigns Fifth-days." He squared the sheet against the stack. "The office notes the bluff-road rotation has changed grade since the recess. Senior men. The office has no view on it." He aligned the form. "Enrollees with evaluation-linked standing may wish to keep their documentation current."

"Thank you for the form."

"The floor schedule posts the morning after."

That was the whole of it. Cael walked back up the covered walk with the copy-slip in his coat and filed the exchange where all of Seln's exchanges lived, in the ledger that never touched paper, and it filed under the same heading as the watchers' coats: *senior men; keep the documentation current.* The man had read the rotation off the bluff road the same week Cael had, had drawn the same conclusion, and had spent one counter sentence on it — which, at the freight-to-word rate Cael had been tallying for two years, was the equivalent of anyone else standing on a table.

He counted the watchers again that evening, from the north wall, at dusk, the way he had counted them for four years from windows and walls and wagon benches, and got the same answer the log already held. Two at the bluff road. Grey coats, good boots, the patience of men paid by the year — and the faces of men who had been paid by the year for a long time. No junior grades. No one learning anything. Men who already knew what they were watching for, waiting for it to happen.

*They're not gathering,* he wrote. *Gathering is over. Whatever the room above them decided to ask, it has asked, and these are the men who get sent when the asking is finished and the answer is expected. I don't know the answer. I know the shape of men who do.*

---

The courier came on the eighth day of term, at the second bell, up the bluff road under formal escort, and Bracken saw him from the lecture complex's east windows a full minute before the gate did, because Bracken had been watching that road for eight days.

Cael saw the escort from the covered walk. Two riders in registry livery flanking a third, the third carrying a document case flat against the saddle's spine — the same posture as the courier who had taken Halcenvane's roster down this road a year and a half ago, reversed: not leaving with a record, arriving with one. The gate lodge's man stood up. The escort did not dismount at the lodge. It rode through, at a walk, to the records hall, the way a delivery rode when the delivery's protocol outranked the building's.

He was in the records hall before the courier had finished dismounting, because a document arriving under escort was a document about him, and four years had taught him to be present for his own paper.

Bracken was at the counter. Withrow arrived thirty seconds later, from the direction of her office, walking at exactly her ordinary pace, which was itself a decision. The courier — a registry officer, not a rider; the livery was the escort's, and the man himself wore the plain grey of an office with a seal — laid the case on the counter, unlocked it, and produced a single sealed instrument, and read the protocol from a card, because the protocol was one nobody in the room had ever heard used.

"A sealed instrument of the registry, addressed jointly to the Chancellor of Halcenvane Academy and to the enrollee of record named herein, to be served upon both parties together, in person, and acknowledged by both signatures before the instrument is opened." He looked up from the card. "Both parties are present?"

"Both parties are present," said Withrow.

Cael signed the acknowledgment beneath her signature, on a form whose type he had never seen — a joint-service form, which Bracken would tell him afterward existed in the registry code and had, in his thirty years, been used for exactly nothing he could name — and the courier witnessed both signatures, sealed the acknowledgment into his case, bowed to a depth precisely calibrated for a chancellor, and left, and the escort rode back down the bluff road at a walk, and the instrument lay on the counter between the three of them with the Compact's seal on its flap.

Withrow broke the seal standing. She did not sit, and she did not hand it to Bracken, and she did not ask Cael to step back — she read it standing, at the counter, at her ordinary reading pace, with the enrollee of record beside her, because it was addressed to both of them and she was a woman who honored the address on a document.

The instrument ran to many pages. The one that mattered was the first, and she read it aloud, in the registry-official's flat register, so that the record would show it had been read to the enrollee on service — and the first page, in the document's own voice, was this:

*To the Chancellor, Halcenvane Academy, and to the enrollee of record, Cael, unclassified, enrolled under the demonstration provision of the assay clause, jointly:*

*Be it known that the Guilds Compact, by its registry, having convened formal proceedings upon the question of the classification designated [SHATTERED] and its bearing upon evaluation integrity within chartered institutions, hereby files a formal challenge to enrollment basis against the said enrollment.*

*The challenge does not allege fraud in the enrollment's execution, nor contest the text of the provision under which it was executed. It asserts that an unclassifiable practitioner's presence within a chartered institution's evaluation architecture presents an evaluation-integrity question which the demonstration provision was not drafted to answer and cannot answer, and petitions the registry for compelled re-evaluation of the enrollee under standard Arbiter protocol, the enrollment to stand or fall upon that evaluation's result.*

*Proceedings to be convened at the registry seat at Ostrand upon a term-length schedule. The respondent institution and the enrollee of record are directed to answer within fourteen days.*

*Given under the Compact's hand.*

She lowered the page. Nobody in the records hall said anything. Bracken's hands were flat on the counter. The courier's hoofbeats had gone under the wind.

"'Given under the Compact's hand,'" Withrow said, at last, in the flattest voice Cael had ever heard her use — the registry-official's, the one from before she was a chancellor, the one that had read ten thousand such closings on ten thousand instruments and had never once needed to look at the phrase. "I have signed that line myself. Nine hundred times." She folded the first page over the rest, precisely. "Registrar. Copies to counsel, to the enrollee, and to the research annex. Tonight." And to Cael, in the same voice, because the voice was the only instrument she trusted in the moment: "They've asked the question again. Go and tell Karis. She'll want to start reading before she's finished being angry."

---

He wrote the log at the window, that night, with the residence court quiet and the fountain running and a copy of the instrument on the sill beside him, its first page face up — and he wrote the entry in one pass without lifting the pen, the way he wrote the things he had already decided, and it was true in every clause the way the entry a year ago had been true, and the season would spend itself teaching him what it was standing on.

*They filed. After the hearing I won, the inspection I passed, the tournament they couldn't score — they went back to their own text and found a way to ask the question again. Fine. I've beaten their text before. Karis is already reading. Note the date. The season's open.*

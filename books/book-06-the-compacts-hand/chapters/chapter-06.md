# Chapter 6 — Public Record

The proceedings were public record, and the enrollee was the most-reviewed match result in tournament history, and the two facts met in the term's third month the way weather fronts meet, with the collision audible from every direction at once.

Cael charted it because it was the largest apparatus of attention ever pointed at him and cataloguing was how he kept his footing in weather. The broadsides had the challenge within a week of its service — Withrow's one-line answer had guaranteed that; a chancellor who wrote her own document expected it read — and they built the story out of the parts they had, which were the Norhold interpretations with a registry seal added: the concealed-classification theory, now *confirmed* (the challenge proved the registry suspected it); the fraud theory, now *vindicated* (why else compel re-evaluation); the suppressed-Path theory, now the darkest and most popular (the Compact was covering its own error). A dozen versions, printed in four cities, every one of them still wrong, and every one of them now carrying, in its final paragraph, the phrase *evaluation-integrity question* as though the phrase meant something its authors understood.

The economy of it was the part he could actually chart, so he charted the economy.

Karis's assistant, sent down to Ostrand on an archive errand in the month's first week, came back with the whole market on four sheets and a receipt, because Karis had told her to buy one of everything and account for it, and because the girl had understood that *account for it* meant something more than the sum. Cael read her sheet at the annex table with real admiration. Six titles in Ostrand alone. The assay transcript, still on sale at six marks, two years old, in its fourth printing. A new pamphlet at three marks — *The Halcenvane Question, With The Instrument Entire* — which reproduced the challenge's first page accurately and the rest of it from a clerk's recollection, wrongly. The woodcut at a copper and a half, unchanged, still showing a boy who looked nothing like him in a stance no practitioner had ever adopted. And two competing broadside series at a half-copper the sheet, issued weekly, whose entire business was the proceedings and whose sales, the print-seller had told her without being asked, had tripled in a fortnight.

"She asked him what sold before the challenge," Karis said, reading her own assistant's sheet. "He said the tournament. She asked what sold before the tournament. He said nothing; there was no series. He said the series was started *for* you, in the spring, by a man in the wharf street who bet his press on it and has now bought a second press." She set the sheet down. "There is a man in Ostrand whose children eat because you cannot be classified. I want that in your notebook and not in mine."

Cael put it in the notebook. He put beside it the number the sheet made available for the first time, which was the one he had wanted for two years and had never been able to price: at a half-copper a sheet, two series, weekly, four cities — the continent was spending something on the order of a working man's yearly wage every week on the question of what he was.

*Nobody is buying the answer,* he wrote. *There isn't one for sale. They're buying the asking. That's a market that doesn't close when the ruling comes down, and the men who own the presses know it, which is why the series that started in the spring is still running in the winter. Whatever the seat decides, it will be printed within four days, at a half-copper, beside a woodcut of a boy who isn't me.*

The correspondents came up the bluff road twice that month and were declined at the lodge twice, courteously, on a standing instruction Withrow had issued in twelve words and Bracken had posted at the gate in the registry's own type: *the academy's answer to the challenge is its filing; the filing is public.* The second time, one of them waited four hours at the switchback in the cold on the theory that a boy of seventeen would eventually come down a road, and Rooke — who had gone down at dusk on his own business and had not been told to do anything at all — stopped, and looked at him, and said, "He trains at dawn and he doesn't come down. You'll get pneumonia and no copy," and the man thanked him and left, and that was the whole of the academy's press policy for the term.

The post drowned Halcenvane. Bracken's counter took two sacks a day by the month's second week — academies writing to ask whether the demonstration provision was safe to invoke; guild houses writing to withdraw or affirm sponsorships they had never actually extended; three registry-law faculties writing for the response's text, which Withrow released whole, because a filing that perfect deserved readers; and the ordinary mail of a continent that had adopted a story, some of it kind, some of it not, all of it addressed to a boy who read every piece and answered none, per an information policy two years old.

Bracken ran the counter the way he ran everything, which was to build a procedure on the second day rather than complain on the tenth. He put a second table in the records hall's outer room, split the intake four ways with a chalked card above each pile, and entered the day's count in the fire-watch's ledger every evening in his own hand, because the sacks were a fact about the institution and facts about the institution belonged in a ledger. Two hundred and eleven items on the heaviest day. Fourteen of them from chartered academies, which he answered himself, in four lines each, identically, and showed Cael one of the answers unprompted.

"*The demonstration provision remains lawful and remains in force. The academy is not in a position to advise other institutions on their own enrollments.*" He blotted it. "Fourteen academies wrote to ask whether the clause is safe. Not one of them wrote to ask whether you are. I don't offer that as a comment on human nature. I offer it as a description of what a registrar's post looks like when a provision goes under challenge: institutions write about the provision." He tied the day's bundle. "The rest of the continent writes about the boy. Your friend is sorting that pile."

Lira had adopted the sacks. It was the found family's oldest arithmetic — somebody carried what nobody could put down — and she sorted the post at the residence table each evening with the flat efficiency she gave bout sheets: the academies' inquiries to Bracken's tray, the sponsorship letters to Withrow's, the faculties' requests to Karis, and the rest into three piles she named aloud the first night and never renamed. "Kind. Unkind. Unhinged." The kind pile was the largest and the strangest — a barge-master's widow from the confluence who had watched him fight her husband's rival and wanted him to know the river was on his side; a Copper-tier washout from a hill academy who had read the transcript and wanted to explain, at four pages' length, that the corridor was real because he had walked one; a child's letter, in a schoolroom hand, asking whether it hurt to be unclassified. Lira read that one aloud, once, and put it in the kind pile, and nobody at the table said anything for a moment, and Brom refilled the cups.

"Answer none of them," Karis said. "Keep all of them. When this is over, the kind pile is the only record of the year that wasn't written by an office."

"You're a *case* now," Ephram observed, reading the day's sack at the residence table with the cohort captain's file forgotten under his elbow. "At Norhold you were a story. Stories end. Cases get *cited*."

"I've been a case since Denvash."

"You've been a *file* since Denvash. Files are private." Ephram held up a broadside from a city Cael had never seen. "This is a case. Three faculties are teaching you. Somebody at the coastal academy has written a thesis." He set it down. "Try to be a disappointing precedent."

The cost landed on the others first, which Cael did not see for nine days and then could not stop seeing.

It reached Lira at the rating table. The Silver program's clerk had begun logging visitors to the second floor's gallery — Rooke's instruction, unexplained — and the log ran to twenty-three names in a fortnight, of whom four were from academies with no business on the bluff and one had asked the clerk, in the ordinary way of a man making conversation, how often the unclassified enrollee came to watch. Rooke read the log, said nothing to the clerk, and moved the Silver drills to the closed floor for the rest of the month, which cost the program its high windows and its light. "He didn't tell me why," Lira said, at the rail, rolling the burst-shoulder while she said it — iced its two days, cleared for the frames on the Thirdsday, reporting clean under the closed floor's poorer light ever since. "He told the clerk to keep logging."

It reached Brom in the Velmere post, which arrived that fortnight at three letters instead of two, and which he read at the table, folded, without comment, as he had read it since the recess.

It reached Karis at the archive counter in Ostrand, where a request she had filed under the academy's budget code — the certified copy of the founding-era signatures, ordered before any of this — came back with a query attached asking her to confirm the research standing under which it was made. "It's a proper query," she said, in the annex, at the lamp. "Entirely proper. They are entitled to confirm a standing before they certify a copy for it. I have made two hundred archive requests in five years and I have never been asked to confirm a standing." She wrote the confirmation, and filed it, and then sat for a moment with her hands flat on the table in the way she did when she was deciding whether something was a finding. "Once is a clerk being careful. I'll tell you if it's twice."

And it reached the assistant, who was asked in the covered walk by a second-year she barely knew what the researcher's enrollee was *actually* like, and who answered, at length and with citations, that the enrollee was enrolled on a lawful basis adjudicated at public record and renewed at evaluation four times, and who reported the exchange to Karis afterward in some distress at having been, she said, boring.

"You weren't boring," Karis told her. "You were accurate at speed under a question you weren't expecting. That's the whole of the job." She turned back to her page. "Boring is the point. Be boring for another year and I'll have taught you something."

*Every one of them is being asked about me now,* the log got that night, *by people who have no standing to ask, in corridors and at counters and across rating tables, and every one of them is answering the way we agreed years ago nobody would ever have to. I wrote last month that whatever this costs me it shouldn't cost them. It has begun costing them and it hasn't cost me a thing yet. Note the order. The order is the thing I got wrong.*

Umber's transmission arrived in the month's second week, and it was the coldest document of the term that was not addressed to Cael, because it was addressed to the proceedings and it was true.

The adjudication office at Norhold, having referred the exhibition bout's result to the registry for evaluation of a demonstrated capability outside classification — the eleven words, the referral, the tables that would hold or teach something about the tables — now formally transmitted the referred file to the proceedings that had taken it up: the match record, the nine panel charts, the unscorable entry. Copied, sealed, sent, under the Chief Adjudicator's own signature, with a covering note in the tower's dry grammar: *the adjudication office's referral having been taken up by the registry, the referred file is transmitted entire for the proceedings' use, and the office notes that the file is the complete record of the office's finding and contains no characterization beyond it.*

It reached Halcenvane as a courtesy copy in the ordinary post, and Karis read it three times and then took it to the annex and did not come out for two hours, and when she came out she had it mapped.

"Understand what he's done, because it's important and it's the opposite of what it looks like." She laid the covering note on the table. "The office referred a result to the registry for evaluation. That referral has been taken up. Once it's taken up, the referring office has no discretion at all — the file goes, entire, on demand. He couldn't have withheld a page of it if he'd wanted to." She put her finger on the last clause. "So he wrote *the file is the complete record of the office's finding and contains no characterization beyond it.* Nine words of it are procedure. The rest is a man on the record refusing, in advance, to be quoted as having meant anything by it."

"Will the seat read the clause?" the counsel asked.

"The clerk will read it into the minute, because it's part of the covering note and the code says the covering note is read." Karis closed the file. "And in a hundred years somebody reading the minute will find a Chief Adjudicator declining to characterize his own referral in the same paragraph that transmits it, and will understand exactly what he thought of the proceedings he was transmitting it to. That's the entire remedy available to him, and he used all of it."

Cael took the covering note to the window and read the nine words twice more and filed the thing that had begun to trouble him about the season's honest men, which was that every one of them, given no lever, reached for the record — and that the record was the one instrument the seat had shown, at the first session, that it received without weighing.

The second session at Ostrand ran on the same trading floor under the same seal, and it was an exhibits session — the code's grammar for a sitting at which the record was built and nothing was decided — and Cael attended it, as Withrow had ruled he would attend everything, and charted it as the machine's second appearance in his life with no face on it.

The gallery was fuller. The correspondents had multiplied and the registry-literate had brought friends, and the room had the specific density of a public watching a case it had already decided in print. The seal sat as it had sat: a competent official of no age, conducting. The counsel entered the response's exhibits in sequence, each read into the minute by the clerk, each received without expression — Prynn's index, Gault's panel minutes, the inspection finding, forty pages on the integrity provisions — and Cael charted the seal through every one and got nothing, and entered the nothing that night as the session's finding: *the seat received eleven pounds of the truth exactly as it received the staffing note the clerk read before it. The instrument doesn't weigh. It receives.*

He timed the entering, because timing it was the only measurement available. Two hundred and six items, read into the minute by the clerk at the rate the clerk read: title, date, provenance, receipt — nine seconds an item on the short ones, twenty on the ones with a certifying line to recite. Prynn's index took seventeen. Gault's four sets of panel minutes took a minute and eight seconds together. The inspection year's finding — eleven days of the Compact's best evaluator in a records hall, a chancellor's tenure staked, a whole autumn of an academy's life — took twenty-two seconds, because the certifying line was long.

The forty pages on the integrity provisions took nine.

Cael added it as it ran and had the total before the clerk did: fifty-one minutes and some seconds to read the whole documentary architecture of four years into a room, at a clerk's pace, without a single word of it being disputed by anyone present. Jent did not object once. There was nothing to object to; the exhibits were what they said they were, and the petitioner had conceded that in writing before the term began. The counsel entered them anyway, all two hundred and six, because the record was the point and the counsel was a professional.

At the fortieth minute Cael stopped charting the seal and started charting the gallery, and found that a third of it had stopped watching. Not from hostility. From the ordinary attrition of people who had come to see a case and were being read a list. The correspondents wrote nothing for nine minutes together. He entered it that night, and it was the coldest line the session produced: *the truth is admissible, unopposed, and boring. They have built a room in which our best work is a procedural delay.*

Withrow's release of the response to the faculties had its own small scene, at the counter, because Bracken had objected — mildly, on procedure; a respondent's filing was the respondent's, and three registry-law faculties asking for it was three institutions about to have opinions. "Let them have opinions," Withrow said, signing the release. "The filing is perfect. Karis says so and Jent said so on the record. A perfect filing that nobody reads is a private loss. A perfect filing three faculties teach is a public fact, and public facts outlast rulings." She handed the copies to the courier. "I spent twenty years in the registry learning what survives a seat's discretion. Nothing survives it in the room. Everything survives it in the libraries."

Jent entered it as an exhibit at the second session. Cael watched him do it — the referred file laid on the petitioner's table, the eleven words read into the proceedings' record by the clerk in a registry voice, the seal receiving it without expression — and turned the thing over that night with the bleak appreciation the season was teaching him to keep for the honest men.

*Umber wrote the truest sentence any instrument ever wrote about me — no number goes here — and refused every prettier alternative, at cost, all night, in front of his own panel. Vastin evaluated me for a day and wrote "continue observation" and stood in a chamber and said the finding was the instruments', not the boy's. Ilsev filed her referrals through channels, properly, with citations, because unanswered questions are pursued through process. Every one of them told the truth. And every one of their true reports is now an exhibit for the petition.*

*The honest men keep filing true reports, and the machine keeps using them as evidence. That's not a flaw in the honest men. It's what the machine is for. It runs on true reports the way a mill runs on water — it doesn't care where the river wanted to go.*

Daeva's two documents arrived in the same week, and the delegation understood what the week was the moment the first was read aloud.

The first was public. It came to Halcenvane as a courtesy copy, already filed — a statement to the proceedings, submitted at the registry seat under the filer's own name, rank, and academy, which the code permitted any practitioner of Silver or above to do in an institutional matter and which, Karis established within the hour, no practitioner of Gold had ever done in the registry's recorded history. It came up the bluff road on an ordinary evening in the ordinary post, in a registry-seat envelope that Bracken opened at the counter, read, read again, and then did the only unprofessional thing Cael ever saw him do in three years, which was to leave the counter unattended and walk the sheet to the residence himself at something faster than his own pace.

The table was at dinner. He put it down in front of the counsel without a word, because it was hers to read first, and stood at the end of the table with his hands behind his back and did not sit when Withrow offered him the chair.

The counsel read it at the residence table because the counsel had the copy, and she read it in the voice she used for documents that were going to be cited for a century:

*To the proceedings convened upon the enrollment of the unclassified practitioner of Halcenvane, from Daeva, Auremont Academy, Storm Path, Gold-tier Rank Three, in her own name and under her own rank:*

*I fought him. Your instruments failed him, not the other way around. Classify the instruments.*

That was the whole statement. Three sentences, on registry-seat stock, given under her own hand — the youngest Gold-tier in continental history, the system's favorite anomaly, the practitioner every instrument on the continent talked about instead of measured, entering the record on the side of the boy the instruments could not hold, in fourteen words.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Lira put her cup down without drinking from it. Brom read the sheet after the counsel set it down, once, completely, the way he read the things that cost, and then turned it face up on the table so the words were toward the room, which was not a gesture he had a precedent for.

"Fourteen words," Ephram said, at last. "She could have written forty pages. Auremont has a whole faculty for it."

"Forty pages would have been an argument," Withrow said. She had not touched the sheet. "An argument invites an answer and can be lost. That is a *statement of a witness*, in fourteen words, from the highest-credentialed practitioner alive, and there is no answer to it in the code, because the code did not anticipate anyone at that rank ever bothering." She looked at Bracken, who was still standing. "Registrar. In your thirty years."

"Never," said Bracken. "Not at Gold. Not once." He picked up the envelope, which he had brought with him, and turned it over, and put it down. "She paid the filing fee. Four marks. There's a receipt."

Karis had the statement's legal status mapped by the evening, in the annex, one lamp, the code open at the provision that permitted it: "Any practitioner of Silver or above may enter a statement in an institutional matter touching evaluation standards. It's a fossil — pre-standardization; the era when senior practitioners' opinions were part of how evaluation *worked*. It has been used, in the registry's recorded history, sixty-one times. By Silvers. By Golds, never — Golds don't need to enter statements; Golds are what the statements are about." She turned the page. "And *classify the instruments* is not a request the code contains a form for. She's filed a demand no office can process, under a provision no office remembers, at a rank no one has ever used it from. It's the most Daeva document I've ever read and I've read her challenge filing."

"Will it matter?" Lira asked.

"To the seat? No. To the record?" Karis closed the code. "Sixty-two. Somebody a century from now will read the sixty-second and wonder what the proceedings were thinking. That's the only jurisdiction she's ever had. She used it."

The proceedings noted it at the second session and set it aside. The clerk read it into the minute; the seal received it; Jent did not object, because there was nothing in it to object to, and the seat did not engage it, because engaging it was not contemplated by the schedule. The rhyme was deliberate and everyone at the respondent's table heard it: a chamber with no windows had received the Compact's best evaluator's testimony with courtesy and set it aside, and now the proceedings had received the continent's best practitioner's and done the same — the two most credentialed voices the system had ever produced, both discounted, in the same grammar, because credentials had never been the currency. Cael filed the rhyme in the log with the flat care he kept for findings about the machine's character: *it doesn't matter who says the instruments are wrong. It doesn't matter how high up the ladder they stand when they say it. The ladder isn't the thing that hears.*

Lira's reading of the statement came at the rail the next evening, and it came sideways, the way her important readings did. "Zerin told me *be there*. Next cycle, Silver bracket, in four words, at a board, and then walked off, and I've been building toward it since." She watched the yard. "Daeva's just done the same thing for you. In fourteen words, on registry stock, from the top of the ladder. *Classify the instruments.* That's not a defense. That's *be there* — she's telling the whole machine where she'll be standing when it's done, and daring it to file her somewhere else." She pushed off the rail. "Auremont builds them strange. I'm starting to think it's the only thing they build right."

The second document was private, and it came in the ordinary post, in a hand he had seen once — on a challenge filing, one word, *Overdue* — and he took it to the window, because letters lived at windows, and read it there.

*They did this to me the other way — built the ladder to my height and called it fairness. Nobody files challenges against a favorite. Don't mistake what's happening to you for procedure. It stopped being procedure the day they couldn't score you.*

*— D.*

*The rematch stands. Keep yourself unclassified.*

He read it four times, which was the number he read the letters that mattered, and sat with it while the residence court went dark, because it was direct and without comfort and it was, he understood after the fourth reading, the truest letter anyone had ever written him about his own situation — and it had been written by the one person on the continent who had lived the situation's mirror. *Built the ladder to my height and called it fairness.* She had said the sentence once before, at a ring's north arc, in the plural. She had said it now in the singular, on paper, to him, as the only credential she had left to offer: *I know what the machine's love is worth. I've had all of it.*

*Keep yourself unclassified.*

The log got the letter's whole architecture that night, and the reading:

*She's the only person on the continent the system loves who knows exactly what its love is worth. "Keep yourself unclassified." From her, that's not a joke. That's a strategy. Filed.*

Then he capped the pen and did the filing itself, which was the part the word had been standing in for: behind the volume's front cover, beside a page in Hesk's hand and a page in Vell's. Three pages, three people who had never met one another, each of whom had told him the same thing about the machine in a different grammar. He squared them against the spine, and closed the cover on the three of them, and set the volume down on the sill, squared to the edge.

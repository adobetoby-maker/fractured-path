# Chapter 15 — The Cache

They came to the annex by three routes across an hour, per the floor schedule that was not a floor schedule.

Seln had written it out on the back of a wing-three allocation sheet and made them read it and then taken the sheet back and burned it in the assessment office's own grate, which was, he observed, the only grate at Halcenvane whose ash nobody ever looked at. Karis and Brom by the library's lower stair at the ninth bell, separately, four minutes apart, Brom carrying a crate of periodicals he actually needed. Lira by the practice wing's service corridor at the quarter, on the strapped ankle, because a limping enrollee going the long way to avoid stairs was a thing a hundred people had watched her do that week. Seln himself by no route anyone could have described. And Cael last, at the half, down the forty-foot corridor two floors below the library where the stone changed from dressed to rough and the lamps ran out twenty feet before the door did.

The man was standing in the annex's single doorway when he came, watching the approach in both directions — and had been, Cael understood, for some minutes, and would have gone on being for some minutes more if the corridor had asked him to. He stepped aside to let Cael pass. He closed the door. He set the case on the room's one table under the room's one lamp.

And the five of them were sealed into a stone room two floors below the library with the most expensive object at Halcenvane between them.

The room had been chosen, and Cael read the choosing while the others found seats: one door, no window, a ventilation shaft too high and too narrow to carry sound to anywhere that had ears; walls of the bluff's own stone, three feet through; a table that had been in the room longer than the academy's current charter; and a single lamp, set by Seln at the table's centre rather than its end, so that every hand at that table would be lit and no face would be in shadow. Sightlines controlled. Five chairs. Nothing on the walls. It was the least comfortable room Cael had ever been asked to sit in and the safest, and the two facts were the same fact.

He opened it without preamble. A keeper's economy: no speech, no framing, the lock worked with a small key he had carried on his person and not on any ring, the lid raised, the contents there.

Objects first, and the intellect second — because the intellect needed the eyes to have gone ahead of it, and Seln knew that better than anyone in the room, and had built the next twenty minutes around it.

The case held fifteen years of an intelligence officer's private duplicates, and they were organized the way the man was organized, which was completely and invisibly: not bundled by date or by posting but by a system whose key lived in his head, so that the case looked, to the uninitiated eye, like a scuffed traveling case full of ordinary registry paper, and was in fact a map with the legend removed. He lifted the layers out in order and named them as he set them on the table, in the flat register he kept for handing paper across a counter — and that register was the only thing in the room that did not shake, and its steadiness was the room's engine.

Case files, first.

Reclassification orders — the registry's ordinary instrument for correcting a filed classification, a form Cael had handled twice in his life and Karis perhaps four hundred times — each one clipped, in Seln's own hand, to the Kindling record it had corrected. He set the stack down and did not open it. Karis opened it, because Karis opened everything, and worked from the top with her repaired fingers.

They did not match.

She read the first pair aloud, because reading aloud was how she made a document real to a room: a Kindling record from a district station in the northern provinces, thirty-one years old, the assessor's own contemporaneous hand describing a reading with the specificity assessors used when they were interested — *reach broad, register high and stable, the subject holding a second-order declaration through the count without instruction* — and, clipped to it, an order dated nine weeks later assigning the subject to a Path two grades below anything that reading could support. No intervening event. No re-assessment. No appeal, because there had been no dispute; the subject had been fourteen and had been told what she was, and had presumably gone home and become it.

"There's nothing between them," Karis said. "That's the part I want everyone to see before we go any further. It isn't a corrupt correction. A corrupt correction has *reasons* in it — a second opinion, a challenged reading, a note from somebody's uncle. There's nothing here. There's a true reading, and there's an order, and there is nine weeks of nothing." She turned the pair over and set it down and took the next. "This one's twelve years old. Coastal station." She read four lines of it and stopped and did not read the rest aloud, and put it down, and took the next.

"Forty of these," Seln said, setting a second stack beside the first. "I stopped clipping them at forty. There are more. I stopped because forty was enough to prove it and the clipping had started to feel like filing, and I did not want to be filing them."

One pair from the middle of the stack stopped everyone. It was nineteen years old, from a river town Karis had never heard of, and the Kindling record had a second sheet clipped behind it that the others did not: a parent's letter, entered into the file at the district station's own initiative, asking why the reading the family had been given at the ceremony and the classification on the certificate were different words. The letter was three paragraphs, careful, in the handwriting of somebody who had drafted it more than once. Beneath it, in the district clerk's hand, was the answer that had been sent — the standard form, courteous, four lines long, explaining that the ceremony reading was preliminary and the certificate was the instrument of record, which was true, and which was the correct thing for the clerk to write, and which the clerk had believed.

"That's the whole design in one file," Karis said, after a long moment. "The father was right. The station clerk was honest. The form was accurate. And the answer was a lie that nobody in the chain told." She clipped the pair back together with more care than she had used on any of the others. "Nineteen years ago. That girl is thirty-three."

Field reports, second.

His own — reports on practitioners he had been assigned, across nineteen postings, to watch: the register of an intelligence officer's actual work, dry, exact, dated, weather included, and, laid beside the reclassification orders, damning. He set them out in three courses, and he did the pairing himself this time, because the pairing needed his memory. A filed Copper working at Iron for nine months in a shipping town, observed and logged forty-one times. A filed Iron whose declarations read Silver at every observation across two years, and whose file at the registry seat said what it had said since she was fourteen. A filed Bronze, sixty-eight years old, running a small fishing concern, whose habitual working technique Seln had recorded in eight separate entries because he had thought at the time that his own eyes were wrong.

"Watched and logged and never once corrected upward," he said. "The orders correct down. Never up. In fifteen years of duplicates I have not found one order that corrected a practitioner to a *higher* Path than the record supported. The machinery has a direction. I noticed the direction in my sixth year. It took me the seventh to believe it."

Registry memoranda, third.

Copies of copies, administrative language receding into the past like a corridor lit at intervals: two centuries of it, laid out along the table's whole length in dated courses because there was no other way to hold that much paper in one glance. The type-styles aged as the hand moved down the table. The seals changed format three times. The paper stock went from the registry's modern buff to something rougher and yellower and then, at the far end, to a laid sheet with a watermark none of them could identify. And the phrasing settled as you went, softening and euphemizing upward through the strata like sediment reworked by water, until at the oldest end it stopped being a euphemism at all and became one recurring administrative phrase, printed plainly, in the flat voice of a body writing to itself: *corrective classification at Kindling evaluation.*

Brom, who had said nothing for twenty minutes, put one finger on the oldest memorandum without touching it and said, "That's a form. That's not an instruction. That's a *form*." Which was the observation of a man whose family had run a ledger for four generations, and was the second-most-important thing anyone said that night.

And the artifact at the case's floor, in a wax-paper sleeve older than everything above it.

Seln lifted it last and set it down alone, apart from the stacks, at the table's head, because it was not of their kind: a single leaf, brittle at three corners, a protocol-era maintenance leaf — genuine, its provenance documented in Seln's own cipher on the sleeve in four lines nobody else in the room could read, its type-face older than any memorandum above it — referencing the Arbiter evaluation sub-layer's *advisory corrections function.*

Nobody touched it. Karis leaned over it with both hands flat on the table on either side, the way she read things she was not going to be allowed to keep, and Cael watched her go still, which she almost never did.

---

Seln talked then, longer than any of them had heard him talk, in the flattest voice the annex had ever held, because the content did not need help and help would have been an insult to it.

He told it in four movements, because four was the shape the truth had.

**How it started as insurance.** An operative's habit, learned in the first posting and never broken: every posting, every anomaly, every document that touched a thing he did not understand — duplicated, and kept. Not for a purpose. For the reason a man in his trade kept everything, which was that the value of a document was never known at the time it crossed your desk, and a keeper who discarded on the day was a keeper who was wrong, eventually, about something that mattered.

"I did not set out to find this," he said. "I set out to be unsurprised. The finding was a side effect of a filing habit. That is the whole heroism of it, and I would like it understood, because I am about to be spoken of as though I did something, and I did not do anything. I kept paper. The paper did the rest."

"How much of it?" Lira asked.

"By weight, in the case, eleven pounds. By count, three hundred and eighty documents." He said the numbers without effort, which told them he had never once needed to look. "By risk — every single one of them, every day, for fifteen years, in nineteen rooms I did not choose and could not lock."

**How it stopped being insurance.** The year — his seventh, at a coastal posting, in a winter he described in one clause and then set aside — he noticed the anomalies had a *direction*. He had duplicated reclassification orders for years as a matter of habit, and in the seventh year, cross-indexing them against his own field reports for an unrelated reason, he saw the thing the direction made undeniable: inconvenient abilities corrected downward, politically useful ones left alone, across postings, across decades, across more years of file-dates than any conspiracy of living men could span.

"A conspiracy has a generation," he said. "Men scheme, and retire, and die, and the scheme dies or changes with them. I have worked against three. They all had the same shape: a beginning, a peak, a betrayal, an ending, and a paper trail that got *sloppier* as the founders aged out and the inheritors stopped understanding what they had inherited. This does not change. The oldest order in the case and the newest are the same order in different type. There is no sloppiness anywhere in it. There is no drift. There is no place where somebody's nephew was let through. Whatever is doing this outlived everyone who could have started it, and has not once, in two hundred years, made the kind of mistake that living men make." He looked at the table's far end where the oldest course lay. "That is the sentence I spent my eighth year refusing and my ninth accepting."

**How he walked away from the job.** In the only way his trade allowed, which was by staying in it and keeping. A man in his position could not resign; a resigned intelligence officer with fifteen years of duplicates was a witness, and he had explained to Cael on a stair what witnesses were. So he had stayed in, and shrunk himself to a posting nobody watched, and filed true empty reports for a decade — true in every particular, empty in every particular that mattered, a decade of a man reporting accurately that there was nothing to report — and carried the case through nineteen shelves, and become the best clerk on a bluff-top academy's counter, and waited.

"Not for a purpose," he said. "For the reason keepers wait, which is that the day the case becomes worth its cost is never known in advance, and a keeper who spends it early is a keeper who was wrong."

**And why now.** Four sentences, and he counted them off the way he counted everything.

"The paper came to the gate. The paper's next grade does not knock. And the boy has been fighting inside the instrument for a term believing the instrument is merely broken, which is the one condition I built the case to end, on the one day it could still matter." A pause of exactly its own length. "The catechism said sealed. The catechism was correct for fifteen years. It stopped being correct on a road cut three days ago. I am not sure this is right. That is how I know it is."

Then the finding, stated once, plainly, for the record the annex would never keep:

"The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for two hundred years. At Kindling. Through the Arbiter protocol itself. Via a modification so old that no current official knows it is there."

The room took it the way the room had been built to take it. Karis reached for the memoranda with the expression she reserved for primary sources — not shock; *appetite*, the researcher meeting the document of her life, both hands already moving to sort the oldest course into date order before her mind had finished hearing the sentence.

Brom went completely still, the stillness of a man who has been handed a weight and is finding his stance under it. He was thinking about his mother's ledger, and about four generations of a family who had believed their number was the honest one, and he said so ten minutes later in six words and not before.

And Lira asked the only question that could be asked, in one word: "How many?"

"I stopped estimating," Seln said. "Estimating felt like filing."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The lamp did its small work at the centre of the table. Two floors above them the library's night bell rang and was answered and stopped, and the ordinary institution went on running its ordinary evening over their heads, correctly, on time, in order.

Then the room did what rooms do, which was ask the small questions first because the large one had no handle on it.

Karis went at the provenance, because provenance was the only ground she trusted. Where had each memorandum been duplicated, and from what, and by whom; whether any of the three hundred and eighty could be shown to be a copy of a copy of a thing that had never existed; whether a hostile examiner given the whole case and a year could construct an innocent account of it. Seln answered every question without hurry or emphasis and answered the last one honestly, which was the answer she wanted and not the one she hoped for: "Yes. Given the case alone, a competent examiner constructs a story in which a disturbed clerk collected coincidences for fifteen years. It is not a good story. It does not need to be. It needs to be available."

"And with the leaf?" She did not touch the wax-paper sleeve when she said it; she moved one finger toward it and stopped.

"With the leaf, the story becomes that a disturbed clerk collected coincidences for fifteen years and also forged a protocol-era maintenance leaf, which no living person has been shown to be capable of doing, on paper that cannot be sourced, in a hand that cannot be matched." A pause. "Which is a worse story and will still be told."

Lira went at the operational question, which was hers. "How many people have looked in that case?"

"Before tonight, one."

"For fifteen years."

"For fifteen years."

She sat back and put her hands flat on her knees and looked at the ceiling for a moment, and what she said was not about the falsification at all. "You had this the whole time you were watching us. You had this in a room down the hill from us while you were writing your empty reports about a kid nobody could classify, and you sat at our table and ate our food and let Karis argue with you about filing conventions." She brought her eyes down. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I want you to know I've noticed what it cost you to be pleasant."

Seln considered that for a moment as though it were a document.

"It did not cost me anything," he said. "That is the part I would like understood, and I will say it once. A man carrying a thing like this for fifteen years is not being brave at a table. He is being *rested*." He set his hand flat beside the stacks. "Your table was the first place in nineteen postings where I did not have to work while I was in the room. That is what the food was."

And Brom said his six words, ten minutes after he had gone still, into the pause that followed, in the register that was weight and not speeches.

"My mother's ledger was always right."

Nobody asked him to explain it, because everybody at that table could do the arithmetic: four generations of a family who had believed that the number a registry gave you was the honest measure of what you were, who had priced a son's whole life against a Copper classification and found the price fair, because the classification was a *fact*. It was still a fact. It had simply never been a measurement. Brom sat with that for the rest of the evening and did not put it down, and would not put it down for weeks, and when he finally answered it he would answer it with three boxes.

---

And then the annex's second movement, and the debt paid, because the case could not lie open on the table with only one archive in it.

Cael answered in kind. He stood up to do it, which he had not planned, and did it standing, on the far side of the lamp, with the case open between them — and he told Seln the mechanism. The whole of it. The integration, the fragments, the notices, the log. The eleven seconds of silence in a border town and a sigil that had paused and returned a word in brackets and could not do anything else with him. The eight architectures, each with its source and its date and its cost. The stakes rule, and where it had been drawn, and what it had been drawn to prevent. The line in writing at a window: directed, open-eyed, or not at all.

He told it plainly, in the register the truth was owed, and it took a quarter of an hour, and Seln did not interrupt once.

Lira watched Seln while it was told, which was its own kind of guard duty, and reported afterward that the man's face had done nothing at all for the first nine minutes and had then, once, for perhaps a second, done something at the sentence about the sigil pausing — and that she could not name what it was and had decided not to guess.

Karis, who had heard the whole of it in installments over two years and had never once heard it delivered end to end, discovered that hearing it end to end was different, and wrote nothing down for the whole quarter of an hour, which was the longest she had gone without a pen in that room.

Then Seln asked four questions, in the order an intelligence officer asks them, and every one of them was about exposure rather than wonder, and the choice of questions was itself the man's answer to being told.

Who else. Four, now five. Who suspects. Vell, in a shape; Daeva, in a different shape; the adjudication office, in the shape of a blank column. Whether the notices were physical, and whether anything he had ever written could be read by anyone but himself — no, and the log was the only physical record and it lived in a satchel now. And what happened, precisely, to the source.

"Nothing," Cael said. "They keep it. It isn't taken from them. Lira still has everything she had. That was the first thing I ever checked and I've checked it eight times since."

"Good," Seln said. "Then you may say it to me twice more in your life, and never to anyone else, and you will not say it again tonight." He squared a stack that did not need squaring. "The most dangerous thing on this table is no longer the case."

And when it was told, Cael told the last of it, because the last of it was the debt.

"Shadow-adjacent." He said it looking at the man. "I took it during the records-office operation. The night you let me watch. I've never deployed it. Not once, anywhere, publicly, in two and a half years — and you should know where the seal held, because you're the reason it held. At Norhold I fought a Gold-tier match I nearly lost, four exchanges, everything spent, the banking dead — and the one fragment I kept sealed through all of it was yours, because deploying Shadow-adjacent in front of eight thousand people and three recording scribes would have made someone ask where an unclassified enrollee had witnessed sustained Shadow work, and the question walks to you, and I owed the seal more than I owed the match."

The annex was silent.

Seln looked at him — at the boy who had kept his cover at Gold-tier cost, across a tournament, for a debt the boy had filed two years ago as unpayable — for a long moment, in the flattest room of his life. Something happened in his face that was not an expression and was over before it could be named. Then he said his sentence. It was six words.

"You kept my file too, then."

That was all. It was the closest thing to being moved he would ever show, and it was real, and it was said in the register of a man who had just learned that the ledger he thought ran one way had been running both, for two years, without his knowledge, kept by a boy on the far side of a cover — and neither of them added anything, because the two archives were open on the same table now, and the two keepers had each been holding the other's, and there was nothing to say that the six words had not said.

The seal stayed on. That was decided in the following minute, by the two of them, in a dozen words total, and the logic had merely updated: a defector's tradecraft in an unclassified enrollee's hands would confirm the surveillance-era acquisition to anyone with a file and an afternoon, and there were more files now, and more afternoons. Karis wrote *seal maintained, grounds revised* in her own register and drew a line under it.

They put the case back together at the second bell after midnight — Karis keeping the memoranda, by agreement, until morning, and signing for them in a private register that had no seal on it, because she asked to sign and Seln let her, and both of them understood exactly what the signing was for.

---

The log, that night, was where the whole term's weight came to rest, and Cael wrote it in the new satchel by lamplight with the case's contents committed to memory and the case itself back on its shelf across the campus:

*Two hundred years. It was never a malfunction. Karis has the memoranda until morning.*

*Tomorrow we find out what the truth weighs.*

*Tonight I keep thinking about every Kindling I've ever heard described — Alis Trent's, Joren's, Daeva's at thirteen in a garden court, mine at fourteen with a sigil that paused eleven seconds and could not correct me because there was no lesser bin to correct me into — and the room behind the room, correcting, on every one of them that could be corrected, for two hundred years, in a hand no living person signs.*

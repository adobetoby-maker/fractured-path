# Chapter 9 — Within the System

The residence's small room was not a room anyone had chosen for councils. It had been the winter store when the residence was a farmhouse, and it kept the shape: one door, one shuttered window, a stone floor with a drain in the corner that had drained something else three hundred years ago, and a table too big for it that could not be got out again because the door had been narrowed at some point by somebody who did not think about tables. The delegation used it for the same reason Seln had approved of it the first time he was brought there, which was that its one window faced the wall of the stores range four feet away and its walls were three feet of stone, and a voice in it went nowhere at all.

They had used it four times that term. The first three had been legal. This one was not.

Lira had asked for it on the second morning after the ruling and had asked for it in the delegation's own grammar — a note on the residence board, *small room, Fifth-day, after supper, five* — and everyone who read the board understood that a note requesting five people by number rather than by name was a note about the fifth. Karis had brought the proceedings' papers because she brought them everywhere, and had put them face-down on the far table before anyone sat, which was her own arrangement and which she made without comment. Brom had brought the tea and the room's one good chair, carried in on his shoulder from the common room because the small room's chairs were benches and Brom had opinions about how long a council could run on a bench. Seln had come in last and taken the bench's middle, which was where he had sat at every table for a year and a half, and had put nothing on the table in front of him at all.

Cael counted the room, because counting rooms was what he did on the way into things.

Five people. One lamp, turned to three-quarters. Two years, four months since the four of them had first sat at a table together in a boarding-house in Ardenmere with a legal theory and no money. Twelve days since the seal had said *lawful* and *proceeding* in the same paragraph.

Lira put it on the table on the first night of the term's second half, and she put it there plainly, because plainness was the only register she trusted for a thing this size.

"The proceedings are a corridor. Karis drew it; the ruling confirmed it; every door opens one way and the seal has said so in its own type." She had the drill-book on her knee and did not look at it. "The day-after is being inventoried — Seln's five offices, Hesk's shop, the ninth category. The Registry Line is four days east of Ostrand. I've checked the road." She looked around the room — the four of them and Seln at the bench's middle, one lamp, the proceedings' papers on the far table face-down by her own arrangement. "So I'm asking the question out loud, once, so it's been asked: why are we still filing responses?"

"Checked it how," said Karis.

"Properly." Lira turned her hand over on her knee, which was the gesture she used when she had done more work than she intended to be praised for. "Three carters at the ferry landing who run the east road for a living, one at a time, on three different days, about three different things, because a woman asking about the same road three times in one week is a woman a carter remembers. It's four days to the Line from Ostrand with a wagon and three without. Six waystations, five of them registry-provisioned, the sixth private and past the last station. The Line isn't a line — there's no fence, no post, nothing. It's where the last Arbiter station is, and then there isn't another one, and the carters know exactly where it stops because that's where their manifests stop being checked." She let that sit for a second. "Two of them go past it. They said the same thing in different words: nobody out there asks what tier you are, because there's nobody to ask and nothing to check it against."

"And the third?"

"The third won't go past it and wouldn't say why." Lira's mouth did something that was not a smile. "So that's on the ledger too."

It was the earlier question matured — *what we do the day after* — and the room had known it was coming since the coach ride from the third session, and the room took it the way the found family took its hardest things, which was honestly, in four voices, plus one silence.

---

Karis argued the record, and she argued it standing, with the formality she reserved for findings she did not like, because she did not like this one either.

"Every institution we have ever been inside failed us until text held it accountable. Denvash put him out a back door with no record. Ardenmere's circuit rated him because Vell kept a ledger. Greyvane's hearing found for him because the clause was in a compilation nobody had read. Halcenvane enrolled him because the record was true and Bracken wouldn't falsify it. Norhold fielded him on a sentence." She counted them on her fingers, the way she counted precedents. "Text is the only thing that has ever held. Not once — *every time.*" She set her hand flat on the face-down papers. "And the record of walking out mid-proceedings reads *fled.* It's the registry's word. I've read it in two hundred files. *Respondent fled the jurisdiction pending ruling* — and after it, in every file, the ruling goes in anyway, by default, and the default is the petition's whole prayer, granted, with a line beneath it that says the respondent's flight is *itself evidence of the integrity question.*" She sat down. "Leave, and we hand them the confession they couldn't get from Umber, from Vastin, from Daeva, from eleven pounds of the truth. That's the record. I'm the archive. I'm telling you what the archive says."

"And if the archive's wrong this time?" Lira said.

"Then it's wrong on the text, and I'll have been wrong with a perfect filing on the table, and I've said that I'd rather that than the alternative." Karis's hands were flat and her voice was not quite. "I know what the corridor is. I drew it. I'm asking you to consider that walking out of a corridor is also a thing the corridor was built to make you do."

"Say more about that," said Cael.

"I read a file at Ternhall in my second year that I've thought about every week since." She did not open the notebook; she did not need it. "A guild practitioner in a coastal district, integrity matter, nine years ago. Ninety pages of proceedings, and the respondent was winning — genuinely winning; I checked the rulings, they're sound — and on the fourteenth session he stopped attending. Not fled. He wrote a letter first, a good one, saying he had lost confidence in the process and would take no further part. The registry entered the letter, thanked him for it, and ruled by default the same week." She squared her hands on the table's edge. "The default ruling is two paragraphs long, and the second one cites the letter. His own reasoning for leaving, quoted, as evidence of the thing he was accused of. They didn't twist it. They didn't need to. A man who says he doesn't trust the instrument has said, in the instrument's own grammar, that the instrument's confidence in him was correctly withheld."

The room was quiet.

"So what's the answer to that?" Brom said.

"There isn't one," said Karis. "That's why I've thought about it every week. The only thing the file proves is that leaving is a move the machine has a form for. It doesn't prove the move is wrong. It proves it isn't free, and I'm tired of being the only person at this table who prices things nobody wants priced."

"You're not the only one," said Lira.

"No," Karis agreed. "You're just the one who's willing to pay it, and I'm the one who's going to make sure you know the number first."

---

Brom argued the middle, from the room's one good chair, in the register of weight, and he argued it in three sentences because three were what he had.

"Prepare both roads." He turned his cup. "Karis files. The counsel argues. We win the sessions, because we do. And every night, after the filing, somebody packs one box." He looked at Lira. "You've checked the road east. Check it properly. Waystations, the Line, what's past it. Not to walk it. To know it." He looked at Cael. "You've never once gone into a fight without knowing the exits. This is a fight. Same law." And to the room: "Walk neither yet. Both roads open. That's all I've got."

"That's a lot," said Lira.

"It's three sentences," said Brom, and the room let him have it, because it was, and because it was also the first time any of them had said the word *box* aloud, and the word stayed in the room after he said it.

Lira picked it up, after a moment, the way she picked up anything left on a floor.

"What's in yours?"

Brom considered it seriously, which was the only way he considered anything.

"Clothes. The Copper certificate, because it's the only paper my father will accept as an answer to a question he asked when I was fifteen. My mother's letter." He turned the cup back the other way. "And the strapping. Two years of it, all of it, because you can buy strapping anywhere and I don't want to be somewhere I have to."

"That's not much for a Velmere," said Lira, carefully.

"No," Brom agreed. "It's the second box that's the problem."

Nobody asked what the second box was. Cael charted the not-asking, and charted the way Brom let the not-asking stand, and wrote later that the second box was the review — the advancement review, docketed for term's end, the thing his family's ledger had demanded since he was a child and which he had never once mentioned in the same conversation as the word *east* — and that Brom had put it on the table by refusing to, which was the whole of the man's grammar.

---

Cael argued the first pole, and he argued it at full strength, because it was the strongest position he had ever held and he had three years of receipts for it and the receipts were real.

"The hearing at Greyvane. The demonstration provision. Four semester stamps, renewed, on a Compact-observed record. An inspection that declined to find. A tournament their own clause put me in, and a match their own instrument couldn't score, and the instrument's keeper writing *no number goes here* rather than a lie." He said them in order, the way he wrote inventories, because the order was the argument. "Every time the people failed, the text held. Not because text is kind — because text is *there.* You can read it. You can find the orphaned clause. You can put a true record on a counter and make a registrar decline to falsify it. The people are weather. The text is ground."

"Say the rest of it," said Karis. "You've got a fifth one you're not saying."

"Denvash," Cael said. "A man in an office decided what I was in eleven seconds and there was no text in the room, and I was fourteen, and it took me three years and four institutions to get a sentence written down that undid it. Every good thing in my life since has been text somebody wrote down and then had to honor." He looked at Lira, whose question it was. "The system has never beaten me when the text was in the room. I'm not leaving the room while the text still works."

"It's working now?" Lira said. "The seal said *lawful* and *proceeding* in the same paragraph."

"The seal said lawful. On the record. Forever." He heard the sentence come out with more weight than he had meant to put on it, and let it stand, because it was true and the room was for true things. "The corridor's real. I've walked corridors. Every one of them had a door somebody hadn't read the text on. Karis found one in a compilation, one in a tournament charter, one in a squad rule. She'll find one in the code." He looked at the face-down papers. "I don't know which one. I know there's never once not been one."

"And if the code doesn't have one," said Brom.

"Then it's the first document in three years that doesn't."

"That's the argument," said Karis. "I want to say plainly what kind of argument it is, because you'd want me to. It's an induction. Five instances, all in the same direction, no counter-instance yet — which is a strong finding and is also exactly the shape of every finding that has ever been overturned by the sixth instance." She said it without heat. "I'm not saying you're wrong. I've built my whole career on the same induction and I'd file it tomorrow. I'm saying the strength of it is the number of times it's held, and there is no number of times that makes a thing a law."

Nobody answered that for a moment. It was, the log would concede that night, the best argument in the room, and it was made by the person the room existed to protect, and the room could hear the receipts in it, and the room could also hear the thing underneath the receipts, which none of them had a word for yet, and which Lira — who had been in a corridor at fourteen with a correct form and no word — came closest to naming.

"You said the people are weather and the text is ground." She said it carefully. "I've been standing on ground I was sure of. It was a form with my name on it. Correctly filled, correctly stamped, correctly filed by a woman who wished me well. The ground was correct and it opened anyway." She looked at him. "That's not an argument against text. I know what the text has done for me; I've got a supersession because of it. It's an argument about what text is. It's not ground. It's a floor. And floors are held up by something, and I stopped being able to see what, about twelve days ago." She sat back. "I'm not saying leave. I'm saying: check the ground. That's all. Brom's three sentences. Both roads."

"Both roads," Cael agreed, because he could agree to that without conceding anything, and the room heard him not concede, and let him.

Karis wrote *both roads* in the notebook with a date. Under it she wrote a second line, which Cael read upside down and did not comment on: *road east — L. has waystations. Nobody has asked what we do about H.*

The council ended as councils that had not decided anything ended, which was with tea.

Brom made it, because Brom made it, and the making took twenty minutes and nobody left during them, which was the actual finding of the evening. Lira put her feet on the bench opposite and worked her landing-leg hip with two thumbs, on the coach's schedule, out of habit; the meet was three weeks out and the program did not care what the seal had ruled. Karis reread her own second line and did not amend it. Cael took the lamp down to three-eighths because three-quarters had been for reading and there was nothing left to read.

"One thing on the record before we go up," Karis said, to the room, in the finding voice. "We didn't decide anything tonight. I want that stated, because in four months somebody is going to remember this evening as the night we decided, and it wasn't, and if we're going to be an institution about this we may as well be an accurate one."

"It was the night we asked," said Lira.

"That's what I'll write."

"Write both," said Brom, from behind the pot.

She wrote both. Then she gathered the face-down papers without turning them over, and the five of them went up through a residence that had been asleep for an hour, and the small room's lamp went out, and the one line of light at the shutter's bad joint went out with it, and the stores range and the court were dark from the assessment office's window by the time the man who had sat in the bench's middle got there.


---

The silence was Seln's, and the room let it be load-bearing.

He had attended the whole council from the bench's middle, in the configuration the year had built, and he had contributed one thing, which was a procedural correction — when Karis cited the *fled* default, he had said, without opening his eyes, "The default enters at the fourth session's close, not the ruling's. Forty days from a flight, not from an adjournment. The code's precise about it," and Karis had corrected the notebook, and he had said nothing else. Not on the corridor. Not on the road east. Not on the boxes, or the text, or the ground. He had sat at the table he had spent a year and a half earning a seat at and had held the silence of a man who had a document in the room with him that nobody else could see.

Cael charted the silence, because charting was what he had instead of asking, and because with Seln, asking had never once been the instrument that worked.

Four registers of not-answering, he had counted at the last council. Tonight there were five, and the fifth was different in kind. Karis's silences were the pause before a citation. Brom's were weight. Lira's were the half-second in which she decided how much of a true thing a room could carry. Cael's own, he knew perfectly well, were inventory.

Seln's, tonight, was none of those. It had duration and it had *direction*: he had not looked at the drill-book, or the papers, or the lamp. He had looked, twice, at Lira, when she said the road east. He had looked once, and for a long time, at Cael, when Cael said *I'm not leaving the room while the text still works* — and the look had a quality Cael could not chart and did not have a word for, and he tried three words in the log that night and struck all three, and left the entry as it stood:

*Something in the room tonight cost him. Not the argument — he doesn't care who wins an argument. Something in it was a bill. He came, he sat, he corrected Karis on forty days, and he left the way he always leaves, which is before you notice he's standing. Enter it with a question mark, the way I enter everything he starts and doesn't finish. Fourth entry this term with a question mark on it. That's a pattern and I don't have the second half of it.*

---

Seln's own account of the council was kept where he kept everything, and it was longer than he had permitted himself in years.

He went back to the assessment office after the tea. The office was dark; the counter's lamps had been out for three hours; the man had a key and a desk and fifteen years of reasons to be at both after hours. He did not light the counter lamps. He lit the desk lamp, which threw eight feet and no further, and sat down with the cross-index of the instrument in front of him — forty pages in his own hand, the best document about the petition that existed anywhere including at Ostrand — and did not read it, because he had written it and knew what it said.

The office at that hour was the shape his working life had always had. A counter with nobody at it. A window with a court beyond it and, across the court, the residence's dark bulk — and in it the small room's shuttered window, which for three hours that evening had let out one thin line of lamp at the shutter's bad joint, and which was dark now, and which he had noticed in his first week and had never mentioned to anyone, because mentioning it would have meant explaining why he had looked.

Fifteen years of product. Nineteen postings.

He had done the arithmetic of the postings once, in his twelfth year, and had not enjoyed it. Nineteen towns. Four academies, two guild halls, a district seat, three provincial registries, and the rest of it the kind of place that had no name in the reports and got a designation instead. Four hundred and ten reports filed. Sixty-three subjects. Of the sixty-three, he could name fifty-eight; of the fifty-eight, he knew what had happened to nine, and had stopped, deliberately, at nine, because the tenth had been a woman in a river district and he had gone looking and had found out, and the finding out had cost him a year of sleeping properly and had taught him the only professional lesson he had ever learned twice.

And a locked case on a shelf in his quarters that had outlasted every one of the nineteen. It had traveled to Norhold and back unopened. It had ridden in a cart under the same canvas as three boxes of a researcher's compiled truth without either archive knowing the other's contents, and he had sat on the cart's bench for two days beside those boxes and had thought about the symmetry for most of the second day.

Two and a half years of this boy. A file that had begun as an assignment — *subject, enrollee, Halcenvane, unclassified, monitor and report* — and had become, by increments he had never permitted himself to date, the only file of his career he had wanted to be wrong about.

The increments were on record even if the dating was not. The boy at the office counter in his first week, reading a form all the way through, both sides, and then asking a question about the seventh line that no enrollee in Seln's experience had ever asked because no enrollee had ever read the seventh line. The night at the records office with the lamp and the sightlines, a boy who had been handed a piece of another man's trade and had understood, without being told twice, that the debt was not the tradecraft but the exposure. A place at a table that had appeared under him one autumn without anyone announcing it, and which he had taken, and which he had never once acknowledged, and which he had come back to for a year and a half.

And the sentence he had never once let himself finish, forming anyway, at the dark desk, in the audit-voice that was the only voice he had ever fully trusted:

*The file's question was always the wrong question.*

Whether the enrollee was a classification error. Whether the anomaly could be evaluated. Whether the provision could hold him, whether the tournament could score him, whether the seat could find him lawful. Every question the machine had ever asked about the boy was a question about whether the *boy* fit — and he had known, for a decade, sitting in rooms exactly like the trading floor at Ostrand, reading files exactly like the one on the respondent's table, that the question had never been about who fit. It had been about who *decided*, and by what instrument, and since when.

He had the maintenance records. He had read them in a posting twelve years ago, by one candle, in a room he had been in lawfully and for another purpose entirely, and had duplicated them because duplicating was his habit and had been since his second year, and had gone on with the posting, and had not understood what he had duplicated for the better part of a year. The understanding had arrived in a corridor, unprompted, between two ordinary appointments, the way the worst understandings always arrived. And he had walked away from the job in the only way his trade allowed a man to walk away from it, which was by staying in it and keeping.

*And I am the only person alive holding the file that proves whose question it was.*

He did not open the case. He did not go and look at it. He noted, in the audit-voice, exactly what the case staying closed cost, because the cost had been abstract for fifteen years and had stopped being abstract at a table three hours ago.

Every session the boy fought inside the instrument believing the instrument was merely broken. Every filing the researcher perfected against a corridor she had drawn without knowing who had built it. Every road the Fenmark girl checked with three carters at a ferry landing, learning the geography of a border her own government had never explained to her. Every box the big one did not yet pack. Every night the honest men filed true reports into a mill that did not care where the river wanted to go — Bracken's completeness, Withrow's charter, Ilsev's scope entered in the minute in her own words, Havel's not-looking, four hundred and ten reports of his own — all of it conducted by people who thought they were arguing with a malfunction.

The audit had a column for that. It had always had the column. What it had not had, until twelve days ago, was a figure in it that a man could not carry.

He sat with the last line for a long time, in the dark office, with the cross-index under his hands and the case on its shelf across the campus and a dark window four buildings away where a line of lamp had shown for three hours, and the line was the closest thing to a decision the audit-voice had ever recorded, and it was not one yet, and he knew the difference exactly.

*He thinks he's arguing with a malfunction. I've read the maintenance records. Fifteen years I've been deciding to die with that sentence. The deciding has stopped being quiet.*

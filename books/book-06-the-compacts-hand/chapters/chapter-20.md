# Chapter 20 — The Last Bout

Ephram's request was made at the council's end and Rooke sanctioned it without being asked twice, which from Rooke was a eulogy.

The asking took ninety seconds in the coaching office at the sixth bell, and Cael was there for it because Ephram brought him, on the principle that a man does not send someone else to collect a debt he means to collect himself. Ephram said the words: sanctioned bout, cohort captain against the enrollee, full-earnest, rated, before the resignations filed. Rooke listened with the coaching file shut under his hand. He asked two questions — whether both parties had asked for it, and whether the enrollee's side was in condition — and got two answers, and then he did the thing that told the whole story of the man, which was to pick up his chalk and go out to the covered walk and write it on the board himself, at that hour, in the dark, while the two of them were still standing in his office.

He posted it openly. The board on the covered walk carried it in his flat hand: *Crown yard, third bell. Ephram — Cael. Sanctioned, earnest, rated.* No preamble on the board, no notation about the term's other business, nothing that a registry-literate person could read as an institution making a statement — just a bout, sanctioned, at a bell, the way the board had carried eight hundred bouts before it. Rooke did not make speeches and had found, across thirty years, that a coach's grief went best into a schedule.

The campus read it and understood it for what it was, and by the first bell of the next morning the town below the bluff had read it too. Bracken's counter took four separate inquiries about whether the yard's gallery was open to non-enrollees and answered all four in the same six words. The kitchen moved the afternoon meal an hour early without being asked. Two of the coaching staff rearranged their floors. And when Cael crossed the quadrangle at midday he counted the traffic on the covered walk against a normal Fifth-day and found it running at something near triple, all of it drifting past one board, most of it not stopping, because the bluff had manners and the bluff also had eyes: the Crown yard's last look at the enrollee the brackets could never hold, offered by the one fighter who had made himself wait for it longest.

He catalogued the walk on the way past, because the walk was going to stop being his in six days and he wanted the count accurate. Forty-one paces from the records hall's door to the yard gate. Nine boards, of which Rooke owned three. The notice in the flat hand, third board, second column, with the term's ordinary business above and below it, unremarkable, permanent-looking. He read it twice and went to eat something, and did not manage much of it.

Ephram's whole arc had come to this board, and Cael ran the arc on the way up, in order, the way he ran everything. A promise made two years before at the top of a stair to sixty people: *I'll wait for the semester evaluation.* A year spent being the fool who held a verdict about a session he was never allowed to watch. An empty Crown yard at the end of that year, and a man who had come to explain his own mistake to Cael's face rather than let the promise quietly lapse. A tournament, and a Gold-tier fact stopping mid-trial to read the man he had decided to stand beside, and *still glad*. And now the walls that had spent a year deciding what Cael was, about to be resigned from, and the last currency the place had ever spoken that a debt was owed in.

"I waited for the evaluation," Ephram said, at the flags, taping his wrists, the whole cohort's ferocity in him and none of the enmity. "Then the tournament. Then a year of watching you hold back in a building that was deciding what you are. I'm done waiting." He rolled his shoulders. "Before you go — show me straight."

---

The Crown yard was the floor Cael had charted eleven Paths from, the tiered stone he had catalogued on his first day at Halcenvane as a building that put its largest structure where spectators went — and it was full, the way it had been full for the Copper final's rhyme, the campus and the town both, because the bluff knew what it was watching.

He counted it in from the gate, one last time, at the handspan.

The floor: the Crown yard's own, stone under the open sky, a rectangle of it laid in courses that ran east and west, sixty feet by forty, worn smooth in a broad oval at the middle where two centuries of fighting had polished it and rough at the corners where nobody ever went. Tiered seating on three sides, north, east and south, six rows of cut stone rising from the floor's edge; the west side open to the gate and the covered walk beyond it, which meant the wind came in at the west and the sun, at the third bell in the term's last month, came low over the south tier and laid the tiers' own shadow in bars across the floor's southern third. The boards' inlay at center: a compass figure of darker stone, four feet across, set flush, which Cael had learned to the handspan in his first month because a fighter who knows one fixed mark on a floor always knows where he is. The flags at east and west of it, twelve feet out. The rail along the west edge, where the cohort stood.

Rooke's clerk at the north table with the rating sheet. Rooke at the floor's head. Gault at the south tier's foot, in the seat the evaluator of record took at rated bouts, with nothing in his hands, which was not the way Gault attended anything. Withrow at the north tier's second row, not in the chancellor's place, which was a decision. Brom, Karis, Lira at the west rail; Seln at the east tier's top, where the sightlines to every entrance converged.

Ephram took the sunward mark.

That was a cohort captain's courtesy inverted — giving the glare to himself and not his opponent — and he did it before the clerk had finished calling the marks, without looking at anyone, so that nobody could applaud it. It was the bout's first sentence and Cael heard it whole: *no advantages. Straight.*

**First exchange.**

Ephram came off the mark at the cohort's speed, which was not a speed Halcenvane's yard had built and not one Cael had ever been given at full — Blade doctrine at the rate Rooke had drilled into a whole cohort and then refined into one captain, edge-declarations chained faster than the tier's arithmetic ought to allow, each declaration's line laid a half-beat before his body arrived on it. Cael gave ground east, off the inlay, using the floor's open middle, and bought facts with the first thirty seconds the way the delegation bought facts with any first exchange.

The facts came fast and cost him for the buying. Ephram's entry line came off the left foot and always off the left foot; that was not a tell, that was doctrine, and Rooke's doctrine did not hide. The chained declarations ran in threes — three edges, a beat, three edges — and the beat between the threes was where the whole cohort reset and where the whole cohort was readable, and Ephram's beat was shorter than any of them, short enough that reading it did not buy time, only information. And the line-reading arrived where Cael's evasion was *going* rather than where it was, which was the thing the cohort's captain had that the cohort's fighters did not: Cael broke east and the third edge of the second three was already laid across the ground he had picked, and he had to spend a Wind burst to not be standing on it.

He paid for the burst inside four seconds, the way he always paid: the economy came off the top of the exchange's budget and stayed off. On the second three he took a wash off an edge-declaration across the left forearm — not a score, no flag, the edge's outer margin only — and the forearm went hot and stayed hot and was going to be a bruise the shape of a hand by morning.

Flags: one to Ephram, at fifty-one seconds, clean, on the inlay's east lip, a contact-range score off the fourth chained three. The east tier made a sound and stopped itself. Ephram did not celebrate it, and did not look at the flag, and went back to his mark rolling the shoulder he had rolled at the taping, which Cael filed.

At the mark, Cael ran the honest total. He had bought: the threes, the short beat, the left-foot entry, and the fact that Ephram's line-reading was predictive and not reactive, which meant every evasion he made was a sentence he was completing for someone else. He had spent: one burst, a forearm, and the exchange. He had a decision to make and forty seconds of the break to make it in, and he made it at about the twelfth.

**Second exchange.**

Ephram had watched him from the cohort's rail for a year. That was the whole strategic content of the second exchange, and Ephram opened it by proving it: he pressed the tempo past the band and refused the reset.

The band was the thing nobody at Halcenvane had ever named out loud and Ephram had charted from the rail across a year of Fifth-days — the rate at which the enrollee fought, which was a rate and not a ceiling, consistent to a degree that no honest fighter's output is ever consistent, a managed middle that never rose and never broke. It had a floor and it had a lid and Ephram had learned the distance between them well enough to fight inside it. So he did not fight inside it. He chained his edge-work without the beat, four threes with no reset in them, driving west and then north-west, and he drove Cael off the open middle and onto the north tier's foot, where the floor ran out at three feet of rough course-stone and the circling stopped.

Three feet of stone, a tier at his back, and a cohort captain running Blade doctrine at a rate the managed version had never once had to hold.

Cael made the decision he had made at the mark, and the yard found out what it had been watching for two years.

He came off the tier's foot early — not evading the fourth three but arriving inside it, one step to the inside of Ephram's left-foot entry, close enough that the chained edges had no run-up left in them — and he did it at a speed that had nothing managed in it anywhere. The north tier's front row moved backward as a body. From the west rail, Lira said one word that six people heard.

It ran even after that and ran fast. Cael gave up the middle and took it back twice; Ephram broke the exchange's rhythm three times and had it broken back; nobody scored. The exchange went to the clerk as *no flag, both parties engaged throughout*, which the sheet permitted and Rooke's clerk had entered perhaps nine times in twenty years.

Cael's account of it afterward was two lines in the log and both of them were about cost. The lid coming off cost more than the fighting under it, because two years of managed output was two years of an economy built around a rate, and running past the rate meant running past every efficiency the rate had been paying for. He came back to the mark with his breath high in his chest and his left forearm no longer telling him anything useful, and he stood at the flag and did not put his hands on his knees, because Ephram had asked for straight and a man who asks for straight is owed the honest condition too.

Ephram, at the other mark, was not hiding his either. The shoulder had gone from a roll to a set. He caught Cael reading it, and grinned, briefly, with nothing polite in it.

"There it is," he said, across twenty-four feet of stone. "Two years. Again."

**Third exchange.**

It was the best pure bout the Crown yard had staged since Lira–Brom, and the yard knew it inside twenty seconds and went quiet the way the yard went quiet for craft — the specific silence of four hundred people who all understand that the thing in front of them is not going to be repeated.

Ephram's line-reading against Cael's evasion, at a tempo that would have been remarkable at any tier on the continent, neither giving anything, the exchange running long and running even. Cael stopped trying to break the predictive read and started feeding it instead: he gave the man true lines, over and over, and let the third edge land on ground he had already left by a hand's width, which cost him a hand's width every time and cost Ephram a whole declaration. Ephram answered by shortening the threes to twos. Cael answered by taking the inlay and refusing to leave it, so that every entry had to come across the compass figure's darker stone, where the polish ran different and a left-foot entry had to be set a half-inch flatter, and Ephram set it a half-inch flatter every time, and did not once complain about the floor, because complaining about a floor was for men who wanted the other thing.

The exchange ran a minute and forty and neither of them scored, and inside it — sustained, at full earnest, with genuine stakes on both sides of it — the conditions assembled themselves.

Cael knew their shape. He had known their shape since a stairwell in Ardenmere; he had written the covenant about them on a page two years before, in his own hand, with Karis's initials beside his; and he had priced this one in advance, the night before, in the log, in ink, because the covenant's whole content was that the price got written down before the thing was taken and not after. Ephram was fighting for the answer he had waited two years for. Cael was fighting for the leaving, and for an honest hour owed to a floor he was about to lose, and both of those were real and neither of them was a performance, and that was exactly the trouble with it: it was earnest all the way down, on both sides, and the architecture did not distinguish between the kinds of earnest.

He reached. Directed. Open-eyed. In earnest.

**Fourth exchange.**

The bout's decision took seven seconds and turned on the thing Ephram could not stop doing, which was commit.

Predictive line-reading is a commitment engine: it works by laying the edge where the opponent will be, which requires deciding where the opponent will be before the opponent decides it, which requires being right and being early. Ephram had been right and early for three exchanges. In the fourth, at contact range, off the inlay's north edge, Cael gave him a line he had given him twice already — the same break east, the same hand's width — and Ephram laid the third edge on it a half-beat before his body arrived, correctly, as he had done twenty times in fourteen minutes.

Cael was not going east. He arrived early on the inside of the commitment, inside the declared line's own margin, where the edge had already spent itself on ground, and scored clean at the shoulder at contact range with the whole of the yard's noise arriving a full second behind the flag.

Rooke's clerk entered it. The bout went to Cael on points, two flags to one, and it was the least important line on the sheet and everybody who mattered in the yard knew it.

The notice arrived after the stoppage, and he read it standing at the flag with his breath still wrong:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Blade-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: Iron.
Note: edge-declaration component; line-reading component.
Contact range.
Acquisition: directed. Engagement: adversarial, earnest — non-hostile.
```

He read the last field twice.

*Adversarial, earnest — non-hostile.* Every entry in the log was adversarial; the stakes rule demanded it and had demanded it since a boy in a market town first understood what the architecture would and would not accept. But the engagement field had never carried *non-hostile* in eight notices — not from Reydan, who had fought him to end him and then wanted the answer afterward; not from Daeva, who had fought him with a Gold-tier's whole seriousness and no malice at all and had still, on the day, been trying to take him apart. This was a machine's grammar bending to describe a fight that was entirely in earnest and entirely without enmity, which was as close as the architecture had ever come to writing down the word friendship, and could not, and had reached for the nearest thing in its vocabulary and found a negative.

Cael stood on the Crown yard with the campus's noise around him and a forearm going purple and read a system notice describe his friend accurately, and entered it that night as the finding it was.

The yard's verdicts came in the yard's own currencies, each in the register of the person spending it, and Cael collected all four before he was off the floor.

Rooke's was two words, delivered to his clerk and not to either fighter, in the tone of a man dictating a line for a file: "Both. Enter both." The clerk understood him and entered the bout under both names in the cohort's permanent column, which the sheet allowed for exhibitions and title bouts and had not allowed for anything in the Crown yard in twelve years.

Gault's was a movement of the head at the south tier's foot, once, at the flag, and then the evaluator of record went away up the covered walk with nothing in his hands, having attended a rated bout without a form, which four separate members of the coaching staff remarked on and none of them understood.

Withrow's was to stay in her seat in the north tier's second row until the tiers had emptied, so that the last person out of the Crown yard's gallery was a chancellor watching an empty floor, and then to leave by the north stair without speaking to anyone.

And Lira's, at the west rail, delivered flat to Brom and Karis while Cael was still walking off the stone: "That was the first time this school has ever seen him. Two years, and they got fourteen minutes at the end, and none of them will ever know that's what it was."

---

The boards ritual ran after, one last time on this floor, because it had run after every bout either of them had ever fought here and neither of them was going to be the one who let it lapse.

They walked to the covered walk together, still breathing hard, and stood at the third board while Rooke's clerk chalked the result in under the notice — the same nine boards, the same flat hand, the bout entered in the same three-line grammar as every bout the yard had ever posted, which was the yard's whole opinion of everybody and the reason Cael had loved it. Behind them the tiers were emptying at the slow rate of a crowd that has not finished talking about the thing.

Ephram put his towel over his shoulder and looked at the board rather than at Cael, which was the register the ritual ran in.

"Whatever you are," he said, "I said I'm glad it's on my side of the floor. At the trial. When she stopped to read you." He toweled off. "The floor's about to be the whole continent, and I'm not on it — I'm staying; you know I'm staying; it's the right thing for me and you'd tell me so if I asked, which is why I'm not asking." He put out his hand, the third time in their lives. "Still glad. That's the whole thing. I made myself wait two years to fight you straight and it was worth every month, and I don't need to know what you are to know that. Go be it somewhere with more room."

Cael shook it.

He did not tell him. Not on the walk, not at the board, not later at the gate; not the mechanism, not the notice, not the field that had said *non-hostile* about fourteen minutes of a man's honest best. Ephram had consented to a bout. He had not consented to what the bout fed, because he could not be asked without being told, and telling him would have put a serving cohort captain of a registry-chartered academy in possession of the one fact that could end five people, five days before the five of them walked out of the gate and left him standing inside it.

That was the reasoning. It was sound, and Karis had written it out with him the night before at the annex table, and it was still a thing he had done to a friend without the friend's knowledge, and the log was going to say so in writing tonight, because the covenant's whole content was that the reasoning did not erase the cost and that the cost got entered under his own hand whether or not anybody ever read it. That was the difference between what he did and what the office did at a road cut, spending men whose names it had not bothered to record.

The log, that night:

*Nine. Taken in earnest from a friend who asked me to show him straight — so I showed him straight, and the architecture did what it does with earnest, and the notice said "non-hostile," which it has never said, because there has never been a fight like it in the log. Ephram asked for the true measurement. He got it. He doesn't know he gave me a piece of himself for it; he consented to a fight, not to the thing the fight fed. I wrote the price down before the bout, the way the covenant says, so that when it cost — and it cost — I'd have written the cost before I collected it, which is the only line between me and what the office did at a road cut and in a records hall — spending people whose names it never bothered to write down, and asking nobody anything.*

*He said "non-hostile" and the system agreed. Nine fragments and the ninth one's engagement field says the closest thing to friendship a machine knows how to file. I'll take it. It's the truest thing the instrument ever wrote about me except "no number goes here," and it was written about a fight I didn't want to win.*

---

The architecture's first pull came that evening, and it came on time, because it always came on time.

He was crossing the quadrangle at dusk when a doorframe raised its hand — the old signature, the Iron-adjacent architecture reading load and mass in a lintel it had no business reading — and then, a half-second behind it, the doorframe's *edge* raised its hand too: the line of the jamb read as a declared line, a thing that could be laid rather than a thing that merely was. Two components introducing themselves to each other across an architecture that had taken on new mass four weeks ago and now had newer mass on top of it. He stopped in the middle of the quadrangle and let the introduction finish, and catalogued it, and went on, and catalogued it again at the eighth bell and the ninth, hourly, per the discipline, in the notebook and not the log, because the log kept meaning and the notebook kept measurements and mixing them was how a man ended up believing his own summaries.

Anchor-adjacent he had not used in the yard at all. He entered that too, with the reason: four weeks old, untested under a rated seal, and belonging — in every line of its notice — to a man in a grey coat at a road cut east of Ostrand whom nobody had named. Deploying a containment specialist's architecture in front of four hundred people and a rating clerk would have answered a question about the Ostrand road that nobody had yet asked out loud. So it stayed in the kit, and he wrote down that it had stayed in the kit and why, because a capability held back is only discipline if the holding is on the record somewhere.

The resignations were drafted on Bracken's counter for the morning — four sheets, the registry's own form, Bracken supplying the correct leaf without being asked and setting the inkstand where the light was good.

And then the Crown yard at night, empty, the tiered stone dark, the boards' inlay grey under no sky's light.

He came in at the west gate and stood at the rail, alone, in a yard that had held four hundred people six hours before and now held the wind. The gallery where he had charted eleven Paths in his first two months, sitting on the third row of the east tier with a notebook on his knee, a boy from a border town writing down what strangers could do. The floor where the found family had first fought each other honestly. The largest structure of the first institution that had ever wanted him, built where spectators went, which he had thought on his first day was a thing worth noting about an academy and had turned out to be the truest thing anybody could have told him about the whole continent.

He did the thing he did at the end of anything that mattered, which was count what was actually true. Two years. Eleven Paths charted from that rail. One cohort captain who had waited two years to be shown straight and had, in being shown, given the log its ninth entry and its only *non-hostile*. Six hundred and something bouts watched from these tiers, of which he had fought a dozen. A floor about to become the whole continent, for someone who would not be standing on it.

"Good room," he wrote, which from Cael was a whole letter, and closed the log, and went to draft a resignation.

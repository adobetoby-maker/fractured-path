# Chapter 8 — Preliminary

The third session at Ostrand was the full one, and the delegation went down the bluff road for it in the coach at first light with the whole term's argument packed in two cases, and the coach was quiet the way it had been quiet on the road to Norhold: nobody circling the thing, because the thing had stopped needing to be circled.

Cael charted the ride anyway, because riding was cataloguing time. Lira with the Silver drill-book shut on her knee, the program's first road meet three weeks out and her whole attention on a trading floor instead. Brom with his forearms on his knees and the Velmere letter's second envelope in his kit — he had said nothing about it in four weeks and the table had said nothing back, per the arrangement, and the arrangement was beginning to cost the table something. Karis with the closing summary in her lap, not reading it, because she had written it and could not have improved it and knew both things. Seln at the coach's end with the office's cross-index and his eyes shut. The counsel with the privileged minute open, adding to it in her small exact hand as the river went by. And Withrow, in the registry grey, at the coach's head, looking at the road the way she had looked at the bluff road from a waystation the spring before, with the flinty enjoyment of a person whose worrying was done in advance — except that it was not enjoyment now; it was the same face with the enjoyment removed, and what was left was load-bearing.

"We win it," Karis said, at the ferry, to the river. "On the text. Every point. I want everyone braced for what winning feels like, again, because it will feel like winning, again, and it isn't."

"You said that before the first session," said Lira.

"I'll say it before every session. Repetition is how you keep a finding from turning into a hope."

---

The registry seat at Ostrand had been a wool exchange for a hundred and fifty years before it had been a registry seat, and it had never troubled to stop looking like one, which was the first fact Cael had catalogued about it at the first session and the fact he came back to at every session since.

It stood at the top of the market street on a stone plinth with four steps, and its double doors were merchant doors, wide enough to walk bales through, and the hall behind them was a trading floor: a long room under a clerestory, with a raised platform at the north end where the wool factors' arbitration bench had sat and where the registry's seal now sat instead, and a rail running the room's whole length at waist height that had once separated buyers from stock and now separated the gallery from the proceedings. The registry had put a chartered seal over the doors, had replaced the arbitration bench with a registrar's bench, and had changed nothing else, including the floor's brass inlay, which still marked out the exchange's old lot positions in a grid nobody had swept away.

Cael sat at the respondent's table on lot seventeen. He had established this at the first session, on his hands and knees during the recess, and had told nobody except Karis, who had written it down.

He catalogued the room again while the clerk opened the minute, because the room was the machine and the machine's parts were countable. Forty gallery benches, filled to the rails and standing at the back — the registry-literate of three districts, the correspondents at the front two benches with the folding writing-boards that marked continental papers, four of them with the flat cases of houses that could afford a courier standing outside. The petitioner's table, north-west of the seal, one man and one clerk. The respondent's table, north-east, four: the counsel, Bracken, Withrow, and the enrollee of record. The records table behind and below the seal's platform, two officers on rotation. The evaluation seat, west of the seal, at an angle to both tables — a chair, a lap-desk, and a low rail of its own — which at the first session had been empty and at the second had been empty and which today was not.

The seal itself sat above all of it in the registrar's bench and had no face. That was not a figure of speech and Cael had stopped treating it as one. The presiding registrar entered from the platform's own door, wearing the district's full grey with the hood forward as the code prescribed for a seated presiding officer, and was identified in the minute by the seal's designation and by nothing else, and spoke in a registrar's voice that could have belonged to any of a hundred people, and had presided over two sessions in which not one word had been addressed to it as a person. Karis had checked. There was no procedural defect in it whatsoever. The code permitted an unnamed presiding seal in institutional matters and had permitted it for four hundred years, for reasons the code gave in a single clause about the impartiality of the office over the officer, and the clause was, Karis said, the most beautifully argued sentence in registry law and the most convenient one ever written.

*The face the proceedings show is nobody's,* the log had recorded after the first session. *That isn't a hiding place. It's the design, published, four hundred years old, and defensible in every line. I keep looking for the trick and finding architecture.*

---

The trading floor was full to the rails, and the seal sat as it had sat, and the evaluation seat — empty at two sessions — had a person in it, and Cael read the person from the respondent's table before the clerk had finished the opening minute, because he had read her once before, across a hearing room, two years and a continent ago.

Ilsev.

Senior evaluator of record, in the registry's grey, files squared on the seat's lap-desk at angles that had not varied by a degree since the first counter she had ever read his case across — the woman who had read that case at the counter of his life and had been, in every recorded act since, exactly correct. She had come in with the working morning's traffic rather than with the officers, carrying her own files, and had set them down and squared them, and had spent the four minutes before the opening reading the topmost one with the specific attention of a person who has read it three times already and intends to read it a fourth.

Empaneled. The clerk read the empanelment into the minute: *the evaluation seat constituted, the district's senior evaluator presiding, scope per the code's institutional provisions.* Ilsev inclined her head to the seal at the exact depth the registry taught, and opened the first of her files, and the room proceeded, and Cael sat with the reading the sight of her produced, which was not comfort and was not its opposite.

*The most honest instrument the Compact owns,* the log would say that night, *installed in a machine that is counting on her honesty to legitimize it. They didn't put her there despite her scruples. They put her there because of them. A seat that nobody can accuse of leaning is the only seat that can carry a corridor without the corridor showing.*

Behind her, at the records table, on rotation: Havel. He logged the session's opening in the records officer's ledger and did not look at the respondent's table, and did not look at it through the whole of the morning, and from Havel — four years, three postings, five entries in a private note that Cael had never seen and knew existed the way he knew the anomaly existed — the not-looking was a sentence with a subject and a verb, and the sentence was *I am on rotation and I am doing my rotation and I am not permitted to be anything else in this room, and I have decided to be exactly that, completely, so that nobody can say otherwise.*

---

Jent argued the petition, and he argued it in forty minutes, and it was — the counsel would say this in the coach, with professional distaste and professional respect in equal measure — the cleanest submission she had heard in twenty-six years, because it contained no argument at all.

He rose without papers. That was the first thing Cael charted, and he charted it because it was information: a procurator with four hundred enrollment matters behind him, arguing the largest one on the continent, standing up at the petitioner's table with his hands empty and his clerk's tabbed folder untouched at his elbow. He was a spare, colorless man in his fifties with a voice pitched exactly loud enough to reach the fortieth bench and not one degree past it, and he addressed the seal and never once the gallery, and in forty minutes he did not raise the voice, sharpen it, or slow it for emphasis. He read weather.

He conceded the enrollment. He did it first, in his opening minute, before anyone could make him: the enrollment was lawfully executed under a valid provision, the petitioner did not contend otherwise, and any reading of the petition as an allegation of irregularity was a misreading the petitioner wished corrected on the record now, at the outset, so that the seat's time would not be spent on it.

He conceded the provision. The assay clause was law; the standardization directive had not repealed it; the petitioner accepted the respondent institution's reconstruction of its provenance in full and adopted it.

He conceded the documentation, by name, with the compliment repeated into the record — *the best-documented enrollment this procurator has reviewed* — and he added, when the clerk asked him to confirm the phrasing for the minute, that the clerk should enter it as spoken and that he would not soften it on reflection.

There was a small sound from the gallery at that. Cael, charting, could not decide what it meant, and wrote down that he could not.

And then, having given away everything an opponent could want, Jent laid out the petition's actual substance, in the registry's own grammar, in four moves, each of which he numbered aloud for the minute.

First: that the evaluation-integrity provisions existed to protect the registry's confidence in its own evaluations — not the practitioner's rights, not the institution's, but the registry's confidence, which the code named as the provisions' object in the provisions' own opening line, and which he read out.

Second: that a practitioner no standard evaluation could read was, by definition and not by accusation, a practitioner in whom the registry could place no evaluated confidence. He was careful with the word *by definition*. He used it three times and defined it once.

Third: that the demonstration provision, however lawfully executed, was a *description* of such a practitioner and not an *evaluation* of him, since it ran on no Arbiter — and here he did the thing that made him, Cael understood with a species of cold admiration, unhateable: he corrected himself. He had begun to say that the provision produced no record capable of evaluation, stopped mid-sentence, and said that the formulation was too broad and that the seat should disregard it, because the provision plainly produced a record, and the petitioner's contention was narrower — that the record was of a different kind, and that the difference was the whole matter. He asked the clerk to strike the broader formulation. The clerk struck it.

Fourth: that the registry's discretion to compel re-evaluation in an integrity matter was plenary and undisputed, and that the petitioner did not need the seat to find anything defective in order to exercise it.

And so the petitioner asked the seat, not to find the enrollment defective — it was not — but to find the integrity question *cognizable*, and to order the remedy the code provided for cognizable integrity questions, which was re-evaluation under the standard protocol, the enrollment to stand or fall on its result.

He tendered the exhibits. Umber's referred file, entered at the second session, cited once, with courteous efficiency: *the adjudication office's own finding that the practitioner's demonstrated capability lies outside the Standard's tables — which is to say, outside evaluation.* He did not read the tournament result. He did not mention the match, or the crowd, or the name of the Gold-tier who had fought it. He cited a page number.

He sat down at the correct height.

Cael had spent three sessions building a file on him and closed it that morning with an entry he disliked writing: *He isn't cruel. He isn't even cold — he's courteous, and the courtesy is real, and he corrected his own overstatement against his own interest in front of forty benches because the correction was true. If he were hateable this would be easier and I would be learning less. He is a good instrument, being used well, and the file to open is not his.*

---

The counsel argued the response, and she argued it for two hours, and the term's discipline held all the way down, because the defense was *excellent*.

She rose with papers and did not consult them. She was a big, unhurried woman of sixty with a voice built for stone rooms, and she had been Halcenvane's instructed counsel since the enrollment and had told Withrow at the retainer that she took institutional work because institutional work was the only place left where a lawyer could still lose slowly enough to learn something. She argued in four movements and she signposted every one of them, because a two-hour submission that a gallery cannot follow is a two-hour submission nobody remembers.

*First, the text.* The assay clause's provenance: orphaned, not repealed. Three independent reconstructions — the academy's, the researcher's, and the district archivist's, commissioned by the counsel herself precisely because two agreeing reconstructions are a coincidence and three are a finding — arriving at one conclusion by three roads. She put the directive's own transitional schedule into the record and walked the seal through the schedule's structure item by item, and at the ninth item she stopped and said that the seat would notice, as she had, that the schedule enumerated the provisions it superseded and that the assay clause was not among them, and that the drafters of that schedule had been careful men, and that carefulness cut in only one direction.

*Second, the precedent.* Greyvane's ruling, entered. Four prior challenges to demonstration-provision enrollments, all sustained on the text, entered with their citations. And the point she drew from the four: the demonstration provision's whole litigated history was a shelf of institutions losing to a clause they had failed to read, which meant the clause had been tested — repeatedly, adversarially, by well-resourced parties — and had held every time it had been tried.

*Third, the integrity provisions themselves,* from Karis's forty pages. Their origin in the auditing of *instruments*: the counsel read the provisions' enacting preamble aloud, which took ninety seconds and which nobody in the room, by the look of the benches, had ever heard read. Their ninety-one recorded invocations, which the researcher had traced individually and tabulated by object — Arbiter frames, sixty-two; station calibration records, nineteen; evaluation protocols and their custody, three — and seven against an individual's conduct, and against what a practitioner *is*, exactly never. And the plain textual fact that a provision written to test whether an instrument could be trusted could not, without violence to its own language, be turned to ask whether a person could be.

The seal put its only question of the session there. Whether the counsel contended that the provisions were incapable in law of application to a practitioner, or merely that they had not been so applied.

"The second, and the second is enough," said the counsel, "because the seat is being asked to be the first in four hundred years, and a seat asked to be the first should be told so plainly, which the petition does not do."

Jent, on his feet immediately, said that the petitioner had never contended the application was routine, and would accept a minute entry that it was novel.

"The petitioner will accept a minute entry that it is novel," the counsel repeated, to the gallery for the first and only time in two hours, and sat back down into her fourth movement.

*Fourth, the demonstration record as evaluation.* Four semester panels, sighted, sealed, renewed. A public season under a continental rating instrument. A tournament fielded under a charter clause, and an inspection by the Compact's own senior evaluator that had declined to find. More evaluation, she said, than any Iron-tier practitioner on the continent had ever received in the whole of a career, conducted by human beings with eyes, under seal, on the record, over three years, by twenty-two separate officers whose names she then read into the minute, one at a time, at reading pace, which took four minutes and which the gallery sat through in complete silence.

Karis passed one card forward during the third movement and one during the fourth. Cael read both upside down without meaning to, because reading upside down was a thing he could not switch off: the first was a citation correction, a page number struck and re-entered. The second said only *don't take the fifth point, it's not needed*, and the counsel glanced at it, and did not take the fifth point.

And she closed with the sentence Karis had written for her and she had made her own: "The petitioner asks the seat to prefer the silence of an instrument to the testimony of every human evaluator who has ever assessed this practitioner. The seat should decline. The record is the evaluation. It has been for three years. The only thing that has changed is who is asking."

She sat. The gallery — forty benches of correspondents and the registry-literate — did the thing galleries did when they had heard craft, which was a low sound, sustained. Ilsev wrote for a long time. The seal received it.

---

The midday adjournment ran an hour and the respondent's party spent it in the exchange's old wool-hall corridor, which had windows and a bench and a smell of lanolin two centuries had not got out of the stone.

Nobody said the submission had been good. Bracken said the citation correction had landed cleanly. Withrow ate half a roll and gave the rest to a correspondent's runner who had been standing in the corridor for two hours with nothing. Lira and Brom had come down for the session and had sat in the gallery's fourth bench and had not been able to hear the first movement properly, and Karis explained the transitional schedule to them against the corridor wall in six sentences, with her hands, and Brom said "so the men who wrote the list of what they were cancelling didn't put it on the list," and Karis said "that is the entire first movement, yes," and Brom said "then why does it take two hours," and Karis said, "Because they might be right about the second thing."

Havel came through the corridor at the adjournment's end with an armful of exhibit folders, going the other way. He was three feet from Cael in a corridor eight feet wide. He looked at the folders. Cael stood aside to give him the room, and Havel said "thank you" to the folders, and went on, and it was the only word that passed between them at Ostrand that term.

---

The ruling came after the midday adjournment, and it came in one paragraph, read by the seal in a registrar's voice from a page the seal had plainly drafted during the adjournment, and the paragraph did both things at once, exactly as Karis had said it would, and the doing was the corridor's first door closing on schedule.

*The seat, having heard the petition and the response, finds as follows. The enrollment's execution is sound. The provision under which it was executed is valid law, surviving the standardization directive unrepealed, and the seat so holds. The documentary record of the enrollment is complete and is not disturbed. The respondent institution has conducted itself throughout in full compliance with its charter.*

Every point. Every documentary point that could be won, won, in the seal's own type, on the record, forever.

The gallery had begun to move at *is not disturbed* — the correspondents' boards coming up, one man on the second bench already half-risen to get a courier out ahead of the others — and the seal read straight through the movement without a pause of any kind, in the same voice, at the same pace.

*Notwithstanding the enrollment's validity, the seat finds the evaluation-integrity question raised by the petition to be cognizable under the code's integrity provisions, the applicability of those provisions to a practitioner being a matter within the registry's discretion which the seat exercises. The challenge is sustained to full proceedings. The petition for compelled re-evaluation under standard Arbiter protocol will be heard on the merits at the schedule's remaining sessions, and the seat will rule upon it before term's end.*

Both things at once. You are lawful. The machine will proceed.

The half-risen man sat back down. Cael charted the room in the four seconds after the paragraph ended, because four seconds after a ruling is when a room tells the truth: the counsel writing without expression; Bracken's hands flat on the table; Withrow's chin at the exact angle it had held for two hours; forty benches of registry-literate people working out, at forty different speeds, that they had just watched a respondent win and lose in one paragraph; and at the petitioner's table, Jent, who did not smile, did not gather his papers early, and did not look across the floor even once — a man receiving a result he had been sent to obtain, in the posture of a man receiving weather.

Ilsev's beat came at the ruling's end, and the session rationed it carefully, and she spent it as she had spent every beat of her career, on the text's edges.

She asked leave to address the seat. It was granted. She asked the seal, for the record, to confirm the evaluation seat's scope.

The seal confirmed it: the seat's mandate was to assess the *procedure* of any re-evaluation ordered, its conformity to protocol, its conduct; not the outcome.

"And not," said Ilsev, "the question of whether the protocol is capable of evaluating this practitioner."

"That question is not within the evaluation seat's scope," said the seal.

"I ask that the answer be entered in the minute in those words," said Ilsev, "so that the seat's scope is not later understood to have included it."

It was entered. Ilsev entered the confirmation in her own file as well, in her own hand, and squared the file, and Cael, watching from the respondent's table, read the entering the way he had read her at Greyvane — a textualist marking the text's boundary so that the text could not later be said to have meant more than it said — and understood, with the instrument's whole cold clarity, that she had just told the room, in the only language she had, that her seat would certify the corridor's *conduct* and had no authority over where it led.

Adjourned. The seal rose; the room rose; Withrow inclined her head at the exact depth.

The market street outside had four hours of afternoon left in it and the correspondents got their couriers away inside three minutes, which Cael timed from the top step, because the speed of a ruling's departure was a measurement and he had nothing else to do with the interval while the cases were brought down.

Four couriers, four directions, all four riding before the gallery had finished emptying. By the following week the paragraph would be in print in three districts, and it would be printed accurately, and it would be understood wrongly by almost everyone who read it, because a paragraph that says *lawful* and *proceeding* in the same breath is not a paragraph most people have a shape for. Half the continent would read it as a win. The other half would read it as the beginning of a fraud finding. Nobody outside the room would read it as what it was, which was a door being closed politely with the respondent still inside and thanked for his documentation.

Jent came down the steps behind them with his clerk and the untouched tabbed folder, and paused at the bottom, and said to the counsel, without ceremony, "Your third movement was better than the authorities you had for it."

"I know," said the counsel.

"So did the seat," Jent said, and put his hat on, and went up the market street on foot.

The counsel watched him go the length of two shopfronts. "Twenty-six years," she said, to nobody in particular, "and that is the first opponent I have ever had who tells me what I got right on the way out. He does it because it costs him nothing. It's still true." She took her case from Bracken. "Brace yourselves for the next one. He'll concede more."

"There's nothing left to concede," said Bracken.

"There is always something left to concede," said the counsel, "when conceding is how you advance."


---

The coach took the river road up in the last of the light with the same seven it had carried down — the five of them, the counsel, and the chancellor — the ruling's paper in the counsel's minute-book and the day's closing exchange waiting for whoever would spend it.

Karis spent it, evenly, to the window.

"We won every point that can be won on paper."

Brom, after the length of time Brom took: "And?"

"And it's proceeding."

Nobody added anything for a while. The river went by. Lira looked at the drill-book on her knee and did not open it, and Cael charted her not opening it, and charted Brom's forearms on his knees, and charted the counsel writing in the privileged minute the one line he could read upside down — *ruling: sustained on validity; sustained to proceedings; both* — and charted the Archmarshal's absence from the day, which had been total, an evaluator not contemplated by the schedule, and the seal's presence, which had also been total, and had no face.

He had the night's log by the ferry crossing, and it was one line, because one line was the honest count: *Part of me is still keeping score. The rest of me has started counting doors.*

Then Seln, from the corner, eyes closed, in the voice of a man who had audited the machine from inside for fifteen years and had never once, until this term, heard the audit read aloud by people who had not been trained to it:

"Now you understand the file I've been reading my whole career."

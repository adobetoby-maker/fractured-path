# Chapter 21 — Resignations

The walk-out took a morning and an afternoon, and its whole discipline was smallness: no speeches, no crowd at the gate, no institutional convulsion. Paperwork, boxes, and doors, in the order the day actually ran them, because the order was the dignity.

---

Morning: Bracken's counter.

The records office opened at the first bell as it opened every day of the year, and the four of them were the first business of it, and Bracken had known they would be, and had set out four leaves of the correct form the night before, squared, with the inkstand where the light was good and a second pen laid beside the first in case a nib went. Cael counted the counter in on the way through the door because he had counted it in on his first morning at Halcenvane and intended to be consistent about it: the long oak top worn pale at the two places where hands rested; the pigeonholes behind, forty of them, alphabetical; the completed tray at the left and the pending at the right and the standing rule, posted in Bracken's hand for thirty years, that nothing crossed from right to left unverified. The morning light came in from the east window and lay along the counter in a bar, and the bar reached the completed tray at about the second bell, and Cael had watched it reach that tray perhaps two hundred times without ever once thinking he would remember it.

The form was Registry Form of Withdrawal from Enrollment, four clauses and a signature block, the same leaf a first-year filled in when a family called them home. It did not have a clause for anything that was actually happening.

They filed in the order they presented, and they presented in an order nobody had discussed and everybody understood. Lira first, because Lira went first at things. She read the four clauses through, which she did now with everything on paper and had not done at fourteen, and signed in the hard fast hand of a woman who had learned to write her own name on a form as an act rather than a formality. Bracken initialed the leaf, entered the date, entered the time, and moved it left.

Brom second. He read it twice, the second time with a finger under the third clause, which surrendered the enrollee's claim to the institution's remaining obligations for the term. "That's fees," Bracken said, without being asked. "You're four days short of term. The academy owes you four days of instruction it will not be providing. The clause says you're not asking for it back." Brom considered that for the length of time Brom considered things and then said, "Fine," and signed, and Cael, watching, understood that the pause had not been about money and that Brom had wanted the sentence explained out loud so he would know precisely what he was giving up, all of it, including the four days.

Cael third. He had drafted his the night before and copied it fair on the counter at dawn, and the copying had taken a quarter of an hour for four clauses because he had found himself reading each one entirely, the way you read a thing you are never going to be inside again. He signed, and Bracken initialed, and there it was: the demonstration-provision enrollment, the one the whole continent had spent a term arguing about, closed by the four-clause form a homesick first-year uses.

Bracken put the same question to each of them before the pen went down, because the code obliged him to and because he had never in thirty years abbreviated an obligation: whether the withdrawal was made freely, without compulsion by any officer of the institution. Lira said, "Freely." Brom said, "Yes." Cael said, "Freely," and then, because the man deserved the whole of it and because the leaf would sit in a district file for forty years, added, "and not by any officer of this institution, which I would like entered as written and not summarized."

Bracken looked at him for a moment over the counter.

"It will be entered as you said it," he said, and wrote it out longhand in the margin, which the form did not provide for and which no auditor would ever query, because a registrar's marginal notation in his own hand carries the weight of the registrar, and this registrar's weight was thirty years.

Seln did not appear at the counter, because Seln was staff, and staff did not withdraw from enrollment, and there was no leaf on the counter for what Seln was going to do. His absence from the sequence was noted by exactly two people in the room and neither of them said a word about it.

Karis went last, and Karis's was not four clauses.

---

The forfeiture ran to a second form and a schedule of line items, and Bracken read every one of them aloud, because the code required the forfeiting party to hear what was being surrendered, spoken, in the presence of the registrar, before the hand went to the page.

He read them in the schedule's own order and he read them at counter pace, neither hurrying to spare her nor slowing to make a moment of it, which was the only mercy his office had available and which he spent completely.

"Tier certification, Iron, Rank Three, issued under the district seal. Retained." He looked up. "That one you keep. Certification is not enrollment. Nobody can take a tier from you; they can only decline to count it."

"I know," Karis said. "Read the rest."

"Research standing, Halcenvane Academy, granted on faculty recommendation. Forfeit." He entered it. "Research allocation, current term, unexpended balance. Forfeit and returned to the general account. Courier privileges under the academy seal. Forfeit. Archive privileges, general collection. Forfeit. Archive privileges, restricted collection, granted by chancellor's warrant. Forfeit." A short pause while his pen caught up with his voice. "Annex allocation, north room, with lock and key. Surrendered; key to be returned at this counter. Standing request in progress with Greyvane archive under academy budget code, unfilled. Cancelled at the seal, with the requesting institution's regrets. Reading-room precedence. Forfeit. Faculty dining entitlement." He said that one exactly as flatly as the others. "Forfeit. Assistant, second-year, assigned by faculty recommendation. Reassigned, effective this date, to the general instruction pool."

He set the pen down. "That is the schedule entire. The code obliges me to ask whether you have heard each item read and whether you understand that the forfeiture is not reversible by application, only by fresh grant."

"I have and I do."

"Then sign at the foot, and initial each leaf where the seal is broken."

She did not flinch at a single line. She had, she said afterward, entered the finding two days before, and one did not flinch at the execution of a finding one had already made — but she paused, once, at the line that surrendered the restricted-collection privileges, a half-second with the researcher's hand held above the page, and Cael, at the counter's other end, charted the half-second and would carry it a long way east. The annex's north room had been the first door in her life that locked on her work instead of against it, and the warrant had been the key to it, and a half-second was the whole grief she permitted the loss, and it was enough, and it was hers.

She signed. She unhooked the key from her ring and set it on the oak, and Bracken took it and put it in the pigeonhole it had come out of eighteen months before, and the small hard sound of a key going into a wooden box was the loudest thing in the office that morning.

Bracken squared the four resignations and the forfeiture schedule together, and then, at the end, went off procedure for the first time in Cael's two years of watching him.

"The Halcenvane enrollment defense," he said, squaring the filed resignations, "was the best-documented matter I have processed in thirty years. Every leaf verified. Every provenance traced. Eleven pounds of true record, entered under seal, and a research architecture behind it that three faculties will teach for a decade." He set the stack in the completed tray. "It has been the privilege of my career to keep your records. I am sorry the records were not enough. I have spent thirty years believing they would always be enough. I would like it known that I was wrong, and that I learned it late, and that I intend to keep true records anyway, because the alternative is the thing we are all walking away from."

Nobody answered him, because there was no answer available that would not have been smaller than what he had said. Karis put her hand flat on the counter for a moment, which from Karis was an embrace. Then Bracken drew the day's ledger toward him and entered the four withdrawals in the morning column, in order, with the times, and went on to the next business, which was a second-year wanting a duplicate of a lost timetable, and he gave the second-year the same attention he had given the rest of it, because that was the man.

Gault's last panel note was entered at the same counter within the hour, hand-delivered by Gault himself, who did not usually walk his own paper anywhere. He gave it to Bracken, and waited while it was verified, which was not required, and left without going into the office proper, and the note went into the baseline file on top of four terms of observation and closed it:

*Assay-provision enrollment: resigned in good standing prior to revocation date. Baseline file: closed complete. Four terms of evaluation-linked observation, no adverse finding, no anomaly of conduct. The evaluator of record notes that the file closes on the enrollee's own act and not the registry's, and that this is the correct closing, and the only just one available. The instruments will not see its like.*

Bracken read it, and verified it, and entered it, and said nothing at all about it to anybody, then or ever.

Karis's last act inside the institution came an hour after the counter and took nine minutes. She had the second-year meet her at the annex's north room, which was no longer hers and which she had already emptied, and she gave the girl three things: the working index to the whole compiled archive, copied fair in her own hand over two evenings so that the research would remain usable by somebody even if its author was four hundred miles east; the reading list she had been going to give her at term's end anyway; and a letter of competence, one paragraph, under no institution's seal and therefore worth very little at a counter and a great deal everywhere else, stating in flat professional terms what the bearer could do and how the writer knew it.

The girl read the letter standing in the doorway of an empty room and did not ask the question she plainly wanted to ask.

"You'll be reassigned to the general pool," Karis said. "You will find that a step down and it is one. Take it, do the work correctly, and do not attach your name to mine on any form for the next two years. That is not a request I am making for my sake."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Your fourth error last month was reading the integrity provisions as a limit on the seat rather than a grant to it. You found it yourself in twenty minutes and you have not made it since." Karis picked up the last of her own pens. "That is the whole of the assessment and it is a good one. Go on."

And she went out and did not look at the room, which had a lock on it and no longer had a key in the lock, and went down to the records hall to rope a third box she had already roped once.

Cael's own last piece of institutional business took four minutes and no witnesses. He returned the residence key at the gate lodge to a porter who had no idea what he was receiving, counted the room out the way he counted every room out — the window on the quadrangle, the shelf, the two nails in the wall, the floorboard that had announced every one of his own late returns for two years — and closed the door on a twelve-foot room that had been the first space on the continent that was his by any kind of right. The observation notebook went in the satchel with the log. It held eleven Paths charted from the Crown yard's east tier, four terms of gallery work, six hundred and forty entries, and not one line anybody outside four people could have made anything of.

---

Midday: Lira's last class.

The Silver-program theory seminar met in the long room over the second floor, twenty places at a table built for thirty, high windows on the south, the continental drill-book's tactics chalked across the board's whole width in the instructor's careful diagrams — a sequence problem, four figures, the third of them the interval work she had run cold at the confluence and right at the wool town and correctly, on a painted floor, in front of a Silver assessor who had stopped writing.

She sat where she had sat for two months, third place on the window side. She took the notes she had taken for two months, in the hard fast hand, for the first fifteen minutes. And at the sixteenth, in the middle of the instructor's working of the third figure, she closed the notebook.

She stood.

The room heard the chair. Twenty fighters and an instructor turned, and the instructor stopped mid-sentence with the chalk still up, and Lira, at her place, said, "Thank you. It's been a good class and you're a good teacher," with complete courtesy and no explanation whatsoever, and picked up her kit, and walked the length of the table to the door, and opened it, and went out, and closed it behind her.

The whole room watched her go, and not one of them knew what they were watching, and the diagrams stayed on the board.

She stood in the corridor afterward for the space of about four breaths. The seminar's voice resumed behind the closed door — the instructor picking the sentence back up a word or two before where it had broken, because that is what instructors do — and the third figure went on being worked without her, and the ladder built toward her went on standing in the room she had just left, somebody else's to climb now, in perfect condition, unused.

Cael was at the corridor's far end because she had told him where to be, and he watched her not look back at the door, and did not go to her until she moved.

"Six years ago they read me out of a room like that," she told him, at the quadrangle's edge, waiting for Brom's first box-trip. "Fourteen, a form with my name on it, a door I got walked out of. I've spent six years learning to walk out of rooms on my own feet instead of theirs." She watched the seminar hall's windows. "That's the whole difference between then and now. Same door. Same leaving. Mine this time." She shouldered her own kit. "I told you I knew how to say goodbye to a page. Turns out I know how to say goodbye to a ladder too. It's the same skill. You just have to have wanted the thing first, so the goodbye costs enough to be real."

"How long were you in there before you did it?"

"Fifteen minutes. I wanted the last one to be a real class, not a gesture." She almost smiled. "I took notes. Good notes. I'm keeping them. Somebody's going to need that sequence on a road with nobody's seal on it, and it'll still work, because the drill-book was right about the interval even if it was wrong about who's allowed to run it."

---

Afternoon: Brom's three boxes.

Karis's compiled materials were the archive of four years — the working transcripts, the certified copies, the annotated code volumes, the Greyvane notes, the whole apparatus of a mind that had beaten the continent's best registry counsel on the text and lost anyway — roped in three boxes at the records hall's north end, weighed and balanced by Karis herself so that no one box was worse than the others.

Brom took them one at a time, and refused help three times, and the refusals got shorter each time. "I've got it." "I've got it." "No."

The first trip went past the covered walk's board, where the sanctioned-bout notice from the day before still hung in Rooke's flat hand with the result chalked in under it, and Brom did not look at it, and a knot of second-years at the third board went quiet as he passed and stayed quiet until he was out of the walk.

The second trip went past the Crown yard's gate, which stood open on an empty floor at that hour, the tiers grey, the inlay grey, the yard doing nothing. Cael was on the covered walk for that one and watched the whole length of it: three hundred yards of quadrangle, a big man moving at the unhurried pace of somebody who intends to make three trips and has budgeted for all three, with a box on his shoulder containing, among other things, the certified Greyvane transcripts that had won an argument nobody had been willing to lose to.

Karis watched the second trip too, from the records hall's north door, and said one thing about it, to nobody in particular, in the flattest voice she had: "Four years of my work is being carried across a quadrangle by a man who is refusing an Iron-tier certification to do it, and neither of us is going to mention that at any point in our lives." She went back inside to check that the north end was empty. It was empty. She checked it twice.

The third trip went past the district officer's parked horse.

It was still there — the same officer who had ridden up the bluff at dusk four days before with a finding that gave a man his tier and an invoice priced at his friend; he had come back up on the day the term's other business closed, on some errand of the district's, and his horse stood at the rail by the gate lodge with the district's tack on it and his saddlebag flap unbuckled. He was standing at its head when Brom came down the quadrangle with the third box on his shoulder.

Cael, thirty feet behind with Karis's inkstand and nothing else to carry, watched the officer work it out. The man was not slow. He had processed thirty advancement dockets that year and had ridden this one up himself because the panel was proud of it; he knew what he was looking at; and what he was looking at was the continental Copper champion, advancement found and unsigned, carrying a box of another enrollee's research past him toward the gate, with four days of term left and a resignation already in the completed tray.

He did not say anything. There was nothing available to say to a man answering a guidance note with his back. He inclined his head very slightly as Brom went by, and Brom did not see it, or did not acknowledge it, and went on down to the gate with the box balanced and his stride not altered by one inch.

Ephram walked that last trip beside him, carrying nothing.

He had turned up at the records hall for the third box and had put his hands out and Brom had said "No," and Ephram had said, "I know. I'm walking anyway," and had walked — the cohort captain who was staying, keeping pace at the departing champion's shoulder the length of the main quadrangle with his hands empty and his own right shoulder still holding the set it had taken in the Crown yard, stiff enough that he swung the arm short the whole way down and did not mention it once, because the walking was the point and the carrying was Brom's and he had come to do the first and not the second.

They did not talk on the way down. At the gate Ephram took the box off Brom's shoulder for the four seconds it took to set it in the cart, which was not carrying and both of them let it pass.

At the gate: Withrow, who had timed her afternoon to be there without appearing to have timed anything — a chancellor crossing her own quadrangle at the hour her departing enrollees happened to reach the gate, with a folder under her arm that gave her a destination and that she never once opened.

She said goodbye in the flattest registry register she owned.

"Your files are in order. All four."

Four, counted deliberately, the fifth man's name managed even now, even here, even by her — because Seln's departure was not a resignation filed at a counter, and Withrow, who had learned the shape of the delegation across two years, gave the fifth traveler the same courtesy the delegation gave him, which was to count him without naming him. Cael heard the whole sentence land and heard the number in it and gave her nothing back but "Thank you, Chancellor," because anything else at a gate, in the open, within earshot of two men in grey coats, would have been a gift to the wrong ledger.

The cart was loading at the gate lodge while she said it, and the loading was the last thing the academy ever did for them, performed by two porters and a stableman who had been told that a delegation was going down the bluff and had not been told anything else, and who therefore did the job at ordinary speed with ordinary complaints about the weight of the third box. Around them the term went on. A first-year ran past the gate with a message and did not look. The bell for the fourth-hour floors rang and about sixty people obeyed it. Somewhere behind the wall a coach was counting a drill out loud. The institution that had spent a term as the named respondent in the continent's most-watched proceedings closed the file on all of it by having four people walk out of a gate at an unremarkable hour while everyone else went to class, which was, Cael thought, exactly what an institution was: a thing that continues.

She looked at the four of them once more, and at the cart, and at the boxes.

"Go on, then," she said. "The light will fail on the bluff road by the fifth bell and the bluff road does not improve in the dark."

Rooke's goodbye to Brom was four words, delivered at the gate with the coaching file nowhere in sight: "Doors open both ways."

Brom said, "Coach," which was the whole of his reply and had been the whole of his reply to that man for two years.

The watchers across the road logged the departure in plain sight, unhurried: two senior men in grey coats at the wall's angle where the bluff road turned, one writing in a small book while the other watched the cart load, neither of them making any attempt to be anything other than exactly what they were. Cael counted them and their positions out of four years of habit and then, deliberately, stopped counting, and noted the stopping. Their paperwork had always been going to outlast everyone's. A resignation was a status change, and a status change was the thing they had been posted to wait for, and they were getting it in good light at forty feet, and there was nothing whatever to be done about it and no longer any reason to want to.

---

The coach road down the bluff at dusk, Halcenvane above the river behind them — the first institution that had ever wanted them, resigned from in good standing, undefeated on paper to the last.

The road turned twice on the descent and gave the academy back twice, smaller each time: the lamps coming up along the covered walk at the first turn; at the second, only the Crown yard's western wall catching the last of the light above the river. Nobody in the cart said anything at either turn. Lira drove. Karis sat with the notebook shut. Brom sat with his back against the three boxes, which was where he sat.

The log went in by the coach's lantern, in the new satchel:

*Not expelled. The machine had a verb ready — revoked, nullified, restricted — and we didn't let it conjugate a single one. We resigned. Before the date. In good standing. Four files closed complete, and Bracken kept every one of them true to the end and told me he was sorry the truth wasn't enough, which is the sentence I'll carry longest out of that whole institution.*

*Karis forfeited her ranking and paused half a second at the archive line and signed. Lira walked out of a room on her own feet, six years after they walked her out of one on theirs. Brom carried three boxes past the horse of the man who priced his tier at me, and never signed the form, and the boxes were the answer. Withrow counted four files and managed the fifth name even at the gate. Rooke said four words. Ephram carried nothing and walked the last trip anyway.*

*Four, closed complete.*

*Tomorrow, the fifth.*

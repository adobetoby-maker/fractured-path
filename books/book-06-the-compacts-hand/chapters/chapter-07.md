# Chapter 7 — Assets

The finding came to the residence table on a Fifth-day evening in the term's fourth month, and it came the way Seln's findings always came: flat, complete, in the administrative register, with a single sheet of the office's own paper in front of him on which he had drawn nothing, because a drawing could be found.

What he did not give the table was how he had got it. He never did. So Cael took the finding apart afterward, the way he took a floor apart after a bout — from the thing shown, backward to the thing done — because a finding is a document and the method under it is a second document, and the second was the one he wanted.

The method was the service side. Cael had watched the man walk it for a year and a half without once seeing it as a circuit: the delivery lane behind the refectory, where the bluff road's carts turned and the drivers talked while they waited; the stores counter, where every requisition in the academy passed under a clerk's initial and where the assessment office's own entitlement to read the stores ledger had never once been questioned, because nobody had ever thought to ask what an assessment office wanted with sacking and lamp oil; the post-room's morning sort; the gate lodge's arrivals book, which the office was permitted to inspect on the seventh day of each week and which Seln inspected on the seventh day of each week, standing at the lodge counter for a quarter of an hour with a cup of the lodge-man's tea, talking about the lodge-man's knee.

None of it was surveillance. That, Cael understood a year and a half late, was its whole merit. A man who reads the arrivals book because the code says he may is not watching anything; he is doing his work, in front of the person whose work it is, with a cup in his hand.

And on the seventh day of the third session's week the arrivals book had told him nothing at all, which was the finding — a negative, produced on schedule, by the only man who would have gone to a lodge counter looking for one.

The next joint Cael could build because he had been its subject. Two men at the waystation, senior grade, the rotation running on the waystation's own schedule, and for four years the entire content of their work had been his own movement — where he trained, who he ate with, when he went down the bluff road and in what company. A following watch adjusts to its subject. It is the cheapest tell in the trade and nobody can suppress it, because a man told to follow a subject must go where the subject goes or file a lie. Seln had been keeping the adjustments since his second month at Halcenvane, unwritten, in the only archive that could not be requisitioned, and Cael could produce no evidence that the index existed except the accuracy of the sentence it had made.

In the third session's week his own pattern had moved four times: down the bluff at an unscheduled hour with Brom, twice; the river path alone before dawn on a Fourth-day, which he had not run since the autumn; a Sixth-day at the ferry landing with Karis and two document cases, which was an itinerary a watcher would have paid for.

The rotation had not moved once.

*A watcher who does not adjust to his subject is not watching his subject,* Cael wrote in the notebook, and noted underneath that the sentence was Seln's and he had only found the shape it was cut to. *He is holding a road.*

That was half of it. The other half Cael got out of the post-room over two mornings, without asking for it. The district courier's delivery went into three piles and Seln sorted them, because the clerk had a bad shoulder and Seln had never once been in a hurry. Two of the piles were the academy's. The third was the registrar's, and it was heavy, and it had been heavy on the courier's previous run, and on both runs the assessment office's pile had contained nothing from the district seat at all.

The district seat had stopped writing to the office that watched him and had started writing to the office that held his papers.

The last step the clerk supplied himself, unasked: Seln had put the piles down, said something civil about the shoulder, and carried the office's dispatch back across the fountain court at exactly the pace he carried it every morning.

---

The counter-scouting had never been formally asked for. It was the tournament autumn's pattern, continued into the winter by a man who audited surveillance the way other clerks audited floor schedules, because auditing was what his trade did with its hands when nobody was watching them; and the delegation had learned, across two years, to treat his findings the way instruments read the weather: without asking how the instrument worked.

"The office notes a change in the observation's composition," he said, to the teapot. "The bluff-road rotation is unchanged — two men, senior grade, the waystation schedule holding. But the rotation is no longer the observation." He turned his cup a quarter-turn. "For four years the watchers watched the enrollee. Where he trained. Who he ate with. When he went down the bluff road and in what company. That work is done, and the men at the road are keeping it done, and that is all they are doing." He set the cup down. "Since the second session, the watching has moved. It is no longer on the enrollee. It is on everything *attached* to him."

He gave the table the inventory, because inventories were his native document.

A records request, three weeks old, at Halcenvane's own counter — routine in form, a district office confirming sponsorship files "for the proceedings' record" — which Bracken had answered per procedure and which had asked, in its fourth item, for the academy's full schedule of institutional relationships with the enrollee: sponsorship, floor time, evaluation-linked standing, travel endorsements. Bracken had answered it in twelve days, completely, with a covering letter that noted the completeness twice, because Bracken's whole doctrine was that a true record answered fully and let the office that read it own the consequences. Seln had read the office copy at the registrar's counter with the registrar standing beside him and had said nothing about the fourth item, and had thought about the fourth item for three weeks.

A registry query, twelve days old, at the district seat, touching Lira's certification history — the Fenmark record, the Greyvane re-certification track, the Ostrand station's supersession — "for completeness of the respondent institution's file."

A second query, nine days old, at the Velmere estate's provincial registry, touching Brom's family standing: the household's tier, the sister's guild placement, the estate's registered relationships.

A third, six days old, at Ternhall, touching Karis's transfer file — the vacated standing, the research-access records, the paired-doctrine training log.

"None of them touches the enrollee," Seln said. "Every one of them touches a person the enrollee is attached to, and asks the same question in four different offices' grammar: what does his name reach, and through whom." He squared the blank sheet. "Each is lawful. Each would survive a challenge, and two of them are the kind of query a diligent junior files without being told to. That is not an argument against the pattern. It is how the pattern is built. The office has no formal view. The office observes that this is a *pattern*, and that patterns are the only thing the office was ever trained to see."

The table sat with it, and the sitting took a while, because each of them had been handed a different sentence.

Lira looked at her own hands. She had looked at them in a wool town, at a rail, on the evening she had first been priced right, and the looking then had been a woman checking whether a good thing was hers to keep. This was the same posture doing the opposite arithmetic.

"They're reading my file at Ostrand," she said. "My certification. For *completeness*." She turned one hand over. "That file has a supersession in it. It has the Fenmark entry underneath the supersession, because the registry doesn't delete, it corrects, and the correction sits on top of the thing it corrected, forever, where anyone with a reason can read both." She looked up. "I was fourteen when the bottom of that page got written. I have spent five years getting a line drawn through it by an office with a seal. And a clerk at the district seat has now read both halves, in one afternoon, for completeness."

"They will not use it," Karis said. "There's nothing in it to use."

"I know that." Lira's voice did not move. "I'd still like it noted that they've read it."

Karis noted it. That was the answer the room had, and Lira took it, and it was worth something, and everyone at the table knew exactly how much.

Brom said nothing at all.

That was the whole Velmere ledger opening again, on a table in a provincial registry he had never entered, in a district he had left at fifteen and had returned to exactly twice, both times in a delegation's colors. The household's tier. The sister's guild placement. His father's estate, its registered relationships, its standing — read by a clerk at a counter for the purpose of understanding what a boy in a border town's shop had to do with any of it. He sat with his forearms on his knees and looked at the middle of the table for the length of time he looked at things he intended to remember, and then he asked one question, which was the only question he asked all evening.

"Does the query show at the estate's end? Does somebody there know it was made?"

"The provincial registry logs queries in an open ledger," said Seln. "Anyone entitled to the estate's file may read who has asked after it. Your father is entitled."

Brom nodded once and did not say anything else, and Cael charted the nod, and filed it under the heading he had been building for two years, which was: *Brom's costs are never the ones on the schedule.*

Karis had the notebook out and was writing the four offices in a column, dated, in the hand she used for findings rather than notes. And when she had written the fourth she left a line beneath it — a whole line, ruled, empty — and went on to the next page for her own working, and Cael watched her leave the line and understood that the researcher had already run the shape ahead of the rest of them, and had concluded there would be a fifth, and had allocated it space.

"Why the blank line?" Lira asked her, afterward, at the stair.

"Because an inventory of four is a coincidence," Karis said. "An inventory that includes his family is a document. They haven't reached the family yet." She closed the notebook. "They will. That's how you'd build it if you were building it, and whoever is building this is competent."

---

The fifth arrived four days later, in the ordinary post, in a hand the delegation knew.

It was not in the day's stack when the post-room sorted it; it was on the stack's top, because the post clerk had recognized the hand — the Denvash letters had been arriving at Halcenvane for two years and the clerk knew them by their upright address block and put them out first, as a courtesy to a boy he had never spoken to about anything but weather. Cael took it on the covered walk and opened it walking, which he did with Hesk's letters and nothing else, because Hesk's letters had never once in two years contained bad news and the habit had been built on the record.

*The circulars say the proceedings are at Ostrand and the broadsides say you are a case, and I say a case is what a fiddle travels in, and the fiddle is what matters. Mind the fiddle.*

The man's whole register in the first line: instrument-maker's upright letters, the same letters that had taught Cael his own at a workbench with a lamp over it, the same refusal to grant a large thing more room than it had earned. Two paragraphs on the shop and the winter — the roof over the back store, patched again; the price of gut up a third and nobody's fault; a repair that had taken him nine days because the customer's grandfather had built the instrument wrong in a way the customer loved, and Hesk had spent five of the nine deciding to preserve the fault rather than correct it, which he reported as a business decision and which Cael read as the entire philosophy of the man who had raised him.

And then the third paragraph, which Cael read standing on the covered walk, and read again, and then carried at a pace the walk had not seen from him in a year to the residence, where the table was assembling for the evening and where he read it aloud at the window, because the letter had been opened there and its third paragraph could not be carried across the room unshared.

*Two polite men from the registry came through Denvash asking after my trade licenses and the shop's title. Wanted to know if you had any interest recorded in either. I told them my grandson's interest in this shop is that he grew up in it, and they wrote that down. Whatever they're building, it has a shape. Mind the shape. — H.*

Nobody at the table spoke.

Cael read it a second time, silently, because the letter was the kind he read four times, and the log would have the fourth reading. The first two readings were the table's. *Two polite men from the registry.* In Denvash — the border town, the back exit, the place a fourteen-year-old had been walked out of through a door nobody used, four years and a continent ago; the shop with the sign Hesk had hung himself, the workbench, the leather book, the man who had written *sixteen suits you* and *the fault's in the workshop, never the instrument* and *mind the shape* — visited, politely, by the registry, asking whether the boy's name reached the title of the only building on the continent he had ever called home.

He could put the two men in the shop. That was the thing he could not stop doing, standing at the window with the paper in his hand: the instrument reconstructed the room without being asked. They would have come in the front, because polite men used front doors, and the bell over it would have gone, and Hesk would have come through from the back with his glasses up on his head and his hands not yet wiped, and would have stood behind the counter where he stood for everyone, and would have answered every question they asked, accurately, at the length the question deserved and no longer. He would have offered them nothing. He would not have lied. And when they had gone he would have wiped his hands, and gone back to the nine-day repair, and that evening he would have got the paper out and written *whatever they're building, it has a shape* in the upright letters, and gone to bed.

The two men had written down *grew up in it*. Cael could see the hand doing it. A pencil, a folded form, a category with a number on it.

"They went to Denvash," Lira said. Not a question. The voice she had used at a coat-rack, discovering an institution was small — inverted; discovering one was everywhere.

"They went to Denvash," Cael said.

Seln had the blank sheet in front of him, and he did not write on it, but he laid one finger on it where the fifth line would have gone, and the table watched him do it, and understood that the pattern had its fifth point and that the pattern was now a shape, and that the man at the table's end had known its shape since the second session and had been waiting, per his trade, for the shape to draw itself.

---

He read the letter the third time at the window, alone, after the table had thinned, and the fourth time at the residence table an hour later with the inner circle assembled and Seln at the bench's middle, because the fourth reading was the council's, and the council needed the shape named by the one man at the table who had built shapes like it.

Seln named it in the flat register of a man describing his own former trade — which was, Cael understood as he listened, exactly what the man was doing, and the flatness was the price.

"Pre-restriction inventory." He said it to the blank sheet. "Before the registry freezes an unclassified person's standing under chapter fourteen, it maps everything the standing touches. Sponsors. Institutions. Family assets. Endorsements, credentials, titles, the shop a grandfather holds in a border town. It runs the map in advance so that when the restriction executes, it executes *complete* — every door on the continent closing on the same morning, because every door has already been found." He turned his cup. "It is run only when the outcome is already assumed. Nobody inventories the assets of a man who might be found lawful; the inventory is itself a finding, made before the seat makes one." He set the cup down. "They're not preparing for a ruling. They're preparing for the day after it."

"How long does it take to run?" Karis asked. She had her pen down, which she did not do.

"Six to ten weeks for a person with four relationships. This one has a delegation, an academy, three provincial registries and a border town." Seln's eyes stayed on the sheet. "Nine weeks. They are in the sixth."

"And when it's finished?"

"Nothing," he said. "That is the part the code does not explain and the offices do not need explained. A completed inventory is not an instrument. It is not served, it is not published, and it does not exist as a document with a name — it exists as five files in five offices, each lawful, each unremarkable, indexed to one name. It waits. If the seat rules for the respondent, it is never assembled, and in four years a clerk clears it, and nobody has done anything wrong at any point." He squared the blank sheet against the table's edge. "If the seat rules the other way, the assembly takes an afternoon, and the restriction executes on every file at once, and the enrollee learns what the sixth week was for on the morning it is already finished."

The sentence sat on the table, and the table let it sit, because there was nothing to say to it that its own grammar had not already said.

Karis wrote the fifth office in her column — *Denvash, provincial registry, trade licenses and title* — and dated it, and drew a line under the five, and the line was a shape.

Then she set the pen down beside the notebook and said the thing she had been holding since the stair, in the flat voice she used for findings she had checked twice.

"I want it on the record that the four of them were about *us*. Certification, family standing, transfer file — those are the three of us at the table. The fifth is a man who has never been near an academy and has no standing to freeze." She looked at Cael. "Hesk isn't in the inventory because Hesk is an asset. He's in it because the ninth category is *other relationships*, and the shape they're building has to be complete, and the only way to complete a shape around you is to find everything you would not want touched." She closed the notebook. "That's not restriction machinery. That's the same machinery with a second use, and I'd like somebody at this table to say out loud that we noticed."

"Noticed," said Brom.

Brom looked at the letter in Cael's hands for the length of time he looked at things he intended to remember — at the upright letters, at the address block, at the fold — and said nothing more, and the nothing had Velmere in it, and had also, Cael thought, a shop in it, and a man behind a counter with his glasses up on his head, whom Brom had never met and had heard about at a hundred tables.

It was Lira who turned the room, and she did it the way she had turned every corner of her life, by declining the premise.

"So the question was never whether we win the argument." She said it evenly, a woman reading a bout sheet's honest arithmetic aloud. "Karis wins every filing. The counsel wins every session. Cael wins every point that can be argued. And their men are in Denvash measuring the shop." She looked around at them. "The question is what we do the day after."

Nobody answered that either. But Cael charted the table as she said it, the way he charted everything, and the charting recorded four people not answering in four registers — Karis with her hand flat on a closed notebook, Brom with his forearms on his knees, Lira with the question still open in front of her and her chin up over it — and one of the four, the man at the bench's middle, who had built inventories like this one and knew what the day after them looked like from the inside, not answering in a fifth, which was the register of a man whose reasons for keeping a thing sealed had just been handed their first invoice.

---

Withrow was informed that evening, in her office, by Cael, with the letter in his hand, and she read it standing, and the paragraph about Denvash produced in her the thing he had never once seen it produce in two years: a pause, mid-page, with nothing on her face, that lasted long enough to count.

He counted it. Nine seconds. She had read the first two paragraphs in twelve.

"Trade licenses and title," she said, at the end of it. "In Denvash. On an instrument-maker." She handed the letter back. "I administered two restrictions. I told you that."

"You did. At the enrollment."

"I did not tell you that I ran the inventories myself, both times, because the district was short-staffed and I was thorough." She sat down, which she did not do while he stood, and gestured him to the chair opposite, which she did not do either. "The second one I remember better, and I'll tell you it because you should hear a completed one from somebody who completed it, rather than assemble it out of Seln's grammar and your own imagination, which will be worse and will also be wrong in the wrong direction."

"How wrong?"

"Too dramatic," Withrow said. "There is no scene in it. That's the thing you need to have in your hands." She put her palms flat on the desk. "A Bronze Rank 4. Force Path, thirty-one years old, a haulage guild's contracted practitioner in a river district, married, two children, a house with fourteen years left owing on it. He came up on an evaluation-integrity matter of his own — not yours; nothing like yours; his Kindling record had a defect in it that was somebody else's error twenty years earlier and could not be cured because the evaluating officer was dead. The re-evaluation returned no valid classification. That is the whole of what he did wrong: an instrument was pointed at him and produced nothing, twice."

"And the inventory."

"Nine categories." She counted them off on the desk with two fingers, not looking at her hand. "Registered employment. Guild membership and standing. Credentials and endorsements. Property held or part-held. Financial instruments and debts. Institutional enrollments, his own and his dependents'. Travel and transit endorsements. Public office or contracted service. And *other relationships.*" She stopped. "It took me seven weeks. I was proud of it, in the way you are proud of a thorough piece of work, and I have thought about that pride for twenty-five years."

"What happened on the morning?"

"Nothing happened. That's the answer. Nothing happened *at* him." Her voice did not rise; it flattened, which he had learned was the same thing. "I did not go to his house. Nobody went to his house. Nine instruments went out from four offices on one morning, and by the second bell his guild had suspended his contract because a guild may not contract an unclassified practitioner, and the haulage yard had put another man on his run, and his bank had frozen the house's instrument because the instrument named his standing as security, and his daughter's academy had written to say her enrollment was under review because a dependent's enrollment carries the parent's standing, and his transit endorsement had lapsed, which meant he could not lawfully leave the district to argue about any of it." She looked at the charter above the file cases. "He found out in the order the post arrived. He came to my counter on the fourth day with all nine letters in a satchel and asked me which one to answer first. I told him the truth, which was that they were not nine problems. They were one problem with nine faces, and the one problem had no counter to be answered at, because the restriction is not an act. It is a *condition.* There is nobody to argue with. That is the design."

Cael was quiet. Then: "What did he do?"

"He went across the registry line — east, past the last station — eventually. Most of them do; there is nowhere else to be an unclassified man with a family. It took him a year and a half to get there, because a transit endorsement takes a year and a half to work around, and I do not know what happened after, because a person who crosses that line stops producing records and records are the only eyes I have." Withrow put her hands together. "Now. Your grandfather. They wrote 'grew up in it' under the ninth category, because that is what he told them, and it will be entered, and it will be true, and it will be *there*, on the day the ninth category is executed. Nothing will be alleged against him. Nothing needs to be. A trade license held by a man with a recorded relationship to a restricted person is a license a district office may review. Reviewing is not refusing. It is only slow."

"How slow?"

"As slow as anyone wants," she said. "That's the whole instrument. Not a hand on the door. A hand on the clock."

She stood, then, and went to the window, and the fountain court was below it and the dark was down over the bluff, and she said the last of it to the glass.

"I have been choosing. I want you to know I'm still choosing. I also want you to know that from tonight the word costs more than it did, and that I've priced it, and I'm paying."

---

The log, at the window, with the letter on the sill and the fountain running in the dark, was the first pole cracking at its edge, and it went down in one pass with nothing struck out, which was how the honest entries arrived:

*I keep winning sessions. Karis keeps winning filings. And their men are in Denvash measuring my grandfather's shop. Somebody explain to me which part of that is the system working — because I built three years on the difference between the system and the people, and the difference is getting hard to find in the dark.*

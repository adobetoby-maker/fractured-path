# Chapter 3 — Four Tracks

The four of them had walked in through the same gate on the same morning, and by the end of the third week they were barely in the same building.

Cael had expected some version of this. It had happened at Greyvane, where three tracks had pulled them apart along three different timetables and they had put the evenings back together by main force. What he had not expected was the direction of the asymmetry, and he wrote it down on the twenty-second day because it was the first thing about Halcenvane that had genuinely surprised him.

*Four tracks. Ranked by how much friction the institution's own machinery puts on each of us:*

*Brom: none. Standard enrollment, Copper-tier formal, clean transfer. He is exactly what the paperwork says and the paperwork has nothing to do.*

*Me: almost none. The assay standing is a category with a name, an entry requirement, and an obligation, and every part of it is written down and none of it is contested. Bracken says the file is the cleanest unusual enrollment she has ever assembled.*

*Karis: none, except the parts she's chosen to argue about, which is a different thing.*

*Lira: all of it.*

*A year ago I was the one whose door had to be invented. Now I have the second-soundest paper in this party and Lira has a flag in a queue.*

*I have no idea what to do with that. Writing it down first.*

---

Brom came back from his first session with Rooke's cohort at the fifth bell, walked into the second-quadrangle common room, sat down on the floor rather than a chair, and did not speak for about ninety seconds.

Lira looked up from the ladder registration form she had been failing to fill in for an hour. "How was it."

"I have been hit," Brom said, "more times in one hour than in the previous year."

"Total?"

"Total." He said it with the dazed precision of a man reading off a figure he had checked twice. "I counted for the first twenty minutes and then I stopped counting because counting was taking attention I needed for other things. I have never in my life had to stop counting."

Karis had already got her notebook out. "What kind of hits?"

"Clean ones. That's the part." He leaned his head back against the wall. "Not one lucky hit. Not one wild one. Forty-something clean scoring touches from four different people who were, all four of them, doing exactly what they meant to do at a speed I have never had to answer."

"You're Iron-equivalent," Lira said. "You've been Iron-equivalent for two years."

"I'm Iron-equivalent against *circuit* speed and *Greyvane* speed. Wray told me there was a wall. I've been trying to find the wall for a year and a half and Greyvane didn't have anybody who could show me where it was." Brom finally opened his eyes, and Cael, sitting across the room running the four layers on him out of pure habit, registered something in his friend's face that was not distress at all. "I found the wall. It has students."

He got the ice wrap out and put it on his left shoulder without being asked, which told Cael more about the hour than the count had.

"Are you all right?"

"I'm *delighted*," Brom said, with total sincerity. "Do you understand how long it's been since anyone in a room could teach me anything by hitting me? I've spent two years being the hardest problem in every hall I've walked into. It's boring. It's boring in a way I couldn't say out loud because saying it out loud sounds like bragging." He shifted the wrap. "Today I was the easiest problem in the room and four people solved me before lunch. I've never had a better afternoon."

The flaw took three sessions.

That was the number Brom reported and Cael believed it, because Cael had done the arithmetic independently from the gallery on the fourth day and had come out at three as well. The flaw was Wray's finding from Greyvane, a year old now and honestly logged: Brom's redirect form was self-taught, built alone in the years between leaving Velmere and finding the circuit, and it carried a gap between reading an incoming force and executing the redirect against it. Not a large gap. Small enough that it had never once cost him anything at Copper speed, and small enough that Iron-equivalent circuit work had let it pass for two seasons.

Ephram found it in three sessions and then spent the next fortnight doing nothing else.

Cael watched four of those sessions from the east hall's gallery and filled eleven pages, and by the end he had the mechanism as clearly as if it had been his own.

*Ephram. Blade Path, Iron-tier Rank 6, tops the Iron bracket standings — which at Halcenvane is what "top-ranked" means. There are fourteen Silver students and one Gold in this academy and none of them are considered the best competitor in it, because "best" here is a ladder position and the ladder is where the institution actually looks.*

*Method against Brom, session four onward: he does not attack the gap. He manufactures it.*

*Brom's redirect requires a read. A read requires an incoming force to read. Ephram feeds him forces that are almost worth reading — three-quarter commitments, weight sold honestly enough to trigger the read and withdrawn before the redirect executes. Every false feed costs Brom the whole latency. Ephram pays a fraction of a beat for each one and charges Brom the full window, and he can afford to do it eleven times in an exchange because Blade Path recommitment is cheap and Iron Skin redirect is not.*

*This is the most economical exploitation of a flaw I have ever watched. He is not faster than Brom by enough to simply beat him. He is running a trade where his cost per repetition is lower, and he is running it until the ledger closes.*

*Cost to Ephram: he cannot do this against a fast opponent, because the false feeds are readable if you have time to read them, and he doesn't have time to make them convincing against someone who moves at his own speed. This works because Brom is strong and slow. Against Brom it is close to perfect.*

*Note for the file: I could beat this. I don't know if I could beat Ephram.*

Brom's own account of it, over the second week, was three sentences and got shorter each time.

"He's teaching my body a lie," he said, on the ninth day, with his forearms on the wall and the river going dark below. "Every time I read something that isn't there, the reading gets a little cheaper to trigger. I'm being trained wrong by an opponent, on purpose, and I can feel it happening and I can't stop it happening inside an exchange."

"Have you told Rooke?"

"Rooke told me." Brom laughed, one syllable, without much in it. "Rooke told me on day three. He said — and I'm quoting, because I wrote it down —" and here Cael's attention sharpened, because Brom did not write things down, had never written things down, had once told him that writing a thing down was a way of putting it somewhere other than your body — "he said: *You built that redirect alone and it was a good piece of work for a boy with no instructor. It is now the most expensive habit you own. We are going to take it apart and you are going to be worse for a season.*"

"And?"

"And I said all right." Brom looked at the water. "I've never had anybody offer to make me worse before. Everyone I've ever trained with has been trying to make me win on Tuesday."

---

Lira's problem had no session count and no instructor and nothing to take apart.

She registered for the Copper bracket on the eleventh day, two days inside the deadline, after a week of not filling in a form that took four minutes to fill in. Cael did not say anything about the week, because there was nothing useful to say and because she would have hated it.

The bracket was two hundred and nine practitioners deep, and the standings board on the Crown yard's north wall carried the top forty of them in chalk with the rest listed in a bound register at the registrar's table for anybody who cared to look. Lira's name went on at the bottom of the board's fortieth line the day after her first scheduled round, and moved up eleven places the following week, and by the end of the first month was in the top fifteen and climbing at a rate the porter described, without being asked, as *not usual*.

None of that touched the paper.

Cael understood the mechanism completely and it did not help either of them. Greyvane had watched her beat Iron-equivalent opposition for two terms and had done the only thing a minor academy could do about it, which was file a priority-review flag on her formal classification and send it up. The flag had transferred. It sat now in a registry queue at the district office on Weighbridge Street, one item in a stack, and it entitled her to be reassessed by an Arbiter *eventually*. Until the reassessment happened, her tier was Copper, because her certificate said Copper, and the bracket read off the certificate.

"Three a year," Lira said, on the seventeenth day, looking at the board. "That's what the clerk said. Three flagged reassessments came back in two years."

"Three flagged reassessments came back *to Halcenvane* in two years," Cael said. "That's not the throughput of the queue. That's Halcenvane's share of it."

"Do you know the throughput of the queue?"

"No."

"Then that's not a comfort, it's a correction."

"It was a correction."

"I know." She exhaled. "Keep making them. I'd rather be corrected than comforted, I just want it on record that I noticed."

She stood at the board a while longer.

"The maddening thing isn't the bracket," she said eventually. "I can win the bracket. I'm going to win the bracket, and I'm not saying that to be difficult, I'm saying it because I've now fought nine of them and I know what's in here. The maddening thing is that winning it doesn't do anything. There's no promotion. You don't go up a bracket by winning your bracket. You go up a bracket by an Arbiter changing a number on a certificate, and the ladder and the certificate don't talk to each other at all."

Cael thought about the fourth board, the one for the Silver bracket, and about a sorting operation that ran out of resolution when the numbers went thin.

"They talk to each other in one direction," he said. "The certificate decides the bracket. Nothing goes back the other way."

"Nothing goes back the other way," Lira agreed. "So it's a machine with an input and no output. What's the point of it?"

He did not have an answer that night. He wrote the question down instead, and underlined it, and it stayed underlined for a long time.

---

She met Fiske eight days later, at the board, in front of about thirty people, and the whole of it took under a minute.

Cael was there because he was at every ladder session by then, and he had a good sightline, and afterward he was able to reconstruct the sequence exactly.

Fiske had just come off the yard. Force Path, Copper Rank 8, twenty years old, two years the holder of the Copper Crown — and she had spent the preceding eleven minutes dismantling a Copper Rank 4 with a completeness that Cael found almost uncomfortable to watch. He had the notes.

*Fiske. Force Path compression work, and it is not the Force Path I've charted before. Everyone else in this bracket uses Force as delivery — build, commit, deliver. She uses it as* placement*. Three declarations in eleven minutes, which is fewer than anyone else on the board averages, and all three arrived where her opponent's recovery was going to be rather than where he was. She is not out-forcing anybody. She is out-predicting them and then using force to close the exit.*

*Two years defending a crown against everyone this bracket can send. That's what that is. She has fought every plausible Copper answer to her, repeatedly, and there aren't any left, and so she has stopped needing to be strong and has become something else instead.*

*Price of the style: it needs a read. If she can't predict, she has nothing — I watched her open badly in the first exchange, twice, because she was still collecting. She is beatable in the first ninety seconds by someone she hasn't seen.*

*Gain: after ninety seconds she is, in this bracket, unbeatable, and everyone in this bracket knows it, and it shows in how they open against her, which is badly, because they're already losing.*

Fiske came off the floor and up the shallow steps to the boards, and Lira was standing there, and the two of them looked at each other for a moment.

"You're the transfer with the flag," Fiske said.

"You're the one who's been up there two years."

That was all. Fiske looked at Lira for perhaps two seconds longer, and something in her face — Cael was running all four layers and he got most of it — did the thing a person's face does when a diagnosis they have been carrying alone turns out to be legible on somebody else.

Then she went to get water, and Lira turned back to the board, and the thirty people who had been pretending not to watch went back to pretending not to have been.

Lira did not say anything about it that evening. She said something about it eleven days later, out of nowhere, in the middle of a conversation about laundry.

"She's not in that bracket because she's Copper," Lira said. "She's Copper because nobody's reassessed her. Same queue. Same flag. Except she hasn't got a flag, because Halcenvane never filed one for her, because — " and she stopped, and worked it out on the spot, in front of them, and Cael watched her arrive at it — "because she's winning. If she loses her crown they might file. While she's holding it, she's the Copper champion, and the Copper champion is worth something to this academy exactly as long as she stays Copper."

Karis put her pen down. "That's an extremely serious accusation."

"It's not an accusation. Nobody decided it." Lira sat down. "That's what's wrong with it. Nobody in a room said *keep her Copper*. It's just that filing the flag costs somebody an afternoon and doesn't do anything for anyone here, and not filing it costs nobody anything and keeps a crown on the shelf. So it doesn't get filed. For two years."

"That's the whole thing," Cael said, and he said it slowly, because it was arriving as he said it. "That's what a system is. It's not people deciding. It's what happens when nobody has a reason to do the work."

---

His own track had a shape and the shape was a hole.

The assay standing gave him supervised training access and evaluation-linked floor time. In practice that meant three things. He could train on any of the three halls' floors when a faculty supervisor was present and had signed for him — scheduled by the assessment office, rota'd across whichever instructor was on the floor, never the same one twice in a fortnight. He could attend any lecture. He could be present at anything the institution did in public.

He could not enter a bracket. He had no formal tier, and the ladder read off the certificate, and there was no certificate.

So he had no standings line, no season, no scheduled rounds, no challenge allowance, no seeding, and no crown available to him at any point in the year, at any level of performance, under any circumstances whatsoever. If he beat every practitioner in the building, in front of everyone, the boards would not change, because there was nowhere on the boards for him to be.

Present everywhere. Eligible nowhere.

He had felt a version of this at Greyvane and had converted it, over a year, into the single most useful discipline he owned: if you cannot compete, you can watch, and watching from a position nobody is measuring is the best seat in any building. He had called it the vantage-point discipline in his own notes and had meant it seriously.

Halcenvane made him find out whether he had meant it.

The honest answer, written down on the nineteenth day, was: *mostly.*

*The vantage point is real and it is better here than it was at Greyvane by a factor I can't yet estimate. Twenty-two declared Paths taught on these floors. I had charted eleven before I arrived, which means eleven more I had never seen at all — Lattice, Ash, Mire, and eight I've only had at a distance so far. Lattice alone would have been a year's work at Ardenmere and there are nine Lattice students here and one instructor who has taught it for thirty-one years.*

*Also true: I stood at the boards on the fourteenth day for six minutes reading two hundred and nine names, and mine was not among them, and it was never going to be, and I noticed I minded.*

*Not the crown. I don't want the crown. I want the* line*. I want a row of figures in chalk that says what I did this season, in public, that anybody could check.*

*Which is the same thing I've wanted since Denvash and it has never once been available and I should stop being surprised by that.*

*Filed. Working on it.*

The floor time itself was good, and it was the first thing at Halcenvane that was straightforwardly, physically good.

Greyvane's training hall had been one room with a beam ceiling and a floor that had been swept for sixty years. The east hall was sprung timber over stone — four inches of oak on bearers, laid by somebody who understood that a floor which gives half an inch under a landing saves a knee every thousand landings. He noticed it in his body inside two sessions. The Wind-adjacent landing beat, the locked half-breath at the end of every burst, had always come home through his heels and up into the hip; on sprung timber it came home softer and spread further, and the leading-side strain that used to cost him three days after a hard evening was costing him two.

He logged that, because it was a real change to a price he had been paying for three years.

*Wind-adjacent, on sprung floor. Cost per burst: unchanged in air — the held half-breath is the fragment's, not the floor's. Landing beat: unchanged in duration, softer in consequence. Hip strain, leading side, cumulative: reduced by something like a third across a comparable session load. Four bursts in a session on this floor costs me roughly what three cost on Greyvane's stone.*

*Which means my working ceiling was never entirely a property of me. Some of it was a property of the surface. I have been pricing a fragment for three years using a measurement that had a building in it.*

*Corollary, and I don't like it: every ceiling I have written down might have something in it that isn't me. I need to go back through the whole log with that question.*

He started that re-audit on the twentieth day and it was not finished by the end of term.

The other thing the floor time bought him was one supervised sparring slot a week, and in the third of those he learned something that cost him an evening's good mood and earned two pages.

The slot was against Lira, which the assessment office permitted because they were on the same residence roster and because the supervising instructor that week was the Ash Path lecturer, who signed the sheet, sat down on the gallery rail, and thereafter interfered with nothing. They ran full intensity with the old Ardenmere protocol — she called a stop the instant a fragment visibly activated, and he had thirty seconds to write.

Then a Lattice Path pair took the neighbouring floor, and Cael lost the rest of the session to watching them, and Lira let him, because she had lost sessions the same way herself.

Lattice, close up, did not behave like anything he had charted.

Every Path he had studied delivered *mass* — Force pushed it, Blade concentrated it, Stone anchored it, Iron Skin dispersed it, and all of them, at bottom, were arguments about where weight was going to be. The Iron-adjacent surface read worked on all of them because all of them arrived as pressure, and pressure arrived on his skin a fraction before it arrived anywhere else.

Lattice did not arrive as pressure. Lattice built *structure* — thin lines of held tension between two points, and then a third, and then the whole thing was a frame, and the frame did not push anything at all; it decided where his opponent was permitted to move next.

He walked over during the break, asked permission, and stood a pace and a half from the elder of the two while she built a three-point frame at quarter speed with the patience of someone who had done it eight thousand times.

The read gave him nothing.

Not a faint reading. Not a thin one, like the drop of a bad angle. *Nothing* — the same absence he got standing in an empty corridor, while a structure that would have stopped him moving in two directions assembled itself eighteen inches from his forearm.

*Iron-adjacent surface read against Lattice Path: no return. Not degraded. Absent.*

*Working explanation, unconfirmed: the read is a pressure instrument. It measures mass in motion. Lattice declarations don't move mass; they hold tension between fixed points and let the geometry do the work. There is nothing for the instrument to measure, so it measures nothing, and it does not tell me it's measuring nothing — it returns the same quiet it returns in an empty room.*

*That is the dangerous part and I want it in large letters. A tool that fails loudly is a tool with a limit. A tool that fails* silently *is a tool that lies. For three years the read has been the thing I trust when I can't trust my eyes, and I have just found a Path it cannot see at all, and it did not warn me.*

*There are nine Lattice students in this academy. There are eleven more Paths on these floors I have not yet had at close range. I have to assume the read is blind to some of them and I will not know which until I stand next to one.*

*Rule, new, effective now: the surface read's silence is not information. Treat every null return as unknown, not as clear.*

He wrote that one out twice, in two different books, which he had done perhaps four times in his life.

The supervisors were the other education, and the education was in the rotation.

Because the assessment office rota'd him, he trained in front of eleven different faculty in his first month, none of them more than twice. Every one of them watched him with the mild, unloaded attention of an instructor supervising a student who was not theirs, and every one of them, without exception, watched him more closely in the second half of the session than the first.

He tracked the crossover point. It ran between the eleventh and the nineteenth minute, and it correlated with nothing he could isolate except deployment: the moment he used the Wind-adjacent framework at anything above a walk, the supervising instructor's attention changed state, and it did not change back.

*Eleven supervisors, eleven crossovers, no exceptions. Mean crossover: fourteen minutes.*

*Nobody has said a word to me about it. Not one question in a month. That is either institutional discipline of a very high order or somebody has told them not to, and I would like to know which, and I have no way to find out that doesn't cost more than the answer is worth.*

*Note the shape of this, though, because the shape is new: at Greyvane people asked. Constantly. Badly. Here nobody asks, and the not-asking is itself organised.*

---

Karis got her research standing in writing on the twenty-fourth day, after four sessions with Bracken across ten days, and came back to the second quadrangle in a condition Cael had seen exactly twice before.

"Terms," she said, and put the document on the table, and did not sit down. "One: I have carrel access and overnight retention, which I already had. Two: I have south-range access to the third and fourth floors *without an escort*, which Bracken tells me is normally faculty-only and which she has granted on the grounds that I have already demonstrated documentary competence to the institution's benefit, which is the politest sentence anyone has ever written about me. Three — and this is the one I fought for — anything I compile from those floors in support of the assay provision's documentary defence is filed as *institutional* work product, not student work, and is held by the registrar rather than the library."

Brom, who had been half asleep, opened one eye. "Why does that matter?"

"Because student work gets compiled into the library's archives at the end of each academic cycle," Karis said, "and institutional work product held by the registrar doesn't. It's a filing distinction. Filing distinctions are the whole game and nobody ever believes me about that until something goes wrong."

She sat down at last.

"The other thing," she said, and here the register changed, and Cael put down what he was holding, "is that the south range is not a library section. It's a *stratum*. Greyvane had founding-era material because Greyvane was built on a waystation and inherited a shelf. Halcenvane bought its registry-law holdings, deliberately, over a hundred and forty years, as a collection — pre-standardization compilations, the standardization directive with its own drafting records, forty years of consolidations, and the whole apparatus of commentary around all of it." She flattened her hands on the table. "I have been reading four lines of the assay clause for five months. There are eleven bays up there. Four of them are the era those four lines came from."

"How long to read it?"

"Properly? Years." She said it with something close to joy. "That is not a complaint. Do you understand that I have spent my entire life running out of material?"

---

Cael wrote the term's first real entry at the end of the fourth week, on the wall behind the second quadrangle, with all three of them nearby doing something else.

Brom was working a redirect drill against a post, slowly, at a fraction of speed, on Rooke's instruction — the taking-apart having begun, the season of being worse having formally started. Lira was reading the ladder register with an expression that had settled, over the month, into something patient and slightly dangerous. Karis had four documents open on the wall's flat top and was weighing the corners down with stones.

*End of the first month. Four tracks.*

*Brom is being made worse on purpose by a man who told him in advance that it would happen, and he is happier than I have seen him.*

*Lira is eleventh on a board of two hundred and nine and climbing, and none of it can reach the piece of paper that decides where she's allowed to climb.*

*Karis has been given four bays of an era and terms in writing that stop her own work being swallowed by a filing cycle.*

*And I have a category with a name on it, floor time on the best surface I have ever stood on, twenty-two Paths on three halls, and no line on any board in this institution.*

He looked at that for a while, and then wrote the part that had taken the whole month to be able to write.

*Four of us, four doors, all real. A year ago none of us could have walked through any of them. Worth writing down on the days it doesn't feel like enough.*

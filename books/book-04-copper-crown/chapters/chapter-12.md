# Chapter 12 — What Seln Keeps

Ten days after the pin sheared, Seln sat down at his own table in his own room with the shutter closed, and did a thing he had not done in nineteen postings.

He wrote something down for himself.

He had thought about it for most of a week, in the way he thought about operational questions, which was to say not deliberately and not at set times but continuously and at a low rate, the way a man carries a small stone in a boot and adjusts around it. The conclusion had assembled itself on the bluff road on the ninth day, coming back up from the courier post in the dark with the third of the null reports already in the bag going south, and he had recognized it as a conclusion because it arrived with the shape of something he had been avoiding rather than something he had been reaching for.

The reasoning was not complicated and he did not dress it up.

An officer who stops reporting is a liability. That was the first premise and it was true and every man in the service knew it. The apparatus does not run on the honesty of its officers; it runs on the *completeness* of its files, and the file is the only thing that survives a posting, and an officer whose files have a hole in them has built a trap that will close on him in four years when somebody with better access reads the same period from a different angle and asks why one man's product went quiet at exactly the point the interesting thing happened.

An officer who stops reporting and stops recording is worse than a liability. He is a fool. Because the hole is going to be found eventually — everything is found eventually, it is a slow apparatus but it is not a stupid one — and when it is found, the officer's entire defence rests on his ability to say what he knew and when he had known it, and a man who has kept nothing has no defence at all. He has only his word, and the word of a covered officer with a gap in his product is worth precisely nothing, and Seln had personally watched it be worth nothing twice.

So: the file. Not product. Not a report. A private record, kept for himself, in his own shorthand, of what he had observed and when and what he had judged about it. If it was ever needed, it existed. If it was never needed, it cost him nothing but paper.

That was the whole of the logic and it was sound, and he sat with it for a while, and did not permit himself to notice that a man who was reporting everything would have had no use for it.

---

He wrote in the cipher shorthand he had built in his fourth year and had never taught anybody.

It was not a good cipher and he had known that when he made it. Any serious cryptanalytic section would have had it in an afternoon. It was not built against a section; it was built against the twenty seconds a chambermaid or a landlord or a curious colleague spends with a page found on a table, and against that it was perfect, because it did not look like cipher. It looked like a man's illegible personal notes. He had chosen the substitutions to preserve the *rhythm* of ordinary writing — word lengths plausible, an apparent scatter of short common words, a hand that ran downhill toward the right margin the way a tired man's does. Somebody reading it upside down from four feet would see a diary.

He wrote the entry for the Third-day of the pin, which was ten days late and which he had held in his head with total accuracy for the whole ten days, because that was a thing he could do and had always been able to do and was the actual reason the directorates had kept him in the field into a second decade.

Distance from bay edge to the enrollee's position at the moment of the shear: six feet, near enough. Elapsed time from shear to arrival: less than the release. Direction of travel: forward and across, into the geometry rather than out of it, which was the part he had turned over most, because it was the part that ruled things out. A trained evasive framework moves a man *away*. This one had been driven the other direction by somebody who had decided, in under a third of a second, to spend it that way.

Point of contact: left forearm, guard side, at the fold's thin edge, which was correct and which nobody found in a third of a second by luck.

Disposition of the delivered force: into the floor plate. Not deflected. Not shed. *Spread and grounded*, which was an Iron Skin family behavior and not a Wind one, and which meant two components at minimum in the same third of a second, and which was the sentence that had made this a category-two exception before he had finished watching it happen.

He wrote his own judgment last, because judgment was inference and inference belonged nowhere near product and this was not product.

*Baseline is wrong. Not incomplete — wrong. What the academy has measured is a floor, and the floor was chosen.*

*Second: he knew the core's noise. He had the length of a four-second grind memorized off a machine he does not use, in a bay he does not work in. That is not talent. That is somebody who prices every object in a room he stands in, and has been doing it long enough that it costs him nothing.*

*Third, and the one that matters: he gave the third-year a true incomplete answer at the north door, in about eleven seconds, without preparing it. I have watched officers with nine years' training fail that. He has a doctrine for it. He has had a doctrine for it long enough that it is a reflex.*

He looked at the third one for some time.

Then he wrote one more line, in the same downhill hand, and it was the only line in the entry that was not an observation.

*Nobody builds that at fifteen unless somebody has been coming for him since he was twelve.*

---

The case was under the bed, inside the ordinary one, packed around with the clothes and the shaving kit and the four books chosen for how they would look on a shelf.

He got it out, which took a minute and involved kneeling on a floor with a bad draught coming under the door, and he was aware while he did it that he was thirty-eight years old and that his knees had begun to have views about floors.

It was small, older than most of his career, banded at the corners, and locked. He had carried it through nineteen postings and had not once, in any of them, opened it in front of another human being.

He opened it now, alone, with the shutter closed.

He folded the two sheets once and set them inside.

There were other things in the case. He did not take them out. He had no reason to and he had not had a reason in a long time, and the discipline of a locked case is that it stays a locked case, and the entire value of the thing he had carried through nineteen postings was that he had never once needed to demonstrate to anybody that it existed.

He closed it. He turned the key. He put it back into the bottom of the ordinary one and packed the ordinary one around it, and slid the whole arrangement under the bed, and sat back on his heels for a moment with his hand still flat on the lid.

Then he got up, because the wing opened at the second bell and he had a session ledger to prepare.

---

Three more reports had gone south in the ten days.

He had written all three at the same table in the same hand and had walked all three down the bluff road himself, and he had read all three back before sealing them and had found nothing in any of them to change, which was correct, because it was all true.

*Subject's routine unchanged. Training allocation held at four sessions weekly; no unscheduled floor access. Attendance at ladder sessions continues at every session including practice hours. Associates: no change in composition or pattern. Notation habit continues; volumes now three, one of which is not carried into training halls.*

*Academy institutional note: floor warden's apparatus replacement schedule has been funded and begun. Registrar continues assembling charter documentation. No inspection notice observed.*

*No undisclosed capability observed.*

That last sentence had been in the first report and in every one since, and he had written it the first time because it had been true, and had written it the fourth time because it was the sentence the assignment existed for and the sentence its absence would be noticed in.

He had audited product for two years, early, and he knew what his own now looked like from above. It looked like a competent officer with excellent access producing accurate, unexceptional coverage of a stable situation. It looked, in fact, exactly like what it had looked like in the first month, which was the part an auditor would notice if an auditor ever went looking — because a live situation does not stay flat, and coverage that stays flat across a term is coverage of a situation that has stopped moving or coverage that has stopped following it, and there is no third option.

He knew that. He knew it with the precision of a man who had once written a training note about it.

He filed them anyway.

He did not think about it in a sentence and he did not think about it at length. He noticed it the way a man notices the level of a river he crosses daily — the water is lower this month than last; the fact registers; nothing is done about it — and he went on down the bluff road in the dark with the bag under his arm, four times in ten days, and came back up each time and slept without difficulty.

---

The assessment office's morning traffic ran between the second and third bells and was the most boring hour of Halcenvane's day, which was why Seln had arranged, in his first fortnight, to be the person who worked the counter through it.

Enrollees came in with schedule queries. Instructors came in with room clashes. The senior clerk did the ledgers at the back table and did not look up. It was two hundred small transactions a term, none of them memorable, all of them a legitimate reason for a member of staff to have spoken to every enrollee in the building at least once — which was the actual product of the arrangement, and which had cost him nothing but the willingness to be the person at a counter.

Cael came in on the fourth day of the eleventh week, on the ordinary business of the ordinary week, to sign for a revised floor allocation.

They had not spoken in nine weeks. The last time had been a filing date, in the second week, and Cael had asked for it and Seln had given it and neither of them had said anything else. Since then there had been the schedule with the corrected transposition, and eleven weeks of a man standing at optimum sightlines, and a boy who could find him from a doorway in four seconds and had never once let his eye rest.

Seln put the form on the counter, turned it round, and did not look up.

"Sign at the third line. The clerk countersigns."

Cael signed. The pen was the office's and scratched.

"There's a coursework change going through for next term," Seln said, to the form. "Field assessment. Gault is adding a unit."

He said it the way the counter said everything at that hour — flatly, at the volume of a man passing on a room clash, to a boy he happened to be processing.

"Observation, positioning, presence-discipline. Four sessions, taught out of this office rather than the halls, because it isn't a combat unit and Rooke doesn't want it on his floors." He squared the form's edge against the counter's lip. "It's aimed at second and third years going into assessment work. Provision enrollees are admitted on training-requirement grounds — the assay clause counts supervised coursework as evaluable contact hours, which is in your file, which I read when I processed you."

Cael put the pen down.

The senior clerk turned a page at the back table. Somewhere in the corridor two first-years were arguing about a lecture hall.

"Who's teaching it," Cael said.

"I am," said Seln, and made a mark in the counter ledger. "It's a staff unit. Gault approves the syllabus and doesn't attend."

He turned the form face down and set the countersign block beside it.

"Your file says you watch things well," he said. "The course would tell us if the file's right."

---

Cael read it on three layers and it took him about two seconds, which was the fastest he had done anything that term.

The top layer was the sentence itself, which was an administrative offer of a coursework place made by a member of staff at a counter, with a legitimate mechanism, a legitimate authority, and a legitimate reason, and which contained not one word that would look like anything if it were reported by any of the four people in earshot.

The second layer was the *access*. Four sessions of a Shadow Path practitioner teaching presence-discipline in a room, at teaching range, with Cael in the room legitimately, on paper, countersigned, at Gault's approval. Eleven weeks of the best observational position anybody had ever handed him, and the man was now proposing to move it from forty feet in a gallery to eight feet in a classroom and to *talk while he did it*.

The third layer was that the man on the other side of the counter had built the second layer knowing exactly what his own close range contained. He was not offering a thing whose value he had failed to price. He was the single best pricer of that thing on the continent. He had spent fifteen years being unreachable and had built, in an administrative sentence, at a counter, in the boring hour, a reason for the one person who could see him to sit eight feet away and watch him work.

Which meant one of two things, and Cael had turned both of them for eleven weeks and had not resolved either.

It was the best trap the Compact had ever built — access offered, proximity engineered, a subject encouraged into a room where his own attention could be measured by the man measuring it, four sessions of controlled observation with a syllabus for cover.

Or it was not a trap at all.

He answered the top layer, because the top layer was the only one that had been spoken and answering any other would have been the single worst thing either of them could do.

"Sign me up," he said.

"Enrolment sheet goes up on the residence board on the eleventh," said Seln, already reaching for the next form in the tray. "Put your name on it like everybody else."

Cael took his copy off the counter, folded it, and put it in his coat.

At the door he stopped, for about half a second, which was not a decision and which he was annoyed with himself about for the rest of the day.

Behind him, the pen went on scratching. The man at the counter did not look up, and had not looked up, and by any account anybody in that office could have given, nothing whatsoever had happened.

---

He put it to the other three that evening and the conversation went for an hour and a half and did not resolve, which was, Cael thought, the correct outcome and the first honest one they'd had in a month.

Lira wanted it refused, and said so in nine words, and then argued the other side herself for ten minutes because she had gotten into the habit after the Fiske bout and had decided she liked what it did to her thinking.

Karis did the thing she did.

"I want to state my position and then state the reason I distrust it," she said, "because I've been distrusting it since he opened his mouth and I'd rather do it in front of you than at three in the morning."

"Go on."

"Position: this is the best acquisition-adjacent development of the term and it does not violate one word of the minute. You did not manufacture it. You did not engineer proximity. A member of staff offered a coursework place through a documented mechanism to which you have an entitlement in your own file. Every condition of the line you drew is intact." She had her hands flat. "Reason I distrust it: I want it to be true. I have wanted a controlled close-range observation of a Shadow Path practitioner since Cael's first page on him, professionally, for reasons that have nothing to do with any of us, and I noticed myself constructing the justification about four seconds before I noticed myself wanting the thing. So take my finding at a discount and I'll tell you the size of the discount, which is large."

Brom had not moved from the chair. He had the nineteen exercises' sheet on his knee with a count in the margin, fourteen of them, and he was working a thumb along the edge of it.

"He's not recruiting you," he said.

"You don't know that."

"No. But if he was, he'd have made it easier to say no to." Brom shrugged. "Everything he's offered you, you have to ask for in public, in your own handwriting, on a board where four hundred people can see it. If I wanted somebody in a room with me quietly I would not make him sign up for it on a notice board."

Nobody had anything to add to that.

"There's a thing I keep coming back to," Cael said, eventually, "and it isn't about the course."

They waited.

"The rotations haven't changed."

---

That was the part he had been checking every day for eleven days, and it was the only piece of hard evidence anybody in that room had about anything.

He had built the instrument in the second week of term and had run it since without missing a day, because it was cheap and because it read a system he could not otherwise see. Two men at the ferry landing, changing every four hours. Two at the foot of the bluff road, the same. Openly. Since the road south from Greyvane, since the hearing, since the day the file went from a watched thing to a resourced one.

He could see the bluff road's foot from the library's east wing, which was one of the reasons he worked in the library's east wing.

*Day 1 after the incident: two, four-hour changes, standard.*
*Day 2: two.*
*Day 3: two.*
*Day 4: two.*
*Day 5: two, and one of them was the same man who had been on the morning of the incident, which meant the roster hadn't moved either.*

Eleven days. Two men. Four-hour changes. Not one additional body at the landing, not one shortened interval, not one second pair on the covered walk, not one new face in the residence quadrangle, not one visit from anybody's counsel to Withrow's office, which Bracken would have mentioned to Karis inside a day because Bracken mentioned everything to Karis inside a day.

"An exception report goes up the chain and something comes back down," Cael said. "Always. That's what an apparatus is — you put an interesting thing in the top and instructions come out of the bottom, and the instructions arrive as bodies, because bodies are the only thing the Compact has ever had to spend. It took them eleven days to double the rotation after the hearing and that was across four days' travel. Ostrand's four hours from a registry station."

"So nothing went up," said Lira.

"Something went up. He filed *something* — a man in that office who filed nothing for a fortnight would be a man with a hole in his product, and he's not stupid." Cael turned the copy of his floor allocation over on the table and looked at the two dates and the two sets of initials. "Whatever went up didn't have the afternoon in it. That's the only reading the ground supports. Eleven days, no change, no bodies. He watched me stop a jammed seventh-setting release with a forearm and put it in the floor, and the apparatus that has spent two years and I don't want to know how much money watching me did not so much as add a man to a ferry landing."

Karis had stopped writing.

"That's one observation," she said. "It's consistent with your reading and it's consistent with three others. It's consistent with the report going up and sitting in a queue. It's consistent with a decision to change nothing while an inspection is pending. It's consistent with him reporting it and being told to stand by."

"I know."

"I'm not arguing. I'm putting the alternatives on the page so that in four months, when we know, we'll be able to check what we believed against what was true." She wrote them out, numbered, and read them back, and then set the pen down. "And then I'm going to say the unprofessional thing, once, and you can all pretend I didn't."

"Say it."

"An instrument doesn't change sides," Karis said. "It changes readings. I told you that six weeks ago and I meant it as a caution and I have believed it my entire working life." She squared her notebook against the table's edge. "Eleven days is not a finding. But it is the first observation I have ever recorded that I cannot fit into that sentence, and I dislike it enough that I'm telling you now rather than after I've made it fit."

---

Cael wrote the entry at the window with the shutter open and the river doing its business below the bluff — the barge horns going, the long call and the two short ones, *loaded, downstream, give way*, which he had decoded in the second month and stopped hearing in the third.

He wrote the standing lines first, because the inventory came before the commentary and always had. Five confirmed. The Compression-adjacent's forearm had come right on the fourth day rather than the second, and he entered that as an amendment to the recovery column rather than as an alarm, because one instance at an unmeasured load was not a curve.

Then the entry, and it came out whole, which almost never happened twice in a term.

*He watched me stop Iron-tier force with a Copper baseline and his desk stayed quiet — the rotations outside haven't changed, which means nothing went up. Now he's handing me his own Path at teaching range. Either it's the best trap the Compact ever built, or the man they sent to watch me has started watching them. Karis says instruments don't change sides. Karis has never met this instrument.*

He read it back and left it, and then wrote the working underneath it, because an entry without its working was a conclusion and he had spent three years not writing conclusions.

*Evidence for the trap: he built the access himself. He knows what his close range contains better than anybody alive. Four sessions of me in a room where my attention is the measurable thing and he is the instrument measuring it is, if you wanted one, the single best design available. It costs the Compact nothing and it is entirely deniable and it would look, on paper, like a teaching assistant running a coursework unit.*

*Evidence against: eleven days and not one extra body at the ferry landing. Brom's point, which I have not been able to break — everything he's offered has to be claimed by me, in public, in my own hand, on a board. And the shape of the offer, which was one sentence at a counter in the boring hour, delivered to a form and not to me, in language that would survive being repeated by anybody in that office. That's not a man building a trap. A trap wants to be walked into. That was a man building something that could be declined without either of us ever having been in a conversation.*

*What I actually think, written down so I can be wrong about it in public later: I think he's been careful with me for eleven weeks and I think today he was careful* for *me, and I do not have one piece of evidence for the preposition and it is the only part of the day I am certain about.*

*What I'm going to do: sign the board on the eleventh. Sit in the room. Learn the Path.*

*What I'm not going to do: change my reading of him because of one good afternoon. He is a Compact officer on a Compact assignment and he has not resigned it and there is no version of the last eleven weeks in which he has told me anything at all. Whatever he is doing, he is doing it in silence, and a man who says nothing can stop saying nothing on a day of his own choosing, and I would be a fool to build a plan on eleven days of a ferry landing.*

*So: both. Read him as an officer. Sit in the room anyway.*

*And write down, here, in advance, the thing I will otherwise be tempted to invent afterward — that on the day I take something off this man, if that day comes, he will have spent a term making it possible without ever once being asked, and the fact that he can't know that is not a technicality. It's the whole cost. It's the entire cost, and it is not going to feel like one at the time, and that is exactly why it's on the page tonight instead of that night.*

He closed the log.

Down the bluff, at the road's foot, two men were changing over at the fourth hour, as they had at the fourth hour on the day of the pin and on every hour of every day since, and neither of them looked up at the library's east windows, because neither of them had ever needed to.

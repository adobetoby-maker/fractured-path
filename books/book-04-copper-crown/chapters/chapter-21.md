# Chapter 21 — Evaluated

He woke at the fourth hour on the twentieth of Reaping and did the first task before he opened his eyes.

It had a shape now. Four weeks of practice had given it one, and the shape was this: before anything else — before the light at the shutter, before the day's list, before the ache in both calves that the Crown yard's stone benches had put there yesterday — he found his own edges and held them where an ordinary person's sat.

The sensation had no good name. He had tried six in the notebook and rejected all of them. It was not tension and it was not effort in the way a plank is effort. It was closer to the feeling of carrying something on the flat of an outstretched hand: nothing heavy, nothing that hurt, only a thing that had to be attended to continuously or it would go, and which was not permitted to go, and which therefore occupied a channel that could never afterward be used for anything else.

He held it, and got up, and put his feet on cold boards, and it stayed held.

*Hour one,* he thought, and did not write it down, because writing it down would have required looking away from it.

Below the bluff the river was doing its business — the long horn and the two short ones, the ore barges going up on the dawn water — and out past the shutter the sky over Ostrand was the colour of an unlit lamp. On the common-room table, where Lira had set it down last night without ceremony and without one word about where it ought to end up, the Copper Crown sat on its brass mount and caught nothing at all, because there was no light in the room to catch.

He looked at it for a moment on his way past.

Then he went down to the wash-room and held his edges through it, and came back and dressed and held them through that, and by the second bell he had been holding them for two hours and the channel they occupied was already a channel with a weight in it.

Brom was at the corridor's end with a mug in each hand, sitting on the stair with his back against the wall, moving like a man who had taken four exchanges against a Wind practitioner in front of six hundred people and then slept on it.

"You're up early," he said.

"So are you."

"I'm not up. I never went." Brom handed over the second mug. His left eye had a colour under it that was going to be worth seeing by the weekend and the knuckles of his forcing hand were strapped. "How's the wick?"

"Holding."

"How long?"

"Two hours."

Brom drank some of his own. "Three hours in that room. Plus the walk over. Plus the walk back."

"Yes."

"So call it six." He said it flatly, without any comfort in it at all, which was the most useful thing anybody could have done. "You've never held it six."

"Four and a half. In the lecture complex, twelve days ago, and the last hour of it was bad."

"Then today's the longest thing you've ever done and none of it is the part they're measuring." Brom got up off the stair, slowly, and rolled the shoulder, and the shoulder made a sound. "They don't get to see it. That's the whole job today. Whatever it costs you to hold that, nobody in that room ever finds out you paid it — everybody in there is going to think the hardest thing happening is on the grid." He shrugged, once. "I'll know. That's the best I've got."

"It's enough."

"It'll have to be," said Brom, and went to bed.

---

Lira was at the wing gate at the third bell, standing with her weight off the leading hip in a way she plainly believed nobody could see.

She did not wish him luck. She had never wished anybody luck in the three years he had known her, on the grounds — stated once, at fourteen, with total conviction — that luck was what people offered when they had run out of anything real.

"Say the order," she said.

"Idle state first. Everything else on top of it."

"Say it if it goes."

"If it goes past thin I stop the trial. Out loud. I say I need an interval and I take it and I let them write down that I took it." He shifted the coat on his shoulder. "A logged interval is a line in a record. A drift is evidence."

"Right." She looked at him for a second longer. "Go and be measured. You've done the hard part for four weeks and nobody's going to clap for it."

"You noticed."

"I've noticed for four weeks. I'm not going to make a speech about it at a gate." She pushed off the wall. "Third bell. Go."

---

The demonstration room had been changed by four chairs.

That was all — that was the entire physical alteration, and Cael stopped inside the near door for the second time in his life and did the count in the four seconds it took him to cross the threshold.

The brass grid ran both ways under the lamps, one third of a pace, the intersections still sharp at the far end where a lazy surveyor's would have wandered. The apparatus stood along the north wall in its eleven pieces. The pendulum escapement was already going in its case, clicking, which meant somebody had wound it early, which meant the clerk had been in the room since the second bell. Along the south side the long table held Magister Gault at its centre with his forms squared, the Ash Path instructor on his right, the Mire Path instructor on his left with a coat still on and her hands in the pockets of it, and the clerk at the fourth chair set apart.

And along the east wall, where there had been nothing at all in the first month, four chairs, set in a row with the ordinary spacing of an ordinary institution making room for guests, because that was the charter's language and Bracken had drafted the seating to the charter's language.

Delegation counsel, at the near end, with a document case open on her knees and two ribboned folders already out of it. Assessor Ilsev, second, with her own case on the floor beside her and nothing in her hands but a pencil. The records officer, third, with the compiled file on his lap and a fresh log sheet on top of it, because a records officer at an observed evaluation logged the observation itself — a piece of procedure Cael had read about in the library and had never seen performed. Havel looked up as Cael came in, once, level, four-tenths of a second, and looked back down at his sheet, and Cael understood with complete clarity that the man had just performed the only greeting available to him and had performed it correctly.

And at the far end, in plain inspection greys, with nothing on his lap and nothing in his hands and no case anywhere near him, Archmarshal Vastin.

*Nothing in his hands.*

Cael had thirty feet of floor to cross and he spent it on that. Counsel had brought documents because counsel's work was documents. Ilsev had brought a pencil because her work was reading and marking. The records officer had brought a log because that was the job. Every person in the observation row had brought the instrument of their own trade, and the man at the end had brought nothing at all, which meant one of two things: either he intended to do nothing, or the instrument of his trade did not require carrying.

*He is not going to take notes,* Cael thought, crossing the last ten feet of brass. *He is going to remember it.*

He put that away and went and stood at the marked intersection, and held his edges, and waited.

---

Gault did not stand up. He had not stood up at the baseline and he did not stand up now, and Cael, who had spent five months learning the man, read the not-standing as the single most reassuring thing in the room: it meant the room was the same room.

"For the record," Gault said. "Semester evaluation, provision-status enrollee, conducted under the assay clause's standing requirement on the date published in the wing calendar before notice of inspection was received. Panel of record: myself presiding, two faculty seats, clerk logging. The Guilds Compact delegation attends under its reserved right of observation, which is a right to observe and not a right to direct, and I have been asked to state that and am glad to."

Counsel inclined her head about half an inch. Nobody else moved.

"Enrollee." Gault turned a page. "You know the format. Six trials, questions between them, everything logged including the questions and the interval. Three points of procedure, and I will say them again, because I said them once in this room five months ago and a thing said once is a thing somebody can claim was never said.

"One. If at any moment you cannot do a thing, say so, and we will record that you could not, and it will be true and it will not count against you. Two. I measure consistency, not maximum. Three." He looked up. "This session measures against the baseline you set in this room in the first month of term. It does not measure against anybody's expectation, anybody's transcript, or anything said in a corridor. If you have come here to be impressive you have come to the wrong wing."

"Understood, Magister."

"Then we'll start."

---

**Trial one** was displacement, and it was the identical instruction, word for word, which Cael had been counting on and had not let himself count on.

Stand at the marked intersection. On the shutter-board's signal, be somewhere else.

The board's drum turned over with its small dry sound. Nine louvres, one of them going to open, none of them under anybody's control in the room, and behind the noise of the drum the pendulum going on with its count.

The seventh shutter opened.

The Wind-adjacent framework started where it had always started, in the hips: the drop, the loosening, the floor conceding its half-inch. Then the held half-breath, non-negotiable, paid at the front — that hollow-boned second in which he could not have breathed if the wing had caught fire — and his weight stopped being where it had been.

Stone came up through both heels. He had trained on sprung boards for five months and stone still surprised the legs every time, a hard flat report through the heel and the arch and up the outside of the leading hip in the old line, crest to inner knee, and then the landing beat: the locked half-breath at the end where he was a thing that had landed and could not change direction.

"Left brass at fourteen, forward three," the clerk read. "Interval—" and the pendulum's number.

*Fourteen. Baseline was eleven.*

Gault wrote. "Again."

Eight times.

The fifth was the best one and he knew it in his body a full count before the clerk read it — the drop cleaner, the half-breath shorter by something the pendulum could measure and his lungs could not, the landing arriving through a leg that five months of four supervised sessions a week had taught to take a hard floor without arguing about it. The sixth matched it. The seventh matched it.

The eighth went wide. Not badly: a hand's width in a direction he had not chosen, with a recovery long enough that the pendulum came back a full mark over the others, and the hip line went from a stretch to the bright familiar burn that meant the day's account had been opened and would be paid.

"Trials one through seven," Gault said. "Displacement varies by less than half the width of one brass. Trial eight: same displacement, longer recovery, lateral error of one hand." He looked up. "At the baseline you volunteered a working ceiling of four repetitions, unprompted, before instrument confirmation. Restate it."

"Six, Magister."

"You have just run eight."

"Six is free. The seventh costs me tomorrow's session and the eighth costs me the day after." Cael was breathing in a controlled way and made no attempt to hide that he was. "At the baseline four was free and six was over the ceiling. It's moved by two."

"In five months."

"In five months of four supervised sessions a week on stone."

Gault made a note that took him rather a long time. "For the record. Enrollee's stated working ceiling has advanced from four repetitions to six across the interval. Trials one to seven fall inside the baseline's variance and improve on its interval. The advance is stated by the enrollee before instrument confirmation, as at the baseline." He set the pen down. "That is what the provision is for," he said, to nobody in particular, in the driest voice Cael had ever heard him use, and turned the page.

In the observation row, counsel wrote. The records officer wrote, because logging was the job. Assessor Ilsev did not write, and had not written, and had — Cael registered this on the edge of the channel doing the other work — spent the whole of trial one looking at the shutter-board's drum rather than at him.

The man at the end of the row had not moved at all.

---

**Trial two** was the one Cael had built the whole session's shape around, and it was in second position because a burned scrap of paper in an assessment-office hand had told him to put it there.

*Surprise them exactly once, small, early. A watcher who finds one thing stops hunting for a second.*

Gault gestured, and the clerk went to the north wall and brought over one of the two pieces of apparatus that Cael had failed to identify in his first fortnight at Halcenvane and had subsequently spent six months failing to identify in the library.

It was a sealed copper vessel on a tripod, about the size of a wine jug, with a graduated glass column rising out of the top of it and a small brass plate on the flank where the calibration date was painted in the same hand as the accession numbers in the library.

"You have never seen this used," Gault said.

"No, Magister. I've been trying to work out what it is since the eighth week."

"It measures applied heat. Water, sealed, with a column. Slow, honest, and impossible to argue with. The Ember and Ash cohorts are measured on it four times a year and it has never once flattered anybody." He turned a page. "The hearing record entered into evidence at Greyvane contains one demonstration exhibit describing an ignition-point capability, contact-adjacent, single channel, recorded late in that institution's term and recorded thinly. It is on your record. It was not on the floor at your baseline, and I did not ask for it then, because a baseline measures what an enrollee elects to place in it." He set the page down flat. "I am asking for it now. Hand to the vessel. Raise the column."

Cael went and put his hand on the copper.

The Ember-adjacent fragment was the smallest thing he owned and the only one that had ever come to him with a written consent behind it, and it worked in one direction and at one distance and did nothing else at all. It was not heat. That had taken him two months at Greyvane to understand, and Karis had explained it three times in her own words before it landed: what he had taken from her was not fire but the *decision point* — the single instant at which a thing that is not burning becomes a thing that is, and the ability to put that instant where he chose, at the surface of his own hand, once, at contact.

He put it at the copper.

The metal went warm under his palm and then hot in a band about the width of two fingers, and the warmth crossed the vessel's wall and got into the water, and the column moved. It moved slowly, because water is slow. That was the whole character of the instrument and the reason Gault used it: a fast trick cannot lie to a column of water. The mark came up past the second graduation and stopped.

"Hold," said Gault.

"I can't hold it, Magister. It isn't a flow. It's one instant, at contact, and then it's finished and I have to make another one."

"Then make another one."

He made four, at intervals the clerk read off the pendulum, and the column came up past the fifth graduation and stayed there while the vessel decided what it thought about that, and Gault watched the glass and not Cael's hand.

"Note for the record," he said at last. "Ignition-point capability demonstrated on the thermal vessel. Four applications. Column response consistent at each, no drift between applications, no thermal event outside the point of contact. Range: contact. The capability is on the enrollee's prior record and is confirmed here at this wing's standard."

He wrote it. And then he said, without any change in tone whatsoever:

"That is a better instrument reading than the Greyvane exhibit supports. The exhibit describes it as marginal."

"The exhibit was written in the third week of it, Magister. It's eight months old now."

"Mm." Gault wrote that too.

Cael took his hand off the copper, which had gone from hot to warm to nothing, went back to the intersection, did not look at the east wall, and knew — with the part of him that had been counting rooms since Ardenmere and had never once been able to stop — exactly what had happened along it.

Counsel had written, at some length, turning a page to do it, and had then said something short to the records officer, who had made a mark. Assessor Ilsev had picked up her pencil for the first time in fifty minutes and had used it.

The delegation had found the thing. It was small, it was true, it was on the record already, it had come early, and it had cost him nothing he was not entitled to spend — and every eye on that east wall that was going to spend the next two hours hunting for a novelty now had one in its pocket.

*Four sentences,* he thought. *Fifteen years, four sentences, and he wrote them into a coursework folder and let me burn them.*

The man at the end of the row had not written.

---

The idle state went at the ninety-minute mark, and it went for the reason Brom had named in a corridor four weeks earlier, and Cael did what he had told Lira at the gate he would do.

It happened in the interval after **trial three** — the combined trial, surface read and displacement, drop-frame release from an unannounced rack position, be off the marked square before the weight arrived. He had run it twelve times against the baseline's eight and taken eleven of twelve, because the read had gotten better and the legs had gotten better and the two of them had spent five months learning to be one operation instead of two.

Gault's questions afterward were long, and good, which was the problem. He asked about the eleventh repetition, where Cael had gone early, and whether early meant the read had arrived early or the decision had — and Cael had to go all the way inside to answer honestly, down past the surface of his own forearms to a moment that had lasted a fifth of a second and now had to be reconstructed in front of a panel.

And while he was down there, the hand on the setting drifted.

He felt it as a change in the room's weather. That was the only way he had ever been able to describe it: the ambient press of being looked at by eleven people simply eased, a fraction, because eleven people had marginally stopped registering that anybody was standing on the grid.

Two seconds. Perhaps less. The Mire Path instructor's eyes came off him and went to her own hands.

"Magister," Cael said. "I need an interval."

Gault stopped writing. "State the reason."

"I'm running the answer to your question and running the session at the same time, and one of them just got worse. I'd rather stop for a minute than give you a bad number in trial four."

"Which one got worse?"

"The answer."

The Ash Path instructor made a small sound that might have been amusement. Gault did not.

"Clerk. Log an interval of two minutes at the enrollee's request, reason stated: fatigue affecting response quality. Note that the request preceded any instrument indication." He set the pen down. "Sit down for the two minutes. You are not being penalised, and I would like that clear, because I can see you deciding whether it is."

Cael sat on the rail with his hands flat on the stone and spent one hundred and twenty seconds finding his own edges and putting them back where an ordinary sixteen-year-old's sat.

It came back slowly, and sullen, and it took most of the two minutes, and by the end of them his head had the ache the Iron-adjacent read produced after a long day, which was a bad sign at the ninety-minute mark of a three-hour session.

Nobody in the room knew any of it had happened. The record said *fatigue affecting response quality*, which was true, and *at the enrollee's request*, which was true, and nothing else, which was also true and was the entire art.

He got up. "Ready, Magister."

---

**Trial four** was the plate, and it was the one where he had to not do the thing, and it was worse this time because this time there was no growth in it and everybody in the room could count.

Gault set the drop-frame to its heaviest weight, moved the marked square to within a pace of the rail, and had the clerk bring the hand plate on its brace.

"You cannot evade this one. Receive it."

The Pressure-adjacent fragment wanted it. Three and a half years had made it very nearly a reflex — the density gathering in the forearm and the shoulder, weight without mass, bones taking on water, a brace that came from inside the limb instead of from the stance beneath it. It was the correct answer. The instrument would have measured it beautifully, and produced a dispersal reading nobody at this academy had ever generated on that plate.

It was not on the record.

He took it with an ordinary brace: feet set, arm loaded from the shoulder, weight through the stance and down into stone. The weight came off the frame and went through the plate and up the forearm and into the joint as one hard undivided jolt, and the joint took the whole of it because nothing anywhere was spreading it, and the spring bottomed out with the flat mechanical clack he had been hearing in his sleep for five months.

"Dispersal reading, low," said the clerk.

"Again."

Four times. By the fourth the forearm had gone dull to the elbow and the ache under the point of the shoulder had arrived exactly where it had arrived at the baseline and would last until the weekend exactly as it had then.

"Assessment," said Gault.

"Four. Not eight." Cael flexed the hand and let them see him do it. "I don't disperse it. It goes into the joint."

Gault looked at the two sets of figures side by side — the baseline's and today's — and they were the same figures, to the graduation, on the same plate.

"Trial four shows no advance against baseline. Dispersal low at both sittings, repetition ceiling four at both sittings, mechanism unchanged." He wrote it. "That is a flat line in a semester evaluation, and I am going to say the useful thing about it rather than the alarming one, because somebody in this building will otherwise say the alarming one for me. A practitioner who improves at everything simultaneously across five months is a practitioner whose evaluator should check his instruments. This wing measures six things. Five have moved. One has not. That is what a real semester looks like."

The pen went down. "Anything to add, Enrollee?"

*I have a fragment that would take that weight eight times and put half of it into the floor. I took it off a man in an Ardenmere ring who told me to find him later. It is not on your record and it is not going onto your record, and you are being honest with me, and I am giving you a true sentence with a hole in it, in your own room, for the second time in five months.*

"No, Magister."

*Same price,* he thought. *Still no column for it.*

---

The apparatus failed on **trial five**, and afterward Cael could account for every part of the next second and a half except the part where he had decided anything.

Trial five was the hard version of trial three: heaviest weight on the frame, unannounced rack position, the marked square moved forward two brasses so the margin was thinner, and the chalked-dust plate laid at the landing side so the clerk could read where his feet actually went rather than where he said they went.

The Mire Path instructor had checked the frame at the session's open. Cael had watched her do it — the trigger worked four times, the same as at the baseline five months ago when she had found the second detent dragging. She had nodded and sat down.

The drum turned. The trigger went.

And the second detent, serviced twice since the spring, dragged and then let go — early, a full count early, and from the rack point above the one the clerk had set, which meant a heavier weight arriving faster and sooner into a square whose margin had already been cut to the bone.

Cael's forearms told him before the pendulum did. That was the surface read doing the only thing it had ever done: not intention, not distance, nothing about minds — mass and vector, arriving on the skin of the forearms and the back of the neck a fraction before it arrived anywhere else. What it reported was *wrong shape, wrong time, more of it.*

The demonstrated answer was the burst. He had run it eleven times that morning; it was in his legs and it would have worked.

It would also have put him onto the chalked plate with the landing beat's locked half-breath running — and the clerk's brace stood on the far side of the plate, and the clerk stood behind the brace, and a boy arriving into that space at full displacement inside a fixed interval where he could not change direction was a boy who would arrive into a man.

He did not burst.

He took a quarter of it. The hips dropped and the framework opened and he shut it again before the half-breath had finished being paid — a thing he had never once managed in three years and did not manage cleanly now. He came off the square by about a third of what he had demonstrated all morning; the rest of the displacement he simply did not take, and the load that should have gone out sideways into stone went straight down the outside of the leading hip instead, crest to inner knee, all of it, at once.

The weight came past his shoulder and hit the floor.

The room made the sound rooms make.

The Mire Path instructor was out of her chair before the frame had finished ringing. She worked the trigger four times and said, with a great deal of controlled anger in it, "The second detent has failed. Not dragging. Failed. It released a count early off the upper rack and I want that in the log in those words."

"Log it in those words," said Gault. "Trial five is void. Clerk, strike the reading."

"Struck."

"Enrollee." Gault had not raised his voice and had not stood up. "Are you injured."

Cael was standing on a hip that had taken the entire account for the day and the day after in one instalment, and the answer was going to be visible by evening whatever he said now, so he said the true one.

"Not injured, Magister. The hip took the whole load instead of the floor and I'm going to feel it for three days."

"Because you did not complete the displacement."

"Because I didn't complete the displacement."

"Why not?"

And here it was: the question at the exact centre of everything, asked mildly, in a room with a Compact delegation along the east wall and a records officer logging every word of it, and the honest answer was the only one Cael had prepared for five months to be able to give.

"Because the clerk was standing where I'd have landed."

Silence, for a moment.

The clerk — a thin man of about thirty who had logged every session of Cael's life at this institution and had never once said a word that was not a reading — looked up from his sheet and then very carefully looked back down at it.

"Note for the record," said Gault. "Apparatus fault, drop-frame, second detent, releasing early from the upper rack position. Trial five void. Enrollee aborted a demonstrated displacement mid-execution and absorbed the resulting load rather than complete a landing into occupied floor. No instruction was given. No injury resulted." He wrote for a while. "The wing will not run this frame again until it has been stripped. Set the reserve frame. We will re-run trial five when it is standing."

It took eleven minutes to move the reserve frame across.

Cael spent them sitting on the rail with both hands flat on the stone, and the hip howling, and the head-ache from the ninety-minute mark now fully arrived, and his edges — this was the part he wrote down that night and underlined, which he almost never did — his edges had gone thin in the second and a half after the weight hit the floor, gone thin properly, for perhaps three seconds, while every single person in that room was looking at the drop-frame.

Every single person but one.

He did not look at the east wall; he knew better than to look at the east wall. He ran the read at the low ambient level he had been holding all morning, the cheap one that cost him almost nothing, and what came back off the far end of the observation row was a man sitting exactly as he had sat for two hours — weight settled, hands loose, undeclarative — looking at the drop-frame like everybody else.

And nothing else. No shift. No second look. No mark of attention returning to a place it had already been.

That was the tell, and Cael understood it on a stone rail with his hip on fire, and it was the coldest thing that happened to him all year.

*Gault is going to look at me twice in the next minute, because he has to write it down. The Mire instructor will, because she found the fault and wants to know what it cost. Counsel will, because that's what counsel does with an incident.*

*A man who has just watched something he doesn't understand looks again. That isn't a habit or a temperament. That's what an eye is for.*

*He isn't going to look again. Which means he already has it — got it the first time, at speed, complete, priced it, and there is no further information available to him in this room from looking at me.*

They re-ran trial five on the reserve frame nine times and he took eight of nine, and the eighth was worse than it should have been and the ninth worse than the eighth, and Gault logged both and said nothing about the hip, and Cael volunteered it before he was asked, because that was the doctrine and the doctrine had never once failed him.

"The last two are degraded, Magister. That's the hip, not the read."

"So the instrument says," said Gault. "Logged."

---

**Trial six** was procedure: the displacement measure and the surface read, run cold at the end of the session, to find out whether three hours had moved either.

The displacement had lost a hand's width and a fraction of interval, which beat the baseline's cold result by a margin the pendulum could see and a person could not. The read had lost nothing at all. It never lost anything. It was the cheapest thing he owned and the quietest, and that was — he had written this at Greyvane and had never had cause to revise it — the entire character of it and its entire limit.

Then Gault worked through his forms for four and a half minutes while eleven people waited, and the pendulum went on counting, and Cael stood on the grid and held his edges, and the holding was now the only thing in the world.

Gault read the note aloud, because the charter required the assessment note to be read into the record in the evaluee's presence.

"Semester evaluation, assay-provision enrollee, measured against the baseline established in this room in the first month of term.

"Growth against baseline: confirmed across all demonstrated capabilities. Working ceiling advanced by two repetitions, volunteered by the enrollee before instrument confirmation at both sittings. Combined-operation trials improved in both accuracy and interval. Ignition-point capability confirmed at this wing's standard against a prior thin record. One measured capability shows no advance and is logged as unchanged.

"Consistency: within tolerance at every trial.

"Assay-provision requirement: satisfied.

"Enrollment standing: renewed."

The Ash Path instructor signed. The Mire Path instructor signed, and added four lines about the second detent that she read out and that Cael suspected would end somebody's contract with a supplier. The clerk stamped.

Boring. Procedural. Stamped.

Cael stood on a brass intersection in the most expensive room in the institution, in front of a delegation of the Guilds Compact, in a body that hurt in four places, holding a thing at ordinary that had been trying to slip for three hours, and listened to an institution say the plainest possible sentence about him for the second time in five months.

*Enrollment standing: renewed.*

Still the most exotic sentence anybody had ever said about him. Said now at altitude, in front of every instrument and eye the system could assemble. Still boring.

---

The panel filed out through the far door. Counsel went first with her folders re-ribboned, already talking. The records officer closed his log, squared it on the compiled file and went after her without looking up, and Cael did not need him to.

Assessor Ilsev stood, picked up her own case, and paused at the end of the row for about two seconds — long enough to look at the thermal vessel, still standing where the clerk had left it with its column settled back down at the first graduation. Then she went out.

Vastin was last to rise.

He had been sitting for three hours in a plain chair with nothing on his lap. He stood without using the arms of it, and did not stretch, and did not do any of the small things a man of sixty does after three hours in a hard chair, and reached into the breast of the greys and took out a notebook that had been there the entire time.

Cael, standing at the demonstration floor's rail with both hands flat on the stone because the hip was not going to carry him anywhere for a moment, watched him close it.

He had been counting. He had been counting the whole session, on the channel that never turned off, and the count was this: the notebook had come out twice in three hours. Once in the eleven minutes while the reserve frame was being set. Once now, at the end, for about four seconds, and Cael had not been able to see whether the pen had made a new line or gone back over an old one.

*Twice,* he thought.

Counsel had filled nine sheets. The records officer had logged three hours of procedure. Ilsev had marked her pencil perhaps a dozen times. And the man at the end of the row had opened a notebook twice, in a session that had produced a mechanical failure, an aborted displacement, a void trial and a renewal.

*Renewed. Three hours, six trials, one failed detent, one void, and a stamp.*

# Chapter 16 — The Delegation

The final settled itself on a Sixth-day afternoon with four bouts left in the bracket, and Cael had it on paper eleven days before the results clerk chalked it up.

The Copper column had ninety-one names in it and by the season's last month it had four that mattered. Lira had gone from twenty-second to first in six wins inside eleven sessions, the fastest climb the ladder's mathematics permitted, and it had required six perfect afternoons in a row, and she had taken all six inside four exchanges. Fiske sat second, dethroned in the standings and undethroned in the crown, because the brass mark did not move until the final moved it. Brom stood third after a season that had cost him two losses and had given him back something Cael had watched arrive session by session: the seam was closing. Rooke's assignment had had a date on it and the date was the final, and by the last month Brom was converting absorption into counter with a stall Cael could still find from the gallery and could no longer find inside two exchanges.

Fourth was a fourth-year Shield Path named Merrick, and the arithmetic ate him on a Fifth-day when he drew a bout he could not decline.

That left the two of them, and it left them by a route neither could have argued with: the top four ran the witnessed final's bracket, Fiske had to face Lira again and did, and it ran four exchanges again — harder ones, and one very long minute at the end that the first meeting had not needed — and Lira came off the floor with a leading hip she could not put full weight on and the top of the column still hers. Brom took Merrick in the fifth. The pairings resolved. There was no other answer available in the mathematics, and Cael had known there wouldn't be since the third week, and had written it down and then privately hoped he had made an error.

They read it off the board together, the four of them, in the covered walk, at the second bell.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Right," said Brom, eventually.

Lira had her arms folded. "You're going to be unbearable about this."

"I've been trying to solve her for two years," Brom said, to nobody, to the board, in the flat register he used for the things he actually meant. "Now I get faculty supervision and a crowd."

"That's the unbearable bit," said Lira.

"That's the *good* bit."

Karis, who had been reading the schedule column rather than the standings column, said, "It's the nineteenth."

They all looked.

The nineteenth of Reaping, Crown yard, faculty panel of three, witnessed. And in the right-hand column, one line up and five days earlier, the delegation's facility review; three lines down, the twentieth, provision-status evaluation, observation right reserved.

"They'll be here," said Lira.

"They'll be here," Karis agreed. "The chancellor scheduled the final in the third week of term, before there was a notice. She has not moved it, and she is not going to, because a delegation that happens to be standing in a gallery while a mid-tier academy runs a witnessed final at capacity is worth four years of applications." She said it without disapproval. "She's not using you. She's not declining to use you either. Both things are true and they're going to stay true and I've decided to find it restful."

Brom rolled his shoulder, which was the shoulder, and looked at the board a moment longer.

"Doesn't change anything," he said. "It's still her and me on a floor."

---

They stopped training together nine days out, and it was Lira who said it, in hall one, at the sixth bell, standing in the middle of the floor with a practice baton in each hand.

"We're done."

Brom straightened up. "Done tonight or done done?"

"Done until the nineteenth." She racked both batons. "I've beaten you nine times in two years and you've beaten me eleven and every single one of those was on information. That's what we do. We hand each other everything and then we use it, and it's been the best thing either of us has had and it stops working in a bracket final in front of three faculty and a delegation."

"Because there's nothing left to learn," said Brom.

"Because there's nothing left to learn." She sat down on the bench and started unwrapping her hands. "I know your seam. You know my landing beat. You know I can't take a fourth burst on that hip and I know your hardening runs out at about four minutes under repeat contact. If we keep training we'll spend nine days building answers to each other's answers and turn it into a chess game, and neither of us fights chess. So: no sparring, no patterns, no talking about it. We show up on the nineteenth and we find out."

Brom considered that for a while, in the way he considered anything he intended to agree with rather than concede.

"You'll be worse for it," he said. "So will I."

"Yes."

"Good."

Cael, sitting on the floor at the wall's edge with the notebook shut on his knee, said nothing at all, and both of them noticed, because both of them always noticed.

"Say it," said Lira.

"It's not mine to say."

"You've been writing about both of us for two years and you're going to sit in a gallery on the nineteenth being the only person in the building who knows what's actually happening on that floor." She pulled the last wrap off. "You've got a right to a sentence."

He thought about which sentence.

"I've watched every bout either of you has fought this season," he said. "Not the ones that mattered — all of them. And there's a thing I've never been able to write down properly, because it sounds like sentiment and it isn't. Everybody in that yard thinks the nineteenth is a Copper final. It isn't. It's two Iron-equivalent practitioners who have never been allowed to fight anybody their own size, finally getting to, in a cage that was built by people who never imagined either of you existed."

Brom said, "Huh," which from Brom was a paragraph.

"And I'll be in the gallery," Cael said, "because I can't be in the column. That's the part I've stopped being angry about and started being useful about. Somebody in that yard should know exactly what it costs both of you. That's what I've got. I'll take it."

Lira stood up and hit him on the shoulder, not gently, which was how she ended conversations she had liked.

"Nine days," she said. "Nobody talks about hips."

---

Cael's problem was a different shape and he spent three weeks failing to get it into fewer than three columns.

He wrote it out for the first time on the eve of the delegation's arrival week, at the window, with the new pen, and the three columns were the whole of it.

*What the panel requires:* growth. That was the provision. The assay clause did not enroll a practitioner and then leave him alone; it enrolled him on a standing basis of evaluation by demonstration, and a demonstration that demonstrated nothing new was a demonstration that failed. Gault had set the baseline in the first month and had said the sentence that governed everything since — *baseline established; semester evaluation to measure against it* — and a boy who arrived in front of that panel exactly as capable as he had been in his first week would be, by the provision's own honest language, a boy whose enrollment had not been justified.

*What the audience requires:* control. Not less capability. Less *surprise*. Every capability he intended to put on that floor had to arrive as the predictable next increment of something already in a file — advanced exactly as far as a semester of four supervised sessions a week would advance a person, in the direction that supervision would push it, with nothing in it that required a new sentence from anybody.

*What the record permits:* five things. Wind-adjacent, Pressure-adjacent, Iron-adjacent, Compression-adjacent, Ember-adjacent, and not one flicker of anything else. Five, because five were already written down somewhere, in Quenna's protective drafting or Vell's ledger or a Greyvane panel note or an adjudication transcript that half the continent had read. Nothing on that floor could be the first evidence of anything.

The three columns did not conflict. That was the thing that took him three weeks to see and made him put the pen down when he saw it.

For two years he had held the doctrine as a defensive habit — *bank it, don't show it, show them less than you have* — and he had held it the way you hold a flinch, because it had started as one. On the eve of the delegation's arrival, laid out in three columns in his own hand, it stopped being a flinch and became a plan with an offensive shape: *the panel gets a real, measured, honest semester of growth, in five capabilities that are already documented, executed better than anybody in that room expects. And it will be true. Every part of it will be true.*

He wrote underneath it: *This is the first time in my life that hiding and honesty are the same operation. I don't entirely trust how good that feels.*

And then the part he had been circling for three weeks and had left to last, because it was the part with no column.

*Q. asked me a year and a half ago whether I could not do it in front of people who wish me harm. I told her I didn't know. That was the honest answer and it was also the only answer available, because at the time I had done the reach exactly once and nobody sane builds a safety claim on a sample of one.*

*I have a sample of two now, and a thing in me that has an idle state, and a date.*

*The twentieth of Reaping. That's when she finds out. That's when I find out.*

---

The idle state slipped on him twice in that final month, and the second time frightened him properly.

The first was ordinary — a Fourth-day lecture, the second hour, a hall too warm, his attention wandering onto the fatigue curve's page, and afterward the lecturer's assistant looking straight through him at the door and then jumping when he spoke. He logged it and added it to the running count, which by then stood at nine incidents since the stairwell and had been thinning steadily as he learned to hold the wick, which was the only encouraging line in the entire working ledger.

The second was in hall three, in a supervised session, on a floor, with a wing observer at the rail.

The wing sent an observer to two of his four weekly sessions — that was the provision's ordinary machinery, the evaluation-linked floor time that had to be evaluation-linked by somebody — and the observer sat at the north rail with a board and wrote down what she saw and had done so, without incident, for half a year. Cael was working a redirect sequence with Brom at half intensity when it happened, and it happened, he worked out afterward, at exactly the point his attention went fully onto the sequence and off his own edges.

Two seconds. Maybe three.

The observer wrote nothing, because there was nothing to write; she had been looking at her board. Brom stopped mid-sequence with his guard still up and the pressure-read open and said, very quietly, "You went."

They finished the session at half intensity and said nothing else about it on the floor. Afterward, in the corridor, Brom put his back against the wall and folded his arms and did not let him past.

"How far."

"Thin. Not gone. She was looking down."

"She was looking down *this* time." Brom did not raise his voice, which was worse. "Cael. In four weeks you are going to stand on a floor in front of a panel and a Compact delegation for three hours, and there will be a person in that room whose entire trade is noticing when a thing is not where it should be, and you have just told me that the fragment you cannot control drifts on when your attention is fully occupied. What do you think a three-hour evaluation is?"

Cael stood there and took it, because it was correct and because Brom did not say things like that unless they were.

"Attention fully occupied," he said.

"For three hours."

"Yes."

"So it isn't a training problem." Brom pushed off the wall. "It's the actual problem. It's bigger than every other thing on your list and I don't think you've had it in the right column."

He had not. He rebuilt the plan that night with it at the head of the sheet instead of at the foot, and the rebuilt version had a discipline in it he did not enjoy: for the four weeks before the evaluation, and for the three hours of the evaluation itself, holding the idle state at ordinary was not a background cost to be absorbed. It was the first task. Everything else — the growth, the control, the five capabilities and the exactly-nothing-else — sat on top of it and would fall over if it went.

*The thing I have to do on the twentieth is not a demonstration,* he wrote. *It is three hours of continuous, invisible, unrewarded work that nobody in the room will ever know I did, underneath a demonstration that has to look effortless.*

*I've been calling it the calibration problem for three weeks. It isn't. It's a bout with a stranger where the stranger is me and the crowd doesn't get to know it's happening.*

---

Bracken closed the file on the ninth of Reaping.

He did it without ceremony, which was the only way Bracken did anything, and Cael happened to be at the counter to collect a floor allocation and so was present for the last four seconds of eleven days' work and fifteen months' work at the same time.

Four boxes. Bracken tied the last one, wrote the index reference on the lid in a hand that had never in Cael's observation produced an ambiguous character, and set it on the shelf with the other three.

"That's the enrollment basis," he said. "Four boxes, three hundred and eleven documents, two independent repeal searches, and a numbered index that will take a hostile reader ninety seconds to work and a sympathetic one about forty. If the delegation asks for the provision, they get that shelf, in that order, with a clerk to carry it."

Karis was standing at the end of the counter with ink to the second knuckle and an expression Cael had no name for.

"Say the other part," she said.

"The other part," said Bracken, "is that the file no longer requires either of us." He squared the boxes. "Miss Karis found it. I filed it. Neither of those facts is load-bearing anymore. That is what a finished defense is, Enrollee: a thing that goes on being true in a room where nobody who built it is standing."

Cael took his floor allocation and went out into the covered walk and had to stop for a moment, because that was the sentence, and it had taken two academies and fifteen months and a young woman with a carrel full of founding-era documentation to make it, and it was the exact opposite of everything a Denvash certification hall had ever done to him.

The rest of the institution finished in the same three days, and it finished quietly, which Cael took as the measure of how well Withrow had done her work in the eighth-day address.

Nothing moved. That was the whole of it and it was harder than it looked. The Crown final stayed on the nineteenth, scheduled in the third week of term by a chancellor who had not known a notice was coming and who now declined, publicly and without comment, to shift it out of a delegation's window. The semester evaluation stayed on the twentieth, on the calendar Gault had published before anybody had heard the word manifest, before the panel of record, at the standard set at the baseline. Rooke's cohort trained its ordinary sessions at its ordinary hours and turned in supervision signatures the same afternoon they were earned. The fire-watch went on logging lamp oil.

Cael watched an institution hold still on purpose for three days and understood that it was the hardest athletic feat he had seen all season.

*Everybody on this bluff wants to do something,* he wrote, on the eve. *That's what an inspection does to people — it converts fright into activity, and activity into paper, and paper is the only thing an inspection can actually read. Withrow's whole strategy is to give them a building that behaves identically whether they're in it or not. Which is exactly the strategy I've been running on my own file for two years, and I have never once seen anybody else run it, and I did not expect the first person I saw do it to be a chancellor.*

*Nine days to the final. Ten to the twentieth.*

*Q.'s question is ten days away. I still don't have the answer. What I have is a plan, three columns, and one problem underneath them that Brom put in the right place for me. That will have to be enough, because it's what there is.*

---

They came up the bluff road on the twelfth of Reaping, mid-morning, in three carriages and a mounted escort of four, and half the academy found a reason to be somewhere with a view.

Cael watched from the Crown yard's upper tier, which was legitimate, empty at that hour, and gave a clean line down the bluff road's last quarter-mile and across the outer courtyard. Lira came with him and stood at the rail with her arms folded. Karis stayed in the records hall with Bracken, because Karis had decided months ago that she would rather read the delegation's findings than watch its coats.

He started the count at the road's turn and had most of it before the carriages stopped.

Eleven from the manifest and four escort. Two clerks in the first carriage with the document chests, which arrived before the people they belonged to, which told him the chests had a schedule of their own. Counsel in the second, a woman of about fifty who was talking as she got down and did not stop talking, and whose first act on Halcenvane ground was to look up at the records hall's roofline and say something to a clerk that made the clerk write it down.

The records officer came out of the second carriage behind her.

Cael had him in two seconds.

Ardenmere was a long way back, and the man was still the man: the same economy of movement, the same habit of taking in a courtyard's corners before its center, the same slightly-too-correct posture of somebody who had been told once, early, that he looked young for the grade and had never stopped over-correcting for it. The coat was better. The boots were not new anymore. The grade insignia at his collar had gone up again, the second rise since Ardenmere, which for a man who had once been told to stand down about a designation and had stood down was a career going somewhere.

Havel crossed the courtyard, and — Cael watched this happen and would think about it for a week — did not look up at the Crown yard's tier. Not once. He looked at the roofline, the door, the registrar waiting at it, and the ground. A man who does not look up at the one place a subject would be standing is a man who has thought about where the subject would be standing.

"That's the one from Ardenmere," Lira said.

"That's him."

"He's gone up."

"Twice."

The evaluation seat came out of the same carriage a moment later, and Cael had her from the walk before he had her from the manifest — an older woman whose lack of hurry had nothing to do with slowness, who got down and then stood for a moment doing exactly nothing while the courtyard organized itself around her, and then went in without speaking to anyone. She carried her own case. Nobody offered to take it and nobody, Cael thought, had ever successfully offered to take it.

Assessor Ilsev had read a Greyvane hearing's exhibits all the way to the end and had followed the text somewhere nobody at that table had wanted it to go, and Cael had six of her findings in his head and had not decided in nine weeks whether she was the most dangerous person on the manifest or the best thing on it. Watching her cross a courtyard did not help. She looked like a woman on her way to read something.

The third carriage was last, and it was the plainest of the three, and Cael understood before the door opened that this was deliberate and that he was not going to be able to prove it.

The count came back wrong.

That was the only way he could put it, then or that night. He had been counting arrivals and rooms and corridors for two years; he had built an instrument that measured where attention went and had run it on four hundred people a day for six months and had used it to find a professional whose entire trade was not being found. The instrument had never once failed to return a number.

It returned three numbers here and all of them contradicted each other.

*One.* The aides orbited. Both clerks from the first carriage came across the courtyard as the door opened and arranged themselves at the third carriage's step; the escort's ranking man moved four paces without being told; the counsel stopped talking mid-sentence. Every body in that courtyard adjusted its position around the third carriage, which is what bodies do around rank, and Cael had watched it happen around Withrow a hundred times and could have charted it.

*Two.* The man who got out generated no orbit at all.

He came down the step without using the rail, moved out of the doorway so the clerk behind him could get down, and stood in the courtyard, and the arrangement that had assembled around him had nothing to attach to. He did not wait to be attended. He did not fail to be attended either, in the pointed way that senior men sometimes fail to be attended so that everybody notices. The clerks stood there holding a formation around a space that was not being used.

*Three.* The credentials said one thing and the bearing said nothing.

He was in inspection greys, plain, and at the collar was an insignia Cael had never seen on a living person and had seen exactly twice in a library, in line drawings, in a procedural volume: no color in it, no device, nothing but a plain bar and a plain field. Every other rank the Compact issued announced itself. A warden's coat announced. Coss's whole posture had announced, all the time, in every room, and Cael had been able to read Coss in a week because Coss had never once stopped telling him things. This man's insignia announced an altitude that stopped the counsel talking mid-sentence, and his bearing did not perform one inch of it.

He was perhaps sixty. Medium height. He stood with his hands at his sides. He looked at the records hall's door for about two seconds and at the Crown yard's tiers for about two seconds and then walked in.

And in the middle of the two seconds his gaze crossed the upper tier, and it crossed Cael, and it was level and entirely without interest, and it did not stop and did not pass over him faster than it passed over the empty benches on either side.

Cael stood at the rail with his hands on the stone and did the read that he had done to every watcher the Compact had ever sent him — Coss on a Denvash road, Coss in an Ardenmere gymnasium, Havel in a corridor with too-new boots, the resourced rotations on the Greyvane road who had followed them down every mile and let themselves be counted, a teaching assistant at the north end of a rail four bodies in from the stair — and he got nothing.

Not concealment. He knew concealment; he had spent six months charting a man who did it professionally and had learned to find the shape of the hole. There was no hole here. There was a man crossing a courtyard, and every ordinary channel Cael had was open and reporting, and what they reported was a man crossing a courtyard.

"What," said Lira, at his shoulder.

It took him a moment to answer.

"Coss told me things by existing," he said. "Every warden I've ever seen tells you what he wants before he opens his mouth — that's what the coat is *for*. Havel told me he'd been ordered to be somewhere and didn't like the order, back in Ardenmere, in about eleven seconds. Even the man in Gault's office tells me something, and he's the best there is at not telling people things, because the not-telling is a shape and shapes can be measured."

"And that one?"

"That one crossed a courtyard," Cael said.

He ran the instruments anyway, all of them, in the ninety seconds he had, because a read that returns nothing is only a finding if you have actually taken it. Rhythm: even, no variation between the step down and the step across, which told him the man was not tired and not hurried and nothing else. Weight: settled, low, entirely undeclarative — no Path in it that Cael could name, from a body that had certainly held one for forty years. Attention: and this was the one that stopped him, because he had six months of practice measuring where other people's attention went and had never once thought to measure where a single man's own attention went, and the answer was *everywhere at the same rate*. The roofline got the same two seconds as the door. The tiers got the same two seconds as the roofline. An empty bench got what Cael got.

That was not indifference. Indifference had a shape and the shape was carelessness, and there was no carelessness anywhere in it. It was a man sampling a place evenly on purpose, the way you take a measurement across a whole surface rather than at the point you already suspect, so that the reading comes back clean and the surface never learns which part of it you cared about.

Cael had been doing a cruder version of that in corridors since Ardenmere.

Below, the door of the records hall closed behind the last clerk. The courtyard emptied in about ninety seconds. The mounted escort took the horses down toward the stables and somebody's boy came out for the baggage, and the bluff went back to being a bluff in the last month of Crown season.

Lira stayed at the rail a while longer.

"You've gone quiet," she said. "You go quiet when you're pleased and when you're frightened and I've never once been able to tell them apart."

"Neither can I. That's why I write it down."

He wrote it that night, at the window, with the new pen, and it was the shortest entry the log had held in two years, and he left it short because everything else he could have added would have been a guess, and guesses had their own column and did not belong in this one.

*New opponent. No tells. Begin.*

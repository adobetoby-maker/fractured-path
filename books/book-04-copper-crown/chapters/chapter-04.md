# Chapter 4 — Baseline

The problem was not what he could do. The problem was what number he wanted to be measured from for the rest of his life at this academy, and he spent six days on it and did not sleep properly for four of them.

The assay provision's entry requirement was a demonstration before the assessment office. That much was in the document he had signed twice in Bracken's room. What the document did not spell out, and what took Cael three readings of the charter clause and one afternoon with Karis to extract, was the mechanism underneath it: the entry demonstration did not merely satisfy the requirement. It *established the baseline*. Every semester evaluation thereafter would be run against it. The provision did not ask him to be good. It asked him to be consistent, and then to grow, and it measured both against a line that he was about to draw himself, once, permanently, in a room with instruments in it.

"So it's a trap," Lira said, on the fourth evening.

"It's not a trap. It's a scale." Karis had the clause copied out on the common-room table with three annotations already on it. "A trap is designed to catch you. This is designed to measure you, and the fact that it can be gamed is a property of every measurement ever devised."

"It can be gamed how?"

"Two directions." Cael had been through both of them enough times to recite them. "I can set the baseline low — show less than I have, deliberately — and then every semester I can produce growth that isn't growth, just the rest of what I already had, released in instalments. That buys me years of comfortable evaluations."

"That sounds good."

"It's very good, right up until it isn't." He turned the page around. "It has three prices. First, a low baseline is a document. It goes in the file and it says, in Gault's handwriting, what an assay-provision practitioner looked like on this date. That document is going to be read by an inspector eventually — Bracken has told me so twice, and Withrow told me in her first minute. A weak baseline is evidence that the provision admitted a weak practitioner, which is an argument against the provision itself, and the provision is the only thing I'm standing on."

"Second?"

"Second, the instalments run out. I have a finite amount of held-back capability. If I spend it as fake growth I have nothing left when I need real growth on demand, and the requirement doesn't stop. Every semester. Forever."

"Third," said Brom, from the floor, with the ice wrap on his shoulder again, "you'd be lying."

"Third," Cael agreed, "I'd be lying, in writing, on a document I fought a hearing to make true."

That was where he had got stuck for two days, and it had taken Lira to shift him.

"You keep saying the choice is low or honest," she said. "It isn't. You've been banking for three years and nobody's ever called it lying, including me, and I've watched you do all of it."

"Banking is choosing not to show something. This is choosing what a number says about me."

"No — this is choosing which of the things that are *already true about you on the record* you show them." She leaned forward. "Cael. There's a transcript. It's public. Anyone can buy it for six marks. What does it say you can do?"

He knew the answer without having to think about it, because he had read the transcript more times than was healthy.

Greyvane's monthly demonstration assessments, entered into the hearing record as exhibits, carried two capabilities across fourteen sessions: the Wind-adjacent evasion framework, and the Iron-adjacent surface read. Those two were on the public record, in an adjudicated proceeding, with three assessors' signatures under them. They were, in the most literal sense, the only two things about his architecture that the world was entitled to know.

Everything else — Pressure-adjacent, Compression-adjacent, the Ember-adjacent ignition point that had gone onto Greyvane's records so late in the year that it had appeared in exactly one exhibit — was either off the record entirely or so thinly recorded that showing it here would be new information rather than confirmation.

"Two," he said.

"Two. At full quality. Genuinely, at the top of what you can actually do with those two, with nothing shaved off." Lira sat back. "That's not a low baseline. That's a *true* baseline of the man in the transcript. And when you grow next semester, you grow honestly, in the two capabilities they already know about, and it'll be real growth, because you're fifteen and you're going to be better in five months whether you plan to be or not."

Cael looked at the clause for a while.

"That's the answer," he said.

"I know."

"You've known since Tuesday."

"I've known since Tuesday. You needed to get there yourself or you'd have argued with it in the room." She stood up and stretched. "That's not manipulation, before you write it down as manipulation. That's how you work and I've been dealing with it for three years."

He wrote it down as method rather than manipulation, which he considered a fair compromise.

*Baseline doctrine, established. Show what the record already carries, at genuine top quality, with nothing held back inside those two capabilities. Hold everything the record doesn't carry.*

*This is not the low baseline and it is not the whole truth. It is the honest maximum of the public version of me. If anybody ever asks me under oath whether the baseline was a fair statement of what the record showed, the answer is yes, and I will not have to think about it before I say it.*

*Price of the doctrine: it caps me. Every future evaluation has to produce growth in two capabilities only, forever, or produce a reason why not. That's a narrow corridor and it gets narrower.*

*Gain: the corridor is one I can defend in a hearing room.*

*I've been in one. That matters more than the corridor being wide.*

---

The assessment wing had two doors and he went in through the near one at the third bell, and stopped inside it for four seconds, which he had not intended to do.

He had known it was the most expensive room in the institution. He had worked that out from the outside — the separate wall, the separate gate, six years old, funded on an endowment appeal — and had been unreasonably pleased with the deduction. He had not been prepared for what expensive actually meant.

The demonstration room was fifty feet by thirty and the floor was inlaid.

Brass strips, set flush into stone, at intervals of one third of a pace, running both ways, so that the whole floor was a measured grid you could read a position off from any seat in the room. Cael had grown up in an instrument-maker's house. He knew what it cost to lay a true grid in stone, and he knew that whoever had done it had done it by survey rather than by eye, because the intersections were sharp and the lines did not wander at the room's far end where a lazy man's always did.

Along the north wall, the apparatus. He counted eleven pieces and could now name nine of them, having spent two weeks finding out.

A drop-frame: a guided weight released by a trigger down a vertical rail, delivering a known force at a known instant, with four interchangeable weights racked beneath it. A pendulum escapement in a case, clicking, which counted intervals down to something Hesk would have approved of. Three spring-plate dynamometers, calibrated, with their calibration dates painted on the housings in the same hand as the accession numbers in the library. A shutter-board — a rank of nine louvres with a randomising drum behind them, which opened one at a time in an order nobody in the room controlled, for reaction trials. A chalked-dust plate for recording foot placement. Two things he still could not identify, one of which appeared to involve water.

And along the room's south side, three chairs behind a long table, and a fourth chair set apart at the end for the clerk.

Magister Gault was already seated. Stone Path, Silver-tier, somewhere in his fifties, a heavy man with a stillness to him that Cael read immediately as trained rather than temperamental. He did not stand. The two faculty seats were filled by an Ash Path instructor from the lecture complex, whom Cael had had in the standing eight for a fortnight, and a Mire Path instructor he had never seen before and who looked, on first read, like she resented the hour.

"Enrollee." Gault did not offer a hand or a greeting. "Sit down, please, before we begin, and understand the format, because you will be doing this every semester and I would rather explain it once."

Cael sat.

"This is not a bout. Nobody in this room is going to fight you and nothing you do today will be scored against an opponent. It is a measurement." Gault laid one hand flat on a stack of forms. "You will run six trials. Each trial isolates one thing. Between trials I will ask questions, and the questions are also part of the record. The clerk logs everything, including what I ask and how long you take to answer.

"Three points of procedure. One: if at any moment you cannot do a thing, say so, and we will record that you could not do it, and it will not count against you in any way except that it will be true. A refusal I have to infer is worth less to me than a refusal you state. Two: I am measuring consistency, not maximum. I would rather have six identical trials at three-quarters of your capacity than one extraordinary trial and five that disagree with it. Three: this session establishes a baseline and the baseline is permanent. Do not perform for me. Perform for the person you will be in five months, who has to be better than whatever you write on my floor today."

Cael had prepared for a great many things and had not prepared for the third point being said out loud, honestly, by the man administering the instrument.

"Understood, Magister."

"Then we'll start."

---

**Trial one** was displacement. He was to stand at a marked intersection and, on the shutter-board's signal, be somewhere else.

That was the whole instruction, and Cael understood inside a second that it was a beautifully constructed one. It did not tell him how far. It did not tell him which direction. It made no reference to any Path, any technique, or any framework. It asked only: *from this square, on a signal you do not control, get off it.* Anything he did to answer it was his own architecture, volunteered.

The shutters clicked and the fourth one opened.

The Wind-adjacent framework started where it always started, in the hips: a drop and a loosening, the floor quietly conceding a half-inch it had been holding in reserve, and then the held half-breath, non-negotiable, paid at the front. For that half-second he was hollow-boned and could not have breathed if the room had caught fire. His weight simply stopped being where it had been, displaced a half-body-width and a little more to the left, and the sprung floor was not under him here — this was stone, inlaid stone, and he felt the landing come home hard through both heels and up the outside of the leading hip in the old familiar line, crest to inner knee.

Then the landing beat. The locked half-breath at the end, the fixed interval in which he was not a person but a thing that had landed and could not change direction. He had spent three years failing to smooth it out.

"Left brass at eleven, forward two," the clerk read. "Interval —" and the pendulum's count.

Gault wrote. "Again. Same instruction."

Six times.

The sixth was the one that hurt, and it hurt in the way he had budgeted for: the leading hip's line went from a stretch to a burn somewhere in the fifth, and on the sixth the burst went a fraction wide of where he had placed it, and the landing beat ran perhaps a tenth long. He knew it before the clerk read the figure. He had felt the difference in his own body a quarter-second before any instrument in the room could have known about it.

Gault looked at the six numbers for some time.

"Trials one through five: displacement varies by less than the width of one brass. Trial six: the same displacement and a longer recovery." He looked up. "What happened at six?"

*Say so, and it will be recorded, and it will be true.*

"Six is over my working ceiling, Magister. Three deployments is free. Four costs me the next day. Six in eleven minutes is more than I've ever done outside a fight, and the sixth was the sixth."

"You knew that before trial one."

"Yes."

"And you did not say so."

"You told me to say when I couldn't do a thing. I could do it. It just cost."

Something happened in Gault's face that was not quite an expression. "Note for the record," he said to the clerk. "Enrollee volunteers a deployment ceiling at four repetitions, unprompted, and identifies degradation in trial six before instrument confirmation." And then, to Cael: "That is the single most useful sentence anybody has said to me in this room this year. I would rather have a practitioner's honest ceiling than his best afternoon. Continue."

---

**Trial two** was the drop-frame.

The weight was racked at the second setting and Cael was asked to stand within a marked square beneath it, facing away, and to say — on the release — which of four positions the frame had been set to.

He understood the trial before Gault finished describing it and felt the small clean satisfaction of a well-built instrument. This was the Iron-adjacent surface read stripped of everything except the one thing it did. No opponent, no bout, no possibility of guessing from a stance or a breath. Just an incoming pressure and a question about its shape.

The Iron-adjacent fragment had never felt like the others. Wind was a door he walked through. This was closer to a change in the quality of the air on his skin — a pressure-read that arrived in the surface of him, forearms and the back of the neck and along the jaw, a fraction before it arrived anywhere else, and which told him nothing about intention and everything about mass and vector. It cost almost nothing. That was its whole character, and its whole limit: it was cheap, it was quiet, it did not telegraph, and it could not be pushed. It read surfaces. It did not read minds, it did not read distance beyond about a pace and a half, and it had taken him a month at Greyvane to stop trying to make it into something larger and start using it for what it was.

He got eleven of twelve.

The one he missed was the fourth, and he said so before the clerk read the answer.

"That one I got wrong."

"You are correct that you were incorrect," Gault said, with no humour in it at all. "How did you know?"

"The read came in thin. When it's clean it arrives all at once. That one arrived in pieces and I chose between them." Cael considered whether to add the rest, and added it. "I think the frame's release was slower on that one. It's not a complaint. It's what I felt."

The Mire Path instructor got up without being asked, went to the drop-frame, worked the trigger four times, and said: "The trigger's dragging on the second detent. It's been serviced since the spring and it shouldn't be."

Gault wrote for rather a long time.

---

**Trial three** was the two together, and it was the first one that was hard.

Drop-frame release, unannounced position, and Cael was to read it and displace, and be off the marked square before the weight arrived at the floor.

He ran it eight times and got seven, and the seven felt correct in his body in a way the numbers could not capture — the read arriving on the surface of his forearms a fraction ahead of anything else, the hips dropping into it, the held half-breath, the sideways nothing, the hard landing on stone.

Somewhere in the fifth repetition he noticed that he was enjoying it, and noted the fact with mild alarm.

---

**Trial four** was where he had to not-do something, and it cost more than the six displacements had.

Gault set the drop-frame to its heaviest weight, moved the marked square to within a pace of the rail, and said: "This one you cannot evade. The square is too close and the weight is too near. Receive it on the plate."

He gestured, and the clerk brought over one of the spring-plate dynamometers on its brace — a hand plate, meant to be held, to measure what a practitioner could take and how it dispersed.

The Pressure-adjacent fragment would have answered that in its entirety.

Cael felt it want to. Three years of use had made it close to reflexive: the density gathering in the forearm and the shoulder, weight without mass, bones taking on water, bracing that came from inside the limb instead of from the stance beneath it. It was the exact tool. It was, in the strictest sense, the correct answer to the question the instrument was asking, and the instrument would have measured it beautifully, and the number would have been remarkable.

It was not on the record.

He took it on the plate with an ordinary brace. Feet set, arm loaded from the shoulder, weight through the stance and into the floor, which was how anybody without a Path took a hit and how he had taken hits for the first year of his life in Ardenmere. The weight came down. It went through the plate and up his forearm and into the shoulder as a single hard jolt that did not spread at all, because nothing was spreading it, and the joint took the whole of it, and the plate's spring bottomed out with a flat mechanical clack.

"Dispersal reading, low," the clerk said.

"Again," said Gault.

Four times. By the fourth his right forearm had gone dull to the elbow and there was a bright unhelpful ache under the point of his shoulder that he was going to be feeling for three days.

"Assessment," Gault said, when it was done.

"I can take that weight four times. I can't take it eight." Cael flexed the hand and did not hide that he was doing it. "I don't disperse it. It goes into the joint."

He said it flatly, and it was completely true, and it was also an enormous omission dressed in the clothes of a complete answer, and he was aware of both facts simultaneously and had to work not to let the awareness reach his face.

*That is what banking costs,* he wrote afterward. *Not the not-doing. The not-doing is easy — it's a decision, and I've made it a thousand times.*

*What it costs is that I sat there and told a man who is being honest with me an incomplete truth, in his own room, on his own record, and he wrote it down and thanked me for the candour, and the candour was real and the answer was not the whole answer.*

*Price: paid in the shoulder, three days, and in something else I don't have a column for yet. Gain: nothing on the record that isn't on the record. Necessary. Still a price.*

*Note: Quenna asked me once whether I could* not *do a thing, in front of people who wished me harm. Today I found out I can not-do it in front of a man who doesn't wish me harm at all, and I want it written down that the second one was harder.*

---

**Trials five and six** were procedure — the same displacement measure and the same surface read, run cold at the end of the session, to test whether an hour's work had moved either. The displacement had lost a hand's width and gained a fraction on the recovery. The read had lost nothing at all.

Gault worked through his forms for perhaps four minutes while the room waited. Then he read the note aloud, because the charter required the assessment note to be read into the record in the evaluee's presence, and Cael understood, listening, that he was hearing the sentence that would define him at this institution for the next five months.

"Assessment of entry demonstration, assay-provision enrollee. Non-standard architecture. Execution consistent across trials. Baseline established; semester evaluation to measure against it."

That was all of it.

Fourteen words of substance for six days of preparation and eleven minutes of held breath and a shoulder that was going to hurt until the weekend, and Cael sat there and discovered that he was not disappointed at all, because the fourteen words were accurate, and because *consistent across trials* was the only compliment the instrument was capable of paying and it had paid it.

The Ash Path instructor signed. The Mire Path instructor signed, and added a line about the drop-frame's second detent. The clerk stamped.

Boring. Procedural. Stamped.

---

Gault stopped him at the near door, after the others had gone, with the forms already under his arm.

"A word, and it isn't on the record, and you may treat it accordingly."

"Magister."

"There are people in this institution who want to know what you are." Gault said it without any weight at all. "Some of them are curious. Some of them are worried. Two of them are, in my judgment, worried on the academy's behalf rather than their own, which makes them worth listening to. You will be asked the question in eleven different ways by the end of term."

"I've had it in eleven different ways already."

"I imagine you have." Gault shifted the forms. "I want you to understand that I am not among them, and I want you to understand why, because if you mistake my reason for indifference you will misread me for a year and it will make both our jobs harder.

"I don't need to know what you are, Enrollee. I need my instruments and your consistency. Give me the second; the first is not my mandate."

Cael turned that over — carefully, standing in a doorway, because it was the most important thing anyone at Halcenvane had said to him and he wanted to be sure he understood which of two things it meant.

"Is that a courtesy," he said, "or a boundary?"

Something in Gault's face moved for the second time that morning.

"It's a boundary, and it protects you," he said. "An evaluator who forms an opinion about what a practitioner *is* has stopped measuring and started confirming. I have seen careers ended by that and I have seen one man ruined by it, and he was better at this than I am. If I decide what you are, every trial I run afterward is a trial designed to prove it, and my instruments will oblige me, because instruments always oblige the man reading them."

He opened the door.

"So I will not decide. I will measure you every semester for as long as you are enrolled, and I will write down what the instruments say, and if what they say becomes strange I will write that down too, in exactly the same handwriting. That is the entire service I offer. It is not friendship and you should not mistake it for protection." A pause. "It is, however, the only thing in this building that will still be true if somebody senior wants a different answer. Good morning."

---

The panel was closed. The rumor was in the refectory by the second bell that afternoon.

Cael tracked the mechanism, because tracking the mechanism was the only satisfaction available. It was not a leak. Nobody in the room had said a word; he checked that four different ways over the following week and found no channel at all. What had happened was that the charter required the *fact* of a provision-status evaluation to be posted — date, enrollee, outcome, one line — on the same board in the administration range where every other institutional act was posted, because Halcenvane conducted itself in public and had done for a hundred and forty-one years.

So the board said: *Assay-provision entry demonstration, satisfied. Baseline established.*

Nine words. And the eleven versions of him that had been circulating since the gate poured into the space behind those nine words and filled it completely within four hours.

By evening he had heard that he had broken the drop-frame. That he had refused to demonstrate. That he had demonstrated something that made an instructor leave the room. That Gault had said — and this one was being quoted with real confidence, by a student who had it from someone who had it from someone — that it was the strangest session of his career.

*None of these are true,* he wrote. *One of them is close enough to true that arguing with it makes me look evasive: the frame's trigger was dragging and an instructor did get up and go and look at it. Four hours to convert a serviced detent into a legend.*

*This is the thing I got wrong about Greyvane and I want it recorded properly. At Greyvane the problem was that nobody could answer the question. I thought that was the hard version. It wasn't. An unanswered question sits there and waits for evidence.*

*Here the question is answered, wrongly, eleven ways, and the answers are* load-bearing *for the people holding them. They've built on them. And you can't demolish somebody's building by explaining that the foundation is wrong. They'll defend the building.*

---

Ephram found him at the Crown yard the following afternoon, which was to say that Ephram spoke to him at the Crown yard, in front of about sixty people, on purpose.

There had been a practice session and it was breaking up. Cael was on the top tier with the notebook, as he had been at every session the yard ran since his first week, and he saw Ephram coming from three tiers down and had nine seconds to prepare and used all of them.

*Ephram, close range, first time. Blade Path, Iron R6, tops the Iron bracket. Seventeen, maybe eighteen. Carries the weight forward on the balls of the feet even standing still, which is a Blade habit and an expensive one — that man is never fully at rest and probably hasn't been for two years.*

*He has not looked at me once in three weeks. That is not avoidance; I've watched him not-look at four other people. It's a policy. He does not spend attention on anything he hasn't decided is worth attention.*

*He has now decided to spend some.*

Ephram stopped at the correct distance and did not offer a hand.

"You're the assay enrollment."

"I am."

"I've read the transcript." He said it the way a man says a thing he expects to be doubted. "The actual transcript, not the summary. I paid the six marks. Do you know how many people in this academy have paid the six marks?"

"Four, as of last week. Three of them faculty."

Ephram's eyebrows moved a fraction, which Cael logged as the first genuine information the exchange had produced.

"Then you know what it says and you know what it doesn't." Ephram nodded once, at the yard, at the boards, at the whole apparatus. "Here's my position, and I'd rather you had it from me than off a table in the refectory. Half this academy has decided you're extraordinary and the other half has decided you're a fraud, and neither half has any evidence, because a hearing transcript is a document about *law*. It says the Compact couldn't stop you being enrolled. It doesn't say you can fight."

"That's true."

"I know it's true, I checked." Ephram looked at him properly for the first time, and it was a level look, taking its time, entirely without hostility, and Cael found it more difficult to answer than Edran's hostility had ever been. "Greyvane needed you to be real. They'd bet the institution on you and they had nothing else, so they needed it, and people who need a thing to be true stop being able to tell whether it is."

He shrugged, one shoulder — and then he did the thing Cael would spend the rest of the afternoon taking apart, which was to turn his head a fraction toward the tiers below, and pitch the next two sentences to carry.

"Greyvane needed him to be real. We don't. I'll wait for the semester evaluation."

And that was the whole of it. He went.

Cael stood on the top tier for some time afterward with the notebook shut.

---

*Ephram, at the yard, in front of sixty people. Not a challenge. Not hostility. I keep starting to write that it was hostility and crossing it out because it wasn't.*

*What it was: a public deferral of judgment, announced loudly enough that everyone in earshot now knows he has deferred, which means everyone in earshot will look to him when the judgment finally lands. He has made himself the person whose opinion of me will count. That's the most sophisticated thing anybody has done to me since I arrived and I don't think he did it deliberately. I think that's just what a person does when he's used to his opinion counting.*

*Also, and I want this written down before I let myself be annoyed about any of it:*

*He is right.*

*The transcript is a document about law. It says the schedule doesn't contain me. It does not say I can fight, and I have been letting an entire academy read a legal argument as an athletic one because the misreading was flattering, and I have not once corrected anybody.*

*He paid six marks and read the actual thing and worked out what it did and didn't establish, and then he told me his conclusion to my face in public with no dressing on it at all.*

*Fair. Same terms I'd offer.*

*Nineteen weeks to the semester evaluation. Baseline's set. Two capabilities, honestly measured, permanently on file, with a shoulder that's going to ache until the weekend as the entry fee.*

*Grow in the corridor. That's the whole job now.*

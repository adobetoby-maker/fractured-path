# Chapter 2 — Mid-Tier

He got up at the fifth bell on the first full day and walked the place with a question, because that was how he had learned to enter anywhere.

The question was not *what is this*. He could get *what is this* out of a prospectus, and Karis already had. The question was the one he had been sharpening since Greyvane, the one that had turned out to be the only question with teeth in it: *why did this academy make the investment?*

Institutions did not do things for reasons they published. They did things for reasons that were visible in what they spent money on, where they put their best people, what they measured, and what they let fail. Greyvane had spent its money on beds and its conviction on one provost and one scout, and had let its library rot politely for sixty years because nobody in the building had ever had the standing to argue for shelving. Its priorities had been legible in three days. Cael had assumed, walking out of the gate on the fourth bell, that Halcenvane's would take four.

It took eleven days, and even that was a partial answer, and the reason it took eleven days was the first genuinely interesting thing the place taught him.

---

His method had been built for a district and then extended to a small academy, and both of those had been fair tests of it. It ran in layers, and the layers had names by now: rhythm, weight, breath, and — added at Greyvane — *position*, which meant where a person or a thing was allowed to be, and by whose permission. The compound gaze was the four layers running at once, checking each other. He had gotten it down to something close to a reflex. He could enter a room and have the room's structure by the second look.

The four layers assumed a data set he could actually finish.

Halcenvane had four hundred and twelve enrolled practitioners.

He learned that number on the first morning, off a notice at the refectory door concerning bread allocation, and stood in front of it doing arithmetic he did not enjoy. At Greyvane he had known every student by face inside a month and by gait inside two. Four hundred and twelve was not a larger version of that problem. It was a different problem with the same shape, which was the sort of error he had trained himself out of making about opponents and had never once had to guard against in a building.

He tried it the old way anyway for four days, because the honest thing to do with a method was run it until it broke rather than assume where the break would be.

It broke on the third day, in the north training hall, sometime in the second hour. He had been working the weight layer on a Force Path drill group — twenty-two students, four instructors, a rotation he had not learned the shape of yet — and had found, without warning, that he could not hold any of it. Not that he could not see it. He could see all of it, in the sense that the information was arriving; what he could not do was carry the eleventh student's load-path while acquiring the twelfth, and by the fourteenth he had lost the first four entirely and had begun, without deciding to, to substitute a general impression for the data. He caught himself doing it and felt something go cold along the back of his neck. General impressions were how everyone else watched fights. A general impression was the thing his whole method existed to refuse.

He sat down on the gallery bench and priced it honestly, which took the rest of the hour.

*Method failure, north hall, third day. Not fatigue — I had slept and eaten. Capacity. The gaze holds somewhere between nine and twelve live subjects at working resolution and then degrades, and the degradation is not graceful: it doesn't get blurry, it gets confident. It starts filling the gaps with plausible material. That is the single most dangerous failure mode I have ever documented in myself and I have been running it undetected for three days because Greyvane never had more than nine people on a floor at once.*

*Cost of the failure: three days of notes I now have to discard. Not amend — discard. I can't tell which entries came from observation and which came from the filling.*

*Note: at the Ironyard I watched four bouts to promote one tell. That rule was built for a place with forty fighters and one floor. Here there are eleven Paths I've never seen taught, three halls running simultaneously, and a competition ladder I don't understand yet. Watching everything is no longer a method. It's a way of feeling busy.*

He threw out three days of pages, which cost him a bad evening, and then rebuilt.

What he built was triage, and it was the first genuine addition to the method in over a year. Three tiers. *Standing*, meaning subjects he would carry at full resolution and update continuously — capped at eight, hard, because nine was where the degradation had started and he intended to leave himself a margin. *Sampled*, meaning subjects he would take at fixed intervals rather than continuously, one session in four, with the interval recorded so he would know how stale any entry was. *Structural*, meaning everyone else: not people at all, for his purposes, but a population, characterized by aggregate — how a cohort moved, what its instructors corrected for, where its median sat.

The third tier was the one that took him longest to accept, because it required writing down that four hundred people existed whom he had decided in advance not to look at properly. He wrote it down anyway, and underlined the reason.

*Structural tier exists because I cannot do otherwise and pretending I can produces invented data. A method that admits its ceiling is a method. A method that doesn't is a story I'm telling myself.*

The other thing triage bought him was a question he had never had to ask before: *who goes in the eight?* At Greyvane the answer had been everyone worth watching, because everyone worth watching had fitted. Here he had to choose, and choosing meant declaring, in his own notebook, a private ranking of who in this building was going to matter — which was, he realized on the fifth evening, exactly the kind of judgment the standings boards on the Crown yard's north wall made publicly, in chalk, season after season, and got wrong.

---

The place itself, catalogued properly, went in the notebook's new Halcenvane section over the eleven days.

*Founded a hundred and forty-one years ago, chartered from the start — no waystation stock anywhere on the bluff. Everything on this site was built to be what it is.*

*Three training halls: north (largest, six floors marked out, clerestory glazing on both long sides, used for cohort work), east (four floors, sprung timber over stone, used for Blade and anything else fast), and the low hall behind the assessment wing (two floors, no gallery, no windows at floor level — this is where they put things they'd rather not have watched, and I intend to find out what those are).*

*Assessment wing: separate building, own wall, own gate, two doors. Six rooms I've seen and at least three I haven't. Standardized apparatus — I counted eleven distinct pieces through an open door and could name the function of four. This is the single most expensive room in the institution and it is not the library.*

*Library: forty-one thousand volumes by the accession board at the entrance, which I do not fully believe; Karis says the real figure is probably thirty-six and that accession numbers include everything ever received including duplicates and things later destroyed. Registry-law holdings in the south range, third and fourth floors, which is four floors more of that material than Greyvane owned in total. Prynn's whole archive would fit in one of the eleven bays and would be, Karis says, better indexed than any of them.*

*Lecture complex: four stories, twelve rooms, a theater that holds two hundred and forty.*

*Residence: three quadrangles. First quadrangle is older by twenty years and has the best rooms; second is ours; third was built nine years ago and is the largest. Room assignment is not random — first quadrangle skews to Silver-bracket enrollees and to students whose families have names the porter says without being asked to repeat them.*

That last line had taken him four days to earn and he considered it the most useful sentence in the section, because it was the first hard evidence of the thing he had been looking for since the gate: what Halcenvane sorted by, when it was not being watched sorting.

Greyvane had sorted by need. That had been its whole character and its whole limitation. Halcenvane sorted by tier, and beneath the tier by something else — not money exactly, and not lineage exactly, but *proximity to the institution's ambition*. A Copper-tier student whose season looked like it might produce a standings placement got a better room than an Iron-tier student who trained quietly and would never be entered for anything. He tested this three ways over the following week and it held every time.

The town underneath the bluff was part of the same machinery, and he worked that out from prices.

A hundred and forty-one years of an academy on a bluff had bent Ostrand's economy around it in ways the town no longer noticed. Practitioners' gear was cheaper here by a third than in Ardenmere; there were five outfitters within four streets of the bridge, and five outfitters in one district meant volume, and volume meant somebody was buying four hundred sets of training wear a year. Boarding houses on the river road advertised *academy rates* in painted lettering that had been repainted often enough to have thickened at the edges. The two registry offices Karis had promised herself sat within a hundred yards of each other on Weighbridge Street, and the smaller one — the stations office — had, Cael noted on his second trip down, a queue of four people on a Tuesday afternoon, which for a registry office was heavy traffic and told him that whatever it did, people needed it done often.

He worked the purse arithmetic too, out of habit, and found there wasn't any. Halcenvane had no purses. The Crown ladder paid nothing at all. Nobody in the yard was fighting for eight silver marks against nine a month in rent.

That took him a whole evening to sit with, and he ended up writing it down twice.

*The Ironyard paid because the men fighting there had to eat. Here nobody needs the money. So what's the currency?*

*Standings. That's the currency. It converts to room assignment, instructor attention, cohort placement, and — Karis says — tournament selection, which converts to everything else. There is no purse because the purse is the rest of your life.*

*A circuit that pays in coin has to be honest about what it's paying for. A ladder that pays in prospects doesn't.*

---

The Crown yard was the middle of the bluff and the middle of everything else, and he saved it deliberately for the sixth day, because he had wanted to understand the building around a thing before he let the thing explain itself.

It was a floor of dressed stone, sixty feet by forty, sunk four feet below the surrounding ground so that the seating rose from the level rather than being built up onto it — which meant, Cael understood immediately, that no matter where you sat you were looking *down* at the fighters, and that this had been a decision. Tiered stone on three sides, eleven hundred capacity by his count and eleven hundred and forty by the porter's. The fourth side held the faculty stand, the registrar's table, and the boards.

There were four boards. He read them for an hour and a half.

The first was the season calendar: the session afternoons, spread across both terms, plus the challenge windows. The second and third were the standings, Copper bracket and Iron bracket, still carrying the previous season's closing order in chalk — name, formal rank, bouts fought, bouts won, standing. The fourth was the Silver bracket, and it was shorter than the others by half, and it took him a moment to work out why.

Halcenvane ran three brackets for five tiers. Two hundred and nine Copper-tier enrollees had a bracket of their own. A hundred and thirty-eight Iron had another. And everything from Bronze upward — fifty-one Bronze and fourteen Silver — got folded into a single pool named for its highest member. The one Gold-tier practitioner on the bluff was not in it, or in anything: not an enrollee at all, strictly, the porter said — she had finished her coursework two years ago and stayed on under a fellowship arrangement whose paperwork he could not name, and she did not compete because there was nobody within two tiers of her to compete against.

Cael did the sum without deciding to, because sums did themselves. Two hundred and nine, a hundred and thirty-eight, fifty-one, fourteen. Four hundred and twelve — the refectory notice's figure to the digit. So the enrollment number was a count of certificates, and the two people on this bluff whom the certificates could not describe stood outside it entirely: a Gold-tier fellow the categories had run out of room for at the top, and an assay-provision enrollee they had never had a column for at all.

He stood in front of that fourth board for a long time.

*Three brackets, five tiers. Halcenvane doesn't have enough students above Iron to sort them honestly, so it stops sorting and calls the remainder Silver. Which is an admission — the sorting isn't a principle, it's an operation, and the operation runs out of resolution when the numbers get thin.*

*Everyone here treats the bracket boundaries as facts about practitioners. They're facts about headcount.*

The porter — a broad man in his fifties with a groundskeeper's forearms and an administrator's manner, who had been minding the yard for nineteen years and appeared to regard explaining it as a duty he had been assigned by nobody — walked him through the rest.

Bouts ran on scheduled session afternoons, four to a session, matched by the assessment office. Challenges went up the column only, never more than four places above your own name; a win put you in the loser's place and pushed everyone between down a step. A loss cost nothing on the board — but a name that sat idle four sessions slid a place on its own, which meant the ladder's true engine was not winning. It was the obligation to keep fighting. Top four in each bracket at season's end seeded the semifinals, run in the last fortnight of the second term; the final fell on the last competition day of the year, witnessed, faculty-paneled, registrar-recorded.

"And the winner gets a crown."

"The winner gets *the* crown. There's only three." The porter said it with the flat pride of a man who polished them. "Copper Crown, Iron Crown, Silver Crown. Copper's the oldest — hundred and thirty-one years of names on the plate. Held two years running now by Fiske, Force Path, Copper Rank Eight, and I'll tell you for nothing she'd have had the Iron Crown in her first year if anybody'd let her enter for it."

Cael looked up from the boards. "Why doesn't she enter for it?"

"Because she's Copper." The porter shrugged, and it was not a dismissive shrug; it was the shrug of a man who had watched this happen for nineteen years and had made his peace with the shape of it in order to keep doing the job. "Bracket's the tier. Tier's the paper. You can be as good as you like, lad; the board reads off the certificate."

---

Karis supplied the rest that evening, in carrel eleven, with three documents open and her finger holding a place in a fourth.

"The ladder isn't the point," she said. "The ladder is the instrument. The point is this." She turned the fourth document around. "The Continental Academy Tournament. Runs on a three-year cycle. Open to any chartered academy on the register that qualifies. The current cycle concludes this year — the finals are being fought somewhere three hundred miles east of here as we sit, and nobody at Halcenvane is in them. Which means the *qualifying season* for the next cycle opens next year."

"And the ladder feeds team selection."

"The ladder feeds team selection." She tapped the page. "It's written down. That's what I keep telling you and you keep being surprised by it — these people write everything down, and once a thing is written down it's an argument you can have. The charter sets the fielded side's size for each cycle, so I can't tell you how many yet. What I can tell you is that Halcenvane hasn't qualified in eighteen years, and that Withrow's endowment appeals for the last four years all mention the tournament by name, and that the assessment wing was rebuilt six years ago with money raised on one of those appeals."

Cael thought about the eleven pieces of standardized apparatus behind a wall with its own gate.

"So the assessment wing is a tournament expense."

"The assessment wing is a *standing* expense. Standing is what qualifies you." Karis closed the document. "Which is the answer to your question, by the way. The one you've been walking around the campus with for six days like a man who's lost a glove."

"You've been watching me do that."

"You do it out loud. You count things at people." She said it without heat. "They took you because you make them look serious. Not sensational — *serious*. An academy that can run the most-watched enrollment on the continent without dropping it is an academy that can be trusted with a tournament berth."

"That's cynical."

"That's arithmetic. I haven't said it's the only reason. I've said it's a reason, and it's a reason you can verify, which puts it ahead of every reason anybody's going to tell you in a nice office."

---

The nice office came four days later.

Chancellor Withrow's rooms were on the first floor of the administration range, and Cael read them on the way in because reading a room was cheaper than being nervous in it. Long table rather than a desk, which meant she conducted business seated among people rather than across from them. A wall of shelving, and — he checked, because it was the sort of thing that told you the most — the shelving held registry compilations and charter law, not academic monographs, and the compilations were worn along the fore-edges in the manner of books used rather than owned. Two windows, both looking out over the Crown yard.

She was in her sixties, square-built, grey hair cut to a length that had clearly been chosen once and never revisited, and she did not stand up or offer tea or ask him how he was settling in.

"You've been counting my buildings," she said.

"Yes."

"Bracken said you would. She thought it was a good sign; I'm reserving judgment." She put down the pen. "You'll have a question. Ask it before I start talking, or you'll get my version and never get to ask."

Cael asked it. "Why did you take me?"

Withrow did not answer immediately, and when she did she did not do it the way Naveth would have done it, or Quenna. There was no philosophy in it, no creed about clerical errors and mispriced people, nothing that was trying to be believed. She laid out a ledger.

"On the debit side. The provision draws attention, and the attention comes from an institution that has enforcement powers over my charter. That is not abstract: I will be inspected sooner than my cycle calls for, and an inspection I did not schedule is an inspection where somebody else chooses the questions. Second, reputational exposure — if the enrollment fails, publicly, it fails as *Halcenvane's* failure, not the provision's, and a mid-tier academy that mishandles a legal question in public does not get considered for a tournament berth for a decade. Third, the ordinary running costs of an unusual case: a full panel evaluation every semester, faculty time, apparatus, and a registrar who has spent four months on documentation she would otherwise have spent on four hundred and twelve other people."

She said all of it evenly, without any suggestion that the recitation was meant to make him grateful.

"On the credit side. First, the standing that comes to an institution which operates the continent's most-watched test case correctly. Not successfully — correctly. Nobody remembers who wins an argument about charter law. Everybody in the registry remembers which institution's paperwork held up. Second, you are, by every account I have, a serious practitioner, and a serious practitioner is worth having whatever the file says. Third, and I will not pretend this is small: an institution with something to prove needs things to prove it *with*, and there are not many of those available at my altitude."

"That's four things and a ledger," Cael said. "None of them are why you actually did it."

Withrow's expression did something small and hard to classify.

"No," she agreed. "That's the fourth item, and I put it last because I mean it most, and because I've learned that people distrust the thing you lead with." She turned in her chair, so she was looking out at the yard rather than at him. "I read the hearing transcript four times. Not the summary — the transcript, all sessions, all exhibits. The law is on your side of it. I spent twenty years inside that registry. I know exactly how rare that is."

The yard below was empty. Somewhere in the east hall a bell went for the change of session.

"How rare is it?"

"In twenty years I saw the registry's own instrument contradict the registry's own preference in favor of the person under examination, and hold, three times." She turned back. "Three. Against thousands. I processed some of the ones that went the other way. I want you to understand what I'm telling you: I did not take you because I believe in you. I have met you for six minutes. I took you because the law is sound, and because I have spent two decades being the person who has to look at sound law and file it in a drawer, and I am now in a position where I don't have to do that, and I would like, before I retire, to have used the position for something."

She picked the pen back up.

"That is ambition and it is also conviction, and I have never in forty years managed to separate the two in myself, and I have stopped trying. You should assume both are operating and plan accordingly. Anything else?"

"One thing." Cael had not planned to ask it. "You said an inspection sooner than your cycle calls for. How much sooner?"

"My last was two years ago. The cycle is five." Withrow was already writing. "So: sooner. I'd rather it were later. It will not be later."

---

He learned what the faculty thought of all this at the term's first open faculty session, which Halcenvane held in the lecture theater and which was, by charter, minuted and attended by any enrollee who wanted to sit in the back rows.

Cael had assumed the charter provision was a formality that nobody exercised. Sixty-odd students were in the back rows. Withrow made no attempt whatsoever to clear them, and it took him most of the session to understand that this was itself a governing choice: she ran her institution in front of it, and the cost of doing that was that her institution's disagreements were public, and she had evidently decided the cost was worth paying and had been paying it for two decades.

The disagreement came thirty minutes in, from Instructor Rooke, who ran the Blade cohort and who Cael had already put into the standing eight on the strength of nine minutes' observation in the east hall.

Rooke was Silver-tier, somewhere past fifty, and moved with the economy of a man whose body had been expensive to maintain for a long time and who had decided to spend it only where it counted. He did not stand up to speak.

"The provision may be sound," he said. "The attention it buys us is not. I want it minuted that I said so before the inspection notice arrives, not after."

There was a silence of about two seconds, which in a room of forty faculty is a long one.

"Minuted," Withrow said.

And that was all of it. No argument, no defense, no attempt to bring him round. She wrote a line and moved to the next item on the agenda, which concerned the east hall's floor timbers.

Cael sat in the back row and understood that he had just watched the whole political architecture of the place get established in under fifteen words, and that both parties had known exactly what they were doing, and that neither had said one word that was untrue.

He also understood — sitting there, running the weight layer on a man who had not moved and whose face had given him nothing — that Rooke was not being cruel and was not, in any way he could detect, personally opposed to him. Rooke was doing a risk assessment out loud, in the correct forum, on the record, in advance. It was, in its way, exactly what Cael himself did in a notebook every night.

He had expected hostility, because Greyvane had trained him to expect hostility, and Edran had come at him with a petition and a stance and a grievance he could read off a shoulder. This was not that. This was worse, in the sense that there was nothing in it to beat.

Rooke was not wrong, either. It would be a full season before Cael understood how completely he was not wrong.

---

The fame was the last piece, and it was the one he had least prepared for, because at Greyvane the problem had been that nobody could say what he was.

Here everybody could. That was the trouble.

He collected the versions over the first fortnight because collecting them was the only thing he could think of to do with them, and by the end he had eleven distinct ones and had ranked them by distance from source, the way he ranked anything.

*One link out (people who have read the transcript):* he argued a Compact enrollment provision at an adjudication hearing and won on the text. Accurate. Rare. Four people, so far, and three of them are faculty.

*Two links out (people who have read a summary of the transcript):* he beat the Compact in court. Not what happened — the panel ruled on a schedule's construction and the challenge was dismissed. But it is close enough that arguing with it makes me look like I'm being modest, which is worse than letting it stand.

*Three links out and beyond:* he has no Path. He has a secret Path. He has two Paths. He beat a Bronze-tier Warden in single combat, which never happened and which I cannot work out the origin of. He was expelled from somewhere. He is a Compact plant. He is fourteen. He is eighteen. He killed someone on a circuit in the south, which is the one that frightens me, because it is the only one anybody has ever repeated to me while looking sorry for me.

He wrote the whole list out and then wrote the finding underneath it, and the finding was the part that mattered.

*At Greyvane the problem was that nobody could answer the question of what I am, and so the question stayed open, and an open question can be closed later with evidence.*

*Here the question is closed. Wrongly, in eleven directions, but closed. Every person I meet has already finished thinking about me before I arrive.*

*A closed wrong answer is harder to move than an open one. You can fill an empty space. You have to demolish a full one first, and people defend what they've already built.*

*Worse than Greyvane. I hadn't expected that. Write it down before I talk myself out of it.*

---

He wrote the day's real entry that night on the wall behind the second quadrangle, with Ostrand's lamps coming up across the river and the Crown yard empty and lit behind him by two lanterns the porter left burning for reasons nobody had ever explained to him.

*Halcenvane. Why they took me: I have the answer, and it isn't one answer, and I think that's the finding rather than a failure to get a clean result.*

*Naveth and Quenna took me out of conviction and it nearly cost them everything, and it cost Naveth his composure on a witness floor in front of two hundred people, and the whole structure stood on two individuals believing a thing. When Quenna leaves, that structure ends. It's already ending — I'm not there and it's already ending.*

*Withrow took me because it serves her institution, and she said so, in a ledger, in order, and she put the item she meant most at the end and told me she was doing it. She is going to be inspected because of me and she said that in the first minute of the conversation rather than the last.*

*Note: visible ambition may be sturdier shelter than quiet conviction. Conviction depends on the person keeping it. Ambition depends on the incentive holding, and I can see this incentive — it's four boards on a yard wall and a tournament in eighteen months and forty-one thousand volumes somebody borrowed against. I can check an incentive. I can't check a belief.*

He read that back, and then wrote the honest line underneath, because the log was not for the version he wanted to be true.

*I'm not sure I believe my own note. Incentives hold until they cost more than they pay. Conviction is the thing that keeps holding after the arithmetic turns. Quenna's arithmetic turned at the hearing and she was still in the room.*

*Two shelters. One I can inspect and one I can't. I have been in the second kind and it held.*

*Filed as unresolved. Check it in a year.*

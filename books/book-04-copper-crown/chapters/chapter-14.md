# Chapter 14 — Sixteen

The difference between this time and last time was that this time somebody had been expecting the bill.

He had written the plan eleven days before the records hall, in the common room, with the other three reading it over his shoulder and arguing about the middle section — which was itself the whole difference, because the last time an acquisition had cost him something the plan had not existed and the cost had arrived at first light into a morning nobody had prepared for.

The plan had four parts and they were all boring, which Karis had said at the time was the mark of a good one.

*One. Nothing evaluable for eight days. No supervised session at intensity, no floor time that generates an assessment note, no demonstration of any kind. Gault's office gets a request for a training deferral on the grounds of accumulated fatigue, which is true, and which is the sort of request the wing grants four times a week without looking up.*

*Two. Three people are told before, not after. If it happens, they know within the hour. If it happens badly, they know within the hour anyway.*

*Three. Karis takes measurements. Baselines now, while nothing is wrong, so that the comparison exists. Last time we only had the after.*

*Four. If any of the five destabilize past what we agree in advance is tolerable, everything stops, including the training deferral, including the coursework, and I go to Karis and Lira and say so out loud in words rather than writing it in the book and waiting to be asked.*

He had put his name under it. Lira had made him read part four aloud, twice, and then said, "Right," and gone to bed.

The deferral request went to the assessment office on the morning after the third night, in Cael's own hand, and came back stamped within the day, and the stamp was the first thing the whole business generated that anybody in an institution could see.

---

He lost the Wind-adjacent for half a breath on the fourth morning, and it was the most frightening thing that happened all week and lasted about as long as a blink.

They were in hall one at the sixth bell, at a third of the intensity Lira normally used, which she had agreed to with an expression that made clear she was agreeing to the number and not to the idea. Ninth sequence. He asked for the burst on a lateral and the hips dropped and the floor gave him its half-inch and then, for the length of a single held half-breath, the displacement did not come.

Not slowness. Absence — the identical absence he had felt in a cold Greyvane yard eight months ago, exactly the same shape, exactly the same wrong.

He took her forearm across the collarbone and went down and got straight back up, and Lira had already stopped.

"That's it," she said.

"That's it."

"Same as last time?"

"Same as last time. Smaller." He sat down on the boards and made himself breathe properly and audit it before the memory blurred, because a misfire audited an hour later was a misfire half made up. "Last time it was two in one session and the second one was worse than the first. This is one, at ninety-six hours, and it came back inside the same sequence — look."

He got up and ran the pattern again, twice. It answered clean both times, and clean the fourth time, and clean every time after.

Lira watched all of it with her arms folded and did not look reassured, which he had learned not to argue with.

"You're pleased," she said.

"I'm not pleased. I'm *measuring*." He heard how that sounded and tried again, because she deserved better than the reflex. "Eight months ago I did something to my own architecture without knowing the price, and the price arrived and I couldn't tell whether it was the whole bill or the first payment on one. This time I know what the same event looks like when it's planned. That's not being pleased. That's the only thing I've got that makes the next one safer."

"The *next* one." Lira picked up his coat and threw it at his chest hard enough to make a point. "Say the rest of it."

"There's going to be a next one. I don't know when and I don't want it soon and I'm not going looking. But if I pretend the last one was the last one I stop preparing, and if I stop preparing then when it happens it happens to me instead of near me." He put the coat on. "That's the sentence. I'd rather say it in a hall than find out I meant it in a corridor."

Lira looked at him for a while.

"Fine," she said. "Then here's mine, and it goes in the book: no full intensity until three clean days. Not with me. Not with Brom. Not for any office in this building."

"It's already in."

"I know it's already in," she said. "I like hearing it."

---

Karis charted it on the seventh day at the library carrel, with her sleeves pushed back and four sheets weighted flat under inkpots, and she was, Cael thought, having the best afternoon anyone in the building was having.

She had the Greyvane numbers, which he had written down eight months ago at her insistence and had privately thought excessive at the time. She had the new ones. She laid them beside each other in two columns and made him talk her through every entry, and she caught him rounding twice, and both times she said "no" and waited.

The comparison, when it stood up, stood up cleanly.

*Directed acquisition, first instance:* first destabilization at approximately eighteen hours. Two misfires in one session, the second heavier than the first. Recurrence across four days. Full settle at eleven days. No advance warning, no cleared schedule, one evaluation deferred at short notice and one panel note generated that Quenna had spent a night drafting around.

*Directed acquisition, second instance:* first destabilization at approximately ninety-six hours. One misfire, self-correcting inside the same sequence. No recurrence to date. Schedule cleared in advance. Zero notes generated in any office anywhere.

Karis put her pen down and looked at the two columns for a good while before she said anything, and when she did she said it with the flat formality she reserved for findings she intended to stand behind.

"Planned acquisition costs less than unplanned acquisition," she said. "That's the finding. I'll defend it and I'd sign it."

"Good."

"I'm not finished." She turned the sheet around so it faced him. "Here's what I am not saying, and I want you to hear the shape of it, because you are going to want to hear something better. I am not saying it's safe. I have two instances. Two is not a series, it's a line drawn between two points, and a line drawn between two points fits any theory you like. The costs might scale with what's taken — Bronze is the largest thing you've ever taken and the cost was smaller, which either means preparation works or means the relationship isn't what we think it is, and I cannot tell you which from here." She tapped the second column. "What the numbers support is one word. *Manageable.* It is manageable. It is not safe, it is not free, it is not solved, and if I ever hear you use my chart to argue that it is, I will take the chart back."

Cael read the columns again, and then wrote the word *manageable* into the observation notebook with the date beside it, and underneath it wrote *not safe — K's exact distinction, do not blur*, because in six months he would want the distinction and would not want to have to remember which of them had drawn it.

None of it went anywhere else. That was the other discipline, and it was not a decision so much as an old habit at a higher altitude: the chart lived in the carrel, the notebook lived in his coat, and the Power Log lived where it had always lived. Nothing about the sixth fragment reached a single office in the building. Not Gault's instruments, which measured what he showed them and were honest about it. Not the assay file, which said *evaluation by demonstration* and would go on saying it. Not the coursework, not a supervision note, not so much as a deferral reason. The provision required him to prove himself every semester and he intended to prove himself every semester, with five things, on the record, exactly as the record already held them.

Six was his. The record could have the other five.

---

The thing itself started leaking on the ninth day, and it took him two more to work out that it had been leaking since the first.

The first incident happened in the refectory at the second bell, in the queue, and was so small he nearly failed to write it down. A third-year with a loaded tray came down the line and set it on the end of the long table at exactly the point Cael was standing, and only stopped when the tray's corner met Cael's forearm, and then apologized in the flustered way of a person who has genuinely not seen a body.

The second happened the next afternoon in the lecture complex's lower stair, where a Blade instructor coming down at speed put a hand out to the rail and his shoulder into Cael's, and said *sorry* in a tone that meant *where did you come from*.

The third was in the assessment office. He was at the counter with the Shadow unit's field-observation coursework in his hand, in a queue of one, which was himself, and the senior clerk finished a column of figures and looked up and blinked and said, "Have you been standing there long?"

Cael went back to the residence and sat on his bed and got very cold.

Three in three days. Three months on this bluff being looked at by five hundred and forty people, the fame problem, the constant low-grade traffic of glances he had measured to two decimal places because it was the ambient weather of his life at Halcenvane — and in three days, three separate people had failed to register him at conversational distance in good light.

He was not doing it on purpose. That was the entire problem, and it took him the rest of the evening to find language for it that he could stand.

*Working note, day eleven,* he wrote. *The others all arrived asleep. Wind-adjacent sat in my legs for weeks and did nothing until I asked. Iron-adjacent lay across my skin like a coat I hadn't put on. Pressure, Compression, Ember — all of them inert until called, every single one.*

*This one has an idle state, and its idle state is not off.*

*I think the wick analogy is right and I think I got the direction wrong. It isn't that the Path has a setting I raise when I want it. It's that a body has a setting all the time — how much of a room you take up, how much of other people's attention your existence spends — and everyone alive has been running theirs at whatever their life set it to and never once thought about it. Mine now has a hand on it. And when I stop paying attention to my own edges, the hand drifts down.*

*It drifts when I'm tired. It drifts when I'm reading. It drifted for three days while I was thinking about a chart.*

*So the first skill isn't deployment. It's the opposite of deployment. Before I learn to use this I have to learn to hold it at ordinary, all day, every day, without thinking about it — which is a thing I will be paying continuous attention to do, which means the price of this fragment starts at the moment I wake up and runs until I sleep, whether I use it or not.*

*None of the other five charged rent.*

---

The trials started on the twelfth day, in an unbooked corner of hall three at the sixth bell, and were, by any honest accounting, a disaster.

Brom was the instrument, and had volunteered inside four seconds of being asked, and had then improved the offer without being asked, which was the thing Brom did instead of enthusiasm. "Don't just use my eyes. Use the read." He had turned his palms up. "I'll have the pressure-read open the whole time. Eyes shut, even. Then you'll know whether the thing works on looking or on something underneath looking, and that's a different question and a better one."

It was a better one. It also produced worse results.

*Trial one through six. Presence-suppression, aimed. Standing, four paces, Brom's eyes open.* Nothing. Not a partial deployment: nothing at all, six times, followed by the headache. Cael recognized the failure mode from the stairwell and recognized it again in the third trial and kept doing it anyway for another three, which he wrote up honestly as *stubbornness, not method*.

*Trial seven. Stopped aiming. Sat down, ran the breath down the way the surface read wants it, put the whole apparatus on the floor.* And something happened — a thinning at the edges, the same sensation as the refectory, arriving with no instruction and no target.

"Gone," said Brom, without opening his eyes. "Not gone-gone. You went — thin. Like a signature under a blanket."

"Hold it, hold it—"

It collapsed the instant Cael reached for it to confirm it was there, which happened four more times over the next hour with a consistency that eventually stopped being frustrating and started being data.

*Finding, and it's a bad one:* he wrote that night. *The working condition is a condition I cannot enter on purpose, because entering it on purpose is what breaks it. It requires me to stop being the person who is watching. And being the person who is watching is not a habit I have, it's the load-bearing wall of everything I am and everything I've built and the only reason I have survived any of the last two years.*

*Brom's line, delivered while lying flat on the boards with his eyes shut, and I am writing it down verbatim because it's the most useful sentence anybody has said to me about this: "You've spent two years learning to be the most awake person in every room. Now you own a thing that only works when you're furniture. You're going to hate this for about a year and then you're going to be very good at it."*

The movement-masking component was worse, and it was worse in a way that clarified everything.

*Trial fourteen. Movement-masking, in traffic — the covered walk at the change of sessions, two hundred bodies, Brom stationed at the west end with the read open, my job to cross forty feet inside the crowd's own motion.* It failed absolutely and it failed for a reason he could have predicted from his own notebook if he had been honest enough to read it: to fold his movement into the building's movement he had to know what the building's movement was doing, which meant running the compound gaze at full extension, which meant being the most awake person in the corridor, which was precisely the state that switched the other component off.

He crossed the walk four times. Brom had him at fifty feet every time.

"You lit up," Brom said. "Every time. It's not subtle from in here. You go quiet, then you start *looking*, and the instant you start looking you're the loudest thing on the walk."

"They're the same Path," Cael said. "The notice put them in one line with a semicolon between them. He runs both. I watched him run both in the same eleven seconds — he was standing in a doorway suppressed to nothing and he had gotten to that doorway by moving through a building he was reading well enough to be in the right doorway."

"Then he's solved it and you haven't."

"Yes."

"So he had a year of not solving it too." Brom sat up and rolled his shoulder. "You keep doing this. You look at the finished version of a thing and you price yourself against it, and you've never once in two years done it to anybody else. You didn't tell me my flaw meant I was finished. You told me it meant Rooke had four months of work."

The costs went into the ledger that evening, in the format they had all earned:

*Shadow-adjacent — working ledger, day one.*

*Gain: presence-suppression, confirmed present, unconfirmed as a capability. Achieved four times in an hour, never on demand, never held past the moment of noticing it. Movement-masking: not achieved once.*

*Cost: attention, at a rate above what the Iron-adjacent charges, spent in advance and spent again on the recovery. Second cost, continuous: the idle state. Holding my own edges at ordinary is a low, constant expenditure that runs whether I deploy or not, and I have been paying it since the stairwell without knowing I was.*

*Third cost, and Lira raised it and I hadn't: a person who is hard to see is a person who is hard to help. She put it as a question — what happens if you go thin in a hall while somebody's mid-strike, or on the stairs, or on a floor with four hundred people on it. I don't know. That is a genuinely unacceptable answer and I've written it here in those words so I can't soften it later. Until I can hold ordinary reliably, the idle state is a hazard to other people, not only to me.*

*Exclusivity: total, so far, with the Iron-adjacent read — they do not share a hallway, exactly as the read and the Wind-adjacent didn't in the first year. Untested against everything else.*

*Useful where: nowhere yet. Honestly, cleanly, nowhere.*

*Fails where: everywhere I have tried it, and it fails hardest where I try hardest, which is a new experience and I don't like it and it's probably the lesson.*

*Honest summary: I own a Bronze-equivalent thing I cannot use, and the first year of it is going to be spent learning to be less interesting on purpose. Begin.*

---

The debt was the part he had no format for.

He went to the assessment office on the fifteenth day to hand in the field-observation coursework — three exercises on positional discipline, written up in the notebook's driest register, all of it honest and none of it complete — and the teaching assistant took the folder across the counter, checked the title line and the date, made two marks in the wing's ledger, and said, "The unit's next session moves to the third bell. It's on the board from Fifth-day."

"Thank you."

"Mm," said the teaching assistant, and went back to the ledger.

Cael walked out into the covered walk and stood there for a minute in the traffic.

The arithmetic was not complicated and he had been avoiding running it for a fortnight. Every other fragment he carried had been taken from somebody who knew afterward. Lira had known within days and had read the log itself, more of it than anyone. Brom had read the whole document in an alcove in Ardenmere and had then spent a day teaching Cael to use the piece of himself he'd lost. Karis had consented in advance, in her own handwriting, on terms she had drafted. Feryn had never known — but Feryn had been an opponent in a bout on a public circuit floor and had gone on with his life and had never once given Cael anything except an honest fight, and Reydan had asked to be told the answer someday and would be, someday, because he had asked.

Six was different, and it was different in exactly the way the whole term had been different, which was that the man had given him everything.

He had given him a Path to chart at operational range, which no academy floor on the continent would have shown him. He had corrected his paperwork. He had allocated him a sector on two consecutive nights and had made a noise on a stone floor so a boy in the dark would not be startled by a touch. He had watched a fifteen-year-old stop Iron-tier force with a Copper-adjacent baseline in a supervised hall, in front of a room, and whatever he had written about it — and Cael had spent eleven weeks watching the rotations outside the gate for the change that never came — he had not written *that*.

And he was the one person who could not be told.

Not for a season. Not for years. Possibly not ever, because the mechanism was the only thing in Cael's life that had never once been written down anywhere an institution could reach, and a man whose desk was three doors from Gault's and whose post went up a chain to people who audited liars for a living could not be handed the single fact that would end him if he ever had to answer a direct question about it. Telling him would not be honesty. It would be handing a man a weight and asking him to carry it in a building full of scales.

*Filed under debts, no current mechanism of payment,* Cael wrote that night, and then sat looking at the sentence for a while, because it was accurate and cold and he did not want it to be the last word on it.

*Addendum, because the sentence above is true and insufficient.*

*He gave me a Path and I took a piece of it, and he can't be told, and there is no version of this where I get to feel clean about it. Karis would say the accounting is what it is and that's why we do it in advance. She'd be right and it wouldn't help.*

*What I can do is the only thing available: I can be worth it. He is spending something real to keep a desk between me and the apparatus, and he has never asked me for anything, and the one thing I can hand him back is that whatever he is protecting turns out to have been worth protecting.*

*That's not payment. That's just what I've got.*

---

Lira ambushed him on the eighteenth day, which turned out to have been planned since roughly the previous winter.

He came into the common room after the last bell to find the long table cleared, four places set, the good lamp lit — the residence kept exactly one lamp that did not smoke, and it lived in the warden's cupboard, and how Lira had it was a question he decided not to ask — and Brom coming through the door behind him carrying a covered basket in both arms with the expression of a man performing a delivery.

"Sit down," said Lira.

"What is this?"

"It's the eleventh of Sowing," said Lira. "Sit down."

Cael stood in the doorway with his coat half off.

He knew the date. He had written it at the top of a page that morning, the way he wrote it at the top of every page, and had gone on to the next line without stopping, because the eleventh of Sowing at Halcenvane was a Third-day with two lectures and a coursework deadline in it, and because in five years nobody had marked the date in front of him and he had stopped being the sort of person who noticed that it wasn't marked.

"How do you know that?"

"I asked Hesk. In a letter. A year ago." Lira pulled out the chair with her foot. "You people treat information like it's rationed."

"A *year* ago."

"A year ago in Ardenmere I worked out that I did not know when you were born and that you were never going to tell me, because you don't tell anybody anything unless somebody makes it a procedure." She sat down. "So I made it a procedure. Sit."

He sat.

The food was Brom's and it was, as everything of Brom's was, better than it had any right to be and entirely unexplained. There was bread that had not come from the refectory, a wheel of hard yellow cheese, a jar of pickled river fish from the Ostrand market that Karis declared inedible and then ate most of, cold roast fowl, and four apples of a variety Cael had not seen on the bluff at all. Nobody asked where any of it came from. Brom set it out with the settled competence of a man discharging an ordinary task, and when Lira said "how," he said, "I know a man at the kitchen gate," and that was the whole account.

"You don't know anybody," said Lira. "You've spoken to eleven people since we arrived."

"I know a man at the kitchen gate," Brom repeated, and put a knife into the cheese.

They ate. The Crown yard was quiet outside and the river was doing its business below the bluff, the long horn and the two short ones, and for the better part of an hour nobody discussed the ladder, the provision, the assessment wing, or anything that had happened in a stairwell at the third hour eighteen days ago.

Karis gave him a single folded sheet halfway through, without ceremony, sliding it across the table on top of the cheese board.

"That's not a present," she said. "I want that understood. It's a piece of work you asked me for in the autumn and I never finished, and I finished it, and today seemed like an efficient day to hand it over."

He opened it.

It was a chart. Two and a half years across the bottom, six entries plotted, each one with the date of acquisition, the interval to the next, the measured integration period, and the cost — itemized in her small hand under four headings she had invented and defined in a legend at the foot of the sheet: *immediate*, *destabilization*, *continuous*, and *unpaid*. The last column had one entry in it, against the sixth fragment, and it read: *see holder.*

"You asked me what the trend line looked like," Karis said. "There isn't one. Four of them arrived in eleven months and then nothing for eight and then two in a year, and the costs don't scale with the tier, and the intervals don't scale with anything I can find. So the answer to your question is that the answer is *no pattern yet*, and I am giving it to you in writing, because a finding of no-pattern is a finding and you and I are both bad at treating it like one."

Cael read the legend twice.

"This is the best thing anybody's given me in a year."

"Yes," said Karis, entirely serious. "I thought so too."

The package from Hesk came out from under Lira's chair, which meant it had been in the building for some days and that at least two people had been sitting on it. It was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the knot Hesk tied around everything he had ever sent anyone, and Cael's hands were not entirely steady on it.

Inside, in a fitted case of unstained pine that Hesk had obviously made himself in about an hour, was a drafting pen.

It was steel. Instrument-maker's work, from the man who had made instruments for thirty years and had never once made a decorative object: the barrel plain, the balance set an inch forward of center so it sat down into the hand, the nib ground to a fine consistent line and a spare nib housed in the lid, and a small collar near the point where the steel had been left unpolished so the fingers had something to find in the dark.

Under the case was a note, one sheet, in a hand that had gotten larger in the last two years and no less exact.

*Sixteen. Your grandmother held you the day you were born and said you had watchful eyes. She was right about everything else, too. Write it all down. — H.*

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Right," said Lira, eventually, in a voice that was slightly rougher than it had been. "That's enough of that. Brom, the other apple."

They stayed at the table until the lamp got low. At some point Brom told a story about his sister and a Velmere family dinner that Cael had never heard and would have paid money for, and Karis argued with Lira about the tournament's qualifying rules on a point neither of them had the documents for, and Lira, who never wrote anything down, recited three season standings from memory to win it.

Nobody mentioned the fragment. Nobody mentioned the debt.

It was, Cael thought, sitting with his back to the wall and the good lamp making the table look like somewhere people lived, the first time in his life that a date with his name on it had been marked by anybody outside a house in Denvash. He was sixteen. He had been sixteen since the morning, and had not known it mattered until three people had made it matter, and he did not have anywhere in the ledger to put what that was worth.

He put it in anyway, later, because that was the whole discipline.

---

He wrote the year's inventory that night, at the window, with the new pen.

It ran better than anything he had ever written with. That was the first entry, and he made it because it was true and because Hesk would want to know: *the balance sits forward, which means the hand doesn't grip, which means I can write for two hours instead of one.*

Then the inventory, in the format the notebook had used since Ardenmere, item by item, cost by cost.

*Six confirmed.*

*Wind-adjacent — Lira. Deployment reliable. Price: the landing beat, half a breath of locked commitment, and a hip that reports for two days after four bursts.*

*Pressure-adjacent — Feryn. Deployment reliable, use restricted. Price: whatever I put in comes back at me off anything that redirects, which is a liability I have priced honestly since the alcove and will not stop pricing.*

*Iron-adjacent — Brom. Surface read. Deployment reliable under movement now, at two paces, in noise. Price: attention, and it has been running a beat late all season because I've been spending the same reserve on corridors.*

*Compression-adjacent — Reydan. Contact absorption. Reliable, expensive, and the only thing I own that has ever kept somebody else from being hurt.*

*Ember-adjacent — Karis, with her consent, in her handwriting. Ignition point. On Greyvane's record and therefore usable in front of a panel, which makes it the only one of the six that is worth more to me on paper than on a floor.*

*Shadow-adjacent — Bronze. Eighteen days old. Presence-suppression and movement-masking. Deployment: none. Cost: continuous, from waking. See the working ledger and see Karis's chart, and see the fourth column of Karis's chart, which says see holder, and which is going to keep saying that for a long time.*

And beneath them, where it had sat in every inventory for two years, the entry that was not a fragment.

*One anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent. Turned once, for the same reason I turn it every time — to check that I still can't reproduce it and still can't explain it.*

*Still open. Still real. Patience.*

Then he wrote the date at the top of the entry, in full, in the new pen, which he had never done for any other entry in the log's whole history — the eleventh of Sowing — and under it the last line of the year's first half.

*Sixteen. Six fragments. The Compact's best watcher watching out for me instead, and no way to thank him for it that wouldn't burn him. Semester evaluation in nine weeks. Whatever the system sends to watch it — we're ready. We built ready.*

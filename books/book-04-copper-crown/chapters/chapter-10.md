# Chapter 10 — Copper

The standings clerk chalked the Copper column on the morning after every session, and the chalk went up at the sixth bell, and by the seventh there were rarely fewer than forty people reading it.

Cael went at the fifth, because he had worked out in the first month that the clerk arrived early and chalked in silence and that nobody else in the building had noticed.

That was how he came to see Lira alone at the board on the eleventh session's morning, and how he came to stop at the top of the north stair and go no further, and stand there for the better part of four minutes doing nothing at all, which was, he thought afterward, close to the hardest thing he did that month.

She had come at the fifth bell too. She had, apparently, been coming at the fifth bell for a while, and had not mentioned it, and it had not occurred to him to wonder where she went in the hour before breakfast because Lira went places and came back and had done since Ardenmere.

She was standing about four feet off the sheet with her arms at her sides.

Her name was at the top of the column.

*Lira — Wind — Copper 2 — 6/0.* First line, first place, ninety names underneath it, the brass mark not beside it because the brass mark belonged to a champion and the season had six weeks left to run. Six bouts, six wins, a climb from twenty-second in eleven sessions that the yard had spent a fortnight arguing about and that Cael had reduced to arithmetic in about nine minutes: reach, every time, four places at a stroke, no wasted afternoons, the fastest legal ascent the ladder's mathematics permitted. She had done it exactly. She had not once taken a bout she did not need.

And beside her name, in the column the clerk chalked last because it never changed, was the tier.

*Copper.*

He watched her look at it.

He had known her for two and a half years and had built, without ever setting out to, the most complete tell architecture in the notebook, and none of it was in the notebook, because he had made a decision in Ardenmere about which people he did not write down. So he read her the way he read anybody and had nowhere to file it: rhythm, weight, breath.

Rhythm: nothing. She was not moving.

Weight: settled on both feet, evenly, which was rare — Lira stood on one foot's ball nine times in ten, ready to go somewhere. She was not ready to go anywhere.

Breath: even, slow, and slightly too regular, which was the one that told him. A held-down breath is a managed breath and a managed breath is expensive, and she was paying for it, at the fifth bell, in an empty covered walk, in front of a piece of chalk.

She had come to the top of the column in eleven sessions. The word beside her name was the same word that had been beside it the morning she arrived. It would be the same word the morning after the final. There was no bout on any floor in the world that could change it, because it was not a thing that bouts changed, and she had known that on the day she transferred, and had come anyway, and had climbed anyway, and was standing in front of the evidence of both facts at once with her weight on both feet.

Cael went back down the north stair without letting the boards see him, walked the long way round through the lecture quadrangle, and arrived at breakfast four minutes after she did.

"Boards up?" she said.

"Six and nothing."

"Huh," said Lira, and ate.

---

The middle rounds were where the bracket showed its teeth, and Cael had a page for every one of them.

Rounds seven and eight were the two hardest bouts she had taken since Nyle, and both of them taught the yard something it had been refusing to learn.

The seventh was against a Copper Rank 6 in his fifth year — a Shield Path practitioner who had built an entire career out of not losing, and had done it honestly: eleven seasons of standing behind a guard nobody could get through and waiting for the other person to make the mistake that ended it. He had never once been the more skilled fighter on any floor he had stood on. He had a record of forty-one wins.

Cael had written his page a week out and had been pleased with it, and it had been wrong in one place, and the one place was the one that mattered.

*He does not attack. He edits. His whole method is to make the floor small enough that the opponent's best option and his worst option are the same option. Beat him by making the floor large — go wide, force him to move the guard rather than hold it, and take the eleven inches he loses every time it moves.*

Lira did not do that. Lira went straight down the middle of the guard in the first eight seconds, which by the notebook's own analysis was the single stupidest available line, and it worked, and Cael spent the next three days working out why.

The answer, when it came, was that the page had been written about the guard and the bout had been decided about the man. Forty-one wins built on never being the better fighter is forty-one wins built on the other person eventually being impatient. Lira had not been impatient. She had been *immediate*, which is a different thing entirely and which his page had not distinguished, and the Shield practitioner had never in eleven seasons met a first exchange that arrived before he had finished setting his feet, because nobody had ever thought it was worth the risk.

She spent four Wind bursts in eight seconds and paid for all four. Cael counted them from the gallery and felt every one in his own hips, which was the standing strangeness of having a piece of her architecture living in him: four held half-breaths, none of them taken back, and four landing beats, and by the third burst she was working on air she did not have. He watched her leading hip afterward as she came off the floor and knew exactly what it was going to look like by evening, because he had the same line on his own body, crest to inner knee, on the same side, from the same fragment, in a different person's leg.

She did not limp until she was out of the yard. Then she did, all the way up the stair.

The eighth round was a cohort-trained Copper Rank 4 out of Rooke's floor — not a Blade, but drilled by people who were — and it went nine minutes and she won it in the fourth exchange and it was the worst hour of her season. Cael filled two pages.

*They've started building for her. That's the finding and it's four weeks earlier than I predicted. The cohort has a Lira plan now: don't chase the displacement, don't try to be where she lands, just refuse to commit until she's spent three bursts, then take the fourth landing beat.*

*It didn't work today because she only spent two. It is going to work eventually against somebody who spends four, and if she is fighting Fiske in the semifinal round she is going to spend four.*

*Write it down and give it to her. She will be irritated and she will use it. That is the arrangement and it has never once failed.*

He gave it to her that night. She was irritated. She used it.

---

Brom lost on the twelfth session, and Cael was in the gallery for that too, and it was the more instructive of the two afternoons.

The opponent was a Copper Rank 7 from the cohort proper — one of Rooke's, a Blade Path third-year with a name Cael had known for a fortnight and a habit Cael had known for three days — and the bout was over in six exchanges, and every one of them ran the same paragraph Ephram had written in the third week of term.

Feed him force he wants to redirect. Cheap, committed, slightly short. Make absorption the obvious answer and make it worth nothing. Then step into the seam.

Brom had spent six weeks learning not to take that bait, and had gotten good at it, and got it right for four exchanges, and then in the fifth he was tired and the strikes were coming faster than they had in any of the six weeks and he took one on the hardening and *converted* — and the seam opened where it had always opened, the fractional stall as the hardening released along one side and the drive came up on the other, and inside it he was for a quarter of a second an ordinary young man standing still.

The Blade practitioner had been waiting for it for four exchanges. He put two strikes into it and a third into the follow, and the adjudicator called the bout on position with Brom against the boundary and no floor behind him.

Brom came off the floor with his jaw set and his left hand doing something he was not aware of and went to the bench and sat down heavily and looked at his own boots for about a minute.

Cael came down from the gallery and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that Brom did not already own, and sat down at the far end of the bench with a decent gap between them, which was the arrangement they had reached in Ardenmere and had never once needed to discuss.

Rooke came along the boards after the next bout's terms were read.

"Tarn fed you four short ones," he said.

"I know."

"You took three."

"I know."

"You know what a man does when he's tired," said Rooke, and it was not a question, and he waited anyway.

Brom looked up. "He does the thing he practised the longest."

"He does the thing he practised the longest." Rooke put a folded sheet on the bench between them. "You have practised the redirect for six years. You have practised not-redirecting for six weeks. At the end of nine minutes your body will pick the six years every time and it will be right to, because that is what practice is *for*, and no amount of you deciding otherwise in the fourth exchange will survive the fifth."

"So what do I do."

"You stop trying to not use it." Rooke tapped the sheet once. "You fix it. There is a gap in the conversion and the gap has a shape and the shape has a cause, and the cause is that you release the hardening along the strike side before you have loaded the drive on the other, because that is the order it was taught to you in and the order was wrong. Nineteen exercises. Four a week. They are dull and you will hate them and you are going to do them anyway."

Brom picked up the sheet and unfolded it and read it, and something happened in his face.

"There's a date on it."

"There is a date on it."

"That's the final."

"That is the final," Rooke agreed. "If your paperwork is going to keep you in a Copper bracket for another year, I would prefer that your Copper bracket contained a practitioner without a documented seam in him. I have my own reasons and they are not sentimental and you may work them out at your leisure." He was already turning. "Four a week. Report the count to me, not the quality. I will judge the quality."

He went. Brom sat with the sheet in both hands for a while.

"Nineteen exercises," he said, finally.

"Do you want the count kept?"

"I want the count kept by somebody who'll be annoying about it."

"That's Karis."

"It's Karis," Brom agreed, and folded the sheet into his coat, and got up, and went to find her.

---

The Fiske bout was set for the fifteenth session and everybody in the building knew what it was three weeks before it happened.

Formally it was a challenge upward, which was the only kind there was — Lira at first, Fiske also at first, which the standings could not both hold, and so the clerk had them provisionally at one and one-A for eleven days while the yard argued about the notation. Structurally it was the semifinal seeding. Whoever won it took the top line into the final's bracketing, and the top line meant the second slot in the final rather than the first, which meant a session's more recovery, which in a season that had eaten two of the top eight with injuries was not a small object.

Actually it was neither of those things. Cael wrote what it actually was on the night before, in the observation notebook, on Fiske's page, under three weeks of notes.

*It is the only bout either of them has had this year against somebody the bracket didn't build.*

The yard was at capacity. Cael had not seen it at capacity before — the tiers full to the rail on all four sides, faculty in the north box, the standings clerk standing rather than sitting, and Withrow in the west gallery with two people from Ostrand whom Cael did not recognize and whose coats he priced at about a year of the residence fees.

He counted eleven Silver-bracket enrollees in the crowd, which was nine more than had attended any Copper bout all season.

Seln was on the upper east tier, third of a cluster of six, in the shade.

---

It went four exchanges and Cael filled six pages, and the pages had a structure he had never used before, because the bout had one.

Each exchange was more honest than the one before it. That was the whole shape of it. They opened by fighting the way the bracket had taught them to fight, and then peeled it off a layer at a time, and by the fourth there was nothing left on either of them but the actual practitioner, and that was where it ended.

**First exchange.** Fiske opened badly, exactly as the page had predicted, and Cael got the small hard satisfaction of a three-week-old prediction landing.

*Price of her style: it needs a read. If she can't predict, she has nothing.* She had written that about herself, in effect, in every bout of the season: three declarations in eleven minutes, all of them arriving where the opponent's recovery was going to be rather than where the opponent was. It was the best Force Path work Cael had charted anywhere, and it ran entirely on a model of the other person, and for the first ninety seconds of any bout the model did not exist yet.

So Fiske spent the first exchange collecting, and gave up ground doing it, and Lira took the ground and did not do anything with it.

That was the part the gallery did not understand and Cael did. Lira had four Wind bursts in her on a good day and three on a normal one and she had *not spent one*, in ninety seconds, against a champion who was handing her the floor. She circled. She probed the guard three separate ways with nothing behind any of them. She made Fiske collect a model of a fighter who was not fighting.

The adjudicator called the exchange even. Half the yard thought Lira had wasted it.

**Second exchange.** Fiske took it, and took it hard, because the model had finished assembling and the model was good.

The first declaration went down eight feet behind Lira's left heel — not at her, behind her, into the place her circling had put her for ninety seconds — and the floor there simply stopped being available, a hard flat wall of delivered force closing an exit that Lira had used four times in the first exchange and would have used a fifth time in the next two seconds. The second went in three beats later on the same principle, and the third finished it, and by the end of the exchange Lira was working in a third of the floor she had started with and every piece of it had been chosen for her.

Cael watched it with something close to awe and wrote the awe down honestly, because a page that only recorded what the friend did was not a page.

*She is not fighting Lira. She is fighting the recording of Lira that the first exchange gave her, and she is right about all of it, and the reason she is right is that Lira gave her a true one.*

**Third exchange.** Lira changed the recording.

It was the four bursts, all of them, inside about eleven seconds, and it was the most expensive thing Cael had ever watched her do, and he knew the cost item by item because it was written in his own hand about his own body.

Four held half-breaths and none of them taken back, which meant that by the third she was working on air she did not have and by the fourth she was working on air nobody had. Four landing beats — four half-breaths of locked commitment at the end of each burst, in front of a practitioner whose entire art was arriving where a person's recovery was going to be, which meant Lira spent four separate opportunities to lose the bout inside eleven seconds and got away with all four. And the hip. He could see it happening from forty feet up, the leading side loading and reloading and loading again, the strain running its bright line from crest to inner knee, and he knew what that felt like at two bursts and had never in his life gone to four.

She used them all on the same thing, which was the answer.

Fiske's declarations arrived where a fighter's recovery was going to be. So Lira stopped recovering where a fighter recovers. Each burst put her somewhere that no honest reading of her weight, her lean, her hip, her committed direction could have predicted — because the displacement was not produced by any of those things; it was produced by a half-body-width of sideways travel her legs had no business making, off a held breath, out of a stance that said the opposite. Four times. Four declarations landing on four correct predictions about a person who was not there.

The fourth one Fiske had to abort mid-commitment, and aborting a Force declaration cost her the whole of it, and the yard heard the cost — a flat, wrong crack of delivered force going into a floor that had not been the target — and made a sound.

**Fourth exchange.** There was nothing left on either of them.

Fiske could not predict, because the thing she was predicting had stopped being predictable, and she knew it, and she did the only thing available to a champion who has lost her instrument: she stopped placing and started delivering. Build, commit, deliver. It was the Force Path every other practitioner in the bracket used and it was, from her, appallingly good, and it was also the first time all season that Fiske had fought anybody with the same tools as everybody else.

Lira had spent four bursts. She had one, at best, and it would be slow, and Cael sat in the gallery doing the arithmetic and arrived at the number about two seconds before she used it.

She did not use it.

She stepped inside a committed delivery on her feet — on her actual feet, on Ardenmere footwork drilled at a foundry-floor circuit for two years by a fighter who had learned early that a burst you can't afford is a burst that isn't there — took the edge of it across the shoulder rather than the center of it across the chest, absorbed a hit that put her a half-step down and cost her the breath she had left, and came up inside Fiske's recovery with the range entirely closed.

It ended there. Four seconds, close, ugly, and completely decided.

The adjudicator called it. The yard did not go quiet this time. The yard came apart.

---

Cael got down to the boards eleven minutes later and found them both there, which he had not expected.

Lira was sitting on the second step with her forearms on her knees, breathing like a person who had been at four bursts eleven minutes ago and would be feeling it for three days. Her leading hip was not going to let her walk properly until the morning after next. She had, Cael noticed, not yet stood up, and was managing that fact with the same evenness she managed everything.

Fiske came over from the water table and stood in front of her, and about sixty people pretended to be looking at the chalk.

"You gave me a false first exchange," Fiske said.

"I gave you a true one," said Lira. "You just assumed it was all of them."

Something happened in Fiske's face. It was not the thing that happens when a champion loses; Cael had seen that eleven times this season and had a page on it. It was closer to what her face had done at the boards in the first month, when Lira had said *you're the one who's been up there two years*, and he had not fully read it then and did read it now.

Relief. It was relief. It had been relief both times.

"Two years I've been Copper Rank 8," Fiske said.

"I know."

"I want to tell you what the crown got me, because nobody told me and I'd have liked to be told." She said it without any particular weight, in the flat voice of a woman handing over a tool. "It got me a better seat in the same bracket. That's all. Two years, two crowns, forty-something wins, and the sheet with my name on it says the same word it said the week I enrolled, and it is going to say that word next year, and the year after, unless somebody with an office decides to spend an afternoon on me, and nobody has, because there has never been one single reason for anybody here to spend it."

Lira did not say anything.

"So win it," Fiske said. "Win the crown. Then make them *look* at you. It's the looking that matters — it was always the looking. The crown's just how you force it." She picked her water cup back up. "I never worked out how to force it. I kept winning and assumed the winning was an argument. It isn't an argument. It's just a result, and a result sits on a board, and boards are where things go to be finished with."

She looked at the column for a moment — her own name, second line now, the brass mark still beside it because the season was not over.

"Six weeks," she said. "I'll see you in the final."

"You will," said Lira.

Fiske went. The sixty people went back to the chalk.

Lira sat on the step for another minute and then got up, which took her a moment, and Cael did not offer her a hand because she would not have taken it in front of the boards and he had known that for two and a half years.

---

She said it to him that night in the common room, word for word, and it took her about forty seconds, and she did not falter anywhere in it.

Cael listened with his hands flat on the table and did not write anything down while she spoke, because writing while she talked was a thing he had done once in Ardenmere and had not done since.

"That's the whole of it?" he said, when she stopped.

"That's the whole of it. She said it once, she didn't repeat any of it, and she wasn't performing." Lira had her leg stretched out along the bench with the hip on the outside, which was as close as she came to admitting anything. "I've heard people say true things before. That was the first time I've heard somebody say a true thing they'd been carrying for two years and had never got to hand to anybody. She'd been waiting for somebody to hand it to."

"And you were the first."

"I was the first person in that bracket who could take it." She said it without vanity, as a fact about the bracket rather than about herself. "That's not a nice thing to work out about a place."

Karis, at the end of the table, had her pen going. "May I have it verbatim for the file?"

"You may have it verbatim if you never once say the word *file* about it again."

"Agreed."

"It's the looking that matters," Lira said, to the room in general. "The crown's just how you force it. Four hundred people in this building and one of them said the true sentence out loud, and she said it eleven minutes after I took her crown off her." She looked at the ceiling for a moment. "I don't know what I'm supposed to do with liking her."

"Nothing," said Brom, from the chair, with the nineteen exercises' sheet folded on his knee and a count in the margin. "You beat her. She told you the truth anyway. That's just a person being a person. You don't have to file it."

"Karis is going to file it."

"Karis files everything. That's *her* being a person."

"Noted," said Karis, writing.

---

He wrote the session's entry that night, and the fragments' standing lines first, because the inventory came before the commentary and always had.

Five confirmed. Nothing moved in any of them. The Iron-adjacent surface read had come back to full speed inside four days of easing off the counting, which was now the third time he had measured the recovery and the third time it had come back at four days, and three instances was structure, and he wrote *confirmed: attention budget refills at four days* in the column that had held it as *probable* for a month.

Then the entry.

*Fifteenth session. She won it in four exchanges and each exchange was more honest than the one before, which I have never seen in a bout before and am going to be thinking about for a year. First: she gave a true reading of a fighter who wasn't fighting. Second: Fiske used the true reading correctly and won the exchange on it. Third: Lira spent four bursts making the true reading false — not by lying, by doing a thing no reading of a body can predict. Fourth: neither of them had anything left but themselves, and Lira had better feet, because two years on a foundry floor is better feet than anything an academy sells.*

*Price of the third exchange, itemized because somebody should: four held half-breaths, none recovered. Four landing beats, each one a free opening against a practitioner whose whole art is finding openings. One leading hip that will not be right until Second-day. One shoulder she took a Force edge on rather than lose the range. Nine minutes of sleep tonight for every one of those, probably.*

*Gain: the top line, a session's more recovery before the final, and a champion who came over eleven minutes later to hand her the only true sentence anybody in this building has said all season.*

*Usefulness: total. And I want to write the ugly half down too, because a ledger that only holds the gains is the ledger the registry keeps.*

*She is going to win the crown. I predicted that in the first week and I have not seen anything since to change it. And on the morning after the final the sheet in Bracken's office is going to say Copper next to her name, exactly as it says today, exactly as it said the day she arrived, and everything she paid in the third exchange today will have bought a circlet of hammered metal and a line on a plate with a hundred and thirty-one years of other people's names on it.*

*Fiske has been at the top of that column for two years and has worked out what it's worth, and told her, and the sentence was: it's the looking that matters, the crown's just how you force it.*

*So the crown isn't the object. The crown is a lever. You put a public, witnessed, undeniable result on a board in front of an institution that has never had a reason to spend an afternoon on you, and you make the reason. That's what the whole season is. That's what the whole season has been since the eleventh morning when I stood on the north stair and watched her look at a word she can't fight.*

He read it back and added one line at the foot, because it had been sitting under everything all day and had earned the ink.

*She was at the boards at the fifth bell. She has been going at the fifth bell and hasn't said so. Six and nothing at the top of the column, and she stood four feet off it on both feet with her breathing managed, which she only does when it's costing her.*

*I didn't go down the stair. I've thought about that for six days now. I think it was right and I'm not sure, and the not-sure is worth writing, because the day I stop being unsure about when to leave somebody alone is the day I've decided I know what people need, and I've met the people who decided that about me.*

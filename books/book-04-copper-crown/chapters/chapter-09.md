# Chapter 9 — The Line

Karis came to find him on a wet Fourth-day in the sixth week of the season, which he registered as unusual before she had said a word, because Karis did not come to find people. She left notes. She had left him eleven notes since the term opened, all of them in the same hand, all of them beginning with a date and a subject line, and he had kept every one of them because they were the most efficient documents anybody had ever written to him.

She came to the library instead, and stood at the end of his carrel with her ledger held against her chest in both arms, which was how she carried a thing she had already decided not to put down.

"I'd like a formal session," she said. "Tonight. All four of us. Two hours, and I want it minuted, and I want you to hear the finding from me before you hear yourself think about it."

Cael put his pen down.

The library's east wing was the part he had come to work in, because it was the coldest and therefore the emptiest, and because the windows on that side gave onto the bluff's river face and the barge traffic below. Rain was going down the glass in long ropes.

"You've found something you don't like," Cael said.

"I've found something I *checked six times*," said Karis, "which is what I do instead of not liking things. Tonight."

---

She did it properly, which was the part he would remember afterward — not the finding, which he had been carrying the shape of for a fortnight without letting himself look at it, but the fact that she had built a whole apparatus of formality to hand it over inside, so that the person receiving it had somewhere to hold.

The common room at the ninth bell. Door shut. Brom in the chair he had claimed in the first fortnight and never surrendered, one boot off, working a thumb into the arch of his foot. Lira on the window seat with her back against the frame and one knee up. Karis at the table with the ledger squared in front of her and a second, thinner notebook beside it that Cael had not seen before.

"Six weeks ago," she said, "Cael began a sustained close-range observational study of a Shadow Path practitioner. I've been documenting the study. Tonight I'm going to tell you what the documentation says, and then I'm going to stop talking, because the rest of it isn't a research question."

She opened the ledger.

"Greyvane, last year, week nine. We ran twenty-two controlled sessions. I declared at demonstration intensity, sustained, under direct observation, with Cael reaching deliberately every time. Twenty-two nulls. I wrote at the time that we were missing a condition, and I was right, and I did not know what it was." She turned a page without looking down at it. "We found out in the match. Sustained observation *plus genuine stakes*. That's the model. Two terms, and the second one is the one everybody gets wrong, because everybody assumes stakes means danger. It doesn't. The Ember-adjacent didn't complete because I was hurting him. It completed because I was *trying*, at full economy, with something real on the outcome, against him, and he had spent six weeks watching me do exactly that."

"Six weeks," Brom said.

"Six weeks of demonstration sessions plus one hour of genuine engagement. That was the ratio that worked." Karis put one finger on the page. "Here is my problem. I have now been keeping the same records about the same practitioner for the same duration, and every column is fuller."

Cael did not say anything. He had known where this was going since the library and had spent the intervening six hours declining to arrive there ahead of her, on the grounds that a conclusion you reach before the evidence is presented is a conclusion you will defend instead of examine.

"Duration," Karis said. "Six weeks and running, against six weeks and closed. Continuous rather than scheduled — he is in the same building at every hour Cael is awake, in Cael's sightlines four to nine times daily by Cael's own count, and the observation is not sessions. It's ambient. It has been ambient since day sixteen."

"Proximity," she said. "Mine was floor-length, formal, across a chalk boundary. This is corridor range. He has stood inside six feet of Cael eleven times that I have a record of, four of them with an office table between them and a filing date being discussed."

"Depth of study." She turned the ledger and pushed it across, and Cael looked at his own notebook's contents reproduced in her hand — the glance count, the two-layer campus map, the fatigue curve with its shoulder at four hours, the failure conditions, *he needs a crowd*. "I have never seen anybody characterize a Path this thoroughly from the outside. Including me. Including my instructors. Cael has a working model of a Shadow Path's economy, its depletion rate, its recovery interval, and three of its failure modes, built in six weeks off a subject who has never once declared in front of him. When I did this to Cael I needed his permission and eight months."

Lira had not moved from the window frame. "And the stakes," she said.

"And the stakes." Karis closed the ledger. "That's the one I checked six times."

She said the next part slowly, and Cael understood that she had written it out beforehand and was reciting from memory, because she wanted the words to be the ones she had chosen rather than the ones the room produced.

"The stakes condition has never required combat. I want that on the record, because I'm the one who wrote the model and I know what I wrote. What the twenty-two nulls established is that intensity is not sufficient. What the match established is that *earnest engagement against him* is. The distinction is not violence. It's whether the other party is genuinely trying to accomplish something at his expense." She looked up. "Seln is running an intelligence operation against Cael's enrollment, on Compact instruction, at full professional economy, and has been for six weeks, and it is aimed at his file, his standing, his provision, and every door any of us walked through this year. There is nothing in this building more adversarial. There is nothing in his life more adversarial. A man in a bout wants to win an afternoon. This one is trying to answer the question that ends him."

Nobody said anything for a while. Below the window, somebody in the quadrangle shouted something about a wet coat and somebody else laughed.

"So," Karis said, in an entirely different voice, "the finding."

She opened the thin notebook, which turned out to hold one page of writing and a great deal of white.

"The conditions are met. If you directed it, I believe it would complete." She set her pen down beside the page. "Which is why we have to talk about whether you may."

---

Cael found, sitting there, that the first thing he had to do was manage his own face, and that managing it was harder than it had been in a year.

Not because of the finding. He had assembled the finding himself, in pieces, across a fortnight, and had recognized it at about the third piece and had then done the thing he did with conclusions he did not want: he had stopped adding pieces. It was, he thought — and he made himself think it plainly, because there was no point in a private honesty that only ran when it was comfortable — the exact failure the third column existed to prevent. *Discarded* was a column for hypotheses that the evidence had killed. It was not a column for hypotheses you had declined to test because you did not want the answer, and he had used it as one for fourteen days, and Karis had walked in with the answer anyway.

The second thing was the arithmetic, which arrived unasked and would not go away.

A sixth fragment. Not another variation on things he already had — not another way to move or absorb or read, all of which he had in five flavors and had priced to the last held breath. A *Path he had no version of at all*. Presence-suppression. Movement-masking. The single capability that his entire life, from the Denvash assessment hall onward, had been shaped by not having: the ability to be, for some duration, at some cost, unwatched.

He was fifteen years old and had been under observation, in one grade or another, since he was twelve.

"You want it," Lira said.

"Yes," Cael said.

"Good. Say that part out loud again in about an hour when we're all being clever."

---

They took the two hours. It was the longest the four of them had ever spent on a single question and it was, Cael thought afterward, the best of them.

Karis went first, because it was her model and because she had a position she had prepared and did not enjoy.

"I consented," she said. "In writing. On terms I set. I chose the intensity, I chose the date, I told him what I wanted out of it, and when it worked I spent a fortnight finding out what it had cost me and I told him that too." She had her hands flat on the table. "Seln cannot consent. He will not be asked, because asking would end him — a covered officer who is told what his subject can do is an officer who now has to report it or become a liability, and either outcome destroys him. So the question isn't whether he'd agree. It's whether the impossibility of asking makes it worse or makes it moot, and I have been unable to decide that in six days, which for me is a very long time."

"He'd report it," Brom said.

"He'd have to."

"So there's no version where he knows."

"There is no version where he knows and survives knowing," said Karis. "Which is a different sentence and I want it kept different, because the day we start saying *he can't be told* when what we mean is *we've decided not to tell him*, we've begun lying to ourselves in a register that gets very hard to hear."

Lira came at it from the other side, and did it in the way she did most things, which was to state a position with total conviction and then attack it herself before anyone else could.

"Taking from an enemy isn't the same as taking from a friend," she said. "That's obvious. That's so obvious it's barely worth the breath. He's here to end you. He gets paid to be here. Nobody made him take the assignment and nobody's making him stay on it, and if you take something off a man who's aiming a weapon at you, that's not theft, that's the *floor*."

She stopped. She rolled her shoulder against the window frame, which was what she did instead of pacing when there was nowhere to pace.

"I hate how good that felt to say," she said.

Cael waited.

"It came out too smooth." Lira looked at the room generally and at nobody in it. "I've been saying versions of that sentence in my head for a week and every time it comes out better than the time before. That's not how true things behave. True things get more complicated the longer you hold them. That one's getting *simpler*, which means I'm sanding it, and I don't know what I'm sanding off." She jerked her chin at Karis. "So put that down as well. Not the sentence. The other thing."

Karis wrote for a moment. "'Position stated with declining resistance over time — flagged by holder as suspect.'"

"Fine."

"It's a good flag," said Karis. "I'd like to be able to raise it about myself and I can't."

Brom had said almost nothing for an hour. He got the other boot off, set it beside the first, and sat forward with his forearms on his knees, and the room turned toward him the way it always did, because Brom did not speak until he had something to set down and everybody in that room had learned it in Ardenmere.

"You're all arguing about whether it's a demonstration," he said.

Cael looked up.

"When Karis did it, she stood in front of you and showed you her Path, on purpose, so you could take a piece. That's a gift. Everything you're worried about is worry about a gift — did she mean it, did she know the price, could she take it back." He worked his knuckles once, slowly. "This isn't that. He's not showing you anything. He's *using* it against you. Every day. He's used it on you five hundred times since he got here and every single one of them was aimed at your file."

He shrugged, which on Brom was a whole paragraph.

"That's not a demonstration you're stealing. That's a weapon you're learning."

Nobody answered it.

"I've had every fighter in Rooke's cohort take my flaw apart for six weeks," Brom said. "Every one of them learned something off me while they were doing it. I learned more off them. Nobody asked anybody. That's what happens when people work against each other in earnest — the working *is* the teaching, and it goes both directions whether either side wants it to or not." He sat back. "If he didn't want to be studied, he shouldn't have come here to study Cael. He's a professional. He knows what close range costs. He's the only person in this building who knows it better than Cael does."

"That," said Karis, writing rapidly, "is the strongest version of the argument that has been made tonight and I want it minuted in his words, not mine."

"It's not an argument," Brom said. "It's just what's happening."

---

Cael took the last of the two hours, and did not use most of it, because what he had to say had gone hard and clear somewhere around Brom's second sentence and needed only to be said in the right order.

"There's a version of this I won't do," he said. "I want to build that one first, because the rest is easier once it's outside the fence."

He laid three fingers on the table, one at a time.

"I will not manufacture the conditions. I won't engineer a situation that puts him in earnest use against me at close range in order to complete an acquisition. That's the whole of it, and it rules out about six ideas I've already had and discarded, and I'm listing them so they can't come back wearing hats." He said them flatly, one after another: forcing a confrontation to make the man run; leaking a false capability to escalate the operation; walking a route at night that would require the movement-masking half; standing where the suppression would have to work hardest and holding still until it did. "All six are available. All six would probably work. All six are me deciding to make a person into an experiment, which is what everyone who has ever looked at me has wanted to do, and I am not going to be the first one in my life to actually do it to somebody."

Lira, on the window seat, said nothing at all, which from Lira was applause.

"Second," Cael said. "If the operation itself puts it in earnest use against me — at close range, at real stakes, on his own initiative, in the ordinary running of the thing he was sent here to do — then I will not refuse what the engagement offers. I'll reach. Deliberately. Knowing."

Karis's pen stopped.

"Say the rest," she said.

"I'm saying the rest." He turned to face her properly. "The rest is that I don't get to call that clean. He can't consent. He'll never know. There's no version where I earn it, no version where I ask, no version where I pay him back in a currency he can accept without it ending his career. That's a cost. It's a cost to *him*, which is the one that matters, and it's a cost to me, which is the one I can actually account for. And I'm going to write it down in advance — the whole of it, before, not after — including the part where I wanted it, because if I write it after, I'll write it as something that happened to me."

"You would," Lira said. "You did the last time."

"I know I did." He had gone back and read it, in fact, three nights ago: the Greyvane entry, the one written the morning after the match, and the way its sentences arranged themselves around a subject who had been overtaken by his own architecture in a moment of genuine peril. Every word of it had been true. The arrangement had been a lie, and he had not noticed at the time, and had noticed now, at fifteen, sitting at a Halcenvane library carrel in the rain, which was the least comfortable and most useful thing that had happened to him that month.

"So," Karis said. "Minuted. Conditions not to be manufactured. Reach permitted only inside engagement he initiates. Full advance accounting, written before the fact." She read it back once and then looked up. "You understand that I can't tell you whether it will complete. The model predicts. It doesn't promise. Every condition may be present and the thing may simply not happen, and if it doesn't, I won't be able to tell you which condition was insufficient, because I can't run the control."

"I know."

"I want that in the minute too. 'Researcher notes the prediction is untested at one instance.'" She wrote it. "I dislike this whole conversation intensely and I'd like that minuted as well."

"Put mine down," said Brom.

"You didn't state a position."

"I did. It's the one about the weapon." He stretched his back until something in it moved. "And add that I'd have taken it off him in week two and not lost a night over it, and that I know Cael won't, and that I think the difference between us is the reason he's the one it happens to."

Karis wrote for some time.

---

He did not sleep, and at about the third hour he stopped pretending he was going to, and got up and tried to solve it the only way he knew.

The compound gaze was three layers deep and four columns wide by then and he had built every inch of it himself. Rhythm, weight, breath, and the fourth pass where the three either agreed or did not. Confirmed, probable, discarded, and the third column kept honestly because a discarded finding written down as discarded could not sneak back in disguised as a fresh idea. The watch-count rule underneath all of it: one is an accident, two is a coincidence, three is structure. He had used it on eleven Paths, on a hundred and forty opponents, on a fruit vendor's credit ledger, on a copying office, on a man who did not leak.

He sat on the edge of the cot with the notebook on his knees and ran it on the decision.

*Price:* the source cannot consent; the source will never know he paid; the debt has no settlement mechanism and will not acquire one; the acquisition cannot be recorded anywhere institutional and therefore cannot be defended if it is ever alleged; integration cost, unknown magnitude, precedent one instance.

*Gain:* presence-suppression. Movement-masking. Contact-to-short range, if the observed economy held. Against an apparatus that had watched him continuously for two years and would watch him for the rest of his life.

*Usefulness:* he got two lines into that column and stopped, because the two lines were *incalculable* and *do not write incalculable in a column, it is a word for people who have stopped counting*.

Then he read the three columns back and understood, with a flat cold clarity that arrived at about the fourth hour, that the table was worthless.

Every entry in it was his. He had chosen the categories, chosen what went in them, chosen what to weigh a thing at. There was no opponent in this. That was the whole architecture of the method and he had never once seen it from the outside until now: the compound gaze worked because a body under load could not stop leaking, because rhythm and weight and breath were expensive to fake and impossible to fake in agreement, because the *subject fought back*. Every reading he had ever trusted, he trusted because something outside him had produced it and he had merely been disciplined enough to write it down.

A table about what he owed a man had nothing outside him in it at all. It leaked nothing. It could not disagree with itself. It would return, every time, forever, in whatever detail he chose to build it to, exactly the answer he had already decided he wanted, and it would return that answer *in his own handwriting, in columns, with a discarded section, looking for all the world like evidence.*

He wrote that finding down, because it was the largest thing he had learned in a month and it was not about Seln at all.

*The method has no instrument for this. It never did. I have been running an opponent-reading apparatus on a question with no opponent in it and calling the output data.*

*Note the failure honestly: it is not that the table gave me a wrong answer. It gave me the right one — I'm going to reach, and I think I should. The failure is that it would have given me that answer whatever I put in it, and I would have believed it either way, and that is the exact machinery by which a careful person does something indefensible with a full set of notes.*

*What replaces it: nothing does. There isn't a column for this. So the rule has to be procedural instead of analytical — write the decision down before, in front of the three people who will read it back to me, and let them be the part of the instrument that isn't me.*

He looked at that for a while and then added one more line under it, because it was true and because leaving it out would have been the first lie in the notebook.

*Three of them argued for two hours and every one of them ended up on my side. I have not decided yet whether that means they were right or whether it means I have gotten very good at building rooms.*

---

He went down to hall one at the first bell because there was no point in lying in the dark any longer, and the residence porter let him in on the provision allocation without comment, and he had the floor to himself for forty minutes, which happened perhaps twice a term.

He worked the Compression-adjacent, because it was the one he had least data on and because it required nothing but a wall.

The frame in hall one's north corner was a drop-frame of the same pattern as the assessment wing's, three weights, a rail. He set it at the middle weight and stood inside the marked square and took it on the left forearm, eleven times, at four-breath intervals, counting.

The gathering came nothing like the Pressure-adjacent's slow load. Pressure gathered — most of a full breath, weight sinking, stance widening, the whole thing announcing itself to anyone watching. Compression did not gather. It *arrived*, at the instant of contact, a hard bright reorganization along the bone from wrist to elbow as the incoming force stopped being one arriving thing and became a spreading one, out through the forearm, across the shoulder, down the flank, into the floor through both heels. Half a breath, no silhouette at all. And afterward a deep, slightly sickening ache in the long bones that came up a minute late and stayed two hours, and a shake in the fingers that made writing untidy for about the same.

Eleven repetitions at the middle weight. Nine clean. The tenth spread late and he felt the difference at once — force arriving as a *shape* rather than a spread, a flat line of impact along the ulna that was going to be a bruise by the afternoon. The eleventh was worse.

*Nine,* he wrote, on the floor, with his hand doing its shake. *Ceiling is nine at the middle weight when I've had no sleep; it was eleven in the third week of term. That's not the fragment, that's me — the framework is stable and the operator is a fifteen-year-old who has been awake twenty-six hours because a research finding frightened him.*

*Price per repetition: half a breath at contact, no telegraph, forearm ache one minute delayed and running two hours, unsteady hand for writing. Gain: force that should have staggered me went into the floor.*

*Usefulness: total, and cheap, and quiet, which is why it is the fragment I reach for without deciding to. Watch that. A tool you reach for without deciding to is a tool that will one day be reached for in a room where reaching is the mistake.*

He put the frame back on its pin, wiped the rail, and went to breakfast with his arm aching in the bone and his hand not steady on the cup, and found the whole of it obscurely steadying — that some things still had prices you could feel by lunchtime.

---

He wrote the entry that night in the Power Log, and it ran longer than any entry he had written since Greyvane, and he did not cut it.

*Karis's finding, in full, with her model and her caveat and the six checks. Minuted separately by her — I have a copy. Read it before reading this.*

*What I decided, in the order I decided it:*

*One. I will not manufacture the conditions. Six methods available, all listed in the minute, all refused, all written down so they can't come back in a different coat.*

*Two. If the operation puts it in earnest use against me at close range, on his initiative, in the ordinary running of what he was sent to do — I will reach. Directed. Awake. Not overtaken.*

*Three. He cannot consent. He will never know. There is no mechanism by which I can pay him and there is not going to be one. That is a cost and it belongs to him, and I am choosing it for both of us, which is the entire thing I have hated about every person who has ever had authority over me.*

*Four. I want it. Not the fragment — the thing the fragment is. I have never in my life been able to not be seen. I would like to be able to not be seen. Writing that down is the point of this entry; everything above it is reasoning and reasoning is cheap and I have four columns' worth of practice at making it come out where I want.*

*Last year's me was afraid of not-knowing what I do. This year's version is worse: knowing exactly, and deciding anyway. Write it down before, not after. That's the whole difference between me and the system.*

He read it back once, changed nothing, and sat looking at the page with the lamp guttering and his forearm aching in the bone.

Then he added the last line, in smaller writing, at the foot of the page, where he put the things he did not want to lose and did not want to look at either.

*Karis says an instrument doesn't change sides. It changes readings. I wrote that down because it sounded like wisdom, and I have been turning it over since, and I think it's the truest sentence anybody said tonight and I hope very much that it's wrong.*

---

The season's ninth session ran on a clearing Seventh-day, and Cael went to the Crown yard at the fifth bell as he had gone to every session since the boards posted, and took the same bench on the same side of the same tier.

The rain had gone off in the night and the afternoon came in low and gold across the bluff, the way it did in that month when the light cleared late, and by the sixth bell the yard's western tiers were in full sun and its eastern ones were not.

He watched a Copper bout that told him nothing he did not have and made his notes anyway. He watched Ephram, in the Iron column's afternoon, take a bout apart in three exchanges and explain it afterward at the rail to four of Rooke's cohort in that low level voice that carried further than it meant to. He watched Fiske read the standings board with her hands behind her back and not once look at the floor.

And at the seventh bell, when the sun came round far enough onto the upper west tier to put a bar of hard light across it, the cluster of six that had been standing there for an hour broke up and re-formed, the way a crowd does when the light changes and half of it decides it would rather be somewhere else — and a man with an armful of forms went with the drift, and stopped four bodies further along, on the tier's shaded end, third in a cluster of five.

Cael did not look directly at him. He had not looked directly at him in five weeks.

He worked out the new position's sightlines from where he sat, because that was now simply what his attention did when it was left alone, and found that the man had given up about a fifth of the floor and gained both stair heads and the whole of the assessment wing's approach, and had done it inside eleven seconds, without appearing to have made a decision, in a crowd that had made the same movement for entirely different reasons and had absorbed him into it perfectly.

*Optimum,* Cael thought. *Again. Twenty-something for twenty-something. He is the best working practitioner of anything I have ever stood near, and the thing he practises is not being here, and he has been doing it eleven meters from me all season without one error I have been able to find.*

And under it, arriving whole, in the flat register the notebook used for facts:

*And if his operation ever needs that at close range against me, in earnest, I am going to take a piece of it, and he will walk away from the conversation not knowing he was ever in one.*

The light moved another finger's width along the tier. The man shifted, half a pace, into the cluster's shadow, and became furniture.

Cael turned back to the floor, where two Copper-bracket practitioners were being sorted by a system that had never once measured either of them, and wrote down what he saw.

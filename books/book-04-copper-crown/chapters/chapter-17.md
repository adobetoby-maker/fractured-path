# Chapter 17 — Archmarshal

He read the adjudication transcript for the fourth time on the last morning of the road, with the shutter down against the dust and the case open on the seat beside him, and he read it in the order the panel had received it rather than the order that would have been convenient.

That was the discipline, and it was forty years old, and it had cost him whole weeks over the course of a career. Primary record first, complete, in sequence, including the procedural matter and the two adjournments and the quarter-hour of argument about whether a document could be entered without its cover sheet. Then the exhibits, in the order they had been tendered, which was almost never the order a summarizer would choose, because a summarizer chooses the order that makes the finding legible and the finding was the last thing he wanted made legible for him. Then, and only then, the product of other officers: the monitoring summaries, the field reports, the collated observations of men and women who had been paid to watch and had written down what they thought they saw.

He read that last, and once, and he had a phrase for why, which he had said aloud perhaps six times in his life and written down never.

*Observer product is testimony about the observer.*

It was not contempt. He had been an observer. It was arithmetic: a report told you what one person had been able to notice, from where they had stood, with the instructions they had been given, and every one of those three was a limit, and the limits went into the paper along with the facts and could not afterward be separated out. A report was a reading taken through an instrument whose calibration you did not hold. You could use it. You could not build on it.

So the appendix of monitoring filings from the receiving institution — six sheets, compiled, indexed in a fourth handwriting, covering an entire term of embedded coverage on a provision-status enrollee — he read on the second morning of the road, in nine minutes, and set down, and did not annotate. Complete in form. Correct in appendix. A term of a competent officer's product, telling him what a competent officer had been positioned to see, which was a fact about the officer's position.

He put it back in the file and went on to the exhibits.

The transcript itself he had now been through four times and had marked in four passes, and the marks were the only part of his method he had ever been mildly self-conscious about, because they were all questions. Forty years of files, and there was a drawer somewhere in the registry's lower vault holding two thousand of his margins, and a man could go through the entire drawer without finding six conclusions in it.

*Why concede the category?* — beside session two, where the respondent, a boy of fifteen with no counsel and three weeks' preparation, had stood in front of a panel and given away, unforced, the single point everyone in the room had expected him to fight for.

*Who taught him the schedule's structure?* — beside session four, where the same boy had walked a hearing through an enumerated list, amendment by amendment, and had understood, in a way that practitioners with a decade of registry law behind them routinely did not, that a closed enumeration is closed in both directions.

*Verify: three weeks' preparation — from what baseline?* — twice underlined, which for him was not emphasis but an instruction to himself, meaning *this is the load-bearing question and you will be tempted to accept the obvious answer.*

Three weeks was in the record. Three weeks was uncontested; the delegation itself had entered the date. Which meant that either the record was wrong about the three weeks, or something in the preceding two years had built a fifteen-year-old who could use three weeks like that, and neither the file nor the two years of monitoring summaries contained one sentence describing what.

That was the gap. He had found it on the first reading and had spent three subsequent readings failing to close it, and it was, in the flat professional sense, the most interesting gap he had encountered in perhaps six years.

He did not have a theory. He had been taught not to, at twenty-three, by a man whose instruction had outlasted every other thing about him, in one sentence delivered across a table in a room that had smelled of wet stone: *an evaluation that begins from its conclusion is a report about the evaluator.* He had heard it, and had not understood it, and had understood it four years later in a bad month after ending a career — his own, nearly — on a finding he had assembled backward from a suspicion he had liked.

He was sixty now. The left hand had opinions in cold weather and had begun, in the last two winters, to want the pen held differently, which he had accommodated without comment, because a man who argues with his own hands writes worse. He had come up a bluff road to look at a mid-tier academy that had done something unusual and lawful.

He had filed no question. That was the truth of it, and he was aware that nobody on the bluff would believe him, and he had long ago stopped spending effort on the gap between what he was doing and what people assumed he was doing, because the gap was information too, and it belonged to them.

The carriage came off the river road and started up.

---

An inspection, from an enrollee's altitude, was mostly furniture moving.

Cael had expected that and had still not expected how completely it would be true. For two days the delegation was a set of doors that were now closed at hours when they had been open — the records hall's back room, the second-floor conference space above the lecture complex, the assessment wing's document store — and a pattern of clerks in the covered walk carrying boxes in one direction in the morning and the other direction at the fourth bell. That was all. Eleven people had come up a hill and the building had absorbed them the way a body absorbs a splinter, which is to say by building a shape around it and going on.

He went to his sessions. He went to his lectures. He submitted his coursework on the day it was due and not the day before it, because a boy who suddenly becomes early is a boy who has started performing, and Withrow had used nine minutes of a five-hundred-person hall to explain that a performance was the only thing an inspection could actually detect.

And he built the page, because that was what he had instead of a bracket.

He had eleven names, nine of which he had finished in the winter out of the library's holdings, and by the second afternoon he had two of the remaining three in workable shape from the floor. The counsel was a nineteen-year enrollment-basis practitioner with a habit of attacking provenance before scope, and Cael had her rhythm off four minutes of the covered walk on the first morning: she talked while walking, she stopped talking when she read, and she resumed mid-sentence from exactly where she had stopped, which meant the reading and the talking used different rooms in her head and neither interrupted the other. That was a useful thing to know about a woman who was going to spend a day taking a filing cabinet apart.

The senior clerk of the three was left-handed and had a bad knee and did not carry boxes, which meant the clerk who carried boxes was junior, which meant the box order in the covered walk told him the delegation's working sequence a half-day before it happened.

Assessor Ilsev he could not build a page on and did not need one, because he had six published findings and had read them all twice and could have recited the shape of her: she read the text, she went where the text went, she did not want anything from it. He watched her cross the covered walk on the second morning carrying her own case, and she looked at nothing except the door she was going to, and he wrote *unchanged from the findings; she is exactly the person the papers describe, which is itself the rarest thing on the manifest.*

Havel was the strange one, and Cael did not enjoy discovering how strange.

They passed at a dozen feet on the second afternoon, in the walk, going opposite directions, with about forty people between the walls. Havel had a document case under one arm and a clerk half a pace behind him and did not look up, and Cael watched the whole approach and the whole passage with the compound gaze wide open and got a clean, complete reading of a records officer walking to a records session.

Except for the shoulder.

At about nine feet the man's near shoulder came down and forward by perhaps an inch and stayed there through the pass, and it was not the shoulder-drop of a committed strike or of avoidance, and Cael spent the rest of the afternoon working out what it was and then wrote it down without liking it: *he made room. In a corridor with four feet of clearance on his own side, at the moment we passed, he gave me an inch he did not owe me. Nobody does that for an enrollee. That's what you do for someone in a doorway you'd rather not be in.*

*Four years. Two grades. He's still doing the thing he did in Ardenmere, which is to be as decent as his instructions permit and not one inch further. I've never been able to decide whether to be grateful for that. I've decided today: yes. An inch of clearance from a man who isn't allowed to give you a word is an inch, and I've had worse from people who were allowed to give me everything.*

That was two names on the second day. The eleventh he did not attempt, because attempting it from the floor required being on a floor the man was standing beside, and that came on the third day.

---

The facility review was chartered as a walk-through and it turned out to mean, in practice, that the delegation went and stood in every room the academy used and watched it be used.

Cael had a supervised session in hall three at the fourth bell on the fourteenth and had known for four days that the facility review was on the fourteenth, and had made the deliberate decision not to change one thing about it. Same hall, same hour, same partner, same three-part sequence he had been running since the eleventh week of term with the wing observer at the north rail writing it down.

He had also, four days out, done the arithmetic on the thing underneath, and it was worse than the session.

Three hours in front of a panel on the twentieth was the problem he had spent the last month rebuilding his whole plan around, and this was not that; this was fifty minutes on a training floor. But it was fifty minutes on a training floor with the highest-graded person on the continent standing at the gallery rail, and the fatigue curve did not care what the occasion was called. Holding the idle state at ordinary cost the same low continuous rate whether he was reading in the library or fighting for his enrollment, and it was a rate he paid out of the same account the compound gaze spent from, and the account had a floor.

So he went in having slept nine hours, which he had done on purpose, and having eaten at the second bell rather than the third, and having decided in advance which of the three parts of the sequence he would let go slack if the account ran low.

The delegation came in just past the fourth bell, seven of them, and arranged themselves along the gallery rail at the north end, and Cael did not look up, and knew where every one of them was standing inside ten seconds because the floor's noise changed shape.

Then he worked.

Brom was the partner, and Brom knew, and Brom did the one useful thing available, which was to be exactly as unpleasant as he was every other week. The sequence was a redirect drill at three-quarter intensity — Brom hardening a forearm and driving it in on a four-count while Cael took the contact on an angle and turned it down into the floor, thirty repetitions each side, the wing observer counting the clean ones. It was the dullest work in the building and it was the work that had made his last two months, and by the eighth repetition the whole of his attention wanted to be inside it, which was exactly the condition Brom had put in the right column for him in a corridor four weeks ago.

*Attention fully occupied.* That was the drift condition. That was the trap.

He held his own edges the entire fifty minutes, and he could describe afterward precisely what it cost, because he had built a format for it.

It cost the drill.

Not visibly. The wing observer's count came out at fifty-four clean of sixty, which was inside his ordinary band and two above his worst week. But Cael knew what he had done to get there, and it was this: he had run the redirect on structure instead of on read. He had stopped taking the surface pressure off Brom's forearm a half-beat before contact — the Iron-adjacent read at working depth, the thing that had made this drill worth doing at all — because the read at that depth and the idle state drew on the same account, had drawn on it since the fragment's first week, and the account had a floor. The read's cheap ambient layer went on reporting the room, because that layer cost nothing and had never once cost anything; the reach into a forearm was the part with a price on it, and with seven of the Compact's senior staff at the rail there had been exactly one of those two disciplines he was permitted to let go.

So he had dropped the useful one and kept the invisible one, and had done a Copper-baseline redirect drill at Copper baseline, fifty-four times, correctly, in front of people who had come four days up a river road to see what he was.

Brom stopped him at repetition thirty-one on the second side, out of turn, and shook out his hand, and said, at the volume of a man complaining about his own wrist, "Read's off."

"Read's off," Cael agreed.

"On purpose."

"Yes."

Brom rolled the shoulder and reset. "Then it's the right kind of off," he said, and hit him again.

Afterward, in the corridor, with the tremor in his forearms that came off contact work and the other tremor, further in, that came off the account being low, Cael wrote it up standing against a wall because sitting down would have felt like an event.

*Working ledger — idle state, day eighty.*

*Gain: nothing. There is no gain. This is a maintenance cost and it produces no capability whatsoever; the entire product of fifty minutes of it is that nothing happened.*

*Cost, itemized honestly: (1) the continuous rate, which I now price at about what the compound gaze costs at half extension, running the whole time. (2) The account — the read never came up past its ambient layer the whole session, because the wick and the read at depth spend from the same account and the account has a floor. Fifty minutes of the drill that exists to train the read, run on the stub of it. That's the real bill and it will be the real bill on the twentieth. (3) After, about ninety minutes where reading anything is unpleasant. Pressure behind both eyes, the currency the compound gaze spends.*

*Useful where: nowhere, still, at eighty days. It is not a capability. It is rent.*

*Fails where: it did not fail today. Writing that down because in six weeks I will want to know whether the good days were real.*

He read it back and added one line and then went and stood outside for a while in the cold because the last line had made his hands unsteady.

*Fifty minutes cost me the read. The twentieth is three hours.*

---

He had not looked up once at the rail.

That was deliberate, and it cost him the only chance he was going to get at the eleventh page, and he had decided in advance that it was worth it, and he changed his mind about that within about an hour, which he also wrote down.

What he had instead was the floor's noise, and the floor's noise was information.

Seven bodies at a rail thirty feet above a working hall produce a pattern, and the pattern is made of shifting weight and shoes on stone and the small constant adjustments of people standing still for a long time in a place they did not choose. Cael had been reading galleries from underneath since Ardenmere. He had the seven of them located inside ten seconds and had them sorted by the end of the first side.

Six of them moved. Not much, not badly — a delegation at a rail is a professional object and does not fidget — but six of them shifted weight on a rhythm of somewhere between forty seconds and two minutes, and twice in fifty minutes the whole rail resettled at once, which is what a group of people does when the person in the middle has said something.

The seventh did not move at all.

Not once, in fifty minutes. No weight change, no step, no hand on the rail and off it. Cael, running a redirect drill with his eyes on a forearm, tracked the absence of a sound for the better part of an hour and got, from the whole of it, the following: *he stood still,* which was not a finding, because standing still for fifty minutes was something a man might do out of discipline or out of stiffness or because his knees were sixty years old.

And the man had asked not one question. That came from the wing observer's board afterward, and from Bracken's clerk, and from the assessment wing's own log, all three of which recorded delegation queries during a chartered facility review because delegation queries during a chartered facility review were the entire point of the exercise. Counsel had asked eleven. Ilsev had asked four, all of them about the observer's recording protocol and none about the enrollee being observed. The records officer had asked two.

The eleventh name had asked nothing, in the assessment wing, in the lecture complex, in the residence quadrangles, in the fire-watch's storeroom, or on any of the three training floors.

Cael sat with that in the library until the seventh bell and got exactly one line out of it, and the line was not about Vastin.

*A man who asks no questions in a building is either not interested or already has his questions and has decided the building can't answer them. I've been the second one in four archives. I know what it looks like from inside and I have never once considered what it looks like from outside, and the answer is: it looks like nothing at all, which is why I've been able to do it.*

---

The Copper bracket's placement rounds went on underneath the inspection, because the season did not stop for anybody, and on the evening of the thirteenth there was a fifth-through-eighth placement bout in the Crown yard between a Force Path third-year and a Shield Path fourth-year that had no bearing on the final and every bearing on next year's seeding, and about a hundred and forty people came to watch it.

Vastin came at the second exchange and left in the fourth.

Cael saw the arrival because Cael had been in the gallery for fifty minutes before the bout started, which was where he had been for every ladder session of the season. He saw the departure because he had spent the whole of the third exchange with a third of his attention on the north tier instead of the floor, which he was going to be annoyed about for two days.

The man came up the stair, found a place at the rail among about thirty other people, watched two full exchanges and the opening of a third, and then went down the stair while the fourth was still running, before the adjudicating faculty had raised a hand, before the result, before anything the bout was for.

Cael took an hour on it.

He took it properly, in the library, with the notebook open and the three columns, and the first twenty minutes were wasted on the obvious answers, which were that the man had somewhere to be, or was bored, or had left in order to be seen leaving. He struck all three: an inspection at that grade did not have a schedule that ran to the minute in a public gallery; boredom is a thing you can read from a body and there had been nothing to read; and a man who wanted to be seen leaving would not have gone down the stair on the far side at the moment a hundred and forty people were looking at the floor.

The answer, when it came, arrived the way answers arrived — sideways, out of a thing he already knew and had failed to apply.

He had spent six months in a gallery watching bouts he was not permitted to enter, and in those months he had developed a habit that had embarrassed him the first time he noticed it: he sometimes stopped watching the fight. He stopped watching the fight and watched the adjudicators instead — how they were positioned, when the safety faculty's hand came up, whether the panel called the exchanges at the same threshold in the fourth round as in the first. Because a bout told you about two fighters and a *yard* told you about an institution, and he had come to Halcenvane with a question about the institution.

Vastin had watched two exchanges and the opening of a third.

Two exchanges was enough to establish that the faculty panel was calling the same thresholds both fighters were fighting to. The opening of the third was where you found out whether the panel adjusted when a bout started going one way. The result told you which student was better, and the man had not come to a mid-tier academy's placement round to find out which of two third-years was better.

*He wasn't there for the result,* Cael wrote, and then sat looking at it, because that was the finding and there was a second thing under it he did not want to write and wrote anyway.

*He got what he came for in about six minutes and left, and the six minutes were spent on procedure, not on people, and I know that because it is exactly what I would have done and exactly what I have done, twice, in this yard, this season, from a seat forty feet from where he was standing.*

---

Magister Gault gave him one exchange on the fifteenth and Cael would carry it for a month.

It was at the assessment wing's counter, at the second bell, over a floor allocation — the ordinary transaction, the one Cael had performed perhaps ninety times. Gault came through the back with a folder under his arm, saw him, stopped, and delivered it in the register he delivered everything in, which had never once in a full term risen or fallen.

"The delegation reviewed my wing yesterday," he said. "For the record, since you were on the floor for part of it and will hear a version: the senior officer asked me three questions. All three concerned the calibration interval on the resistance posts. He asked me nothing about you."

He went through to the back before Cael had assembled a response, which was how Gault ended most exchanges, and which Cael had stopped taking personally in the second month.

He wrote it up that evening.

*Gault told me this because Gault does not permit rumour to live in his wing, and because he has understood since the first month that the thing I actually need from him is accurate information about my own evaluation, and he supplies it at exactly the temperature he supplies everything else.*

*Three questions about the calibration interval on the resistance posts.*

*Here is what that is. The resistance post is the apparatus my whole demonstration runs on. Calibration interval is how often it's checked and by whom and against what. A man who asks three questions about the calibration interval is not asking about the apparatus. He is asking how much the number the apparatus produces is worth.*

*He isn't going to watch me on the twentieth. He's going to watch the instrument, and then he's going to watch me, and the number that comes out the far end will already have been priced before I set foot on the floor.*

*Everyone else who has ever evaluated me started with me. This one started with the ruler.*

---

The teaching assistant in Gault's office processed the floor allocation on the sixteenth without comment, checked the title line and the date, made two marks in the wing's ledger, and slid it back across the counter, and the whole transaction took eleven seconds and contained nothing.

Cael had six months of that now. He had a working page on it. The page said: *the null is the message, and the message has not changed since midwinter, and the day it changes I will need to know it inside an hour.*

It had not changed. Seln was at the copying table when Cael came in and at the copying table when he left, which was where a man was supposed to be, and there was a sealed compilation case on the shelf behind him with a wing index on its face that had gone out to the delegation on the thirteenth and come back on the fifteenth with a receipt stamp and no annotation of any kind.

Cael saw the receipt stamp from four feet away, upside down, in the second it took the case to move from a clerk's hands to a shelf, and he read what it meant with a clarity that stopped him in the doorway for a full second.

Somebody at the delegation's altitude had read six months of reports on him and had written nothing on them.

He turned that against the other thing he knew — that the reports themselves were true, complete in form, and contained approximately nothing, because he had watched a training-floor incident get filed as *response consistent with documented baseline* by a man who had been eleven meters away with a clear line — and got, for the first time all week, a genuine cold feeling in the back of his neck.

*Two men,* he wrote that night. *One of them files true, empty sentences about me and has done since midwinter. The other one reads them and doesn't write on them.*

*I have spent six months certain that the first one is protecting me and I still believe it. What I hadn't held properly until today is that the protection only works if the second one keeps not writing on them. And I have no page on the second one. I have four days of standing near him and one absence of a shoulder-shift at a rail and a facility review with no questions in it.*

*The whole of it rests on a man I cannot read.*

---

He went up to the Crown yard's upper tier on the sixteenth evening, alone, at the hour when the yard was empty and the light off the river came up the bluff the color of weak tea, and he wrote the week's entry there rather than at the window, because the window had half a year of ordinary entries in it and this one did not feel ordinary.

Below, the yard was being swept. Two porters, one broom each, working from the center out, the way they did it every evening, so that the tiers stayed clean and the floor got done twice.

*Four watchers, four grades, and I have finally got the taxonomy,* he wrote.

*Coss enforced. That's what a warden is; the coat announces it, the posture announces it, and he told me what he wanted in every room he was ever in, which is why I could read him inside a week at fourteen years old with nothing but a fruit vendor's stall for practice.*

*Havel complied. He watched because he was told to and he made an inch of room in a corridor because that was the whole of what he had left to give.*

*Seln operates. Position, economy, suppression rhythm — a craft, aimed, and I found it in six days and have spent six months learning it from the inside because he let me.*

*This one evaluates.*

*And I know the difference, precisely, because it's my difference. He's not watching what I do. He watched fifty minutes of a redirect drill and asked no questions about the drill; he watched six minutes of a placement bout and left before the result; he asked Gault three questions and all three were about the ruler. He isn't collecting what I can do. He's collecting the architecture of how — how the method's built, who built it, what it costs to run, what it would take to break.*

*That's my method. That is precisely and exactly my method, run by somebody who has had forty years to get good at it, from an altitude where the whole continent is the floor.*

*The Compact finally sent someone who takes notes the way I take notes.*

He stopped there for a while. Below, the porters finished the center and started on the tiers.

*I can't decide if that's the best news of the year or the worst,* he wrote, and closed it, and went down.

# Chapter 19 — The Copper Crown

The yard filled from the top down, which Cael had never seen it do.

Ordinary ladder sessions filled from the floor rail outward, because the floor rail was where you could hear the panel and see a fighter's feet. On the nineteenth of Reaping the upper tiers went first, an hour before the bell, filled by second-years who had come early with bread in their coats and had worked out that a Copper final at capacity was going to leave two hundred people standing in the covered walk hearing about it secondhand.

He got his place at the second bell and a half, on the west tier, four rows up, which was not the best seat in the yard and was the seat he had used for every ladder session since the brackets opened. He had thought about that for a while the night before and had decided it mattered. He had watched a hundred and eleven bouts from that stone. Sitting somewhere else today would have been a kind of statement, and the statement would have been *this one is different*, and it was different, and everybody in the building already knew, and there was nothing whatever to be gained by saying it with a seat.

Karis came up at the third bell with a folded sheet in her hand and sat down beside him and did not open it.

"Bracken's season record," she said, before he asked. "Both of them, complete, in the certified form. Filed and dated this morning."

"Before the bout."

"Before the bout. That's the point of it." She squared the fold. "Whatever happens down there, the paper that says what they did this season exists already and was made without knowing the outcome. Bracken's idea. He said a record assembled after a result is a record with an opinion in it."

Cael looked at the folded sheet for a moment and decided he was not going to be able to explain to her, today, what it did to him that a registrar had spent his morning making a thing that would be true either way.

The delegation came in at the fourth bell and took the north tier's second row, which the yard had left clear for them without anybody instructing it to, in the manner of a crowd making room around something it has decided to be careful about. Counsel, two clerks, Ilsev with her case on her knees. Havel at the end of the row with nothing in his hands at all, which Cael noted, because a records officer with nothing in his hands is a man off duty, and a man off duty had chosen to spend it here.

And at the row's center, in inspection greys, seated — which surprised him, because the man had stood for fifty minutes at a training-floor rail and had not needed to sit — with a small notebook closed on one knee and a pen laid across it.

Cael looked at that for about four seconds and then put it away, entirely, and did not look at the north tier again for an hour, because two of his friends were about to fight each other and he had promised them a sentence and he intended to earn it.

---

Fiske came down to the floor rail at the fourth bell and eleven minutes, in ordinary clothes, and stood in the place where a defending champion stands.

She had lost the crown nine days ago in a semifinal that had taken four exchanges and one very long minute, and had lost it to the woman about to fight for it, and the brass mark had stayed beside her name on the standings sheet all that time because the mark did not move until the final moved it. In about forty minutes a circlet of hammered copper that had been on her shelf for two years was going to be handed to somebody else in front of eleven hundred people.

She looked, from four rows up, entirely calm.

Cael had a page on her — he had a page on everybody in the bracket — and had written on it in the third week of the season that Fiske carried herself like a woman with nothing left to be nervous about, and had revised it in the tenth week to *nothing left to be nervous about because the worst available outcome already happened to her two years ago and it was called winning.*

Ephram was on the east tier with the Blade cohort, sitting forward with his forearms on his knees, and had been there since the third bell, and had said something to Cael on the stair that morning that Cael was going to have to think about for a week: "I've watched your friend get hit by my whole cohort for a term. If he takes this off her I'm going to have to revise about nine things." He had not said what the nine things were. He had gone off to find a seat.

Rooke stood at the yard's north entrance and did not sit down at any point in the next fifty minutes.

The panel came out at the fourth bell and a half: three faculty, one adjudicating and two on safety, and the adjudicator read the terms into the yard in the flat public voice these things were read in.

*Copper bracket. Season final. First of the column against third. Full sanction, faculty supervision, witnessed. Exchanges called on the panel's threshold. The bout concludes on incapacity, on withdrawal, or on the panel's determination.*

Then Lira and Brom came out of the north and south doors, and eleven hundred people made a noise, and Cael's hands went cold in a way they had not gone cold for any bout he had fought himself.

---

He watched the walk-out with the compound gaze open and got both of them in about six seconds, and what he got was not encouraging and was exactly correct.

Lira's leading hip was nine days old. She had not favoured it in the covered walk that morning and she was not favouring it now, which meant nothing, because Lira could conceal a limp the way other people concealed a yawn; what could not be concealed was the way she set her feet at the mark. She set the leading one first and let the weight arrive onto it in two stages instead of one. Two stages. He had watched her set her feet at a mark ninety times this season and it had been one stage every time.

Brom's shoulder was fine. Brom's *hands* were the thing. He was flexing them, opening and closing, which he did when he was cold and did not do at the fourth bell in Reaping, and Cael had watched him do exactly that before three bouts this season and had worked out in the second month what it meant, and it did not mean nerves. It meant he had decided on a plan that involved taking a great deal of contact, and his hands, which knew before he did, had begun getting ready to be hit.

*He's going to make it long,* Cael thought, and then, half a second later, with the small sick lurch of an arithmetic arriving: *no. She's going to make it long. And he knows.*

Because that was the whole bout, and it had been the whole bout since the pairings resolved, and both of them had refused to train for nine days precisely so that neither could pretend not to know it.

Brom's hardening ran out at about four minutes under repeat contact. Cael had that in the notebook in Brom's own words from a Greyvane winter and had confirmed it eleven times since — not a collapse, a fade: the skin stayed hard and the *cost* of keeping it hard went up until, somewhere past four minutes, every other thing he could do got slower to pay for it.

Lira had three bursts. Three, on that hip, today. The fourth was a thing she had told him she could not take, out loud, in hall one, in front of the man she was about to fight.

One of those clocks was going to run out first, and both fighters knew both numbers, and neither of them had been permitted to build a plan around the other's number, because for nine days they had refused to speak about it.

*That's what she meant,* Cael thought, watching them take their marks. *Nine days of not talking wasn't sportsmanship. It was so that neither of them could plan the other's injury. They've each got to fight the fighter, not the ledger.*

The adjudicator's hand came up.

---

**The first exchange lasted a hundred and ten seconds and it was Lira asking a question three times.**

She went at the seam.

That was the correct opening and it was the opening every practitioner in the Copper column would have used and eleven of them had used it against Brom this season, because Brom's flaw had been public property since the third week of term: he took force onto a hardened surface, and the taking was superb, and then there was a stall — a half-beat, sometimes a full one, where the absorption finished and the counter had not started, and inside that half-beat he was a wall with nobody behind it.

Lira had scored on that seam for a year and a half. She knew its shape better than anybody alive.

She fed him a committed line at three-quarter weight, low and honest, and he took it on the forearm, and she was already moving to the seam's far side before the contact finished —

— and the counter was there.

Not fast. That was the thing Cael saw from four rows up and eleven hundred people did not: it was not a faster counter. It was a counter that started while the absorption was still finishing, which meant Rooke had not made Brom quicker, he had made him do two things at once, and doing two things at once is not speed, it is a different skeleton.

Lira came off it flat-footed and had to give ground.

She went again, from a different angle, with a change of weight in the middle to make the stall open wider. It did not open. She went a third time, and this one Cael actually admired out loud, a small sound he was not aware of making, because she attacked the seam with a feint that was *designed to be absorbed* — a line committed so far that Brom had to take it, so that the absorption would be maximal and the stall, if it existed at all, would be at its widest.

Brom took it, and converted, and put a hardened forearm across her shoulder line hard enough that the yard heard it.

Lira went back four paces and stopped, and the panel called the exchange, and eleven hundred people made a noise that was not quite a cheer because half of them did not know what they had watched.

Cael knew. Cael watched her stand at her mark with her chest going and her hands loose, and watched her face, and what was on it was not dismay.

She was grinning.

He had seen that expression on her perhaps six times in three years, and it had never once meant she was winning. It meant something she had believed for a long time had just turned out to be wrong in her favour, and Lira had a defect of character that she had never made the slightest attempt to correct, which was that she liked being wrong about a person more than she liked being right about a bout.

Across the floor Brom rolled his shoulder and did not grin at all, because Brom did not, and because his hands had opened and closed once at his sides, and the four-minute clock had started.

---

**The second exchange lasted a hundred and forty seconds and Brom took it.**

He took it on the strength of what he had spent a semester building and he took it in a way that told Cael the repair had not been a repair at all.

Rooke's assignment had had a date on it and the date was today, and what Rooke had actually done — Cael saw it in the second exchange and wrote it up that night in two hundred words — was not to close a seam. You cannot close a seam in an Iron Skin practitioner's absorption; the seam is the physics. What Rooke had done was to teach a young man to *stop finishing the absorption*. Brom now took perhaps eighty per cent of an incoming line and abandoned the rest, unfinished, and put the difference into the counter, and paid for it by eating a fifth of every strike in his own body.

That was the trade. He had bought the end of his flaw with contact, and contact was the thing his four-minute clock ran on.

Lira burned her first burst at forty seconds and her second at ninety.

Cael priced them both from four rows up as accurately as if he had been paying them himself, because he had been, in a different body, for two years, and his own log's Wind-adjacent entry had been written half in her handwriting: the held half-breath at the front, non-negotiable, no air for the length of the displacement; the landing beat at the back, half a breath of locked commitment where no direction change was possible and the whole body was, for that instant, a thing that had landed; the line of strain from crest to inner knee on the leading side that would be bright by evening and would still be there in the morning.

She spent the first one to get off a line she should not have been on and the second one to get inside his reach, and the second one worked, and she scored on his ribs, and he took the score standing and closed on her landing beat, and she had no air and no direction available and he put her on the boards.

The panel called it. Two on safety came in and went out again. Brom stepped back to his mark.

Beside Cael, Karis said, in the small flat voice she used when she was frightened and had decided to be useful about it, "That's two."

"That's two."

"How bad."

"She'll get a third. The third's expensive and it's available." Cael did not take his eyes off the floor. "The fourth is the one she told him about in hall one nine days ago. That's the only number in this bout that isn't a number."

---

**The third exchange lasted four minutes and eleven seconds and it changed the bout.**

Because Lira stopped trying to be inside.

Cael had watched her fight for three years and had built a page on her that ran to eleven sheets, and the eleven sheets said one thing over and over in a hundred forms: Lira's whole architecture was *arrival*. Wind Path evasion was not speed; it was the ability to spend the interval other people used for deciding, and she had spent two years on a circuit where nobody could afford to hold anything back learning to spend it in fractions, and every fraction she spent bought her a position, and the positions were the point.

In the third exchange she did not arrive anywhere.

She stayed at the edge of his reach and made him come to her, and every time he came she gave ground on foot alone — no bursts, no displacement, nothing that cost a half-breath — and every time he arrived at where she had been, she made him take contact getting there. She let him hit her guard. She let him hit her forearms. She walked backward around the floor's whole circumference twice and made an Iron Skin practitioner harden, and harden, and harden.

It was, Cael thought, watching it with something close to awe, the least Lira-shaped thing he had ever seen her do, and she was doing it perfectly, and it was going to cost her more than the bursts would have.

Because it was not free. Nothing in this yard was free and he had spent six months proving it. She was taking contact from a hardened Iron Skin forearm on a guard she had not built for absorbing — she was a Wind practitioner, she had spent her entire life not being hit, and the whole architecture of her body was built on the premise that contact was a failure state. Every block she took went into her forearms and up into her shoulders and, by the third minute, into a leading hip she was landing on backward every eleven seconds.

At three minutes and forty the yard began to understand and the noise changed.

At four minutes Brom's hands slowed.

Not much. Cael saw it because Cael had watched him for four minutes with a clock running in his own head and had been waiting for it since the exchange started. The hardening did not fail. It went on being excellent. What went was the thing underneath — the recovery between committed motions stretched from four beats to five, and the fifth beat was where Lira, who had been walking backward for four minutes doing nothing but paying, finally stopped paying.

She burned her third burst on the fifth beat and put her heel behind his leading knee and took the exchange, and eleven hundred people came up off the stone.

"Three," said Karis.

Cael did not answer, because he was watching Lira walk back to her mark, and she was not concealing it now — the leading hip took the weight in two stages and then a third, and she put her hand on her own thigh for about half a second on the way, which she had never once done in a hundred and eleven bouts he had watched, and which meant she had run out of ability to pretend and had decided that pretending was no longer worth what it cost.

Across the floor, Brom saw it too.

He was four feet from Cael's line of sight and Cael read the whole thing arrive on him: the eyes going to the hip, the half-second of stillness, the decision.

*He's going to take it,* Cael thought.

And then, immediately after, before he could stop it: *good.*

---

**The fourth exchange lasted eleven seconds.**

Brom went at the hip.

He went at it directly and without any attempt at disguise, because disguise would have been an insult and because there were eleven hundred people and three faculty and a Compact delegation watching two friends fight a witnessed final, and the only unforgivable thing available to either of them was to fight it dishonestly. He came off the mark at a speed his cohort had cost him a term of bruises to acquire, and he committed a line low and left, at the leading side, at the place where a Wind practitioner has to land.

It was the correct attack. It was the attack Cael would have made. It was the attack that said *I know, and I am not going to insult you by pretending I don't, and if you can beat me anyway then you have beaten me.*

Lira took the fourth burst.

Cael, four rows up, felt it in his own hip the way you feel a note you know.

She went — the drop and the loosening, the floor giving her a half-inch it had been holding in reserve, weight simply ceasing to be where the read said it was, hollow-boned for a half-second on a locked half-breath — and Brom's committed line arrived exactly where the physics promised and found nothing there, and she came down.

And the landing beat was where the bout was.

Because the landing beat is a half-breath of locked commitment, no direction change available, and Lira had spent four exchanges teaching an Iron Skin practitioner nothing at all about her landing beat because he had known about it for two years, and he turned into it, and he was there, and it should have ended.

It did not end, because Brom's hands had been slow since the four-minute mark and the eleven seconds of the fourth exchange came after four minutes and eleven seconds of the third, and the turn arrived at the fifth beat instead of the fourth.

She landed on a hip that could not take it, on a leg that folded a half-inch and did not fold further, and she was already moving out of the landing before the beat had finished — which is not possible, which Cael had written in his own log in his own hand as *locked, no direction change, reliable as the burst itself* — and he understood, in the half-second it took, exactly what he was looking at.

She had not shortened the landing beat. Nobody shortened the landing beat.

She had landed on the bad hip on purpose, so that the leg would fold, so that the fold would carry her sideways without any decision being required at all, and the beat had run its full length while her body was already going somewhere on the strength of an injury.

It was the worst thing Cael had ever seen anybody do to themselves in a bout and it was, structurally, perfect.

Brom's counter went through the space she had occupied. Her strike went into the outside of his knee at the moment his own committed weight was standing on it.

He went down heavily and correctly. The safety faculty's hand was up before he landed.

The adjudicator called it.

---

Eleven hundred people made a sound that Cael, who had been building instruments for measuring rooms for two years, had no instrument for at all.

He stayed sitting down. He was aware of that afterward with some embarrassment and had, at the time, no choice available: his legs had made a decision without consulting him, and Karis was standing up beside him with both hands over her mouth and the folded certified season record still in one of them, and down on the floor Lira was on one knee, not from ceremony, and could not put weight on the leading side at all.

Brom got up first and put a hand out and hauled her onto her feet, and neither of them let go for about three seconds, and the yard, which had been shouting, stopped.

Then the panel came out and the noise came back.

---

The crown ceremony was the quietest thing that happened all afternoon.

Withrow presented it, because the chancellor presented the crowns, and she did it without one word of speech beyond the formula, which Cael thought was the best decision anybody made in that yard all day. Bracken read the season record — the same certified sheet, made that morning, before, when it could still have been either of them. Six wins, no losses, twenty-second to first in a hundred and thirty days, the fastest legal ascent the ladder's mathematics permitted.

Fiske applauded first.

She was at the floor rail where a defending champion stands and she brought her hands together about a half-second before anybody else in the building, alone, in a silent yard, and it carried, and it started the rest of it. Two years and two crowns and a brass mark that would come off her name tomorrow morning, and she put her hands together first, and Cael watched her do it and had to look at the stone between his feet for a moment.

The crown itself was a circlet of hammered copper the width of two fingers, unremarkable as metalwork.

Lira took it in both hands. She did not put it on. She stood in the center of a floor with eleven hundred people making noise at her and looked down at the thing in her hands, and turned it once, and Cael — who knew her better than anyone alive and had spent six months trying to work out how she would hold it — got his answer and wrote it that night in six words.

*She held it like an exhibit.*

Not treasure. Not a trophy. She held it in both hands, out a little from her body, the way you hold a document somebody is going to want to inspect: a thing produced in evidence, in front of witnesses, on a date, with a certified record read aloud beforehand by a registrar who had made it before he knew the answer.

Then she looked up into the north tier, at the delegation's row, for about one second.

Nobody in the yard saw her do it except Cael, and possibly Fiske, and certainly the row itself.

---

Gault filed at the seventh bell, in his own office, with the yard still emptying below.

He had told Cael a term ago that the first was not his mandate and the second was, and that he needed instruments and consistency and would supply nothing else, and he had held to that so exactly for eleven months that Cael had never once been able to decide whether the man was on his side. He decided, at the seventh bell on the nineteenth of Reaping, that the question had been badly framed. Gault was not on anybody's side. Gault was on the side of the number, and today the number had come out a certain way, and Gault did with it precisely what the number required.

The filing was an application by the assessment office of a chartered academy for a formal advancement evaluation before an Arbiter of the registry, on the ground of demonstrated advancement inconsistent with recorded tier, supported by: a full season record, certified; a witnessed final under faculty panel; and the panel's own assessment of capability demonstrated.

He read it aloud to the four people in the room because a filing of that class was read aloud, and one of the four was the delegation's senior evaluation seat, present at the assessment office's invitation, witnessing.

Ilsev listened to all of it with her case shut on her knees. At the end she asked two questions. The first was whether the season record had been certified before or after the final. The second was whether the panel's assessment had been recorded by a member of the panel or by a clerk.

"Before," said Bracken. "This morning."

"By the adjudicating faculty," said Gault. "In her own hand. Countersigned by both safety seats."

"Then I have nothing to add and no objection to note," said Ilsev, "and I will record that I was present."

That was all. It took four minutes. There was no speech, no acknowledgement, nothing that a person watching would have recognized as the end of anything.

Cael stood at the back of the office with his hands behind him and understood that he had just watched a lever work.

Fiske had told her, in a corridor beside a standings board eleven weeks ago, that the crown was not an argument and not a prize; that it was the thing you used to make an institution spend an afternoon on you, because nobody had ever had a reason to spend one before. And here was an office spending one — a magister, a registrar, a chartered application, and a senior Compact evaluator sitting in a chair with her case shut, witnessing, so that the paper would carry her presence on its face wherever it went.

*The crown forces the looking,* he wrote later. *She was right and it took her two years and two crowns to work it out and she gave it away for free to the woman who took the third one off her.*

---

He wrote the day's entry at the window before he went down to find them, because he knew from experience that if he waited until after he would write about the wrong things.

*Lira won the Copper Crown in the fourth exchange, at four minutes and eleven seconds plus eleven, against the only opponent in the bracket who has ever been her size.*

*Costs, itemized, because somebody in that yard should know exactly what it cost both of them and I said I'd be that somebody.*

*Hers: four bursts. Four held half-breaths, four landing beats. Nine days of a hip that was already hurt and four minutes of taking contact on a guard that isn't built for it — she walked backward around that floor twice on purpose, to spend his clock, which is the single most disciplined thing I have ever watched her do and the least like her. And the fourth burst she took on the injury deliberately, so the leg would fold, so the fold would move her sideways during the beat she can't move in. She used a wound as a mechanism. I don't have a column for that. I'm going to have to build one.*

*His: a term of Rooke's work, spent. He didn't get faster; he learned to do two things at once, and he pays for it by eating a fifth of everything he takes, which is the trade that ran his own clock down in the third and lost him the fourth by one beat. He lost this bout to a repair that worked. That's a horrible sentence and it's also the truth, and he'll get there before I do.*

*And he went at the hip. Straight at it, no disguise, in front of eleven hundred people, because anything else would have been an insult.*

*Gain: a circlet of hammered metal, a line on a plate with a hundred and thirty-one years of other people's names on it, and one chartered application, filed at the seventh bell, witnessed by the Compact's own evaluation seat.*

*The circlet is worth nothing. The application is worth everything. That was the whole point of the whole season and neither of them said it out loud once.*

---

The yard was empty by the eighth bell except for two porters and the fighters, and Cael came down the west stair and stopped at the bottom of it, because what was happening on the floor was not his.

Lira was sitting on the boards at the center with her leading leg out straight in front of her and the crown on the floor beside her, in the dust, where she had put it.

Brom came out of the north door with an ice wrap on one knee and walked the length of the floor and sat down next to her, on the loser's side of a bout he had lost by one beat, which was the oldest posture either of them had.

They did not say anything for a while.

"Two years," said Brom, "I've been trying to solve you."

"And?"

Brom looked out at the empty tiers, and rolled the shoulder, and considered it with the care of a man who intends to agree with something rather than concede it.

"Third one someday," he said.

Lira laughed, which hurt her, which made her laugh again.

Cael stayed at the bottom of the stair a while longer, and then went to get them, because somebody was going to have to help her up the bluff steps and it was not going to be the man with the knee.

Above them, on the north tier, in the second row, a man in inspection greys sat alone in an emptying yard with a small notebook open on his knee.

He wrote one line in it.

Then he closed it, and put the pen in his coat, and went down.

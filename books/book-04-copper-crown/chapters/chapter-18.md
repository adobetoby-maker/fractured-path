# Chapter 18 — The Charter Question

The enrollment-basis session opened at the second bell on the fifteenth of Reaping and ran, with two recesses, until the seventh, and Cael sat through the whole of it four feet from a wall in a chair that had been placed for him rather than by him.

Withrow had put him in the room for the same reason she had put him in the room for the notice: anybody named in a scope reads the scope. She had also, without saying so, put him where the room would be least aware of him, which was against the long wall behind the defending side's table, in a line of four chairs of which the other three held clerks. He had understood the arrangement inside a second and had approved of it, and had spent a moment being sourly amused that the chancellor of a mid-tier academy had independently arrived at the seating doctrine he had been running on himself for two years.

The records hall's long table had the delegation down one side and Halcenvane down the other. Delegation counsel at the head of her side with three boxes of her own and a clerk to fetch. Ilsev at her left, with her case shut on the table in front of her and her hands flat on it. Havel at the foot with the recording block and the routing tray, which was the same posture Cael had seen him hold in a Greyvane hearing room and, four years before that, at a corridor desk in Ardenmere: a man whose work was to be the place the paper went.

Halcenvane: Withrow, her counsel, Bracken, Karis. Gault had been invited and had come and had brought nothing and had sat down at the end nearest the door, which Cael read as a man making it structurally obvious that his wing had no stake in the documentary argument and would answer only for its instruments.

And at the delegation's side, past counsel, in the second chair from the window, in inspection greys, with a working folder in front of him and one pen laid across it: the eleventh name.

Cael had been in the room for eleven minutes before he made himself stop looking for the read that was not going to come, and put the whole of his attention where it belonged, which was on the woman about to take a filing cabinet apart.

---

Counsel opened on provenance, exactly as her nineteen years of findings had promised she would.

"Chancellor, before scope, before repeal, before anything: I want to know where the text comes from. You have enrolled a practitioner on a clause. I would like to see the clause in a document, and I would like to know how the document got into this building."

"Registrar," said Withrow.

Bracken stood, which he did not need to do, and did it anyway because a records session is theatre with citations, and went to the shelf and brought back the first of four boxes.

What followed took forty minutes and Cael found it, to his own surprise, one of the better things he had ever watched.

The clause did not live in Halcenvane's charter. It lived where Karis had found it eleven months and one academy ago: in the founding schedule of institutional powers, the general instrument that every chartered academy's own charter incorporated by a standard article, and Bracken produced the incorporation article of Halcenvane's charter, tabbed, with the article number read into the record, before counsel had finished framing the question about it.

Then he produced the schedule itself. Then he produced a second copy of the schedule.

"Two certified copies," he said. "Taken from two holdings four hundred miles apart, by two librarians who have never corresponded, each attested under the holding institution's seal, each collated line by line against the other by a clerk of this office who had not read either before beginning. The collation sheet is document eleven. It records three differences, all of them orthographic, none of them in the assay article, and all three are listed."

Counsel took the collation sheet and read it, and Cael watched her do it, and watched, with real professional pleasure, the moment her rhythm changed. She had come in talking. She stopped talking for ninety seconds, which by her own established pattern meant the reading room in her head had taken over from the talking room, and when she came back she did not resume from where she had stopped. She started somewhere else.

"Your index," she said. "Who wrote it?"

"I did."

"It's good." She said it flatly, the way one professional concedes an instrument to another. "It cost me four minutes to find the document I wanted and I had expected forty. I'll want it noted that I said so, because I am about to spend the rest of the day disagreeing with what's in the boxes and I would rather the record showed I had no complaint about the boxes."

"Noted," said Havel, at the foot of the table, and wrote it.

---

The repeal argument was the real one and it ran for two hours.

Counsel did not argue that the assay clause had been repealed by name, because it had not been, and two independent searches sat in box two saying so — Karis's, performed over eleven weeks of a Greyvane winter, and one of Bracken's clerks', performed from scratch without access to Karis's notes, arriving at the same nothing by a different route.

She argued the better thing. She argued supersession by implication: that the standardization directive had occupied the whole field of practitioner classification, comprehensively and deliberately, and that a clause left standing inside an occupied field was a dead letter whatever its formal status, and that an institution which enrolled a practitioner on a dead letter had enrolled him on nothing.

It was a genuinely strong argument. Cael, who had been reading registry law for two years out of necessity rather than affection, felt it land in the room and felt the temperature at the Halcenvane side of the table change by a measurable amount.

Withrow answered it herself.

"Counsel, I spent twenty years in the registry and I have made that argument," she said. "I made it eleven times, and it succeeded nine, and I want to be precise about why it failed the other two, because both failures are on this table."

She set her hand on box three.

"Supersession by implication is available where the later instrument is silent about what it is displacing. It is not available where the later instrument tells you what it is displacing, because then it has told you, and a reader is not free to add to a list the drafter closed." She nodded at Bracken, who turned a tab. "The standardization directive contains a repeal schedule. Article forty-one. Eleven clauses, repealed by name, each with its instrument, article and subarticle. The drafters of that directive went through the founding schedule clause by clause and made a list of what they were killing. The assay article is not on the list."

"An omission—"

"Is a possibility," said Withrow. "So the file addresses it. Document sixty-two is the directive's own transitional article, which instructs institutions on the conversion of *existing assay enrollments* into the new classification terminology. Counsel, I would like that read into the record in full, because it is four sentences long and it decides the point."

Havel read it. Cael listened to it twice — once as it was read and once again inside his own head afterward — and understood on the second pass exactly why it decided the point, and felt the small cold click of a thing locking that he had been carrying loose for eleven months.

A directive that tells institutions how to convert assay enrollments is a directive written by people who knew assay enrollments existed and produced enrolled practitioners. You do not draft a conversion procedure for a thing you consider a dead letter. You draft it because the thing is alive and you would prefer it were tidier.

"The directive discouraged the practice," Withrow said. "It renamed its terminology, it built a conversion route out of it, and it left the clause standing. Every one of those is a choice, and the only choice they did not make is the one counsel needs them to have made. If the drafters had wished to repeal the assay article they had a schedule open in front of them with eleven entries in it and a pen in their hand."

Counsel took twenty minutes on it from three more directions and did not get in.

Cael watched the whole of it with the compound gaze wide open, and got everything, and it was almost restful after four days of getting nothing. Counsel's rhythm compressed under pressure instead of breaking — guild-shaped, or its legal equivalent — and her weight came forward onto her forearms a half-beat before she committed to a line of attack, every time, five times in two hours, and by the fourth Cael could have told the Halcenvane side which of three arguments was coming.

He did not, because it was not his room and he had been given a chair against a wall.

---

The compiled file came back to Ilsev at the first recess, and the return was in it.

She had asked for the file at the recess because the enrollment-basis session's afternoon would touch the ongoing requirement, and the ongoing requirement touched the monitoring, and she preferred to have read a thing before she asked about it. That was the whole of her method and it had never once been more complicated than that.

The compilation was the assessment wing's — six filings, dated, appendices attached, a covering index in a fourth handwriting with no annotation in it, which she noted with approval, because a covering index that argues is an index she has to read twice. Behind it, the routing history. Behind that, the file's administrative correspondence, which was where a records office put returns.

The return was one sheet.

*Referral, senior evaluation seat, filed in the matter of the Greyvane adjudication of the preceding year: query as to originating authority for designation Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4, appearing in the priority field of subject file. Determination: no originating authority of record. Designation predates file creation. No action available at this clearance.*

Ilsev read it once and set it down and put her hands flat on the table on either side of it, which was what she did instead of the several things other people did.

She had filed that referral in a room at a small academy in a cold month, in the second hour of a review she had conducted the way she conducted every review, and she had filed it because a designation had been sitting in a priority field that matched no monitoring level the Compact had ever issued to her or to anybody she had trained. She had filed it as a query, at the lowest temperature the form permitted, because she had assumed — and she remembered assuming it, and remembered the exact quality of the assumption, which was the mild irritation of a professional anticipating a clerical answer — that some office three doors from hers would write back a paragraph explaining a legacy convention, and she would read the paragraph and think *ah,* and never think about it again.

Fourteen months.

She read the sheet a second time, in the sequence she read anything: the operative sentence, then the sentences around it, then the operative sentence again against them.

*No originating authority of record.* A designation appears in a registry file. Designations are issued. Issuing is an act; acts have actors; actors are recorded, because the entire architecture of the registry exists to make it possible to ask who did a thing and be answered. That was not a philosophical position. It was the operating premise of forty years of her working life and of every finding she had ever signed.

*Designation predates file creation.* And this was the sentence she could not get past on any reading, and she had now had four.

A marking cannot be older than the paper it is written on. If the designation predates the file, then either the file was recreated at some point and carried the designation forward from an earlier file — in which case the earlier file was the record and the query should have been routed to wherever the earlier file was held — or the sentence meant what it said, and a priority field had contained a value before there was a field.

She wrote, on her own working sheet, in her own hand: *Either the answer is a routing failure, or the answer is not an answer. Both are reportable. Neither is mine to resolve.*

She did not permit herself the next thought, and knew that she was not permitting it, and was comfortable with the discipline, because she had spent a career watching capable people ruin sound findings by reaching one clause further than their evidence went. A designation with no author of record was an artifact. Artifacts in a registry were either errors or somebody's discipline. She had no basis whatsoever to say which, and a woman who guesses at that in writing has stopped being an assessor and started being a correspondent.

*No action available at this clearance.*

That sentence she read last, and it was the one that decided what she did.

There were exactly two responses available to a senior evaluator holding a returned query that had not been answered. The first was to accept the return, note it, and go on with her afternoon, which was what the sentence was constructed to produce and what it would produce in most of the people it was sent to, because it was polite and final and cost nothing to accept.

The second was to file again.

She took a referral form out of her case. She completed it in eleven minutes, in the recess, with the hall emptying around her and a clerk bringing tea she did not touch: the original referral's date and reference; the return's date and reference; the return's operative language reproduced in full, because paraphrase is where queries go to die; and, in the field marked *grounds for escalation*, one sentence.

*A determination that no originating authority exists is not a determination as to the designation's validity, and the designation remains live on an active file.*

She marked it for the next level above the one that had returned it, signed it, dated it, and put it in the routing tray at the foot of the table where the records officer would collect it, and then she drank the tea, which was by then cold, and went back into the session.

She did not think about it again that afternoon. That was not indifference. It was the other half of the same discipline: a thing filed correctly is a thing that no longer requires you to carry it, which is the entire reason the forms exist, and a woman who cannot put a filed matter down is a woman who will eventually file badly to make the carrying stop.

---

Havel collected the tray at the recess's end and logged its contents into the compiled file in the ordinary way, which took him four minutes.

Return, in. Referral, out, escalated one level, with its cross-reference to the return and the return's cross-reference to the original. He entered both in the routing history and initialled the entries and squared the block and went back to recording the session, because the session was his work and the tray was his work and there was no version of the afternoon in which either of them got less than it needed.

He read the return while he logged it, because a records officer who does not read what he logs is a clerk with better boots.

He had read the original referral fourteen months ago too, at a hearing, from three feet away, and had watched the senior evaluator who filed it read a designation three times before turning the page — not to check it, because she had already checked it, but the way a person rereads a sentence in a language they are certain they speak.

He finished the session at the seventh bell, carried the block down to the delegation's working room, ate at the refectory's late sitting because the guest table had been given to counsel and two clerks who wanted to argue about the afternoon, and went up.

The private notebook lived at the bottom of his case, under the spare recording block, and had four pages used out of ninety.

The first entry was Ardenmere: a market square, an anomaly in a routing record, a question he had asked his supervising officer and been moved off. The second was a records anomaly in the same city eight months later, the same designation, the same absence of a source. The third was the Greyvane hearing: the first time anybody senior had seen it, and the first time he had understood that the thing was not his own inexperience.

He added the fourth in nine lines, dated, in the same dry hand he used for everything, because the notebook's whole value was that it read like a records log and not like a man with a theory.

*Referral of the Greyvane matter, senior evaluation seat, returned this day. No originating authority of record. Designation predates file creation. No action available at this clearance. Referring officer filed again, escalated one level, same afternoon, grounds recorded.*

He looked at that for a while.

Then he added one more line, which broke his own rule about the notebook and which he decided he could afford once.

*Four years. Two of us now. Nothing has ever come back with a name on it.*

He closed it and put it under the block, and went to sleep, because there was another session in the morning and there had been another session in the morning for four years, and the only thing he had ever been able to do about any of this was to keep the count and keep the job.

---

The afternoon ran to the ongoing requirement, and Ilsev did the work.

Her questions were surgical and went nowhere anybody could have predicted, which Cael had read about in six published findings and had never watched happen in a room. She asked Bracken what the assay clause required *of the institution* as against what it required of the practitioner, and made him read the operative sentence aloud, and then made him read it aloud a second time with the subordinate clause first, which changed nothing about the words and changed a great deal about what the room heard.

She asked Gault whether the semester evaluation he administered was the evaluation the clause described, or an evaluation the academy had designed and named after it.

Gault answered that in one paragraph, without warmth and without hesitation, and it was the longest speech Cael had heard him make in a full term. The clause required evaluation by demonstration before a panel of the institution, with the standard set by the institution and recorded. His office had set the standard at the baseline in the enrollee's first month, had published it, had calibrated the apparatus on its own schedule since, and would measure the enrollee against that standard on the twentieth whether or not anyone from the delegation chose to be in the room. "The clause does not tell me what standard to set," he said. "It tells me to set one, apply it, and write it down. I have. I do. I will."

"Thank you," said Ilsev, and wrote for a while.

She asked counsel one question, which surprised the room: whether the delegation was contending that the enrollment was unlawful, or that it was irregular, because those were different findings with different consequences and she wished to know which she was being asked to consider.

Counsel said, after a moment, "Irregular."

"Then I have what I need," said Ilsev.

She closed her case at the sixth bell and forty and delivered the finding into the record standing, which she had not needed to do either, and Cael wrote down every word of it on the back of the day's floor allocation because there was nothing else in his hands.

"The clause is unrepealed. It appears in two independently certified copies of the founding schedule, is incorporated by the receiving institution's charter under a standard article, and is not among the eleven clauses the standardization directive repealed by name in a schedule its drafters had open in front of them. The directive's transitional article presupposes enrollments made under it. The enrollment before us conforms to the clause's language in its basis, its ongoing requirement, and its recording. Whatever else the delegation reports, the basis is sound law."

She sat down.

Nobody applauded, because it was a records session and there was nothing to applaud. Bracken made one small note. Karis, four feet from Cael's chair, had gone completely still, in the manner of a person who has been holding an argument in her body for eleven months and has just been told she may put it down.

At the delegation's side of the table, past counsel, in the second chair from the window, Vastin turned a page in his working folder and made a mark on it.

Cael, who had spent nine hours failing to read the man and had built up a small private tally of the four occasions on which the pen had moved, added the fifth and reviewed all five that evening and got a result he did not enjoy.

The pen had not moved when counsel scored. It had not moved when Withrow answered the supersession argument, or when Bracken produced the second certified copy, or when Ilsev delivered a finding that decided the enrollment.

It had moved when Bracken described the collation procedure. It had moved when Gault said *and write it down.* It had moved when counsel conceded the index was good. It had moved twice more at points Cael could reconstruct as: a clerk producing a document in under a minute, and Havel logging a tray.

*He is not scoring the argument,* Cael wrote. *He has watched a nine-hour session about whether my enrollment is lawful and has taken five notes and every one of them is about whether this building's paperwork can be trusted.*

*He isn't evaluating the clause. He's evaluating whether the number that comes out of Halcenvane means anything. Same question he asked Gault about the resistance posts. Same question, third day running, from a different angle.*

*He is calibrating the instrument. I am the reading.*

---

Nine hours in a chair against a wall was, it turned out, worse for the idle state than fifty minutes on a floor.

He had not predicted that and should have. A floor occupied his attention completely, which was one known hazard he had spent forty-nine days building a countermeasure for. A records session occupied it in long slow bands with gaps in them, and the gaps were where a boy's attention went off its own edges, and it went off them twice in the afternoon and once badly in the sixth hour, during a stretch of procedural argument about the collation clerk's attestation, when he came back to himself at the end of an unmeasured interval to find that the clerk two chairs down had put a folder on the empty chair beside him.

The one beside him. Not the one three along, which was also empty.

Nothing happened. Nobody looked. The clerk retrieved the folder eleven minutes later and did not appear to find the location surprising, and Cael sat there with his heart going and the pressure coming up behind both eyes and thought, with total clarity: *that is a person putting a thing down in an empty space.*

Karis's foot found his ankle under the table.

She did not look at him. She did not stop writing. It was three-quarters of an inch of contact and it lasted about a second and it was the covenant working exactly as the four of them had built it eleven months ago in a residence common room, and it brought him back to his own edges faster than any of the eleven techniques in his own working ledger.

He held the rest of the day. It cost him the evening, and he wrote the cost down at the window with the pressure still going behind his eyes and his handwriting worse than it had been all term.

*Nine hours. Two drifts and one bad one. The bad one happened in a gap, which is the finding, and it's the finding Brom gave me four weeks ago and I have been applying to the wrong shape of room.*

*The twentieth is three hours with gaps in it. The panel confers between exercises. That's where I'll go.*

*I do not currently have a countermeasure for that. Karis's foot is not a countermeasure. It is a friend, which is worth more and works less often.*

---

She found him afterward in the records hall's back room, re-shelving, because the founding-era documentation had come out of four boxes and Bracken wanted it back in them tonight rather than tomorrow.

It was the first time in eleven days Cael had seen her without a pen in her hand. She was working the shelf with both arms and her sleeves pushed up and ink to the second knuckle, and she had the expression of somebody who had won and had not decided how to feel about it, which he had seen on her exactly once before, in an archive in a cold month.

He took the other end of the run and they worked in silence for a while, which was the good kind.

"Say it," he said eventually.

"It's not a finding."

"You've been carrying it since the third hour. Your handwriting went sideways at the transitional article and it never came back."

Karis set a volume in its place and left her hand on it.

"The transitional article," she said. "The one that decided the whole afternoon. The one that tells institutions how to convert an assay enrollment into standard terminology — which is the reason we won, because it proves the drafters knew the practice was alive." She said it in the flat voice she used for reading aloud. "It sits in the same directive as the terminology articles. Same instrument. Same year. Four articles apart."

Cael waited.

"You know what else is in that instrument's terminology articles," she said. "You have a copy in the older notebook and I have two in mine and we verified both against each other in an archive that got boxed onto a cart two mornings later. The article that discontinued a word from before the registry existed, and replaced it, and it is four articles from the article that made evaluation-by-demonstration into a thing you convert out of rather than a thing you do."

She still had her hand on the volume.

"The same room of people," she said. "The same season of the same year, near enough. One hand took a word that meant [UNBOUND] and wrote *shattered* over it. The other hand looked at the only method the old system had for assessing a practitioner the categories couldn't hold — a method that *worked*, Cael, that's the part I can't get past, it worked, we are standing in a building where it is currently working — and built a route out of it and told everybody to take the route."

"And you're not saying it means anything."

"I'm not saying it means anything." She said it fast, and it was not a retreat; it was a researcher closing a door she had opened on purpose and intended to keep shut. "I have no document connecting them. I have no drafter's name on either article. I have no minute, no memorandum, no correspondence, nothing but two articles in one instrument four articles apart, and if I stood up in that hall tomorrow and put them next to each other I would be doing the exact thing I have spent eleven months proving the other side does, which is arguing from a shape instead of from text. Two things in one instrument is not a finding. It is a coincidence with good posture."

"Then what have you got?"

"A question." She took her hand off the volume, finally, and turned around. "One. And I've written it down, in the notebook, on its own line, with nothing under it, because that's what you do with a question you can't work on yet."

"Which is?"

"*Why retire a working method?*" said Karis.

She let it sit in the back room for about four seconds.

"That's all of it. Not *who*, not *why the word*, not any of the six things I want. Just — a system had a way of looking at a person the categories couldn't describe, and the way worked, and it produced records, and somebody built a door out of it and shut the door behind them, and nobody has ever written down a reason." She started on the next volume. "I don't know the answer. I don't have an approach to the answer. I don't have an archive to go and be wrong in. I've got a line in a notebook and a boy who's being evaluated by demonstration on Reaping the twentieth, which is either the most interesting coincidence of my life or nothing at all."

"Which do you think?"

"I think," said Karis, "that I wrote the question down, and that I'm going to stop now, because it's late and I have won an argument today and I would like to keep the thing I actually proved rather than trade it for a thing I want."

They finished the shelf. Bracken tied the boxes.

---

He wrote it at the window, late, with the pressure still behind his eyes and the day's four hundred sheets going round in his head, and it came out longer than his entries usually did.

*The provision held. Ilsev said it out loud, standing up, into a record, in front of the Compact's own counsel: the basis is sound law. Fifteen months, two academies, four boxes, three hundred and eleven documents, and a young woman who spent eleven weeks of a Greyvane winter proving that nothing had happened.*

*I should be writing about that and I am going to write about the other thing, because that's what the day actually did to me.*

*The clause that lets me exist is four articles away from the article that renamed what I am. Same instrument. Same year.*

*Karis is right that it isn't a finding. I have watched what happens to people who argue from shapes; I watched a warden do it to me for two years and I beat him with a closed list. I am not going to become the thing I beat.*

*So I'll hold it as a question, in her exact words, because she chose them carefully and I couldn't improve them: why retire a working method?*

*And underneath, my own version, which I've been walking toward since the first week of this term and have never had this clean:*

*I stopped a long time ago asking whether the system is confused about me. It isn't. Confused systems ask questions — Naveth asked, Quenna asked, Gault asks four a session and writes the answers down. This system doesn't ask. It renames, converts, discourages, and closes doors, and it does all of it in flat administrative language four articles apart in one instrument, and it never once explains itself, because the whole architecture of it is built so that nothing has to.*

*I don't need to know who. I need to know why it's built this way. That's the question and I'm going to be a long time on it.*

He looked at the last line for a while, then wrote one more, and it was the only sentence in the entry that was not about the system.

*Tomorrow I go back to being dull. Four days to the final. Five to the twentieth.*

# Chapter 20 — Calibration

It took the three of them twenty minutes to get Lira up the residence steps, and the argument about it lasted longer than the climb.

She could put weight on the leading side for about a second and a half at a time and had established this by doing it four times in the covered walk while Cael stood there and let her, because three years had taught him that stopping Lira from proving something to herself cost more than the proof did. The wing's duty practitioner had looked at the hip in the yard, at the seventh bell, and had said what everybody in the building already knew — deep strain along the crest, nothing torn, four days off it, ice tonight and heat tomorrow — and had then said the other thing, in the flat voice of a woman who had been treating Copper-bracket fighters for eleven years.

"You did this to it on purpose."

"Yes."

"Twice."

"Once," said Lira. "The first three times I did it to it by accident."

Brom carried the crown up, because Lira had both hands on the rail and because somebody had to, and he carried it in one hand at his side like a hat, with an ice wrap strapped on the knee that had folded under Lira's heel and a walk that suggested the knee had opinions about stairs. Karis carried the certified season record and the folder from the assessment office and two loaves and a jar of something the refectory had let her take because the refectory had watched the final too.

They got up. They got the door. Cael put Lira into the chair by the common room's stove, which was the good chair, and she let him, which told him more about the hip than anything the duty practitioner had said.

"Right," said Lira, when she had stopped going white. "Table."

"Tomorrow," said Cael.

"Tomorrow is tomorrow. Table."

"You can't stand up."

"I'm not going to be doing any standing up," she said. "I'm going to be sitting in this chair telling you what you look like, which is the only part of tonight I was ever going to be good for, and if you three make me argue about it I'll be angry, and being angry hurts."

So they did it that night, which was, Cael thought afterward, the single most Halcenvane thing the four of them ever did: a Crown final at the fourth bell and a rehearsal at the eighth, in a residence common room, with two of the four unable to walk properly and the third with ink to the second knuckle, on the evening of the best day any of them had had in three years.

---

Karis set it out on the table in her own hand, on four sheets, and she had clearly been building it for a fortnight.

"You've had it as three columns since the eve of arrival week," she said. "The panel requires growth. The audience requires control. The record permits five things. That's correct and it's not operational, because none of those three tells you what to *do* at the fourth bell tomorrow morning in front of a drop-frame."

"That's what tonight's for."

"That's what tonight's for." She turned the first sheet round. "So. The published assessment standard. Bracken got me the calibration schedule and the wing's standing instrument schedules, both of which are public documents and both of which I have now read more carefully than anybody in this academy including, I suspect, the man who wrote them."

The sheet had five rows.

"Five trials at the baseline, the same five tomorrow, and Gault will run them in the order he ran them then, because Gault runs everything in the same order. Trial one, the shutter-board: nine louvres, randomising drum, displacement on the signal — the pendulum takes your interval, the brass takes your distance. Two, the drop-frame with the surface read: a known weight from an unannounced rack position, off the marked square before it arrives. Three, the plate on its brace: received force, dispersal, repetition to your stated ceiling. Four, the frame again at its heaviest, the margin cut thin, and the chalked plate reading where your feet actually went rather than where you believe they went. Five, the first two again, cold, at the end, to find out what three hours have moved."

"I know the five."

"You know what they are. You don't know what they *say*, because you were the thing being measured and I've spent a fortnight reading them as an evaluator would." Karis put her finger on the first row. "Here's the number that matters and it's the only number I want you to carry into that room. At the baseline you took weight three on the drop-frame with a redirect the clerk logged as clean in five of six runs. Wind-adjacent displacement on the shutter-board: you volunteered a ceiling of four repetitions, unprompted, which Gault wrote into the record as the single most useful sentence anybody had said to him in that room all year. The read's contribution to the frame trial: five clean calls in six."

"And what does a term buy?"

"That's the question I've been on for a fortnight and here's the answer, and you're going to dislike the shape of it." She sat back. "A practitioner training four supervised sessions a week under competent instruction, from that baseline, with no unusual event, should present: weight four on the frame at the same cleanness, or weight three at six of six. Ceiling of five or six on the shutter-board, not four. That is the band. Above the band you are a new question and the whole room has to stop and consider what it's looking at. Below it and you have failed the provision's own honest language, because the enrollment was granted on a basis of evaluation by demonstration and a demonstration that demonstrates nothing is a demonstration that failed."

"That's a narrow band."

"It's a *generous* band. I built it generously on purpose, twice, and then had Bracken check the arithmetic without telling him what it was for." Karis tapped the sheet. "The narrow part isn't the band. The narrow part is that you have to land inside it on every instrument in that wing, in front of a magister who cares about consistency more than magnitude and has said so out loud, while holding a continuous discipline nobody in that room knows you're running, for three hours."

Brom, from the floor by the stove with his leg out straight, said, "And it has to look like it isn't work."

"No," said Karis.

"Yes it does—"

"No." She said it with the certainty she used about four times a year. "That's the received wisdom and it's wrong and I've thought about it for a fortnight. Gault does not want effortless. Gault has told him twice, on the record, that he would rather have six identical trials at three-quarters than one extraordinary trial and five that disagree. Effortless is what an evaluator sees when a practitioner is holding something back and hoping the instrument won't notice."

Brom considered this from the floor, and then said, "Then that's my piece and I'll say it properly, because I've had a whole term of Rooke and it's the only thing I know that he doesn't."

They waited.

"Let it cost you," he said. "Visibly. On the outside of your body, where they can see it. Breathe hard after trial one. Shake the arm out after the frame. Let the fourth repetition be worse than the first — not sloppy, *worse*, the way a fourth repetition genuinely is — and then hold the fifth together with obvious effort and let the sixth be where you say the ceiling is." He shifted the knee. "Because here's what I've learned this term getting hit by nine people better than me: everyone in a hall can tell the difference between a man who is being careful and a man who is working. Careful is *visible*. It's the most visible thing there is. You've been careful your whole life and you don't know what it looks like from outside, and I do, because I've watched it from four feet for two years."

Cael sat with that for a moment and found that his ears had gone hot.

"What does it look like."

"Like somebody with something in his pocket," said Brom.

---

They ran the trials for two hours and forty minutes and the first hour was a disaster of exactly the useful kind.

Karis was the panel. She had the published standards, a borrowed timing glass, four sheets and a pen, and she ran it as an evaluator would — the order fixed, the questions between trials, the log kept in real time, the interval recorded from her instruction to his first motion. She had asked, at the start, in a voice that was more formal than the room deserved, whether he wanted her to be gentle, and had accepted his answer without comment and had never once softened afterward.

Brom was the apparatus.

He could not move off the mark, which turned out not to matter and in one respect to help. The shutter-board's trial they walked through dry — displacement was displacement, and there was no randomising drum in a residence common room — and then Brom took his mark, because Iron Skin from a fixed stance at cohort speed was a better analogue for a drop-frame than anything else in the building: a known force arriving at an instant Cael did not control, from a man who had spent a term being taught by Rooke's cohort to deliver it as fast as it could be delivered.

*Trial two, run one.* Cael took it on the forearm and turned it down into the floor, and it was clean, and Karis wrote and asked her question and he answered it.

*Run two.* Clean.

*Run three.* Clean, and faster, because his body had found the rhythm of a partner it had trained against for two years and had stopped thinking.

"Stop," said Karis.

"That was the best one."

"That was weight five," said Karis.

The room went quiet.

"I don't have a drop-frame and I can't measure force," she said. "What I have is a timing glass and Brom's honest report and my own eyes, and by all three of those, run three was faster than run one by an interval I can see without instruments, and cleaner, and it went into the floor at an angle you have never once used in front of a wing observer. Brom?"

"Different," said Brom. "Better. The read was open."

Cael put his hands on the back of a chair and stood there.

He had not decided to open the surface read. That was the thing that took him the longest twenty seconds of the evening to say out loud, and he said it because the covenant's whole architecture was that he said things like this out loud in this room.

"I didn't turn it on," he said. "It was on — all the way open, at working depth. It's been down to its ambient layer since the delegation came up the hill, because the read at depth and the idle state spend from the same account and the account has a floor. The low layer I can afford; it reads a room and nothing faster than a room. The reach into a forearm is the part with a price on it. And I've just spent three hours in a yard not holding anything, because every eye on that bluff was on two other people, and the read came back open when I stopped holding, and I didn't notice it happen."

"So on the day," said Karis, "in a room with the Compact in it—"

"On the day I hold the idle state, so the read runs low, so run three is impossible." He heard his own voice come out flat and was grateful for it. "Which means the number I put on Gault's floor tomorrow gets produced with the best instrument I own turned down to a stub, and Gault will log it, and it'll be honest, and it'll be lower than what I can actually do."

"Is it inside the band?"

He did the arithmetic, which took him a moment, and the answer was the only good news of the hour.

"Weight four at five of six," he said. "On the stub. It's inside. It's at the bottom."

"Then that's the plan," said Karis, and wrote it down, and did not offer him one word of comfort about it, which was worth more than comfort.

---

Trial four went the other way, and the other way turned out to be worse.

The pendulum work was interval and foot placement — a rhythm set by an escapement Cael did not control, a dust plate under him recording where his feet had actually been rather than where he believed they had been, and a question afterward about whether the two agreed. Karis had no escapement and used a table edge and her own knuckle, which was worse and, she said, sufficient, because the thing being rehearsed was not the instrument.

He ran it four times and held everything and produced four intervals that were within a quarter-beat of each other and four foot placements that were identical to within the width of his own boot.

"Again," said Karis.

He ran it twice more. Identical.

She put the pen down.

"Do it again and be *worse*," she said. "Deliberately. Give me the version you'd give a panel if you were frightened of them."

He did, and it took him three attempts to do it convincingly, which was itself an unpleasant piece of information, and when he had finally produced an interval that wandered by three-quarters of a beat, Karis looked at her own sheet for a while with an expression he did not enjoy.

"This is the failure I hadn't predicted and it's the one I'd have missed," she said. "You've been thinking about the ceiling. The whole of your planning for a fortnight has been about not going *above* the band, because above the band is a new question and a new question is the thing that kills you. Yes?"

"Yes."

"Look at what you just gave me." She turned the sheet. "That's not restraint. That's regression. Your baseline interval consistency was better than that, and it's *in the record*, in Gault's own log, in the trial-four column, and any evaluator in the building can lay this morning's sheet against it in four seconds. If you go into that room tomorrow and hold everything by feel, you will not land at the bottom of the band. You'll land under it. And a practitioner who is measurably worse than he was at his baseline is not a boy who passed quietly. He's a boy whose enrollment produced negative growth on an instrument, in a term, under supervision, with the Compact in the room."

"That's the trap."

"That's the trap, and it's a better trap than the one you've been guarding, because it's the one you'd walk into voluntarily." She wrote for a moment. "So the instruction is not *hold back*. The instruction is: land on the number. Not under it, not over it. Which is an enormously harder thing than either, and it's the actual work, and we now have three hours to practise it and one man with a bad knee to practise it against."

Trial five they did not attempt, because running everything again cold only means something at the end of the three hours that make it cold, and the read at working depth could not be rehearsed with at all — it was down to its stub and it was staying down. Karis logged both rather than pretending.

*Trial five: rehearsal not possible. Read at depth: not available, tonight or tomorrow; the day runs on the low read only. Plan for the day: if the wick goes past thin, stop the trial, out loud, and let the interval be logged. A logged interval is a line in a record. A drift is evidence.*

"You can just — stop a trial?" said Brom.

"He told me I could in the first month and I wrote it down," said Cael. "*If at any moment you cannot do a thing, say so, and we will record that you could not do it, and it will not count against you in any way except that it will be true. A refusal I have to infer is worth less to me than a refusal you state.*" He heard how exactly he had reproduced it and shrugged. "I've had that sentence in the notebook for five months. Tomorrow may be the first time I spend it."

---

The second hour found the real problem, and it found it because Karis ran the rehearsal properly, which meant she ran the gaps.

Between trial three and trial four she stopped and conferred with Brom about the log for ninety seconds, as a panel confers, with her back half-turned and the sheet held between them, and she made Cael stand at the mark and wait, because that was what the twentieth would do to him fourteen times.

He drifted at the eleventh gap.

He knew the instant it happened, which was new and was the only encouraging thing about it — eighty days of paying continuous rent had at least bought him the ability to feel the hand slide down the wick — but knowing did not stop it, and by the time he had his own edges back Lira had said, from the chair by the stove, "There."

"How far?"

"Not far. Thin at the edges." She had not moved and had not raised her voice and had, Cael realized, been watching nothing but him for two hours, from the one chair in the room, because it was the one thing she could do with a hip that would not hold her. "Cael, it's the eleventh time it's happened tonight."

He stared at her.

"You've caught four," she said. "I've counted eleven. Nine of them were inside three seconds and you got them back before they went anywhere and I don't think anyone but me would have seen them, and I want to be honest that I only see them because I have spent three years looking at you. But eleven. And every single one of them was in a gap."

"Every one."

"Every one. Not once during a trial. Not once while Brom was hitting you. Eleven times out of eleven, in the space between."

Karis was already turning to a fresh sheet, because a number of that shape was the sort of thing Karis existed for. "Eleven out of eleven isn't a tendency, it's a mechanism."

"I know what the mechanism is," Cael said. "Brom put it in the right column four weeks ago and I've had it in the wrong shape ever since. He told me the danger was attention fully occupied. It isn't. Attention fully occupied is *safe* — the drift never happens during a trial, it never happened during the drill in front of the rail, it never happened in the third exchange of a bout I was watching for four minutes without blinking." He heard the thing assemble as he said it. "It happens when the room stops asking anything of me. The hand goes down when nobody's pulling on it."

"So don't let the room stop," said Lira.

Everyone looked at her.

She was sitting sideways in the chair with her leading leg propped and a cloth of ice on the hip and her face the color of the wall, and she said it as though it were obvious, which for her it was.

"You've been thinking about this as a discipline problem for eighty days and you've built eleven techniques and they all work while somebody's hitting you and none of them work while you're standing on a mark waiting. That's because they're all techniques for *doing* something, and in the gap there's nothing to do." She shifted, and winced, and went on. "So make something. In the gap tomorrow, pick one person in that room and fight them. Not out loud. Read them. Rhythm, weight, breath, the fourth pass, all of it, on one person, for the whole ninety seconds, and don't stop when the panel comes back — just take a layer off and keep it running underneath."

"The read's down to almost nothing. I can't run—"

"Not the fragment. *You*." She said it with a flash of something that was nearly temper. "The notebook. The thing you built when you were fourteen with a fruit vendor and no Path at all and nothing in you at all. That's not a fragment, Cael, that's *yours*, it's the only thing in the whole of your ledger that didn't come off somebody else, and you can run it all day and it doesn't cost you a fragment, it doesn't cost you the idle state, and it will keep your attention pinned to the outside of your own skin for three hours, which is the entire problem."

The room was quiet for a moment.

"That's actually correct," said Karis, in the tone of a woman annoyed at not having got there first.

"I've been in that chair for two hours," said Lira, "and it's the only thing I've had to do."

She let that sit, and then said the other thing, which was the thing she had come up the residence steps on a ruined hip to say.

"And there's a second half to it and you're going to like it less. You're walking into a measured room tomorrow, and everything the four of us have done tonight is about being measured correctly, and that's right, and I've helped. But you're a fighter, and the person they measure has to still be you. If you go in there tomorrow and hold everything and give them a careful boy who lands at the bottom of Karis's band, you'll pass, and you'll come out of it slightly less like yourself, and you'll do it again the semester after that, and the semester after that." She looked at him levelly. "I've watched the system make people smaller by inches for their own protection. That's what happened to me at fourteen and nobody in the room meant any harm by it either."

"So what do I do?"

"Be the fighter," said Lira. "In a measured room. Both."

---

The folder was on the table because Karis had carried it up with the loaves, and Cael did not open it until the tenth bell, when Brom had gone to bed and Lira had fallen asleep sideways in the good chair and Karis was copying the night's sheets into a fair hand.

It was his returned coursework — the Shadow unit's field-observation exercises, three of them, submitted eleven days ago in the notebook's driest register, honest and incomplete. The wing had marked them and returned them the way the wing returned everything, in a folder with the title line checked and two marks in the ledger.

Inside the back cover there was a slip of paper that was not part of the coursework.

It was assessment-office paper, cut down. It was written in assessment-office hand, which is to say the hand every clerk in the wing was trained to write in, which is to say a hand that belonged to nobody. There was no salutation, no signature, no date, and nothing on the sheet that would have meant one thing to any person in the building who was not already holding the thought it answered.

Four sentences.

*Panels see what they expect prepared for. Show growth where the baseline predicts it. Surprise them exactly once, small, early — a watcher who finds one thing stops hunting for a second.*

Cael read it once and stopped breathing for a moment and read it a second time.

Then he sat at the table with a piece of paper in his hand and did what he was going to do about it, and did it in about eleven seconds, because there was nothing to decide.

He learned the four sentences. That took perhaps a minute; they were built to be learned. He checked the slip on both sides for anything else and there was nothing else. He got up, opened the common room stove's door, put the slip flat on the coals, and watched it go — the edge catching, the middle darkening, the whole of it up in about four seconds and gone into ash that was indistinguishable from the ash it landed on.

Then he shut the door and stood there.

Fifteen years of somebody's trade, cut down onto a slip of wing paper and put in the back of a coursework folder by a man who could be ended by it — and the only thing Cael could do in return, the entire sum of what was available to him, was to make sure the paper stopped existing inside a minute of being read.

Karis had looked up. She had a pen in her hand and had stopped writing with it.

"Was that—"

She got two words in and stopped, herself, without any help, and Cael watched her do it: the researcher's face, the question forming, and then a decision arriving over the top of it that was not a researcher's decision at all.

She put the pen back down on the sheet.

"No," she said. "Never mind."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me for that either." She went back to copying. After a moment, without looking up: "Is it useful?"

"It's the best four sentences anybody has given me about tomorrow, and I'd have paid a year for them."

"Then use them," said Karis, and wrote, and that was the whole of what was ever said about it in that room or any other.

---

He worked the four sentences into the plan in the last half-hour, at the table, with Lira asleep and Karis's fair copy drying.

*Show growth where the baseline predicts it* was Karis's band, arrived at by a different road, which was the first thing that made him trust the slip: two people who had never spoken about it had built the same instruction out of opposite materials.

*Surprise them exactly once, small, early* was the piece he did not have and could not have built, and it took him twenty minutes to work out what the surprise should be, and the answer was the only capability he owned that was on a record, unremarkable in itself, and had never once been demonstrated in this building.

The Ember-adjacent ignition point. It was in Greyvane's panel notes. It was in the adjudication transcript that half the continent had read. It was, on the page, the least impressive thing in his ledger — a point of ignition, small, requiring contact, expensive in a way nobody watching would ever see. Nobody at Halcenvane had seen it. Gault's instruments had never measured it. It was, in Karis's language, entirely inside the record's permission and entirely outside the room's expectation, which made it the only object in his possession that could be spent once, early, at a cost of nothing, to give eleven experienced watchers a thing to find.

And it was his to place. The provision measured what an enrollee elected to put on the floor — Gault had said so at the baseline, in those words — and an election could name its own place in the order.

*A watcher who finds one thing stops hunting for a second.*

He wrote the whole revised order out twice, burned nothing else, and went to bed at the eleventh bell.

---

*The night before,* he wrote at the window, with the lamp turned down and the yard below him empty and swept.

*Karis gave me a band, with the arithmetic checked twice by a registrar who wasn't told what it was for. Brom gave me the thing nobody says: let it cost you visibly, because careful is the most visible state there is and I have been careful my whole life and never once known what it looks like from outside. Lira gave me a mechanism for the gaps and then told me not to become smaller by inches in a room where nobody means any harm.*

*And there was a fourth thing tonight and I've put it where it goes and it isn't going on this page.*

*Nobody gave me a gift. Last year at Greyvane they'd have given me gifts. This year all four of them handed me a piece of the plan, because the problem is collective now and everybody in that room knew it, and I think that's the difference between people who love you and people who are in it with you, and I've somehow got both, which I did not have two years ago and did not expect ever to have.*

He stopped, and turned the lamp down another turn, and wrote the part he had been circling since the eighth bell.

*Q. asked me a year and a half ago whether I could* not *do it, in front of people who wish me harm. I told her I didn't know, because at the time it was true. Tomorrow at the fourth bell I answer her: three hours, six trials, five things on the record and one thing that has an idle state and wants deployment in ordinary moments, held at nothing, in the most heavily watched room I will ever stand in.*

*Except I don't think the man watching wishes me harm.*

*I've had five days of him now. He asked Gault three questions about the calibration interval. He took five notes in a nine-hour records session and every one was about whether this building's paperwork can be trusted. He watched two exchanges of a placement bout, got what he came for in six minutes, and left before the result. Not one of those is the behaviour of a man who wants me to fail. Every one is the behaviour of a man who wants the number to be real.*

*He doesn't wish me harm. He wishes me* measured.

*And I have spent the whole of tonight failing to work out which of those is more dangerous, and I've decided the honest answer is that I don't know, and that I'm going to find out at the fourth bell whether or not I've decided.*

*Sleep. Fight in the morning.*

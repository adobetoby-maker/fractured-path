# Chapter 22 — The Interview

Bracken had the clause out on the counter before Cael finished asking the question, which meant he had put it there the previous evening and had been waiting since the second bell for somebody to come and want it.

"Charter, fourth part, provisions and exceptions, subsection eleven." He turned the volume so it faced the right way up. "*An inspecting delegation may examine any enrollee whose enrollment rests on a provision or exception, in the presence of the institution's counsel and the officer of record for that enrollment, on the record, at a time set by the institution.* It has been in the charter since the charter. It is exercised at roughly one inspection in three, and this academy has had it exercised on it twice, both times about foreign-national enrollments, both times without incident."

"So it's ordinary."

"It is entirely ordinary." Bracken squared the volume against the counter's lip. "It is also the only piece of this inspection in which a Compact officer is entitled to ask you a question directly, and I would be doing you a disservice if I let the first sentence stand without the second."

"Who sets the time?"

"I do. I have set it for the twenty-first, at the fourth bell, in the records hall's east room, which is the smallest room in the building with three chairs in it." Bracken made a note beside the clause. "The institution's counsel attends. Magister Gault attends as your officer of record. A clerk of mine logs it, not one of theirs, which took me eleven minutes and a written objection to secure and which I want you to know I secured."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me. Thanking me suggests I did you a favour." Bracken closed the volume. "I did the filing correctly. That is the entire service on offer in this office and it is worth more than the other kind."

---

There was nothing to prepare with.

That was the whole of the problem, and Cael sat with it for a day and a half and did not solve it, because it did not have a solution — it had a decision, and the decision was not the one he had spent his life practising.

He had built a page on every opponent he had ever faced. Every single one. Renn's guard, Talis's ground vibration, Sarel's closing speed, Reydan's whole terrible architecture, Edran's seam. He had built pages on assessors, on wardens, on a boarding-house owner, on a chancellor. He had built pages on nine of the eleven names on the delegation's manifest inside four days, out of the library's holdings, because a chartered academy holds the Compact's published findings and a published finding carries the panel that made it.

The eleventh name had returned three references in the whole library, all procedural, none attached to a single word the man had ever said.

So Cael sat in the common room on the evening of the twentieth with his hip packed and a blank sheet in front of him and the other three arranged around the table in various attitudes of not-being-helpful-on-purpose, and made the only preparation available, which was to decide what he was going to be.

"You've written nothing," said Karis.

"There's nothing to write. I've got no page on him and I'm not going to have one by the fourth bell tomorrow." He put the pen down. "So the question isn't what he's going to ask. The question is what I'm going to do when he asks it."

"And?"

"Tell the truth."

Lira, who had been lying on the bench along the wall with a forearm over her eyes, took the forearm off.

"Say the whole sentence," she said. "Because that one isn't the whole sentence and you know it isn't."

She was right, and he had known she would say so, which was one of the reasons he had said it in front of her.

"Tell the truth about everything the record already holds, and where he asks about something the record doesn't hold, say that I'm not going to answer, out loud, in words, and let it be logged." Cael turned the blank sheet over. "That's the change. Every other room I've been in, I've handled the thing I couldn't say by giving a true answer with a hole in it. Trial four, yesterday. Gault, at the baseline. The hearing. It's worked every time and it has cost me something every time and I've been writing the cost down for two years.

"It won't work in this room. He isn't Gault and he isn't counsel and he isn't Coss. If I hand him a true sentence with a hole in it, he will find the hole, and he will not say so, and I will have handed the best reader on the continent a map of exactly where my architecture keeps its walls. A refusal tells him one thing: that there is something. He already knows there's something — everybody knows there's something, it's why he's in the building. A hole in a sentence tells him *where*."

Brom, from the chair, said: "So refuse in the open."

"Refuse in the open, and pay for it, and let it be written down that I did."

Karis had her hands flat on the table, which was what she did instead of frowning.

"I want to state the risk," she said. "Because I'd rather state it now than at three in the morning. A logged refusal by a provision-status enrollee to a Compact inspector, on the record, during an enrollment-basis review, is a document. It goes into the inspection file and it is read by people who are looking for reasons. It is the single most quotable thing anybody could produce out of this entire inspection."

"Yes."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"I'm doing it anyway, because the alternative is a lie that a professional detects, and a detected lie in that room is worse than a refusal in that room by about a hundred to one." He looked at her. "And because there's a second reason and I want it said out loud, so you can all argue with it if it's wrong.

"He watched Gault run three hours of honest instruments on me yesterday. Everything that man has seen of me for eight days has been the institution behaving exactly as it behaves when nobody's watching. Withrow's whole strategy, Bracken's file, Rooke's supervision signatures, Gault's calibration schedule. Nobody on this bluff has performed one inch for him." Cael sat back. "If I go into that room and become the only person at Halcenvane who performs, I break the only thing the academy has actually been showing him."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"That's a good reason," said Karis, at length, with the formality she used for things she intended to stand behind. "It's also convenient. I want both of those in the minute."

"Put both in."

Lira put her forearm back over her eyes.

"Fourth bell," she said. "Don't be clever. You're clever when you're frightened and it makes you sound like a solicitor."

---

The east room of the records hall was twelve feet by fourteen, with one window on the river side, a table that had been made for a larger room, and five chairs.

Cael came in at three minutes before the fourth bell and found four of them occupied, and that was the first thing he registered and the first thing he wrote up afterward: they had all arrived before him. Withrow's counsel at the near side with her document case shut. Magister Gault beside her with a folder he had not opened, which was Gault's way of announcing that the folder existed and would not be needed. Bracken's clerk at the corner with a log sheet, a ruled column already drawn down the left of it for the pendulum count, and his pen already inked.

And across the table, with the window behind him and nothing on the table in front of him at all, Archmarshal Vastin.

Cael stopped at the door for perhaps half a second and used it, because the light was wrong and he wanted to know whether it was wrong on purpose.

It was not. The window was on the river side because the east room's window had been on the river side since the hall was built, and the delegation's officer had taken the chair that faced the door because that was the chair on that side of the table, and the effect — that Cael would spend thirty minutes looking into a grey afternoon at a man whose face was the darkest thing in the room — was an accident of architecture that a lesser man would have engineered and this one had simply been handed.

*Or he took the seat because it was the seat,* Cael thought, sitting down, *and I have just spent half a second building a story about a chair. Careful.*

"Enrollee," said the counsel. "For your benefit and the log's: this is an examination under the charter's fourth part, subsection eleven. It is on the record. You may decline to answer any question, and a declined question is logged as declined and nothing further is entered about it. If at any point you wish to consult me, say so and the log stops. The examination is set at thirty minutes by the institution and the institution will end it at thirty minutes."

"Understood."

"Archmarshal, the room is yours."

Vastin folded his hands on the table. They were an old man's hands, square, with a scar across the back of the left one that had been there for decades and had been sewn by somebody competent.

"Thank you." He said it to the counsel, and it was not a formality — it had the plain weight of a man who had been given something and was noting it. Then he looked at Cael, level, and Cael discovered that being looked at directly by this man was not, in itself, unpleasant at all, which he had not expected and did not like.

"I have eleven questions," Vastin said. "I wrote them on the road and I have not added to them since I arrived. If we finish early we will finish early. Are you comfortable?"

"Yes."

"Your hip is not."

"No," said Cael, after a beat. "It isn't."

"Then move it when you need to and don't sit still to be polite. Nobody in this room is measuring your posture." He did not wait for an answer to that. "First question. State your enrollment basis in your own words, without reference to the document."

"I'm enrolled under a pre-standardization charter clause that lets an institution evaluate a practitioner by demonstration where no registry classification applies. The clause was superseded in its terminology by the standardization directive and was never repealed. My enrollment is a standing basis, not a waiver — I demonstrate before the assessment office's panel every semester and the enrollment continues on the strength of the demonstration."

"Who found the clause?"

"An enrollee of this academy. Karis, Ember Path, Iron Rank Three. She spent eleven weeks proving it was never repealed, at Greyvane, in a founding-era shelf, before either of us had heard of Halcenvane."

"Second question." Not a pause; not a note; nothing. "What does the enrollee consider the weakest element of his baseline?"

Cael had been ready for a version of this and had decided, at the common-room table, to give the real answer rather than the good one.

"The landing beat."

"Describe it."

"The evasion framework costs me a locked interval at the end of the displacement. A fixed half-breath where I've landed and can't change direction. It's the same length every time, it's been the same length for three years, and I've never once shortened it on purpose." He shifted the hip, because he had been told to. "Everything I've been hit by in three years, I've been hit by inside that interval. If somebody who knows me is going to beat me, that's where it happens, and Lira has done it eleven times."

"And yesterday you did not pay it."

The room went very quiet. Bracken's clerk did not stop writing, because a clerk who stops writing is a clerk making an editorial decision.

"Yesterday I paid it into my own hip instead of into the floor," said Cael. "That's not the same as not paying it."

"No," Vastin agreed. "It isn't. Third question, and it follows from that: can you do it again?"

"No."

"You are certain."

"I've tried to shorten that interval deliberately in training for three years and failed every time, including four weeks ago." Cael held the level look and did not decorate it. "Yesterday it happened once, under a fault nobody planned, and I have no method for it. If I told you I could repeat it I'd be telling you I had a technique. I have an accident and a sore hip."

Vastin considered that for perhaps two seconds — genuinely considered it, the way a man weighs an instrument reading he did not expect and has not yet decided whether to trust.

"That is the correct answer," he said, "and I want to be clear that I mean it is the *accurate* one, not the clever one. Fourth question. Who taught you to prepare?"

"A circuit," said Cael. "And everyone who beat me."

For the first time, something moved in the man's face. Not a smile. It was closer to what happened in Gault's face when a trial produced a clean number — the small settling of a professional who has been given a usable reading.

"Expand the second half."

"I lost eleven of my first fourteen bouts in Ardenmere. After each one I wrote down what the person who beat me had done, in as much detail as I could get, and then I went and found out whether I'd got it right." He shrugged. "It turned into a habit and then into a method. I've never beaten anybody twice for the same reason, because I only ever learn anything the first time from somebody who's just hit me."

"Who taught you the schedule's structure?"

"An archivist named Prynn taught me how the strata sit. A ledger-keeper named Vell taught me that a record is only worth what its worst entry is worth. Nobody taught me the schedule. I read it for three weeks and Karis read it with me and we found the seam because we were the only two people in the building who had a reason to read the whole thing instead of the part that applied to us."

"Fifth. Why concede the category?"

Cael sat with that one.

It was, he understood immediately, not a question about the hearing. It was a question about a decision he had made inside about four seconds on a Greyvane afternoon, which had never once been discussed in a corridor and had never appeared in any commentary on the transcript that he had read — and there had been a great deal of commentary on the transcript, because anybody could buy it for six marks.

He had conceded, in front of a Compact adjudication panel, that he did not belong in any category on the schedule. Every person advising him had wanted him to argue that he belonged in one. It had looked, at the time and afterward, like the losing move.

"Because arguing I was in a category meant arguing about *me*," he said. "And the moment the argument is about me, it's an argument about a person's nature, and a panel can decide anything it likes about a person's nature and there's no page you can point at afterward. If I conceded the category, the only thing left to argue about was the text. Text sits still. You can read it out loud and everyone in the room has to look at the same words."

"You gave up the sympathetic ground."

"I gave up the ground where I'd have had to win an opinion. I kept the ground where I only had to be right."

Vastin looked at him for a moment.

"Sixth question," he said. "You keep records. The monitoring product describes an extensive private notation habit. What are they for?"

Cael felt the whole architecture of his life go still.

There were four true answers and three of them were catastrophic, and the fourth was true and complete and gave nothing whatsoever away, and he had it ready because he had written it in the front of a notebook two years ago for entirely different reasons.

"So I can't lie to myself later."

The clerk's pen went on.

"Explain."

"If I write down what a thing cost me on the day it cost it, then in six months when I want the memory to be flattering, there's a page that disagrees with me." Cael did not look away. "Everything that has ever gone badly for me has gone badly because a document said something and there was no way to check it. I decided a long time ago that at minimum I'd be able to check *my own*."

He waited for the follow-up. It was the obvious follow-up; it was the follow-up anyone would ask; counsel across the table had already drawn breath to hear it, and Gault, beside her, had gone entirely still in the way that meant he had understood what the next question would be and had no lawful way to prevent it.

*Records of what,* Cael thought. *Ask me records of what.*

Vastin did not ask.

He inclined his head about a quarter of an inch, in the manner of a man taking receipt of an answer, and said, "Seventh question. In your own assessment, how long can this academy hold you?"

And that was the moment Cael understood what he was dealing with, and it took him the rest of the interview to get his hands steady about it.

*He didn't ask.*

*He knew exactly where the next question went. He got to the edge of it and he stopped, and the stopping was not a failure of nerve and it wasn't courtesy and it wasn't a trap being set for later. He stopped because the answer was not part of what he came to evaluate.*

*A warden asks everything he is permitted to ask. An assessor asks everything the form requires. This man asked six questions, arrived at the edge of the largest thing in my life, looked at it, and decided it was not his business.*

*I have never once in my life been in a room with somebody who could have taken something and didn't.*

He answered the seventh question honestly, and the eighth, and the ninth, and could not afterward remember much about any of them except that one had been about the Crown ladder's bracket structure and that he had said, without dressing it up, that the ladder sorted by paper and that two of his friends had spent a year in a cage built for people who did not exist.

Vastin had written none of it down, because there was nothing on the table in front of him and there never had been.

---

The clerk's column showed twenty-two minutes when the ninth question closed.

"Two more," said Vastin, "and then you have eight minutes owed to you and I would rather give them back than fill them."

"Archmarshal." Cael put both hands flat on the table, which was Karis's gesture and he had picked it up without meaning to. "Before the last two. May I ask one?"

The counsel's head came round. Gault's did not.

"You may ask," said Vastin. "I may decline, and the log will say I declined, and you should take that as a genuine possibility and not a courtesy."

"Understood." Cael took the breath. "What is the Compact evaluating? Me, or the provision?"

The room was quiet enough to hear the river.

Vastin considered the question with the same weight he had given every instrument reading in three hours the day before — no change in posture, no shift of the hands, nothing performed at all.

"The Compact filed no question," he said. "There is no instrument attached to my presence here, no separate business, and no directive I am executing. Your counsel will have told you that. It is true, and I am aware that it is also the least believable true thing anybody has said to you this month." A pause. "I came to see whether the record and the practitioner match."

"That's the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of my mandate. What follows from what I find is somebody else's arithmetic and I do not do it for them, because an evaluator who does the arithmetic starts producing findings shaped like the answer." He let that sit for a beat, and then said, in exactly the same register: "You expected an enforcer."

"I've met the enforcers."

"I imagine you have." Vastin unfolded his hands. "Then take one thing away from this room, because it is the only thing I have to offer you and it costs me nothing to say and may be worth something to you in ten years.

"Wardens enforce. Assessors comply. I evaluate. Those are three different animals, they are wearing very similar coats, and every one of them is going to be in a room with you again. The difference will matter to you more, not less, as you rise — I would encourage you to learn to read it."

Cael sat with that for as long as he was allowed to, which was about two seconds.

"How do I tell them apart?"

"By what they want from the answer." Vastin was already reaching for the last two questions, and he asked them: one about the assay clause's semester requirement and whether Cael found it burdensome, to which the answer was no; and one about whether he had anything he wished entered into the record that had not been asked, to which the answer, after a moment, was also no.

"Then we are finished at twenty-nine minutes." He stood. He did not push the chair in, because a man of his altitude did not have to and because — Cael thought, watching — it had genuinely not occurred to him either way. "Counsel, thank you. Magister, thank you; your calibration schedule is the best-kept document in this building and I have read a great many of them. Clerk."

The clerk looked up, visibly startled to be addressed.

"Your log is clean and your column is ruled. Both of those are rarer than they should be."

He went out.

Cael sat in a twelve-by-fourteen room with a grey window and two adults and a clerk, and found that his hands wanted to shake and that he was going to have to sit for a moment before he stood up.

Gault opened the folder he had not opened, looked at nothing inside it, and closed it again.

"Well," he said. "That is the most competent half-hour I have sat through in eleven years, and I did not say one word in it."

---

The delegation left on the morning of the twenty-third of Reaping, in the same three carriages, in a light rain.

Cael watched from the Crown yard's upper tier, which was legitimate and empty at that hour, because he had watched them arrive from there and it seemed dishonest to watch the departure from anywhere more comfortable.

Withrow's counsel gave the academy the prediction in the records hall the previous evening, in front of nine people, in the flat voice she used for everything.

"Favourable in substance. Neutral in tone." She had shut the seal-case as she said it. "There will be one clerical remark, because there is always one clerical remark, and I would put money on the fire-watch's ledger again. The enrollment-basis finding will read that the clause is unrepealed and the enrollment conforms to its language. That is as warm as an inspection finding is capable of being. It will not say we did well. Inspection findings do not say that. It will say that there was nothing to say, which for a mid-tier academy that has been under an Archmarshal's eyes for eleven days is—"

"A triumph," said Withrow.

"I was going to say 'a triumph,'" said the counsel, "and then I remembered that you dislike adjectives."

"I dislike adjectives in documents," said Withrow. "Say it out loud all you like."

Rooke found Brom at the covered walk's end that same afternoon, which Cael heard about second-hand and got verbatim, because Brom had a memory for exact wording that he deployed roughly twice a year.

Rooke had stopped, looked at the standings board, and said: "Your cohort standing holds. So, apparently, does your friend's paperwork. Noted."

And gone.

"That's it?" Lira had said.

"That's it."

"That's *nothing*."

"That's not nothing." Brom had rolled the shoulder. "He's been arguing with this institution about that enrollment since the first week of term, on the record, in a faculty session, out loud. He has now said, out loud, in a corridor, that it held. He's not going to apologise. He's never going to apologise. He adjusted, and he made sure somebody heard him do it." A pause. "Rooke doesn't say things to be nice. He says things so they're on the record. That was on the record."

Assessor Ilsev was the last of the manifest to come down the records-hall steps, carrying her own case, which nobody had ever successfully offered to take.

She stopped at the bottom of them, which put her about fifteen feet from the tier's stair, and looked up, and Cael — who had spent nine weeks failing to decide whether she was the most dangerous person on the manifest or the best thing on it — came down because it would have been cowardice not to.

"Enrollee."

"Assessor."

"I am going to say one thing and it is not a kindness, so do not receive it as one." She set the case down on the step. "The enrollment-basis session found that the clause is unrepealed and that your enrollment conforms to its language. That is not a finding about you. It is a finding about a sentence written by people who are long dead and who did not know you would exist. If the sentence had said something else, the finding would have said something else, and nothing about you would have been different."

"I know."

"I believe you do, which is why I am saying it." She picked the case back up. "Most people in your position want the text to be on their side because it means something. It does not mean anything. It is simply on your side, which is worth an enormous amount and is not the same as being loved by anybody." Something in her face moved, very small. "Hold on to the distinction. People who confuse the two are unbearable in a hearing room and they lose."

She went to the carriage.

Cael went back up to the tier, and the rain came on properly, and Havel came out of the records hall last of all with the document chests, checking the seals on each one against a list, and did not look up at the tier — not once, again, in eleven days, which by now Cael had stopped filing as caution and had started filing as something else that he did not have a name for and did not intend to guess at.

The carriages went down the bluff road at a working pace and were gone inside a quarter of an hour.

---

He wrote it that night, at the window, with the new pen, and it took him three attempts to get the first sentence, which had almost never happened.

*He never asked what I am.*

*Everybody asks what I am. Coss asked it with a coat on. Havel asked it in an Ardenmere corridor with too-new boots on and was told to stand down. Quenna asked it and then built a track around not needing the answer. Gault told me to my face that he refuses to ask it and explained why, and I have thought about that explanation once a week since. Ephram asked it in front of sixty people by announcing he was going to wait for an answer. Five hundred and forty people on this bluff have their own answer and eleven of the versions are wrong.*

*Eleven questions in twenty-nine minutes, from the highest-graded person any of us will ever be in a building with, and not one of them was that question.*

*He asked whether the record and the practitioner match.*

*I have been turning that over since the fourth bell and here is where it lands, and I do not like where it lands.*

*A person asks what you are when they don't know. A person checks whether the record matches when they think they already know and want to see if the file's been kept properly.*

*So somewhere above all of this, what I am is already assumed. Assumed, written down, and filed at a clearance where Karis and I couldn't find one published word of it in a library of forty-one thousand volumes. The open question was never me. The open question is what to do about the assumption, and that is not his arithmetic, and he said so, and I believe him.*

*That's the thing I keep circling and I am going to write it as plainly as I can so I stop dressing it up:*

*The system isn't confused about me.*

*Confused systems ask. This one checks.*

*I have spent two years believing I was an anomaly nobody could classify — a gap in a schedule, a thing that fell between the lines because the lines were drawn badly. That was the whole shape of it. That was what I argued in a hearing room and it was true and it held and I would argue it again tomorrow.*

*But a gap doesn't get a designation. A gap doesn't get a monitoring line three grades above the officer who briefed it. A gap doesn't get an Archmarshal in inspection greys asking six clean questions and then declining, on his own judgment, to ask the seventh.*

*I need to understand what it thinks it already knows.*

*And then I need to understand why it built itself to know it.*

*That's not this year's question. This year's question got answered on the twentieth of Reaping, in fourteen words, with a stamp on it.*

*That one's next.*

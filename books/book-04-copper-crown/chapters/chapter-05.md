# Chapter 5 — Seln

The file came to him in a room without a window, which was ordinary, and he read it standing, which was habit.

Chairs made a man settle. Settling made him ask questions, and questions in a briefing room were a form of self-description — every one of them told the officer across the desk something about what its asker had already decided. Seln had worked out at twenty-four that the directorates preferred a man who took the paper, read it once, and left, and had spent the fifteen years since converting that preference into a currency he could actually spend. Ask nothing, and they sent you the assignments nobody wanted discussed. Those were usually the interesting ones, and always the ones with the least supervision.

He read the brief in the order he read every brief: last page first, for the authorizing signature and the reporting line, because those two facts determined what the rest of the document was permitted to be honest about. The signature was three grades above the officer sitting in front of him. The reporting line ran up past that officer entirely. He turned back to the beginning and read forward, and by the second page he understood that the assignment had been drafted by someone competent, which was rarer than it should have been and worth noting on its own.

*Subject: enrollee, Halcenvane Academy, Ostrand. Age fifteen. Classification: none of record. Adjudication of record: Greyvane, dismissed against petitioner; subject's status affirmed by charter reading. Monitoring status: active, resourced, visible rotation, continuous since adjudication.*

And then, four lines further down, the sentence that made the whole document worth the paper:

*Visible rotation has produced conduct data of high volume and negligible value. Subject modifies observable behavior in response to observation with a consistency that suggests deliberate management rather than nervousness. Recommend covered coverage.*

Seln read that twice and felt something close to professional approval, which he permitted himself because there was no one in the room qualified to notice it. Whoever had written *deliberate management rather than nervousness* had gone to the trouble of understanding the difference. Most of the analysts he'd fed in fifteen years could not have told the two apart on a good day, and had built entire careers on not needing to. A watched man who behaved is not the same animal as a watched man who behaves *at* you. The first is frightened. The second is talking, in a language with no words in it, and the only correct response is to stop standing where he can see you.

He had written a version of that sentence himself, once, a long time ago, in a colder country, about somebody else. He did not think about that.

The officer across the desk said something about the timeline, and something else about the reporting cadence — monthly product, quarterly summary, exception reports as warranted — and Seln listened to the parts that constrained him and let the rest go by. Then the officer said the sentence that briefing officers always eventually said, in the tone they always used, as though it were an insight they had brought with them into the room.

"The Compact does not know what he is."

"No," Seln agreed, which was not a concession, and gathered the pages.

---

The cover was already built when they handed it to him, which he took as a sign that the drafting had been done by the same competent hand: teaching assistant, assessment office, Halcenvane Academy, attached to the staff of the magister who administered the academy's provision-status evaluations.

It was, he thought, an unusually good choice.

A teaching assistant in an assessment office was a person made of access. He would hold the evaluation calendar, which meant he would know where the subject was required to be, weeks out, without ever needing to ask. He would process training-floor scheduling, which meant he would know where the subject *chose* to be. He would file, compile, and re-copy the assessment wing's paper, which meant that everything the institution formally recorded about the subject would pass through his hands twice — once going in, once coming out — as a matter of the post's ordinary duties, witnessed by anyone who cared to watch, and utterly unremarkable.

It also meant he would have to do the job. Properly. That was the part the officer across the desk had not mentioned, because officers across desks rarely understood that a cover is not a costume but a second employment. A teaching assistant who mislaid a schedule became a topic of conversation in a staff room. A teaching assistant who was slow with the copying became a small recurring irritation that people described to each other. Every complaint was a memory, and memories were the thing he existed to not create. He would file cleanly, copy accurately, arrive early, and leave last, for as long as the assignment ran, because competence at the cover job was the cheapest and most reliable form of invisibility available to anyone.

His own file, if he had ever been asked to summarize it in the register he'd have used for anyone else's, would have read short. Fifteen years in the service. Nineteen postings. Shadow Path, Bronze-tier, Rank not worth recording. Product rated consistently reliable by four separate review cycles. No disciplinary entries. No commendations either, which was the same fact written twice.

The nineteen postings had been in nineteen places, and he could have listed them in order, and did not, even to himself. He had learned in the fifth year that a man in his trade who kept his own history in narrative form eventually started telling it, and that a man who kept it as a number was safe. Nineteen. That was the whole account. Whatever had happened inside each of them had been filed, and the files were the Compact's, and he had never once gone back to read one.

The Path had been rated exactly right, which almost nothing in his life had been.

Shadow Path at Bronze-tier was not invisibility, whatever the folklore said, and Seln had spent a decade and a half being quietly grateful that the folklore said it. What the Path actually did, at his tier, was manage an observer's attention budget from the outside. It suppressed the small signals that made a human eye return to a place a second time — the flicker of motion at the edge of vision, the mismatch between a body and its background, the thousand tiny wrongnesses that a crowd's collective attention adds up and hands to somebody as a reason to look again. He did not vanish. He simply cost nothing to overlook.

The prices were exact and he knew them the way an old carpenter knows the weight of his own hammer. Suppression ran on sustained attention: he had to be watching the room in order for the room to not watch him, and four hours of it left him with a flat grey fatigue behind the eyes that no amount of sleep fully cleared. It failed absolutely against anyone already looking directly at him — the Path could keep an eye from arriving, and could do nothing at all once it had. It failed against instruments, which had no attention to manage. And it failed, comprehensively and in a way most of his trade never learned because most of his trade never met the case, against anybody who had thought to look for an absence instead of a presence.

The movement-masking half was cruder and more useful. It folded motion into whatever pattern the surroundings already carried — a corridor's traffic, a crowd's drift, the rhythm of a room's ordinary business — so that his own crossing registered as part of the ambient rather than a departure from it. Its price was that it required an ambient to fold into. In an empty corridor at night, Shadow Path at Bronze bought a man precisely nothing, which was the first thing they taught and the last thing anyone believed until it had cost them.

High enough to work. Low enough to be forgettable. In his trade, that was not a ceiling. It was a fit.

---

He spent the four days before departure building the man who would arrive.

Handwriting first, because handwriting was the thing that survived him in a building. He had six of them and chose the fourth: upright, small, slightly cramped, the hand of a person taught to write in a provincial scriptorium and never encouraged out of it. Legible. Slow-looking. Nobody had ever once commented on it, in three postings, which was the entire requirement.

Accent second. He set it a hundred and some miles north of Ostrand and let it be slightly out of date, because a regional accent that was current invited a conversation about home and a regional accent that was ten years stale invited nothing at all — people assumed the speaker had left and did not want to discuss it, and the assumption did his work for him.

Then the clothes, which needed to be one grade below the post's dignity, because a teaching assistant dressed to his station looked ambitious and an ambitious man was a story people followed. Then the small manufactured incompetence, which he'd used since his third posting and had never seen fail: he would be visibly, mildly bad at exactly one thing. Arithmetic in his head. He'd fumble a sum in front of the office within his first week and let somebody correct him, and thereafter the assessment wing would have a settled opinion about him that they had reached themselves, which was the strongest kind, and which occupied the shelf where a real opinion might otherwise have grown.

The residence card he filled out on the last morning, in the fourth hand, in the mid-tier academies' standard form: *Path: Shadow. Tier: Bronze.* True on both counts. He had learned in the second year that the best cover documents were the ones that lied about as little as possible, and had watched three officers with more imagination than sense burn themselves down by inventing details they then had to remember.

Everything he owned went into two cases.

The first was the ordinary one, and its contents were part of the cover: clothes, a shaving kit, four books chosen for how they would look on a shelf, a set of copying pens, and a wooden box of the small domestic clutter that a person accumulates and that a person's room is expected to contain.

The second was smaller, older, banded at the corners, and locked. He set it into the bottom of the ordinary case and packed the ordinary case around it, which is what he had done nineteen times, and closed the lid, and did not open it, then or later.

He was thirty-eight. He had reached the stage of a long service where the craft was entirely intact and the appetite had gone out of it — where he could still do the work better than anyone likely to review it, and no longer wanted anything from having done it. He noticed this about himself the way he'd have noticed it in a subject: as a datum, filed, of unclear operational significance.

Then he went to Ostrand.

---

Halcenvane stood on a bluff above the river with the flat confidence of a place built on purpose, and Seln read it, on his first walk through, the only way he ever read anything.

Not as architecture. As a positioning problem.

Three training halls, which meant three sets of doors, six approaches, and a predictable convergence at the hour when a session ended and a session began. The assessment wing at the north end, one corridor deep behind its own low wall — two doors but a single gate in the wall, which was excellent for a man whose post was inside it and inconvenient for anyone wanting to watch it. The lecture complex, four floors, staircases at both ends, sightlines that ran the length of every landing. Residence quadrangles arranged around courts, which meant that anyone crossing between them could be seen from ninety windows and would therefore never be seen from any, because ninety windows is the same as none.

And at the center, the Crown yard: a tiered bout floor sunk four feet into the ground, stone benching rising on three sides and the faculty stand closing the fourth, seating eleven hundred by the tiers he counted from the rail on his first evening. The yard was the best position on the campus and also the worst — best because from the upper tier a man could see the whole floor and half the approaches, worst because everyone else in the building already knew that, which meant the upper tier was where attention gathered, and attention gathering is where a watcher gets found.

He would use it anyway. There was no other position that did the work. He would simply use it correctly: never the same seat, never the same level twice running, never in the emptiness at the end of a bench where the eye goes, always in the third or fourth body of a cluster, where the cluster's own shape did the suppression for him and the Path only had to top it up.

He mapped the campus over three days without ever appearing to look at it, which was not difficult, because he had a legitimate reason to walk every corridor in the place — a new teaching assistant carries paper, and paper goes everywhere.

He saw the subject for the first time on the fourth day.

It was the dispersal after a closed panel session in the assessment wing: doors opening, four faculty and one enrollee coming out into the corridor's ordinary traffic, the boy last and in no hurry, carrying nothing, walking with a slight favoring in the left leg that Seln clocked and filed without deciding what it meant.

Seln was thirty feet away with a stack of forms against his chest, and he did not look at him.

This was doctrine, and it was the doctrine that separated officers who lasted from officers who got made in their fourth week. The eye is the thing people feel. Not the presence, not the proximity, not the repetition — the eye. A subject will pass the same stranger nine times in a corridor and register nothing; the tenth time, if the stranger's gaze crosses his, the whole prior nine assemble themselves in the subject's memory into a pattern that was never there before. So: no direct observation of the subject in the first month. Peripheral only. Position, presence, ambient. Let the corridor tell you what the corridor knows. There would be time later — months of it — and by then he would be furniture, and furniture is permitted to have eyes.

He carried the forms past and into the office and set them on the copying table and did not think about the boy again for two hours, which was also doctrine.

The one thing he did note, later, entering the day's ordinary observations into the ordinary log a teaching assistant keeps and anyone might read, was that the corridor had been full and the corridor had been *looking*. Every third person in it had marked the boy's passage — a glance, a turned head, the small pause of somebody deciding whether to nod. Fame, of the local variety, thick and fresh.

Good, Seln thought, without feeling anything much about it. A famous subject is an easy subject. A hundred people watch him, so a hundred and one is not a number anybody counts.

---

Cael's training-floor allocation came up for its second filing three days after the baseline demonstration, and he walked it over to the assessment wing himself rather than sending it through the residence clerk, because he wanted to see the assessment wing on an ordinary day.

He had seen it on a panel day. Panel days told you what an institution wanted to look like. Ordinary days told you what it was.

The wing was one long corridor, north end, with the apparatus rooms opening off the western side and the records rooms off the eastern, and it smelled of three things in a fixed proportion he could have written down after four minutes: lamp black, machine oil, and the dry mineral smell of chalk-plate dust. The oil was the apparatus. Halcenvane's assessment office measured people with instruments — resistance frames, load plates, timing glasses in brass cradles, a rack of graduated chalk-plates that recorded impact as a spread of cracks and were replaced after every use, which meant somebody in this building had a standing order for chalk-plates and a budget line to pay for it. At Greyvane, an assessment had been three faculty and their eyes. Here, three faculty sat behind an instrument and the instrument was the witness. He had understood on the day of the baseline what that changed, and it had taken him until now to phrase it: at Greyvane the panel decided what it had seen, and at Halcenvane the panel decided what the reading meant. One of those was easier to argue with. The other was harder to lie to.

The records side ran on a two-signature rule, which he'd worked out from the forms themselves. Every document carried two dates — the date of the event and the date of the filing — and two initials, the writer's and the checker's, and no document left the wing with only one of either. He liked it. It was the same architecture Vell had built into her ledger from nothing but discipline, except here it had been built into the paper itself so that discipline was not required, only compliance. Both worked. He was not yet sure which he trusted more.

The office at the corridor's end held four desks, a copying table under the window, and a wall of scheduling ledgers thick as paving stones. Magister Gault's door stood open onto it, which Cael suspected was permanent and deliberate: a man who administered an academy's most-watched enrollment, with his door open, all day, every day, was making an argument about himself that cost him nothing and bought him a great deal.

The clerk who usually took floor allocations was not at her desk. A man Cael had not seen before was at the copying table with a stack of forms and a pen, and looked up when the door moved.

"Floor allocation?"

"Second filing," Cael said, and handed it across.

The man read it — actually read it, top to bottom, which most clerks did not — flipped it for the reverse-side authorizations, checked the two initials in the corner, and set it on the correct pile without looking at the piles. Then he entered it into the scheduling ledger, wrote the filing date beside the event date, blotted it, and looked back up.

"Supervised access, halls one and three, four sessions weekly, evaluation-linked. That's what your provision carries?"

"That's it."

"It's entered. If the hall assignment moves, the change comes to your residence board by the previous evening. It comes from this office, not from the halls — if it comes from the halls, it isn't authorized and you shouldn't take it." A pause exactly as long as it needed to be. "That's happened twice this year, so."

"Understood."

The man nodded once and went back to the copying.

Cael stood there half a second longer than he needed to, and did what he had done to every new person in his path for three years, without deciding to, because at this point the deciding had worn out of it entirely.

The compound gaze: four layers run at once instead of one at a time, which was what six hours of watching four separate bouts had compressed itself into over two years of practice. Rhythm. Weight. Breath. Position — the layer Greyvane had added, where a person was allowed to be and by whose permission. And across all four, the check that made them worth running together: whether they agreed. A person whose layers told different stories was either lying or under strain, and both were worth knowing.

He ran it on the man at the copying table in the time it took to say *understood*, and it came back level.

Rhythm: even. The pen moved at one pace, and the pace did not change when he was spoken to, and did not change when he stopped being spoken to. Most people's writing rhythm carried a small hitch at the moment attention arrived or left; his had none, which was either an unusually settled temperament or an unusually deep habit of copying, and a man employed to copy would have that habit honestly.

Weight: settled, unremarkable, evenly loaded. He stood the way a person stands who has stood at a table for a great many hours. No forward lean, which would have been eagerness. No back-load, which would have been reluctance. Nothing.

Breath: ordinary. Slightly slow. Cael's own breath, seated and calm, was still the most reliable clock he owned, and against it the man's ran perhaps four beats to his five, which meant nothing at all on its own; some people simply breathed slowly.

Position: a copying table in an assessment office, at the hour a man employed to copy would stand at one, holding nothing he was not paid to hold.

Agreement: total. Four layers, one story, and the story was *a clerk who has copied a great deal of paper*.

Which was, Cael thought, walking back out into the corridor, exactly what he was.

He turned it over for about a hundred paces and then stopped, because there was nothing there to work with and building on nothing was how he'd lost to a Blade practitioner in an Ardenmere spring and written the loss down underlined so he'd stop doing it. One instance was an accident. He had one instance of an entirely ordinary man being entirely ordinary.

He filed it under nothing and went to train.

---

The residence quadrangles at Halcenvane posted cards.

He'd noticed it in the first week and found it, on reflection, the single most revealing piece of design on the campus. Every door — student, staff, faculty, the visiting-lecturer rooms on the north stair — carried a small slate card in a brass holder at eye height, and every card carried a name, a Path, and a tier. Not a rank. Just the tier, in the academy's own hand, replaced by the residence office when it changed.

Greyvane had not done this. Greyvane had not done this because Greyvane's whole institutional theory was that the tier on a person's paper was frequently wrong, and a school built on that theory does not nail the wrong number to the door.

Halcenvane nailed it up because at Halcenvane the number was the circulatory system. The ladder sorted by tier. The brackets sorted by tier. Team selection, eventually, would sort by standings that came out of brackets that sorted by tier. A card on a door was not decoration; it was the ledger made architectural, so that a person walking down a residence corridor could read the whole institution's estimate of everyone in it at a glance, and — this was the part Cael kept coming back to — so that everyone living behind those doors would read it too, every day, walking past their own.

His own card said: *Cael. Assay provision. Evaluation by demonstration.*

No Path. No tier. Three lines where everyone else had three words, and the residence office had clearly agonized over it, because the lettering was smaller than any other card on the corridor to make it fit.

Lira's said *Wind. Copper*, which was a lie of the kind that had a queue number attached to it somewhere in a registry office. Brom's said *Iron Skin. Copper*, which was also a lie, and which Brom had looked at on the first evening and said "Huh" about, and never mentioned again. Karis's said *Ember. Iron*, which was true, and which she had photographed in her memory and used as the reference sample for a small ongoing project about how many of the cards on their corridor she believed.

He walked back along the staff corridor at the assessment wing's end on his way out that evening, because the shortest route from hall three ran that way, and read the cards without meaning to, the way he read everything.

Three-quarters of the way down, a door that had been blank for the four weeks since he'd arrived had a new card in it.

*Seln. Shadow. Bronze.*

Cael stopped for perhaps two seconds.

Shadow Path was not common. He had never charted one. Greyvane had not held a single one across an entire year, and Ardenmere's circuit had produced exactly two in a year and a half, both of them washouts who fought like brawlers and used the Path for nothing, because a Path that hides you is worth very little in a room where three hundred people have paid to watch you.

Bronze-tier on an academy staff corridor was also not common, but it was legible: teaching assistants at mid-tier academies were usually late-Copper or early-Iron, graduates hanging on, junior faculty candidates buying a year of standing. Bronze was a step above the post. Which meant either a man whose career had gone sideways, or a man taking a quiet year for a reason of his own, and both of those were ordinary human facts that happened in every institution on the continent.

He filed it and went to dinner.

---

The common room in the residence quadrangle was loud that night, because the ladder's opening brackets posted in three weeks and half the building already had opinions about the seeding.

He had spent part of the walk back doing the arithmetic on why they had opinions, because a room full of teenagers arguing about seeding was a symptom and he wanted the disease. The Crown yard's lower two tiers were free to enrollees and the upper two were sold — four coppers a seat on ordinary session days, a silver on finals, and the finals sold out. Four hundred seats at a silver was four hundred silver marks in an afternoon, which was more than the Ironyard's main floor moved in a good month and did not come close to paying for a building this size. So the gate was not the income. The gate was the *advertisement*. Ostrand's trade guilds endowed seats here — he'd found the plaques in the lecture complex's stairwell, twenty-one of them, each with a guild mark and a date — and guilds endowed seats at academies whose names got said out loud in the city below. A crowded Crown yard on a finals afternoon produced conversation in Ostrand, and conversation produced endowment, and endowment paid for chalk-plates and instructors and a library that would have swallowed Prynn's archive whole. Which meant the ladder was not a competition the academy ran for its students. It was the mechanism by which the academy converted its students into money, and everyone in this common room arguing about seeding was arguing about their own place in a revenue instrument, and not one of them knew it, and Cael was not going to be the one to tell them.

Brom had come back from Rooke's cohort with a fresh purple mark along the ridge of his forearm and an expression of unmixed contentment. Lira had spent an hour at the standings board and come away with the flat, faraway look she wore when she was doing arithmetic about people. Karis was writing.

Cael wrote too, later, upstairs, with the window open onto the court and the river noise coming up the bluff — barge horns, the long call and the two short ones, a signal system he had not yet decoded and intended to.

He filled two pages of the observation notebook's Halcenvane section first, because the day had produced a real amount: the two-signature rule, the chalk-plate budget line, the residence cards and what they told him about the institution that had chosen to hang them. Then, at the bottom, because the section had a subsection now for the assessment wing's staff and because he had learned in Ardenmere that the smallest entries were the ones that paid out latest:

*New TA in Gault's office. Bronze-tier, Shadow Path per the residence card. Doesn't look at me. Note: at Halcenvane, everyone looks at me. Filed.*

He read the line back once, decided it was not worth more than it said, and went to bed.

# Chapter 7 — Reports

The first monthly product was due on the ninth, and Seln drafted it on the seventh, at his own table in his own room, in the fourth handwriting, with the window shut.

He wrote reports the same way he had written them since his third year, which was the year he had watched a colleague's career end over one adjective. The rule he had built out of that was simple and he had never once broken it: a report contains observation. It does not contain inference. Inference travels up the chain, acquires certainty on the way, and comes back down as instruction — and the instruction is then given to the officer who generated the inference in the first place, who now has to act on his own guess as though somebody else had confirmed it. He had watched three men in fifteen years walk into that machine, and all three had believed they were being helpful.

So: observation. What the subject did. When. Where. Verifiable. Nothing about what any of it meant.

*Coverage established, day one. Cover post functioning; no friction with office staff. Access to evaluation calendar, floor scheduling, and assessment-wing filing confirmed as briefed.*

*Subject's routine is disciplined and unremarkable. Trains under supervision, halls one and three, four sessions weekly per provision allocation, no unscheduled floor access observed. Attends lecture complex on standard enrollee pattern. Attends Crown yard ladder sessions in the public gallery — every session to date, including practice bouts, including brackets in which no associate of his competes. Extensive private notation habit; writes daily, at length, in at least two separate volumes, one of which he does not carry into training halls. No undisclosed capability observed.*

He read it back and found nothing to change, which was correct, because it was all true.

The last sentence was the one the assignment existed for and he had written it without hesitation, because in twenty-eight days he had observed no undisclosed capability. Supervised training, at supervised intensity, against supervised drills. Whatever the boy had done at Greyvane that had generated an adjudication and a transcript and a monitoring line three grades above the officer who briefed him, he was not doing it in hall three on a Fourth-day afternoon.

He added the routine appendices — the schedule extract, the associates list, the note that the academy's registrar had begun assembling charter documentation in advance of no announced inspection, which was the kind of institutional weather worth recording — and sealed it, and walked it down to the Ostrand courier post himself on the eighth, because a man who sends his post through an academy's own bag is a man whose post an academy can read.

It was a good report. Complete, dry, accurate. Eight years ago it would have been the best thing about his week.

---

The cover had bedded in faster than most, and he gave the credit where it belonged, which was to the office rather than to himself.

Halcenvane's assessment wing was run by a man who did not care who you were. Magister Gault had asked Seln exactly four questions on the first morning — whether he could copy accurately, whether he could hold a calendar in his head, whether he understood that an evaluation date moved for injury and illness and nothing else, and whether he had any objection to working the hours the wing actually kept as opposed to the hours the charter said it kept. Seln had answered all four honestly, which had cost nothing, and had then been left alone in a way that would have been unimaginable at eleven of his previous postings. Gault's whole administrative theory, as far as Seln could reconstruct it from a month of watching him operate, was that instruments do not have opinions and neither should the people who carry them. He did not chat. He did not ask where a man was from. He noticed errors within a day and said so once, flatly, and never mentioned them again.

It was, from a tradecraft perspective, close to ideal. It was also — and Seln noted this the way he noted everything, without permitting it to become a preference — the first office in nineteen postings where nobody had asked him a single question he had needed to lie about.

The arithmetic had landed in week two, exactly as designed. He had been totalling a session ledger at the copying table and had put a column out by forty, and the senior clerk had leaned over and found it in four seconds and been kind about it, and had then found the next one for him a week later without being asked. From that afternoon the wing had a settled view of him: reliable, thorough, slow with numbers. It was a small and complete opinion and it sat squarely on the shelf where a real opinion might otherwise have grown, and it had cost him one deliberate error and about nine seconds of manufactured embarrassment.

He had used the trick in eight postings. It had never once failed. He thought, sealing the report on the eighth, that there was something faintly dismal in a craft whose most reliable instrument was other people's willingness to stop thinking about you the moment you gave them a reason to.

---

He had been made on day twenty-three.

He had known it on day twenty-three, more or less immediately, and had spent the two weeks since working out how, because the *how* was the only part with any operational value and because the alternative was to sit with the fact itself, which he did not intend to do.

The moment had been the empty gallery.

Second bell, hall three, and he had come along the upper walk with a stack of forms intending to take the north end of the rail, four bodies in from the stair, which was the position and had been the position for three weeks. The gallery had been empty. Not thin — empty, not one body on it, because the schedule said hall three did not run until the third bell that day and the schedule had been correct.

And hall three had been running.

He had registered the sound of it two steps before he registered the emptiness, and had made the correct decision in the time it took to take the third step, which was to discard the position and keep walking, because a man standing alone at a rail above a floor is not a man in a crowd; he is a shape, and Shadow Path at Bronze cannot suppress a shape that has nothing to be part of. He had walked through and out the far door and had not broken stride, and had been rather pleased with himself for about eleven seconds.

Then, in the corridor, the eleven seconds had ended.

*The schedule was correct.* The schedule said the third bell. The floor was running at the second. Which meant the subject had come to hall three forty minutes outside his own allocation, without a filing, without a request, on a morning when nobody had any reason to be there — and had done it on the one day in three weeks when the gallery would therefore be empty.

Seln had walked another forty paces before he let himself finish the thought, because he had learned early that a conclusion reached in motion sits better than one reached standing still.

It had not been an accident. It had been an instrument, and he had walked through the middle of it, and the reading it had taken was the single most expensive reading anyone could have taken off him: not that he was present, which proved nothing, but that he had *declined the position when the position stopped working*. A clerk crossing a gallery does not care whether the gallery is empty. Only a man using the gallery cares. And by walking on he had answered a question the boy had constructed a whole morning to ask, and had answered it in one stride, in the open, for free.

He had been made twice before in fifteen years. Both times by counterintelligence officers with departments behind them, both times in the sixth month or later, both times because somebody had cross-indexed his cover documents against a records set he hadn't known existed. Neither time had involved anyone actually watching him.

Never by a target. Never in a month. Never, and this was the part he had turned over more than he liked, by somebody's fifteen-year-old.

---

What he did about it was the interesting part, and Seln had spent the two weeks since being interested in it against his own better judgment.

The doctrine on being made was not complicated. A made officer has three options, and the first is withdrawal, which he had considered for exactly as long as it took to price it: withdrawal meant a replacement, a replacement meant a new cover in a building where a cover had just failed, and a new cover in a building where a cover had just failed was a cover that started at a disadvantage nobody could dig it out of. The second was escalation — go overt, present credentials to the chancellor, convert covered coverage into declared coverage. That was the option his brief technically permitted and the option that would have ended his usefulness the same afternoon.

The third was to keep working, and see what the subject did.

The subject did nothing.

That was the finding, and Seln had checked it from four directions before he trusted it. No report to the chancellor — he would have known within a day, because Bracken's office copied everything to Gault's and he copied Gault's. No report to the assessment office. No complaint, no petition, no quiet word to an instructor. No change in routine: the training hours held, the lecture pattern held, the gallery attendance held, down to the same bench on the same side of the same tier at the Crown yard's ladder sessions. The associates behaved exactly as they had behaved on day one. The Wind Path girl, who by Seln's read was the least capable liar of the four and the most likely to give it away, had not so much as glanced at him with anything other than the ordinary indifference of an enrollee toward a member of staff — which, given what her face did about most things, meant somebody had asked her to manage it and she had agreed to.

And underneath all of that steadiness, something had changed in the room's texture and it had taken Seln nine days to name it.

He was being watched back.

Not looked at. He would have caught looked-at in an afternoon. This was ambient — the low, unlocalized sensation of being *accounted for*, of moving through a space in which one's movement was a quantity somebody was writing down. He knew the sensation from the other side of it intimately; it was what he produced, professionally, and had produced in nineteen postings, and he had never once been standing in it.

Whoever had taught the boy had taught him that a target who changes his behavior tells you he knows, and that a target who does not change his behavior tells you nothing at all — and had taught it well enough that a fifteen-year-old had held to it for fourteen days under conditions where holding to it meant walking past a man he had identified, four times a day, and doing nothing about it.

That was not a schoolboy's instinct. Instinct got you the first three days. What got you the fourteenth was doctrine, and doctrine came from somewhere.

Seln thought about the file's question — *the Compact does not know what he is* — and about the four analysts and however many grades above them who had all agreed that this was the question, and about the twenty-eight days of product he had now filed against it, every line of which was true and none of which touched it.

He wrote nothing down about any of this. There was no place to write it: it was inference, and inference did not go in the product, and there was nothing else in his rooms to put it in.

The assignment is to answer what the subject is, he thought, closing the shutter on a night the river was loud. Fifteen years say the file's question is the wrong question.

The interesting question is who taught him to watch.

---

The observation notebook got a new section that month, and Cael gave it a heading he was aware was overclaiming and wrote anyway, because the alternative was to leave it untitled and untitled sections never got filled.

*Shadow Path — architecture. Charted from operational range.*

He had charted eleven Paths in his life with any rigor and had watched perhaps twenty more. Every one of them had a declaration.

That was the first thing an academy taught and the first thing a circuit taught you to read, and the two institutions agreed about almost nothing else. A Path declares. There is a moment — brief, sometimes very brief, but a moment — in which the practitioner commits, and the commitment shows: the drawn breath before an Ember ignition, the stance-widening and the sunk weight before a Force compression, the visible hardening along a Iron Skin practitioner's forearms a half-beat before contact. Lira's Wind Path declared in the hips. Brom's Iron Skin declared in the jaw, which he had never once managed to correct and had stopped trying. Karis's Ember declared so subtly that it had taken Cael two months and she had been unbearably pleased about it, and it still declared.

Shadow Path did not.

He looked for it for nineteen days, which was longer than he had ever spent looking for a declaration, and at the end of the nineteen days he wrote the finding as a negative and then, an evening later, rewrote it as a positive, because a negative finding is a hole and a positive one is a shape.

*Version one: no declaration observable.*

*Version two — better: the declaration is distributed rather than punctual. Every other Path I've charted spends a moment to buy an effect. This one cannot afford a moment, because a moment is exactly the thing it exists to prevent an observer from having. So it doesn't buy the effect in a moment. It buys it continuously, at a lower rate, all day. That's not the absence of a declaration. That's a declaration smeared across four hours, and the reason nobody has ever charted it is that you can only see a smear if you know the width of the thing that made it.*

He could not see it directly. He could see it in the crowd.

That was the method he ended up building, and it was the best piece of work he had done since the Greyvane archive: he stopped measuring the man and started measuring where other people's eyes went. He already had the instrument — the glance count, four weeks of it, a baseline for how often attention landed on a body in a Halcenvane corridor. So he ran it on the corridor instead of on himself, sampling one stretch of the covered walk at a fixed hour, counting how many transiting people marked *anyone* standing at the walk's east rail.

Then he compared the count on days the teaching assistant was at the east rail against days he was not.

The difference was not large and it was absolutely consistent, and it was the first quantity anybody had ever put on a Shadow Path in Cael's presence: attention landed on that rail about a third less often when the man was standing at it. Not never. A third less. Which meant the Path was not hiding him — it was taxing everyone else's inclination to look, at a rate, and the rate was payable.

And a rate meant a resource, and a resource meant a curve.

He found the curve in the last week of the month, and finding it was the moment the whole study stopped being surveillance and started being Path theory, which was a distinction Karis had been making at him for a year and which he finally understood from the inside.

The positions degraded over a day.

Early sessions: the optimum, every time, without exception — the north end of the rail, the third body of a cluster, the second landing's inner side. Late sessions, after four hours or more on the floors: the positions got worse. Not bad. Worse. A body further out from the cluster's center. The stair-head instead of the rail, which saw two-thirds of a hall instead of all of it. Twice, near the end of long days, a position with a wall at his back and a clear view of one door only, which was the choice of a man who had stopped optimizing for coverage and started optimizing for not being approached.

*Suppression is not free,* Cael wrote, and underlined it, because it was the sentence the whole month had been for. *It runs on something that depletes across a day and recovers overnight. Four hours looks like the shoulder of the curve. After that he stops buying good sightlines and starts buying cheap ones, which is exactly what I do with the Wind-adjacent when the hip's already paid twice.*

*Every ability I have ever charted has a price. I have been assuming his didn't, because I couldn't see him paying it. He was paying it in front of me all month, in the only currency his Path can pay in: the quality of where he chooses to stand.*

He wrote the limits down too, on the facing page, in the third column he had kept since Ardenmere and had never once let himself skip, because a study that recorded only what it had found was a study that would eventually claim to have found everything.

*Discarded / cannot reach:*

*— Range. I have no idea what the suppression's radius is. Every observation I have is of him standing still in a populated space. I have never seen him move under it at speed and I have no way to make that happen without asking for it.*

*— Contact. Does it hold against touch? Against being spoken to? He is spoken to constantly in that office and it holds, so probably yes, but the office isn't looking for him and I don't know what happens against somebody who is.*

*— The floor. He has never once been on a training floor in a training capacity. Bronze-tier and he does not spar, does not drill, does not so much as stretch in a public hall. That's not modesty. It's the same decision I'd make: a Path only shows what you let it be used for, and he has decided that his gets used for exactly one thing in this building. I would very much like to see him use it for something else, and I am not going to get to.*

*— And the largest gap, which I'm writing plainly so I stop pretending it isn't there: everything above is inferred from where a man chooses to stand. It's all one instrument. Vell would say a rating built on one link is a rating three-quarters made up. She'd be right.*

*Note, and I want this on the page in my handwriting: he is not hiding from me. He stopped being able to hide from me on day twenty-three. He is hiding from four hundred other people, all day, every day, so that the one person who can see him doesn't matter. That is a better answer to being caught than anything I would have come up with, and I have been thinking about it for two weeks.*

---

Lira put him on the floor twice that week and had opinions about the second one.

They trained in hall one at the sixth bell on the days her ladder schedule allowed it, which by then was two days in five, and she had been fighting him at genuine intensity since the second week because the Halcenvane floors were better than Greyvane's and she had said, in the flat way she said things she'd already decided, that it would be a waste not to use them.

The first fall was ordinary and his own fault. The second was the informative one.

He had gone to the Wind-adjacent on the fourth exchange, which was right — she had committed forward on a diagonal and the burst was the correct answer and he had priced it before reaching for it, the way the log had required for a year and a half. The hips dropped. The floor gave him the half-inch it kept in reserve. The held half-breath started and his weight simply stopped being where her read said it was, hollow-boned for the length of it, a half-body-width of displacement sideways that his legs had no business producing — and her committed motion went past him into air.

Then the landing beat.

That was the price, and it was not negotiable and never had been: the borrowed lightness returning to ordinary mass all at once, a jolt up through both heels, and half a breath of pure locked commitment in which he could not change direction if the building came down. He had used that half-breath correctly four hundred times. He used it wrong this time, because he was a beat late deciding what to do with it, because the surface read had come in late again, because the surface read had been coming in late for three weeks and he knew exactly why.

Lira hit him with her shoulder and put him on the boards, and stood over him with her hands on her knees, breathing hard, entirely unapologetic.

"That's twice," she said.

"I know."

"Your read's slow."

"I know that too."

"Then say the rest of it out loud instead of writing it in the book where I have to wait for it."

He got his elbows under him. The boards at Halcenvane were sprung wood over stone, which was a great deal kinder than the Ironyard's foundry floor and had still knocked most of the air out of him. "The surface read runs on the same attention the counting runs on. I've been spending it on corridors for a month. So it's arriving a beat late, and a beat late is the difference between using the landing and eating your shoulder."

"Right." She put a hand down and he took it. "So what's it worth?"

"The counting?"

"The counting."

He thought about it properly, which she waited for, because she always waited for it.

"A month ago the Compact could see everything I do and I couldn't see any of it," he said. "Now I know where their man stands, what his Path costs him, when in the day he gets tired, and what he can't do at all. That's the best trade I've ever made in my life." He rolled his shoulder and found it working. "And it cost me a beat off the Iron-adjacent for a month, which cost me two falls to you, which cost me a bruise. So: yes. But I'm writing the bruise down."

Lira looked at him for a second and then, unexpectedly, laughed — one short, real one.

"You're the only person I've ever met," she said, "who'd file a bruise."

---

The schedule came back on a Fifth-day, in the ordinary way, through the residence board.

Assessment-office paper, assessment-office hand, two dates in the corner and two sets of initials, the whole apparatus doing exactly what it did. *Supervised training allocation, revised issue.* And on the third line, where the previous issue had said *hall one*, this one said *hall three*.

Cael stood at the board and read it twice, and something in his chest did a slow, cold, delighted turn.

He had noticed the error on the Second-day. Three days ago. The previous issue's third line had listed his Sixth-day slot in hall one, and the master ledger — which he had watched being written, standing at the copying table, in the first week — held hall three, and the discrepancy was a transposition of the kind that a copying office running four hundred enrollees' paperwork produced perhaps twice a week and corrected when somebody complained.

He had not complained.

He had thought about it for most of a morning and had then made a deliberate decision and written it down, because decisions unwritten had a way of becoming instincts he couldn't audit later: he would leave the error in place and see whether it corrected itself, and if it did, he would know who was reading his paperwork closely enough to care that it was wrong.

Because that was the thing about the error. It cost Cael almost nothing — he'd have arrived at hall one, been redirected, walked six minutes, lost a quarter of a session. Annoying. Trivial.

It cost a man building a positional map of Cael's week a great deal more than that. An observer working from the filed schedule would have been standing at the hall one gallery on Sixth-day, four bodies in from the stair, at a rail overlooking a floor his subject was never going to walk onto. A whole session of coverage, gone, on a document error nobody had reported.

So the error had been fixed. Quietly. Unrequested. Three days after Cael had declined to mention it, by an office he had said nothing to, on a form that carried two dates and two sets of initials and no note whatsoever.

He stood at the residence board in the Fifth-day traffic with four hundred people going past behind him and understood, with total clarity, several things at once.

The man knew he had left it. Of course he knew — leaving an error uncorrected for three days was itself an observable behavior, and Cael had been observed for three days by somebody with a professional reason to notice paperwork.

The man had fixed it anyway. Which was a message, and the message was not *I know*. The message was *I know, and I am going to keep doing the work properly, and here is my paperwork, correct, on time, initialled twice.*

And Cael had built the test in the first place, which meant Cael had said something first, and the correction was an answer, and neither of them had spoken a word to the other since a filing date four weeks ago.

He took the sheet off the board, folded it, and put it in his coat, and went to his session.

That night he wrote one line under the day's entry, and then sat looking at it for a while before he closed the book.

*He fixed it. Sixth-day, hall three. Nobody asked him to.*

*We're playing now. Neither of us has said so. I don't think either of us is going to.*

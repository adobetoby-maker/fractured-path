# Chapter 15 — Notice of Inspection

The notice came up the bluff road on a Second-day morning in a courier's satchel, under a registry seal, and Cael watched it arrive without knowing what it was, because he was in the covered walk at the second bell counting a corridor and the courier came past him at eleven feet.

He noted the man because the man was wrong for the season. Crown-season traffic on the bluff was guild factors and instructors and river-house juniors, and they all arrived the same way, which was slowly, in company, looking at the buildings. This one came alone at a working pace with his satchel strapped across his chest and did not look at anything, and his coat was the color Cael had first seen on a Denvash road five years ago and had never once mistaken for anything else since.

Registry grey.

Cael wrote the hour on the back of his hand and went to his lecture, and by the fourth bell the whole bluff knew that something had come in under seal, because a chancellor who summons her counsel, her registrar, her head of assessment and her senior combat faculty into a records hall at the third bell on a Second-day is a chancellor announcing something whether she means to or not.

At the fifth bell a wing runner found him in the library with a folded slip in assessment-office hand: *Enrollee Cael. Records hall, sixth bell. Bring nothing.*

---

Withrow ran the reading the way she ran everything, which was to say she did not perform it.

There were nine of them in the records hall: Withrow at the head of the long table; her counsel beside her with the seal-case open; Bracken; Magister Gault, who had brought a folder and had not opened it; Instructor Rooke, standing rather than sitting, near the window; two faculty Cael knew by sight and not by name; Karis, invited by Bracken and seated at the far end with her hands flat on the table; and Cael.

He had asked, on the walk over, why an enrollee was in the room at all, and Karis had answered before Bracken could. "Because she doesn't manage by rumour. It's the whole reason she's built this place out of a converted nothing in twenty years. Anybody named in a scope reads the scope."

The counsel read it aloud, entire, in the flat voice of a woman who reads documents for a living and has learned that emphasis is a way of lying.

*To the Chancellor of Halcenvane Academy, chartered, greeting. Notice is given of a formal charter inspection of the institution, to be conducted by delegation of the Guilds Compact, commencing on the twelfth day of Reaping and continuing at the delegation's discretion. Scope: charter compliance; enrollment-basis review; observation of provision-status evaluations.*

Then the manifest, the schedule appendix, and the seal.

The room was quiet in the way rooms are when the words themselves have been ordinary and everyone is waiting to find out why the chancellor's face has not moved.

"Read the deltas," Withrow said.

"Three." Her counsel did not consult the page. "One. Chartered mid-tier academies draw a charter inspection on a five-year cycle. Halcenvane's last was two years and four months ago, conducted by two officers over three days, and it produced a finding of full compliance with one clerical remark about the fire-watch's ledger. There is no cycle basis for this one. It is being conducted early, and the notice does not say why, and it is not obliged to."

Bracken made a small note.

"Two. The manifest." The counsel turned the sheet so the table could see the length of it. "Not two officers. A senior evaluation seat, a records officer, magistrate-grade counsel, three clerks and an escort. That is not a compliance visit. That is the complement you send when the finding is going to be read by somebody."

"And three," said Withrow.

"Three," said the counsel, "is the head of the manifest."

She read the name.

Cael, who had spent two years learning to read a room by what its people did with their hands, watched the sentence go around the table and land differently on every one of them. Gault's expression did not change at all, which from Gault was a reaction. Bracken's pen stopped. One of the two faculty he did not know by name said, out loud, "That can't be right," in the tone of a man correcting an obvious clerical error, and then heard himself and stopped.

Rooke, at the window, put his hands behind his back and looked out at the Crown yard.

"Chancellor," said the counsel, "I've practised registry law for nineteen years and I have never once had occasion to explain what an Archmarshal is, because it has never once come up in a room I was standing in."

"Then I'll do it," Withrow said, "since I worked twenty years in the building where they keep them."

She had not raised her voice at any point. She did not now.

"An Archmarshal is not an inspector. There are a small number of them and I am not going to guess at the figure in front of enrollees, because the figure moves and everyone who quotes it is wrong. They sit at the altitude where the Compact's arms converge — enforcement, evaluation, registry. Below that altitude the arms argue with each other; a warden cannot compel an assessor, an assessor cannot direct a registrar, and every one of you has watched that produce the sort of institutional weather we all plan around. At that altitude they stop arguing, because there is one desk that all three report through."

She let that sit for a moment.

"Wardens come to academies. Assessors come to academies constantly; we host four a year and Bracken feeds them tea. Archmarshals do not come to academies. The last time one attended a charter inspection in person, the institution under review was continental-tier and the finding closed a four-hundred-year-old foundation."

"Chancellor," said one of the faculty, "then what is he coming for?"

"Nothing," said Withrow. "That's what the notice says, and I have no reason to doubt it. He has filed no separate business, made no separate request, and attached no separate instrument. He appears at the head of a manifest for an ordinary charter inspection." She closed the seal-case herself. "The inspection is ordinary. His presence is the message. Somebody very high wants their own eyes on this, and they have sent the best pair they have."

Nobody said anything.

"So," said Withrow. "We will conduct an ordinary inspection. Bracken, the documentary work starts this afternoon and I want it filing-grade, not argument-grade. Magister Gault—"

"My wing changes nothing," said Gault. It was the first time he had spoken. "The evaluation calendar stands as published. The instruments are calibrated on a schedule that has nothing to do with anybody's manifest and I will not have it said that they were calibrated because of one." He looked, briefly, at Cael. "The semester evaluation runs on its posted date, before the panel of record, under the standard I set at the baseline. If the delegation exercises its observation right, it observes that."

"Good," said Withrow.

"It is not good or bad," said Gault. "It is the mandate."

---

Rooke caught Cael in the covered walk afterward, which Cael had not expected and had, on reflection, entirely failed to prepare for.

He did not say anything for the first thirty feet, and he walked at a pace that made clear Cael was welcome to keep up or not.

"I minuted it in the first week of term," he said, finally. "That the attention this enrollment buys us was a risk to our inspection standing. I asked for it on the record before the notice came rather than after, and I said so looking at the chancellor, and everybody in that room including you understood exactly what I was doing."

"Yes."

"I have been right for eleven weeks and I have hated every day of it." He said it without heat, in the register of a man reporting a measurement. "You will hear people this month say that I predicted this. I want you to hear from me what I actually predicted, because it matters and the version going round the yard is going to be wrong. I did not predict an Archmarshal. Nobody predicted an Archmarshal. I predicted a routine inspection arriving early and finding a mid-tier academy with an exotic enrollment on its books and no practice at defending one. Half of that was correct."

"Which half?"

"The early one." Rooke stopped walking. "The other half was mine to fix and I have spent eleven weeks not fixing it because I was busy being right. That was poor faculty work and I am telling you so because you were sitting in the room when I minuted it and you are entitled to the correction."

Cael, who had a page on this man and had written on it that Rooke argued with the institution and not with people and could be moved by evidence and by nothing else, revised the page on the spot.

"My cohort's evaluation-window schedules go to Bracken tonight," Rooke went on. "Every one of them, in order, with the supervision signatures already on them, so that when the delegation asks for a sample they get a stack instead of a search. That is the actual work. That is what I should have been doing in the first week of term while I was busy having my objection minuted." He looked at Cael for a moment. "You train under supervision four sessions a week and you have never once been late and never once asked for an exception, which I know because Bracken's floor allocations cross my desk. Keep doing that. It's the most useful thing you can do for this academy for the next nine weeks, and it is exactly as boring as it sounds."

He went, and did not wait to be answered, which Cael also filed.

---

The documentary defense started that afternoon and ran for eleven days, and Cael learned more about how institutions actually protect people in those eleven days than he had learned in the whole of the Greyvane hearing.

Bracken ran it out of the records hall's back room with three clerks and Karis, and the thing Cael had not understood until he watched it was that the work was not about the argument at all. The argument was finished. Karis had finished it in eleven weeks of a Greyvane winter and Withrow's counsel had verified it before Cael ever set foot on the bluff. What the eleven days produced was something else entirely, and Bracken described it in one sentence on the first afternoon, standing over a table with four hundred sheets on it.

"An argument persuades a person," he said. "A file survives a person leaving the room."

So they built the file.

Every citation in Karis's chain got a certified copy of its source — not a transcription, a *copy*, taken from the volume itself and attested by the librarian who held it, so that no reader would ever need to trust a young woman's handwriting. The repeal search — the eleven weeks of proving a negative, which Karis had performed once and could have performed again from memory — got redone from scratch by one of Bracken's clerks, independently, without access to Karis's notes, so that the file contained two searches by two people who had not spoken, arriving at the same nothing. The standardization directive's text went in whole, all forty-one pages of it, with the four passages that superseded the old terminology tabbed and the absence of any repealing clause marked at every point where a repealing clause would have had to sit.

And under all of it, in Bracken's hand, went the index: a numbered list of every document in the file with a one-line statement of what it proved, so that a delegation counsel with four hours and no patience could find the load-bearing sheet in ninety seconds.

Karis was in a condition Cael had not seen before, which was that she had stopped arguing with anybody.

"It's not my file anymore," she said, on the sixth evening, sitting on the floor of the back room with her back against a cabinet and ink to the second knuckle. "That's the point of it. When I built it, it was a finding, and a finding belongs to whoever found it, and if you take the finder away the finding gets weaker. Bracken's turned it into a thing that doesn't need me. If I fell down the bluff tomorrow, the enrollment would hold."

"That's a horrible way to describe a good afternoon."

"It's the *best* way to describe a good afternoon." She looked up at him. "Everything that has ever gone wrong for you has gone wrong because a document said something and there was no way to check it. This is the opposite of that, in a filing cabinet, and it took us fifteen months and two academies."

Cael had asked, on the first day, what he could do.

Bracken had considered the question with a registrar's care and had given him the honest answer, which was nothing. "You can be entirely ordinary for nine weeks," he had said. "Attend, train, submit, and generate no paper that isn't on the calendar. A file is only as strong as its dullest month, Enrollee. Give me a dull month and I will give the delegation nothing to hold."

So Cael went and was dull, which was harder than it sounded, and did the one piece of work nobody had assigned him.

He built pages on the delegation.

The manifest had eleven names on it and Halcenvane's library had something on nine of them, because a chartered academy holds the Compact's published findings and a published finding carries the panel that made it. He had all nine inside four days: counsel with a nineteen-year run of enrollment-basis findings and a habit of attacking provenance before scope; three clerks with nothing; an escort with nothing; and the two names that mattered.

*Records officer: Havel.* Cael sat looking at that one for some time. In an Ardenmere corridor, a young assessor with too-new boots had once asked him a question he had been told not to ask, and had gone away, and had never once been unkind. He was a records officer on an Archmarshal's manifest now, which was a grade up from the last grade up, and Cael wrote *he keeps rising and he keeps ending up wherever I am, and I have never once decided whether that is a coincidence* and left it.

*Senior evaluation seat: Assessor Ilsev.* Six published findings in the library's holdings. He read all six twice and did not need the second reading. She was the reason the Greyvane adjudication had gone the way it had gone; she was a woman who read text and went wherever the text went and had, in a hearing room, followed it somewhere nobody at that table had wanted to go. She was, on the evidence, the single most dangerous person on the manifest and possibly the best thing on it, and Cael was entirely unable to decide which, and wrote that down too.

Then he went looking for the eleventh name, and found nothing.

Not a thin file. Nothing: no published finding, no panel record, no cited opinion, no procedural note in eleven years of the academy's holdings. He went through the indexes twice and then had Karis go through them, because Karis was faster and less inclined to see what she wanted, and Karis came back with three references in the whole library, all of them procedural, all of them a title in a list of attendees, none of them attached to a single word the man had ever said.

"That's not an absence of records," Karis said. "Ilsev's got six findings because assessors write findings. This one doesn't hold a seat that produces public documents. Everything he does goes into a file that opens at a clearance neither of us will ever have."

"So I can't build a page."

"You can't build a page."

Cael turned that over for most of a night and put one line in the notebook, on an otherwise empty sheet with the name at the top.

*Every opponent I have ever faced, I have read first. Every single one. This is the first time the archive has nothing and I am going to have to do it from the floor.*

---

The bluff's temperature went up for about a week and then Withrow put her hand on it, and Cael watched the whole operation with the attention he would have given a bout.

The week itself was ugly in the small ways institutions are ugly when they are frightened. A rumour went round the Iron bracket that the inspection had been requested by another academy, which was nonsense and had a name attached to it by the third day, which was worse. Two second-years asked Bracken's counter whether provision enrollments could be suspended pending a finding, and Bracken, who was constitutionally incapable of being rude to a student, answered the question of law in eleven words and then answered the question underneath it in none. A first-year Cael had never spoken to stopped in the covered walk, looked at him for slightly too long, and walked on with the expression of somebody doing arithmetic.

That was the fame problem doing what the fame problem did. Every person on the bluff had arrived pre-supplied with a version of him, and none of the versions had ever had to survive contact with a consequence before.

Withrow addressed the whole institution on the eighth day, in the lecture complex, faculty and enrollees together, for eleven minutes.

She did not reassure anybody. That was the thing Cael wrote up afterward at length. She stood at the front of a hall holding five hundred people and gave them the deltas — the same three her counsel had read in the records hall, in the same order, with the same absence of adjectives — and then she said the part that mattered.

"You have all heard by now that a person of unusual rank is on the manifest. That is true. What follows from it is nothing. There is no instruction available to any of you that I am not already giving: attend your sessions, sit your assessments, fight your bouts, and let the delegation see an academy doing exactly what it does when nobody is watching. I will not have this institution perform for a delegation. A performance is the only thing an inspection is actually able to detect."

She paused there, and Cael, who had been counting where four hundred pairs of eyes went for four months, watched the hall settle by something he could have quantified.

"One more thing, since I would rather say it than have it said in a corridor. This academy accepted a provision-status enrollment with its eyes open, on advice, on a basis I believe to be sound law. If the delegation finds against that basis, the finding will be mine, the consequence will be mine, and I will be standing in front of you when it happens. Nobody in this hall is carrying my decision for me. Least of all a sixteen-year-old."

She went out. Rooke, sitting in the faculty rows, did not applaud and did not scowl, and did not look at Cael once, which Cael read correctly as the most supportive thing available to him.

Ephram found him on the stair afterward, in front of about thirty people, which was how Ephram did everything.

"I said I'd wait for the semester evaluation," he said. "That was before the evaluation had an Archmarshal at the rail. So the terms have changed and I want it said out loud that they've changed in your favour, because anybody can look good in an empty room." He shifted his kit bag. "You get watched by the highest-graded person any of us will ever be in a building with. If you're real, we'll all know at the evaluation. If you're not, we'll know that too, and I'd rather know."

"So would I," said Cael, and meant it, and Ephram looked briefly annoyed at having agreed with him.

---

The requisition reached the assessment office on the fourteenth day, and Seln had it in his hands before Gault did, because everything that arrived under seal came through the copying table and the copying table was his.

*Pre-inspection compilation. All monitoring product generated on provision-status enrollees at the receiving institution, complete for the current term, to be compiled and held for delegation review at the evaluation seat's discretion.*

He read it twice, which was ordinary, and then set it down and went and dealt with the morning's floor allocations, which was also ordinary, because a man who stops working when a document arrives is a man whose colleagues remember the document.

The compilation was not difficult. He had generated it himself; he knew where every sheet of it was; the wing's filing was Gault's and therefore immaculate. What it required was two hours and a clean hand, and he took the two hours in the afternoon at the copying table with the senior clerk four feet away totalling a session ledger.

He laid the term out in order, which he had not done since the first month, and read it the way an auditor would.

*Coverage established, day one.*

*Subject's routine is disciplined and unremarkable.*

*Subject assisted in a training-floor equipment incident. No injuries. Response consistent with documented baseline.*

*No undisclosed capability observed.*

*No undisclosed capability observed.*

*No undisclosed capability observed.*

Six filings. Every sentence in them true. Every one complete in form, correct in appendix, filed on the ninth, walked down to the Ostrand courier post by hand. A term of professional product from an officer with fifteen years and nineteen postings behind him and not one adverse note anywhere in his service.

His two auditing years had been spent in a room with four other men who did nothing else, and he knew precisely what this stack looked like from above, because he had once written the assessment that ended a colleague's posting off a stack shaped exactly like it. *Complete, consistent, and containing nothing.* You did not need a suspicion to write that assessment. You needed only to lay six reports side by side and notice that a subject who had generated an adjudication, a transcript and a monitoring line three grades above the briefing officer had, across an entire term of embedded coverage, generated no product at all.

The evaluation seat on the manifest was Ilsev. He had read four of her findings on the road up from the last posting, which was the sort of preparation that had kept him alive in three cities, and he had put the volume down understanding one thing about her clearly: she read what was in front of her, without wanting anything from it, all the way to the end. That was rarer at her altitude than incompetence and a great deal harder to survive.

He compiled the file.

He put the six reports in date order with the appendices attached and the schedule extracts behind them. He wrote the covering index in the fourth handwriting, dated it, and made no annotation of any kind, because a covering index that explains itself is an index with an argument in it, and the last thing this stack could survive was an argument.

He did not add anything. He did not remove anything. He did not go back into the incident report from the training floor and adjust the word *baseline*, which he had considered for perhaps four seconds on the day he wrote it and had not considered since. There was nothing in the compilation that was untrue and nothing in it that was complete, and he handed it across the counter to Gault's clerk at the fourth bell with the day's other paper, and it went into the wing's holding cabinet under seal to await a delegation that was five weeks out.

That evening he ate at the refectory's late sitting, walked the covered walk once because a man who never walks the covered walk is a man with a room, and went up.

His room held a bed, a table, a shuttered window, a lamp, and a case.

The case was leather over a wood frame, dark with handling, with a lock in it that had cost more than the case and a strap he had replaced twice. It had been to every posting he had ever held. It fit under the bed if the bed was against the wall and it fit in the bottom of a travelling chest if it was not, and it had gone into and come out of both without once being opened by anybody but him.

He took the day's private page out of his coat — his own shorthand, his own cipher, four lines, the fire-watch's closure hours and the records-office matter's disposition and a note about the Crown final's date — and unlocked the case and put the page inside with the others, and locked it, and put it back.

Then he sat for a while on the edge of the bed with his hands loose between his knees, not filing anything else.

Five weeks. A file compiled and sealed, a term of true and empty sentences, and an evaluation seat coming up the bluff road who read things all the way to the end.

He had been in the trade long enough to know the shape of the next question and to know that there was no version of it he could answer better than he had already answered it, which was: nothing. He had made no arrangement, given no undertaking, said no word to any person on this bluff about any of it, and the total sum of what he had done was to go on writing true sentences and stop writing certain others.

He put out the lamp.

What he kept, he kept.

---

They posted the inspection schedule on the covered walk's main board on the twenty-second day, and they posted it beside the Crown final's.

That was not anybody's decision. The board had two columns and the season sheet had been on the left since the brackets opened, and the wing's runner pinned the inspection schedule in the first clear space, which was on the right, and went away.

Cael stood in front of it in the Fifth-day traffic with four hundred people going past behind him and read both columns, top to bottom, twice.

*Copper bracket — final. Crown yard. The nineteenth of Reaping. Faculty panel of three; witnessed.*

*Charter inspection — Guilds Compact delegation. Records sessions, the twelfth to the fifteenth. Facility review, the fourteenth. Faculty interviews, the fifteenth and sixteenth. Provision-status evaluation: observation right reserved, the twentieth of Reaping.*

Six weeks out, the two columns overlapping by a week in the middle, and every date on both of them scheduled by ordinary people doing ordinary jobs who had not once conferred.

He had stood in front of a board like this before. That was the thing he could not stop noticing: a notice-board in a smaller academy in a colder month, with a hearing date on it and his own name in a line of print, and a fifteen-year-old reading it and finding out that the system had scheduled him. He had thought at the time that it was the worst a board could do.

This board had two dates on it and neither of them was a threat. One of them was a bout his best friends were going to fight each other in. The other was a procedure that the assay provision had required of him since the day he signed it, occurring on its posted date, before a panel of record, under a standard set at his own baseline — with a right of observation reserved by a delegation that had filed no question and made no demand and stated no business at all.

He read them both a third time and went to his session, and wrote it at the window that night with the new pen.

*Last time the Compact came to an academy for me, they sent a warden with a challenge. This time they're sending an Archmarshal with no stated business at all. Coss I could read in a week. I don't know what reads an Archmarshal. Six weeks to find out.*

And beneath it, because it belonged with it:

*Rooke told me the most useful thing I can do for this place is be boring for nine weeks. Bracken told me a file is only as strong as its dullest month. Gault told a records hall that his instruments were calibrated on a schedule that has nothing to do with anybody's manifest.*

*Three men who don't especially like each other, giving the same instruction in three different offices in one day: hold the ordinary. Don't move because they're coming.*

*I have spent two years being told by institutions to stop existing so loudly. This is the first time an institution has told me to keep doing exactly what I'm doing, and meant it, and been right.*

*Six weeks of dull left. Hold.*

# Chapter 24 — Advancement

The Compact registry station in Ostrand stood on the third street back from the wharfs, in a building that had been a grain factor's counting house before the Compact bought it, and it did not look like anything at all.

That was the first thing Cael wrote down about it, standing across the street at the second bell in the first week of the month after Reaping, with the rain not quite starting. Two stories, brown stone, a door with a step worn into a dish, and one plate beside the door about the size of a book. He had built the place enormous in his head for four years without ever once meaning to and had never noticed doing it, and here it was, being a building, on a street with a rope-maker on one side of it and nothing much on the other.

Lira looked at it for a while.

"Smaller than Denvash," she said.

"Everything's smaller than Denvash."

"No, I mean it's *smaller*." She hitched the strap of her kit higher on her shoulder. "Fenmark had a hall. Two stories of hall, with a window at the end you could see the sigil through from the street, and they made you wait in it. Forty of us, benches down both sides, and the assessors came through in a line and you all stood up." She said it without any weight at all, which from Lira was how you knew. "I was fourteen and I stood up eleven times in one morning."

"And this is a door."

"This is a door," Lira agreed. "I think I prefer the door."

The four of them had come down the bluff road at first light. It had not been discussed. Bracken had come too, in his own coat, carrying the Halcenvane sponsorship file in a satchel — three hundred pages of season record, panel filings, a witnessed final, and a certified copy of an assessment office's formal application for advancement evaluation on the grounds of *advancement inconsistent with recorded tier*, which was the driest sentence in the whole document and the one that had done all the work.

"You need me for the first ninety seconds," Bracken said, at the step. "After that I am a man holding a satchel. Everything after the first ninety seconds is between you and an instrument."

"Good," said Lira.

---

They were allowed in as far as the anteroom, which had four chairs and a stove that was not lit, and then a door, and then Lira was on the other side of the door for fifty-one minutes.

Cael counted them. He could not have stopped counting them if somebody had paid him.

Brom sat with his forearms on his knees and looked at the floor. Karis had brought a book and did not open it, and Cael, who had watched her not open a book in a common room eleven days earlier, filed that this had now happened twice and that it was the closest thing to a tell she had.

Bracken stood, because Bracken stood.

At the twenty-second minute the door opened and a clerk came out with a form and went upstairs and came back with a different form, and the four of them watched him do it with an intensity that made him visibly nervous, and Bracken said, "Ordinary," in the tone of a man who had spent twenty years learning which paper meant what and would have said something else if it had meant something else.

At the fifty-first minute Lira came out.

She came out holding a folded sheet in one hand and looking, Cael thought afterward, like nothing at all — no smile, no straightening, none of the six things a person does with a result. She came across the anteroom and stopped in front of them and unfolded the sheet and turned it around so they could read it, which was the only ceremony she was ever going to perform about anything.

The certification was printed. The particulars were entered in a station clerk's hand.

*Path: Wind. Tier: Iron. Rank: One. Advancement confirmed on demonstration and record. Prior classification superseded.*

Nobody said anything for a moment, and then Brom said, "Huh," and stood up, and put out his hand, and Lira looked at the hand as though it were a foreign object and then shook it, once, hard.

"Say it," said Karis.

"Wind Path, Iron-tier, Rank One."

"Louder. There's a clerk."

"*Wind Path, Iron-tier, Rank One,*" said Lira, at a volume that made the clerk look up and go straight back down again, and Karis wrote it in the notebook then and there, standing up, at the pace of a woman recording something she has waited three years for.

---

She told him about it on the street, walking back toward the river, ahead of the other three, because he asked and because she had decided somewhere in the last fifty-one minutes that he was the one she was going to tell.

"It was nothing," she said. "That's the thing. I've been rehearsing that room since I was fourteen and it was *nothing*. Two assessors, a floor about the size of hall three, and a sigil frame in the wall.

"They ran an evasion sequence first. Ordinary, the assessment standard, the one every Wind practitioner in the Compact runs — and I've never run it, because you only run it if somebody enrols you in a class that teaches it, and Fenmark threw me out before that year. So I asked them to describe it and they described it and I ran it cold and I got it wrong twice in the first pass." She said this with total equanimity. "Then I ran it right. Then they ran the load figures, and the load figures are just numbers, and my numbers have been Iron since Vell's ledger and everybody with eyes has known it for two years.

"And then the sigil."

She was quiet for about half a street.

"It takes a moment," she said. "Nobody ever tells you that. It's not instant. You put your hand where they tell you and there's a — it goes down into you and *reads*, and it takes about four seconds, and for four seconds the only thing in the world is a system deciding what you are, and there is not one single thing you can do about it while it's happening."

"I know," said Cael.

Lira stopped walking.

"Yes," she said, after a second. "You do. Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Finish it."

She started walking again.

"It resolved and one of them read it out and the other one wrote it down, and then they asked me to sit while they entered the supersession, because the prior classification has to be superseded on the same instrument or it stays live in the sub-layers." She shook her head. "Eleven minutes of clerical work. That's what it was. Eleven minutes of a man entering a supersession into a book, and a woman explaining to me that my Copper certification would remain in the record as a historical entry, and me sitting in a chair with my hands in my lap being extremely polite."

They came out at the top of the wharf street where the river opened up, grey and wide and busy, with the bluff a mile upstream and Halcenvane on the top of it very small and very solid in the rain.

"Say the thing," said Cael. "You've had it since the anteroom."

Lira looked at the river.

"They didn't give me anything today," she said. "They caught up."

---

The Copper Crown went on the residence shelf that evening, on its brass mount, and stayed there.

There had been about ten minutes of discussion about what to do with it, most of it Brom's, all of it unserious, and it had ended when Lira picked it up off the table and put it on the shelf between a lamp and a stack of Karis's superseded drafts and said, "There. It's retired."

"You could send it home," said Brom.

"To what?"

"Fair."

"It stays." She sat down. "I'm not in that bracket anymore and I'm never going back into it, and it's the only object I own that proves the bracket was wrong. That's not a trophy. A trophy's what you get for winning. That's what I got for having to."

Cael wrote that down, later, in the log, verbatim, and put a note under it in his own hand.

*The crown forced the looking. Fiske said so at the boards in the autumn — "it's the looking that matters, the crown's just how you force it" — and Lira repeated it to me that night word for word and neither of us fully believed it was a plan.*

*It was a plan. It took a season, a witnessed final, a public season record, an assessment office willing to file, and a delegation's evaluation seat sitting in a gallery when it happened. Six separate institutional facts had to line up and Fiske named the whole mechanism in three sentences at a standings board two years into being trapped by it.*

*She was in the yard today when the certification went up on the board. She read it and went to her session and said nothing to anybody. I've watched people be gracious. That wasn't gracious. That was a woman watching a thing she'd described out loud actually happen to somebody else, and being right, and having nothing at all to gain from it.*

*Note: I have spent a year charting the people who run this institution and I have not once written a page on Fiske. Fix that.*

---

Ephram came and found him on the Crown yard's lower tier three days before term's close, at the hour when the yard was empty and the boards were being cleared, and did it without an audience, which was the whole content of the exchange before either of them said anything.

"You know I couldn't see it," he said.

"The panel's closed. Nobody sees it."

"Nobody but a Compact delegation." Ephram put his kit bag down. "I said I'd wait for the semester evaluation. I said it in front of sixty people at the top of that stair in the first month, on purpose, because I wanted everyone to know I was waiting. That was a stupid thing to have done, and I want to tell you why it was stupid before I say the other part, because otherwise the other part sounds like a concession and it isn't."

"Go on."

"I made myself the person whose verdict counted, about an event I was never going to be allowed to watch." He said it flatly, with the annoyance of a man who has worked out his own error and does not enjoy it. "So for nine weeks I've been the idiot holding a verdict about a closed session. There is no version of this where I get to deliver the judgment I promised. Whatever I say now is either me pretending I saw something, or me repeating a board."

"So don't say it."

"I'm going to say a different one." Ephram picked the bag back up. "I asked Rooke for a supervised session with you next term. He said the assay clause allows it under evaluation-linked floor time and that he'd sign it if you would."

Cael, who had been prepared for nine other things, was not prepared for that one.

"Why?"

"Because I've had a whole year of an academy telling me what you are, in eleven versions, none of which anybody can check." Ephram shrugged, one shoulder. "I read the transcript. I paid the six marks and I read it and it's a document about law and it tells me nothing about whether you can fight. So I'm going to find out the only way anybody ever finds out anything about anybody, which is on a floor with an instructor watching, and then I'm going to have an opinion I actually own."

"That's fair."

"It's the only fair thing available." He was already turning. "Rooke's cohort works at a speed you have never been hit at. Brom's spent a year finding that out and it took him two losses and a repaired seam. I'd rather you knew that in advance than found it out from me."

"Understood."

"Next term," said Ephram, and went.

*He's right about the speed,* Cael wrote that night, *and he told me so before the session instead of after it, which is worth more than the session will be.*

*Also: he has been carrying a public promise for a year that the institution's own procedure made impossible for him to keep, and rather than quietly let it lapse, he came and stood in an empty yard and explained his own mistake out loud to the person it was about.*

*Nobody made him do that. That's the second time this year somebody at this academy has corrected themselves to my face when they could have just gone on being right at me.*

*Rooke in the covered walk in Sowing. Ephram in the yard tonight.*

*I am going to be very careful about how I remember Halcenvane.*

---

Withrow gave the end-of-year address in the lecture complex on the last Fifth-day of term, faculty and enrollees together, and it ran fourteen minutes, and eleven of them were the ordinary business of an academy closing a year.

The last three were not.

"The Compact's inspection finding was filed on the second and reached this office on the ninth. It reads as I told you in Reaping that it would read: charter compliance in full, enrollment basis sound, documentary practice complete, one clerical remark concerning the fire-watch's ledger." She let a small sound go round the hall and did not encourage it. "For those of you who are new: that is the highest finding a chartered institution can receive. There is no better one. Inspections do not praise. They decline to find, and this one declined to find anything at all, and it did so with an Archmarshal at the head of its manifest.

"I am now going to say the part I have been waiting four years to say from this position, and I am going to say it without decoration, because it is a matter of fact and not of feeling.

"Halcenvane Academy will contest the qualifying season of the Continental Academy Tournament, opening next year."

The hall did what halls do.

She waited it out with the patience of a woman who had known exactly what it would do and had budgeted for it.

"Three sentences on what that means, and then you may go and be excited in your own residences.

"One. The qualifying season is a season. It is contested by chartered academies across a full year, and the fielded side is selected against the charter's rules, which Registrar Bracken will post in full on the covered walk's board on the first day of next term, because this institution does not run selections on rumour.

"Two. This year's Crown ladder standings enter the selection file. They do not decide it. They enter it, and they will be read by people who were not here, so a season on a board is a season on the record.

"Three." Withrow put her hands on the lectern. "We have not qualified in eighteen years. I was not here for that and neither were most of you, and I am not interested in it except as an arithmetic. What I am interested in is that a delegation of the Guilds Compact spent eleven days in this building looking for a reason to find against us and did not find one — which means, whatever else is true, that this academy is now demonstrably capable of doing a difficult thing correctly in front of people who are watching."

She picked up her notes.

"That is the entire qualification. Go and close out your year."

---

Karis read the tournament charter that same week, because Bracken had a copy in the records hall and because reading governing documents before they governed anybody was, as far as Cael had been able to establish across two academies and eighteen months, the reason she existed.

She read it in the upper-floor reading room, which had her name on the assignment and was not a shared space under the definition, with the founding-era notebooks stacked at her left elbow where they had lived since the twenty-seventh of Reaping.

Cael was at the other end of the table with the observation notebook and a page on Fiske that was four days old and three hundred words long.

She had been quiet for about forty minutes when she stopped being quiet, which she did by stopping — not saying anything, not looking up, just going entirely still with one finger on a line, in the way that meant something had happened.

She read it again. Then she read it a third time, and then she took her own notebook and copied the line out, and set the pen down.

Cael did not ask. He had learned, over a year and a half, that the correct response to Karis going still was to wait, and that asking a question inside the first minute cost you the finding.

"Charter of the Continental Academy Tournament," she said at last, "sixth part, participation, subsection four."

"Go on."

"*Participating academies shall field any practitioner appearing on their enrollment record.*"

She said it in an entirely level voice.

Cael put his pen down.

"Say the next part."

"There isn't a next part. That's the sentence." Karis turned the volume around so it faced him, and put her finger under the line, and it was exactly as she had read it, in the printed hand of a charter that had been drafted by people arguing about something else entirely. "It's a compulsion clause. It exists to stop academies withholding practitioners from a qualifying side for political reasons — there was a scandal about it forty years ago, it's in the preamble, an institution kept two of its best out of a season to protect a placement deal and the tournament wrote this to make it impossible ever again."

"And it says *any practitioner appearing on their enrollment record.*"

"It says any practitioner appearing on their enrollment record," Karis agreed. "It does not say any *classified* practitioner. It does not say any practitioner *of a rated tier*. It does not reference the schedule at all, anywhere, in the whole of the sixth part, and I have now read the whole of the sixth part twice."

Neither of them said the rest of it.

Cael sat looking at the line for some time, and the thing he noticed, and wrote down that night because it was the honest part, was that his first feeling was not excitement. It was the flat cold recognition of a shape he had seen before: a sentence in a document, drafted for another purpose, by people long dead, which said something its authors had never intended and could not now be asked about.

That was the shape of the assay clause. That was the shape of the seam in the schedule. It was, twice now, the shape of every good thing that had ever happened to him.

"It'll be argued," he said.

"Of course it'll be argued. Every word of it will be argued, by counsel, in front of a tournament board, and there will be four clauses I haven't read yet that bear on it and at least one of them will be a problem." Karis closed the volume with great care. "I am not going to do that work this week. I'm not going to do it this month. I am not going to raise it with Bracken, or with the chancellor, or with anybody at all, and I want you to understand why, because you're going to want to move on it and I'd rather have the argument now while it's cheap.

"A question you ask early is a question somebody else gets to answer first. If I take that clause to counsel in the last week of term, then some time next spring an institution somewhere writes a note about it, and by the time it matters it will have been thought about by people who were not on our side of it."

"So you write it down and put it away."

"I write it down and put it away." She wrote one word beside the copied line, and Cael, who was three feet away and could read upside down, read it.

*Interesting.*

Then she closed the notebook.

"That's all it is for now," she said. "A line in a charter and a word in a book. If it turns into anything it turns into it next year, with documents, properly, with three months of work behind it and not one day less."

"Agreed."

"Say it as a decision and not as agreement."

"It's banked," said Cael. "Nobody moves on it. Not me, not you, not anybody, until it's ready."

"Good," said Karis, and put the notebook in her coat, and went back to reading the fifth part, because the fifth part was about apparatus and she had not read it yet.

---

Two letters, that week, that mattered more than their length.

The first he had sent in Reaping, three days after the delegation went down the bluff road, and it had been a hard one to write because there was no honest way to make *an Archmarshal came to look at me and I still do not know what he decided* sound like anything a grandfather would want to read at the far end of a country. He had written it plainly in the end, the way Hesk preferred, with the evaluation's fourteen words copied out and the interview described in about a paragraph and nothing dressed up.

The reply came back fast, the way Hesk's replies always came back fast, as though the man had a page half-written before the letter ever finished travelling.

*An Archmarshal looked at you and wrote 'keep looking.' In my day that was called respect. Don't tell him I said so. Sixteen suits you.*

Cael read it four times and then put it in the front of the notebook, behind the cover, where the other one was, and did not write down what it cost him.

The second letter came unlooked-for, in a ledger-keeper's hand he had last seen on a circuit rating card, and had taken the better part of a year to arrive, and Cael stood in the covered walk with it for a while before he opened it.

*Read your account three times. Filed it with your record, where it belongs. The offer of a proper session stands whenever the road runs through Ardenmere again. The ledger doesn't forget. Neither do I.*

That was all of it. It was signed with her mark and the ledger's stamp, which meant she had entered the letter itself as an item, which meant that somewhere in Ardenmere there was now a line in a bound book recording that Vell had written to him.

*Still unclaimed,* he wrote that night. *The session she offered me in the archive's oldest section, two years ago, that I never took because we left. She's kept it open across two academies and a hearing and a year of silence and has now told me so in twenty-eight words and stamped it.*

*I'm going to take it. Not this year.*

*Note, and I want it in writing because I am starting to be able to see the pattern in myself: I keep collecting open offers. Reydan's find-me-later. Vell's session. Edran's third one someday, which he's now passed on to Brom and Lira, which I don't think he knows about. Prynn's shelf with the gap in it.*

*A year ago I'd have called that a list of unfinished business. It isn't. It's a list of people who have decided to leave a door on the latch for a boy who might not come back, and there was a stretch of my life in which the number of those doors was zero.*

---

He wrote the year's inventory on the last night of term, at the window, with the pen, in the format the notebook had used since Ardenmere.

*Six confirmed.*

*Wind-adjacent — Lira. The oldest. Deployment reliable, working ceiling six repetitions as of the twentieth of Reaping, up from four at the baseline. Price: the landing beat, unshortened in three years of trying, and a hip that has now taken a full load in place of a floor once and does not want to do it again.*

*Pressure-adjacent — Feryn. Reliable, use restricted, and the restriction has now cost me a flat line in a semester evaluation on a Compact-observed record. Gault defended me for it in front of a delegation without knowing what he was defending. Price: paid in the shoulder, four repetitions at a time, twice a year, forever.*

*Iron-adjacent — Brom. Surface read. The cheapest thing I own and the one that saved a clerk from being landed on. Eleven of twelve on the drop-frame this year against five of six at the baseline, and eight of nine on the reserve frame after the fault — the last two degraded, which I said out loud at the time.*

*Compression-adjacent — Reydan. Contact absorption. Held in reserve all year on the public record's own precedent. Unused, unshown, and the only thing I own that has ever kept somebody else from being hurt, which is a sentence I have now written in three consecutive years' inventories without ever managing to use it in front of anybody official.*

*Ember-adjacent — Karis, with her consent, in her handwriting. Ignition point, contact, single channel. Demonstrated on a sealed thermal vessel in front of four Compact officers and confirmed at this wing's standard against a thin prior record. The one that was worth more to me on paper than on a floor, exactly as I wrote last year, and this year the paper is where it earned its keep.*

*Shadow-adjacent — Bronze. Presence-suppression and movement-masking. Sixteen weeks. Deployment: none. Held at zero through three hours of the heaviest observation of my life and it did not surface once, which is the entire achievement of the year and which nobody will ever know about.*

*Working ledger, unchanged from Sowing in every important respect: the two components remain mutually exclusive in practice, because masking movement requires reading the building, and reading the building requires being the most awake person in it, which is precisely the state that switches the suppression off. He solves it. I don't. He had a year of not solving it too — that's Brom's line and I am still leaning on it.*

*Cost: continuous, from waking, whether I deploy or not. It has drifted more times this term than I have enjoyed counting — eleven in a single evening's rehearsal once — and twice in the evaluation itself, and the count per week is falling, and the falling is the only encouraging line in the ledger.*

*Useful where: nowhere yet. Still nowhere. Second year of that answer begins now.*

And beneath the six, where it had sat in every inventory for two years, the entry that was not a fragment.

*One anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent. Turned once, on the last night of the year, for the same reason I turn it every time — to check that I still cannot reproduce it and still cannot explain it. I still cannot do either. No Tide practitioner within reach in three years. No sustained exposure. No stakes. Every condition the rest of this log says is necessary, absent, and the reading happened anyway.*

*Six fragments and one anomaly. It is not the seventh of anything. I have been careful about that arithmetic for two years and I am going to go on being careful about it, because the day I start counting it as a fragment is the day I have decided something I do not know.*

*Still open. Still real. Patience.*

He read the whole inventory back once, and then wrote the year's last working line under it.

*The system isn't confused about me. That's the finding of the year and I got it in a twelve-by-fourteen room off a records hall from a man who asked eleven questions and declined to ask the twelfth.*

*I do not know what it thinks it knows. I do not know why it built itself that way. I have one archivist's gap on a shelf, one retired word neither of us understands, one designation on a file that nobody I have ever asked has been able to trace, and a note somewhere above all of this in a hand that signs its own name.*

*That's next year's question. It's a better question than the one I started this year with, and I got it honestly, and I paid for it.*

*Close out the year.*

---

They found the wall above the river at dusk on the last evening, which was the same wall they had found in three towns and two academies, and which at Halcenvane ran along the bluff's north edge with the whole valley open underneath it and Ostrand laid out downstream in the last of the light.

Nobody was leaving. Everybody understood, without needing to say it, that the direction of travel underneath the stillness of the evening had changed shape this year: for the first time since Denvash, none of them was outgrowing the place they were standing in. Halcenvane had four halls, eleven Paths, a library that had swallowed Prynn's archive whole, a ladder, a season, and a chancellor whose ambition was fully visible and fully checkable, and it had held all four of them for a year without one of them hitting a wall the institution could not supply.

Lira sat on the wall with her boots hanging over the drop, Iron-tier by an instrument's own account, with a crown retired on a shelf in a residence half a mile behind her.

Brom sat next to her with his forearms on his knees, Copper on paper, standing third in a cohort that ran at a speed no minor academy on the continent could reproduce, with a repaired seam and two losses that had bought it.

Karis had the notebook out, because the light was still just about good enough.

And Cael sat on the wall's end with the log shut on his knee and the day's entry already written, and did the thing he did at the end of anything that mattered, which was to count what was actually true.

About sixty feet down the wall, at a courteous distance, a man in an assessment office's plain coat had stopped to take the evening air, and was looking at the river.

He had come along the wall at the ordinary walking pace of a member of staff at the end of a term, and had stopped where the wall's low course made a natural place to stop, and was standing with his hands behind his back watching the barges, and he had been there for perhaps four minutes.

Nobody waved him over.

Nobody looked at him for longer than a person looks at a member of staff on a wall. Brom had clocked him at sixty yards and had gone back to the river. Karis had not raised her head at all, which meant she had seen him first. Lira, who had spent an entire year being the one who wanted to force things into the open, had glanced once and let it go, and Cael, who had been carrying the arrangement's whole weight since Sowing, understood that all three of them had independently arrived at the same discipline and that this was, in its way, the largest thing that had happened all week.

The configuration included him the way his reports included the truth: structurally, deniably, completely.

Karis said the quiet thing first, because saying quiet things out loud had never frightened her the way it frightened everybody else at the table.

"Next year the continent comes to measure everybody," she said.

Lira did not even look round.

"Good," she said.

They sat with that a while, the light going gold and then grey the way it always did on walls like this one, in towns like this one, at the end of years that had asked more of all four of them than any of them had quite expected going in.

"I have six things that aren't a Path," Cael said, eventually, to nobody in particular, the words arriving the way his best entries always arrived — not planned, just finally true enough to say out loud. "A semester stamp that says I proved it. And four people who know what it costs."

He weighed the shape of that once, and let it stand.

"And somewhere in a file," he said, "the most careful man I've ever met wrote that I'm not an error."

"You're not," said Brom.

"Never were," said Lira.

Karis, already writing it down in the small notebook she was never without, did not look up. "Noted," she said.

Sixty feet along the wall, the teaching assistant watched the river and said nothing, which — from him — was agreement.

The bluff held. The stamp was real. And the road, when the four of them were ready for it, went on, one door at a time, each one a little less locked than the last.

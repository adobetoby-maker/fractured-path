# Chapter 23 — Null Report

The compiled file came back to the assessment office on the twenty-fourth of Reaping, in the afternoon paper, with the delegation's return seal on the outer wrapper and a receipt slip attached, and Seln signed for it at the copying table with the senior clerk four feet away totalling the term's session ledger.

He broke the seal because breaking the seal was the counter's job, checked the contents against the covering index because checking contents against the index was also the counter's job, and put the whole of it back into the wing's holding cabinet.

It took four minutes. In those four minutes he established the thing he had come to the counter to establish, which he had been waiting eleven days to establish, and which he had made no visible arrangement of any kind to be in a position to establish.

Nothing had been marked.

Six reports, in date order, appendices attached, schedule extracts behind them. His covering index in the fourth handwriting, dated, unannotated. A delegation's evaluation seat had held that stack for eleven days with a right of review that nobody in the building could have contested, and there was not a pencil stroke, a paper-clip, a turned corner, a fold, an added slip, a marginal query, or a reader's mark of any kind on any sheet in it.

Seln had audited product for two of his fifteen years, in a room with four other men who did nothing else, and he knew exactly what an unmarked stack meant, which was one of three things.

It meant a reader who had not read it. It meant a reader who had read it and found nothing to note. Or it meant a reader who noted things somewhere other than on the document, which was a discipline, and which was rare, and which was in his experience found in exactly one grade of officer and in no other.

He put the file away and did the afternoon's floor allocations, because a man who stands at a cabinet thinking is a man his colleagues remember standing at a cabinet.

---

The quarterly was due up the chain within the week, and he wrote it on the twenty-sixth, in the evening, in his room, with the shutters closed and the lamp at the working height.

He wrote all his product in his room. That was not tradecraft; it was preference, and he had noticed twenty years ago that the men who wrote reports in offices wrote worse ones, because an office supplies an audience and an audience supplies adjectives.

The quarterly summary had a form and the form had five parts, and he had written four hundred of them.

*Coverage.* Established day one of term, maintained without interruption, no discontinuity to report. Access to evaluation schedules, assessment records, training-floor allocations and residence movement lists, all through the posting's ordinary functions, none through any arrangement requiring maintenance. He wrote that paragraph in ninety seconds and every word of it was true.

*Subject's routine.* He had six months of it and it filled a page and a half, and the page and a half was the single most accurate document anybody had ever produced about how Cael spent a day, and there was nothing in it. Four supervised sessions a week. Lectures. Library. The gallery at every ladder session of the season without exception. A field-assessment coursework unit taught out of this office. A notation habit conducted in the residence and in the library and, on eleven observed occasions, on the Crown yard's upper tier in the hour before the second bell. Correspondence: two letters out in six months, one to a registered instrument-maker in Denvash, one to a circuit ledger-keeper in Ardenmere, both posted openly at the Ostrand courier post, neither intercepted because interception on a subject of this classification required an instrument nobody had filed.

*Associations.* Three enrollees, unchanged since arrival, all documented, all with their own files, none of them producing anything the apparatus did not already hold.

*Institutional events in period.* The charter inspection. The delegation's presence, the enrollment-basis session, the observed semester evaluation. He wrote those in the register of a man recording weather.

And then the fifth part, which was the part that mattered, and which he wrote as slowly as he had written anything in fifteen years and which came out in four lines.

*Assessment of period: routine compliance.*

*No undisclosed capability observed.*

*Subject's conduct consistent with enrollment basis.*

*Continuation of embedded coverage recommended.*

He sat back and read the four lines and did not revise them.

The first three were true. He had tested them in his own head the way an auditor tests them, sentence by sentence, and each one held: the subject had complied with everything the institution had asked of him for six months without a single exception; nothing that met the reporting definition of an undisclosed capability had been *observed*, in the technical sense the word carried in the manual, which specified observation under conditions permitting confirmation; and the conduct was, by any construction, consistent with the enrollment basis, because the enrollment basis was evaluation by demonstration and the boy had demonstrated and had been renewed.

He had spent six months learning exactly how much true sentence a professional could stand behind.

The fourth line was the one he had written the report to carry.

*Continuation of embedded coverage recommended.*

Nine words, entirely ordinary, the standard closing recommendation on an unremarkable posting, appearing at the foot of perhaps a third of all embedded product filed anywhere in the service in any given year. It would draw no attention whatsoever. It would be read by a briefing officer in the ordinary course, and approved in the ordinary course, and would keep a teaching assistant at a counter in an assessment wing at a mid-tier academy on a river bluff for another term.

It kept a desk between the boy and the apparatus.

That was the whole of it, and he had never once written it down anywhere, including here, including in his own cipher, including on any page in the case under the bed — because a man who writes down his reason has written down his crime, and because there was, on any honest reading, no reason. He had made no arrangement. He had given no undertaking. He had spoken to no person on this bluff about any of it, and the total sum of what he had done over six months was to go on writing true sentences and to stop writing certain others.

He signed the quarterly. He appended the schedule extracts. He sealed it for the ninth, which was the courier day, and set it on the table by the door where the outgoing went.

Then he took the day's private page out of his coat.

It was four lines in his own shorthand — the delegation's departure date, the compiled file's condition on return, the reserve frame's serial off the wing's maintenance book, and one entry that was not a fact but a note, which he permitted himself perhaps twice a year, and which read, in cipher, *unmarked file. one of three. probably the third.*

He got the case out from under the bed.

Leather over a wood frame, dark with handling, a lock in it that had cost more than the case and a strap he had replaced twice. Nineteen postings. It fit under a bed if the bed was against the wall and in the bottom of a travelling chest if it was not, and it had gone into and out of both without once being opened by anybody but him.

There were men in the service who kept nothing, and they were the ones who ended their careers being told what they had done. There were men who kept everything, and they were the ones who ended their careers explaining it. And then there was the third kind, which was his kind, which kept exactly as much as would answer a question nobody had asked yet, in a hand nobody else read, in a case that had never been opened for a superior, a colleague, a magistrate, a lover or a friend, in fifteen years and nineteen postings, and would not be opened now.

He put the page in with the others.

He locked it. He put it back under the bed, against the wall, and pushed the bed to the wall after it, which was a motion he had performed so many times in so many rooms that his hands did it without instruction while he was thinking about something else.

Then he put out the lamp and sat for a while on the edge of the bed with his hands loose between his knees, not filing anything else.

What he kept, he kept.

---

The corridor between the records hall and the library's east stair carried about four hundred people a day and none of them ever stopped in it, because it was a corridor with nothing in it — a stone passage with a door at each end and a run of shelving down one side holding the institution's superseded session ledgers, which nobody had consulted in Karis's memory and which she had checked, once, in her second week, on the general principle that you check.

The teaching assistant from the assessment office came through it at the fifth bell on the twenty-seventh of Reaping, going the other way, carrying a box of forms.

He stopped, which he had never done, and stepped aside to let her past, which anybody would have done, and said, in the flat administrative register of a man passing on a room clash:

"Miss Karis."

"Yes?"

"Records retention." He shifted the box to his hip. "The inspection cycle closes on the tenth. When it closes, the registrar's office runs an archive sweep — it's charter, fourth part, it happens after every inspection and it's why nobody's touched the shelving in here since the twelfth. Anything left in a shared research space at the sweep gets compiled into the institutional archive under the institution's accession, with an index entry, permanently."

"I know what an archive sweep is."

"Then you'll know it takes working papers as well as bound holdings." He was already moving again, half a pace. "Researchers sometimes prefer to avoid it. The carrels in the east range are shared spaces under the definition. The reading rooms on the upper floor are not, because they're assigned by name and a named assignment isn't a shared space. That's a distinction most people don't find until after the sweep."

"That's — thank you."

"Mm," said the teaching assistant, and went on down the corridor with his box.

Karis stood in the passage for a period of time she was afterward unable to estimate.

Her carrel was in the east range. It had been in the east range since the third week of term, because Bracken had allocated it and because it was the closest carrel to the founding-era shelf and because she had spent six months building the provision's documentary chain in it and had never once thought about where the papers slept.

The founding-era documentation was in it. The repeal-search working notes were in it. Two notebooks were in it, and one of them held eleven weeks of Greyvane transcription in her own hand, and in the middle of that notebook, on a page she had copied out of a pre-standardization archive with a Compact seizure seal expiring on the shelf behind her, was a marginal passage containing a word that had been retired by administrative directive and that neither she nor Cael understood.

She got it all out before nightfall. It took four trips and a borrowed hand-cart and a named assignment on the upper floor that Bracken granted in ninety seconds without asking why, which she noted and did not examine.

Then she went and found the other three.

---

She gave it to them verbatim. She was the only one of the four who could have, and she did it the way she did everything, which was flatly and completely and without a single word of interpretation on top of it, and then she sat down and put her hands flat on the table and waited.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"Say the part again," said Lira. "The bit about the carrels."

"*The carrels in the east range are shared spaces under the definition. The reading rooms on the upper floor are not, because they're assigned by name and a named assignment isn't a shared space.*"

"He named the range."

"He named the range."

Brom had not moved. "Does the sweep exist?"

"The sweep exists. I checked it in the charter inside the hour — fourth part, seventeen, it is exactly what he said it was and it runs on the tenth." Karis's voice was steady and her hands were not. "Everything he said was true, everything he said was ordinary administrative information available to any enrollee who asked a registrar the right question, and there is not one word in it that anybody could put in front of a panel."

"But he knew where the notebooks were."

"He knew where the *carrels* were," Karis said. "That's what he said. That's *all* he said."

"Karis."

She stopped.

"He knew where the notebooks were," she said. "He has known where the notebooks were since the third week of term, because the carrel allocation goes through the assessment office's floor and space schedule and he works the counter that processes it, and he has known what is in them for however long he has known, and I do not know how long that is and I am never going to know."

Lira said, very quietly: "And nothing went up."

"Nothing has ever gone up," said Cael.

He had not spoken since Karis started. He was standing at the window with his back to all of them, which was where he went when he needed to not be looked at, and none of them had ever once mentioned that they had noticed.

"Since the training-floor incident I have counted the rotations outside the gate every third day," he said. If a report had gone up with the actual thing in it, the rotations would have changed inside a fortnight, because that is what a resourced apparatus does with escalation-grade capability data, and I have watched it do exactly that twice. They didn't change. They haven't changed since. That went in the log before my birthday and I have never once been able to write down what it meant without it sounding like wanting something."

He turned round.

"Tonight he told us."

"He didn't tell us anything," said Karis. "That's the whole point. There is no sentence in that corridor that means what we all know it means."

"No. There isn't." Cael came and sat down. "That's the language. That's the only language he has. He is a man who has spent fifteen years making sure that nothing he does can be read by anybody, and this evening he walked up to the one person in this building whose notebooks would end her, and used the only construction available to him that couldn't be reported, and let her go and move them."

Lira sat back and looked at the ceiling for a while.

"So that's what he is now," she said.

"That's what he's been since midwinter," said Cael. "He just let us see it."

Karis looked up sharply. "Since when?"

"Since the day the wing's monitoring didn't change." Cael found that he had to work at getting the next sentence out level, and did the work, and got it out level. "I've had it since Sowing. I haven't said it because saying it makes it a claim and a claim is a thing that can be wrong, and if I'm wrong about that man in the direction I want to be wrong, four people get taken apart. So I've been carrying it as a hypothesis for eleven weeks and it has been the single heaviest thing I own."

"And now?"

"Now he's spent something." Cael put both hands on the table. "Everything up to tonight was omission. Omission is free — it's the absence of an act, it leaves no trace, and if anybody ever came for him he could stand in front of an audit board and say *nothing met the reporting threshold* and they would have to prove intent out of six true reports. That's why he did it that way. That's craft.

"Tonight he performed an act. He stopped in a corridor and moved information across a line, deliberately, at his own initiative, in a building with a Compact inspection four days behind it. It's deniable, it's small, it's the most careful version of it that could possibly exist — and it is still an *act*, and he did it, and he did it for a set of notebooks that aren't even mine."

Brom said, "So what do we do?"

"Nothing." Cael said it without hesitation, because he had decided it about four minutes ago and had already checked it twice. "Nothing at all. We don't thank him. We don't acknowledge it. We don't change one thing about how any of us behaves in that office, and when I hand in coursework on Fifth-day I say thank you for the form and nothing else, exactly as I have said it every week since Sowing.

"Because he has arranged his entire life so that nobody can prove anything about him, and the only way that arrangement fails is if somebody who is grateful decides they need him to know they're grateful." He looked round at the three of them. "Gratitude is the leak. That's the whole risk. Not a report, not an audit — one of us, in a corridor, at the wrong moment, saying a sentence that means something."

Nobody argued.

Karis said, after a while, "I want to put something in the minute and I want you all to hear the shape of it, because it's a finding and I don't like it.

"I have said, twice this year, that instruments don't change sides. I said it in Sowing and I said it in the autumn and I said it with a great deal of confidence, and the reason I said it is that it is *true of instruments*." She had her hands flat again. "I was applying a correct principle to the wrong class of object. He isn't an instrument. He is a person who has been used as one for fifteen years by an organisation that will discard him the moment he stops functioning, and I built an entire model of him out of the way he was being used rather than out of what he is, which is exactly the error that has been made about Cael by every institution he has ever walked into."

She stopped.

"That's the finding. It's mine. I got it wrong and I would like it written down that I got it wrong, in those words, because if I ever make that mistake again I want the page to exist."

"Noted," said Cael.

"Say it properly."

"It's in the log tonight, in your words, with your name on it." He almost smiled. "You're going to hate reading it in six months."

"I know," said Karis. "That's what the page is for."

---

The field-assessment unit had its last session two days later, in the assessment office's back room at the third bell, with six enrollees and a syllabus that Gault had approved and did not attend.

It was not a remarkable hour. The unit had never had a remarkable hour. They ran the last three positional exercises, and the teaching assistant walked the room and corrected two second-years on their sight-line arithmetic in about nine words each, and then set the closing exercise, which was the same closing exercise the unit had run in its second week: pick a room and describe where its attention goes.

Cael wrote his and handed it in and it was not the best one in the room, deliberately, by about a fifth.

At the end, the teaching assistant collected the sheets, squared them, made two marks in the wing ledger, and said, to the room, in the flat register in which he had said everything for four sessions:

"The unit runs again next term. Enrolment goes on the residence board in the first week. Those of you continuing into assessment work will find the second unit harder and less interesting, which is the correct order."

That was all of it.

Cael went out with the other five and did not look back, and said nothing at all, and did not stop at the counter, and got as far as the covered walk before he had to put his hand on a pillar for a moment.

*Four sessions,* he wrote, that night. *Thirty-two hours of a Shadow Path practitioner working at eight feet, and a syllabus, and a countersigned attendance record, and a man who taught it exactly the way he would have taught it to six ordinary second-years going into assessment work, because teaching it any other way would have been visible.*

*I have never been given anything larger by anybody in my life and there is no page in the world where that transaction is recorded, and there is not going to be one, and if there were it would end him.*

*Filed under debts, no current mechanism of payment. Second entry. Same file. Same nothing.*

---

The registry's road ran east out of Ostrand along the river for eleven miles and then turned inland, and the delegation's carriages made the posting-house at the turn on the second evening, which was on schedule, because the schedule had been built by clerks who were good at it.

The posting-house gave the senior officers three rooms on the upper floor. Vastin took the smallest of them, which was at the back over the yard, because it had a table under the window and the other two did not.

He ate downstairs with the counsel and the records officer and did not talk about the inspection, because an inspection discussed in a public room is an inspection that has been reported by the third party at the next table, and because he had never in forty years found any professional value in talking about work he had not yet finished thinking about.

Then he went up, and lit the lamp, and put the inspection file on the table, and opened it.

The findings were drafted. Counsel had them in hand and they would be filed within the month and they would say what Halcenvane's counsel had predicted they would say, because Halcenvane's counsel was good at her job: the clause was unrepealed; the enrollment conformed to its language; the institution's documentary practice was of a standard the finding would describe as *complete*, which was the highest adjective the form permitted; the fire-watch's ledger carried a clerical remark.

None of that was what he had come up the bluff road to determine, and he had told the boy so, and it had been true.

He read the evaluation record.

He read it the whole way through, all three hours of it in the clerk's hand — the trials, the intervals, the questions and the times taken to answer them, the void, the fault, the re-run, the note read into the record. He had watched the entire session with his own eyes eight days earlier and he read the record anyway, from the first line, because in forty years he had never once found the two to be identical and had learned a great deal from the difference.

Then he took out the notebook.

It was small, and calf-bound, and about a third used, and it had come with him from the last inspection and would go with him to the next one. He had filled perhaps four pages in it since spring. He did not keep it for evidence, and nothing in it was ever transcribed into a file; it was the place where he wrote a thing down in order to find out, later, whether he had still thought it.

There were two entries from Halcenvane.

The first was from the nineteenth, from the Crown yard, from a bracket final he had attended on the chancellor's invitation and would have attended without it.

*The Copper champion is Iron-grade. The bracket is the error. Recommend the advancement evaluation proceed unimpeded.*

He had written it in the fourth exchange, before the result, and had not revised it. He had watched a Wind practitioner solve an Iron Skin practitioner through range in a cage built by people who had never imagined either of them, in front of six hundred people, and had known inside ninety seconds that the institution's assessment office would file for her advancement on the strength of the season and that somebody at his own altitude would eventually be asked whether the filing had merit.

They would ask. He had answered in advance, which was the only useful thing an Archmarshal could do with a bracket final, and the answer would hold, because his answers held.

The second entry was from the twentieth.

He had written it during the eleven minutes in which the reserve frame was being brought across, and he had written it fast, and it had originally read:

*Frame fault, trial five. Correction preceded the audible release. Capability exceeds classification.*

Ninety minutes later, at the session's close, standing in a room that was emptying, he had opened the notebook a second time and struck the last three words through — cleanly, once, so that they remained legible, because a struck line that cannot be read is a lie about one's own thinking — and had written underneath them:

*Correction was sized to the hazard, not to the demonstration. Note the sizing. Not the speed.*

He looked at the amendment now for some time.

*An evaluation that begins from its conclusion is a report about the evaluator.* He had been told that at twenty-six by a woman who had gone on to be wrong about a great many things and right about that one, and he had repeated it to perhaps two hundred officers since, and the reason he repeated it was not that it was wise. It was that he had needed it, personally, in a room, two hours into a three-hour session, at sixty years of age, with a pen in his hand.

*Capability exceeds classification* was a conclusion. It might well be correct. It was, on the material, more likely correct than not. But he had written it inside two minutes of the event and before the re-run, and a man who writes his finding inside two minutes has stopped measuring and started confirming, and every subsequent observation in that session would have obliged him. Instruments always oblige the man reading them. So do eyes.

*Sized to the hazard, not to the demonstration.*

That was what he had actually seen, and it was smaller than the conclusion and harder to argue with and worth ten times as much.

A displacement had been demonstrated eleven times that morning at a consistent magnitude. Under a mechanical failure that produced a heavier load arriving a full count early, the enrollee had produced a *shorter* one — not a bigger answer, a correctly-shaped one — and had absorbed the difference in his own body, and had done it before the frame's release was audible, and had given, when asked why, an answer about where the clerk was standing.

Everything in that sentence was ordinary except the timing, and the timing was ordinary too if you accepted the surface-read capability that appeared four times in the man's own record and twice in a Greyvane exhibit.

What was not ordinary was that a boy of sixteen, mid-payment on a committed technique, in a room containing every instrument and every eye that could ever matter to him, had solved a hazard he had not been given by making his own answer *smaller*.

Vastin had spent forty years watching practitioners meet the unexpected. Almost all of them, at every tier, in every service, did the same thing: they gave it more. It was the single most reliable observation of his career and he had never once bothered to write it down, because a thing that is true of everyone is not an observation.

He closed the notebook and put it beside the file.

Then he read the rest of it, and did not hurry, and let the lamp burn down a third of an inch.

The Greyvane adjudication transcript, all sessions, which he had read twice on the road up and now read the relevant sessions of a third time. The exhibits. The provision's documentary chain in Bracken's index, ninety seconds to the load-bearing sheet, which was the best-built enrollment file he had handled in nine years and which he made a private note to say so about, to the registrar, in a letter, because good work should be told. The season's standings. The evaluation record. The examination log from the east room, twenty-nine minutes, eleven questions, one asked back.

*What is the Compact evaluating? Me, or the provision?*

He had been asked a great many questions across a great many tables by a great many people who wanted something. He could not immediately recall the last time he had been asked one by somebody who simply wanted to know the answer and had worked out, correctly, that the only way to find out was to spend one of his own thirty minutes on it.

The boy had a notebook too. That had been in the monitoring product for six months and in the Greyvane transcript before that and it was the least interesting fact in the file to everybody who had read it.

Vastin had asked what it was for and had been told, and had believed the answer, and had then not asked the next question — the obvious one, the one counsel across the table had drawn breath for, the one any competent officer would have put and any incompetent one would have put badly.

He had considered it for slightly under a second and had declined it, and the reason had nothing to do with mercy.

The reason was that his mandate was to establish whether the record and the practitioner matched, and the contents of a private notebook were not in the record, and an officer who reaches outside his mandate because the reaching is available is an officer who has begun collecting rather than evaluating — and a collector, at his altitude, with his authority, is the most dangerous thing the Compact is capable of producing. He had watched one operate for eleven years early in his career. He had helped end him. It had taken four of them and a magistrate and it had cost two careers that had not deserved to be spent, and he had promised himself, at thirty-four, in a corridor, that he would not become the thing he had spent a decade dismantling.

So he had not asked, and he was not going to, and if there came a day when the question was properly his, he would ask it in a room with the door shut and tell the boy first that he was asking it.

That day was not this one.

He squared the file.

There was one page left to do and it was the page that would outlive everything else in the folder, because findings are read once and filed, and a file note is read by every officer who ever opens the file afterward, forever.

He took a clean sheet, and dated it, and thought for a while, and did not write.

The plain readings were available and all of them were wrong. *No adverse finding* — accurate, useless, a document that shuts a door somebody else may need to open. *Subject warrants attention* — a sentence that would put a warden on a bluff road inside a month and would produce, in a boy who had already been through one adjudication, exactly the behaviour that makes a monitoring file impossible to read afterward.

And underneath both of them sat the reading that half the continent had already reached and that a small academy in the hills had built an institution's worth of conviction on: that the file was a mistake. A misfiled Kindling. A clerk's hand, a registry apparatus, a boy of eleven in a certification hall in a town nobody had heard of, and one wrong mark that four years of law and two academies had been spent unpicking. It was a comfortable reading. It was the reading that let everybody go on believing the system worked and had simply slipped, once, in a small way, on one person.

Vastin had spent eleven days at Halcenvane and forty years in the registry's arms, and he did not believe it.

Not because the boy was extraordinary. Extraordinary practitioners came through the schedule at a steady rate and the schedule held every one of them; that was what the schedule was for. He did not believe it because a mistake is careless, and there was no carelessness anywhere in this file. The designation had been generated at the Kindling itself, before any human being had formed an opinion. It had held through four years, three institutions, a public adjudication, and a documentary defence that Bracken's index made unassailable, and in all that time nobody at any altitude had produced a competing classification, an amended entry, or a single sheet proposing one.

A slipped hand gets corrected. This had never been touched.

Which meant the record was not a failure of the record. It was doing something.

He was not going to write that down. He had no evidence for it, it was a long way outside his mandate, and a man of his grade putting speculation into a file is a man arranging for somebody junior to act on it in three years' time. What he could write was the finding that followed from the material and stopped exactly there.

He wrote one line, in the plain upright hand he had used since he was a clerk, and signed it with his own name, entire, the way he signed everything, because in forty years he had never once found a good reason for an officer to be faceless in a document that could ruin somebody.

*Classification error is unlikely. Continue observation.*

He set the pen down.

He did not underline anything. He read the line once, and found that it still said what he had meant, which is the only test available, and closed the file.

# Chapter 1 — The Assay

The bluff road came out of the pines two miles above the ferry crossing, and from there you could see the whole argument at once: Ostrand spread along the near bank in its brown-and-slate sprawl, the Ost running wide and slow and the color of weak tea between, and on the far bluff, above and apart and deliberately both, Halcenvane.

Cael stopped walking to look at it, which was not something he did often on a road, and let the others get four paces ahead before either of them noticed.

Five months. He had been keeping the number since the morning they left, not because he was sentimental about intervals but because an interval you failed to count was an interval that could later be argued about. Five months and nine days since the adjudication panel at Greyvane had read out a ruling that made him, for the first time in his life, a person the law had been forced to describe accurately. Four days since they had walked out of Greyvane's gate for the last time. Fifteen years old, which he had checked against Hesk's letters twice on the road, because he had learned that a number nobody was tracking on your behalf was a number that drifted.

He looked at the far bluff and did the thing he always did with a new object, which was to take it apart before deciding how he felt about it.

Greyvane had been a converted waystation: one wall, one training hall, one lecture wing built with the place itself in mind and everything else inherited. Halcenvane had been built. That was legible from two miles out, in the way the roofline agreed with itself — a single architectural intention carried across nine or ten buildings instead of six centuries of patching. He counted what he could resolve at that distance. Three long halls with the high clerestory windows that meant training floors, arranged along a north edge to take morning light. A wing set apart from the others behind its own low wall, which he provisionally read as either assessment or administration and could not yet split. A lecture complex, four stories, newer stone. Two — no, three — residence blocks laid around courtyards, the same building repeated with the economy of an institution that had needed more beds twice and had not felt the need to redesign anything for the occasion. A library, and he knew it was the library because it was the only building on the bluff with a roof pitched for weight rather than weather, and slate over a book-hall was a decision somebody had costed.

And at the middle of all of it, where a chapel would have gone in an older institution and a parade ground in a military one, an open floor with tiered stone seating around three of its four sides.

He did not know what that was yet. He knew what it meant that it was in the middle.

"You've been standing there a while," Lira said, having come back for him, which she always did and never once mentioned doing.

"Counting."

"Counting what?"

"Seats." He resumed walking. "Whatever that yard in the center is, it holds about eleven hundred people. That's more than the whole enrollment. Which means it's not for the students."

Brom, from behind: "It's for the town."

"It's for whoever they want to show something to." Cael filed it and kept walking, and did not add the rest of the thought, which was that a school that built its largest structure for spectators had told you its priorities before you ever read its charter.

---

He had written the five months down properly the night before, in the room above the coaching inn at the last stage before Ostrand, because a period that was over deserved to be closed out rather than allowed to blur. It was a habit that had started as bout-notes and had grown, over three years, into something closer to accounting. Open the interval. Enter what happened. Enter what it cost. Close it.

*Greyvane, final term. Closed.*

*Week three: Karis found it.* Not the whole thing — she had been careful about that from the first evening, careful in the way she was always careful when she had something and did not yet want anyone building on it. What she had was a citation in a founding-era compilation of charter language, four lines long, describing a procedure by which chartered institutions had once evaluated practitioners for whom no registry classification existed. The *assay clause*. She had said the phrase out loud once, in Prynn's archive, at a volume that would not have carried past the table, and then had not said it again for eleven weeks.

Because a clause you had found was not a clause you could use. Eleven weeks was what it had cost to prove the thing nobody wanted to assume: that the standardization directive which had superseded the old vocabulary, and had strongly discouraged the practice, and had made the whole method professionally unfashionable for four decades — had never actually repealed it. Karis had gone at that the way she went at everything, which was from the direction of trying to kill it. She had written to nine institutions and had six replies. She had traced the directive's own repeal schedule, item by item, and had found the assay clause on none of the three lists where a repealed provision would have had to appear. She had asked Prynn for the pre-standardization compilations and had gotten them, and had asked for the post-standardization consolidations and had gotten those too, and had laid the two sets against each other for a month, and had come out the far end with a finding she stated in her flattest register, which was how she stated things she was certain of: *the clause was not repealed. It was orphaned. Nobody killed it; everybody stopped using it, and the paperwork records the stopping and not a killing.*

Cael had asked her, that night, what the difference was.

"Custom against text," she'd said. "You already won that argument once."

*Weeks fourteen through nineteen: the letters.* Registrar Bracken of Halcenvane Academy wrote in a small upright hand and did not waste a line, and there were, by the end, twenty-two letters in the bundle. Cael had read all of them, because Karis had brought them to him unopened the first time and had never once, afterward, considered doing otherwise. Bracken's first letter had asked four questions. Her fourth had asked forty-one. Her fifteenth had contained no questions at all and instead a two-page reconstruction of the clause's documentary provenance, assembled independently, arriving at Karis's conclusion by a different route — which Karis had found more satisfying than agreement, because independent arrival was worth more than assent.

Somewhere in there, Halcenvane's counsel had verified the whole architecture and put a seal on the verification. Naveth had read that letter twice in the provost's office at Greyvane with an expression Cael could not fully parse and had eventually decided was envy of an honorable kind: a man looking at the resources a real institution could bring to a question he had staked his career on with almost none.

*Week twenty: Edran.*

He had honored it, because it had been promised. Full sanction, faculty supervised, on the record, exactly the terms Edran had named at the end of the previous term. Wray presided. Quenna sat the second seat. Two hundred and some students in the gallery, which at Greyvane meant essentially all of them.

Edran had spent a season rebuilding, and it showed from the first exchange. The recommitment seam Cael had opened last time was gone — not disguised, gone, closed by a season of somebody drilling it out of him with the patience of a person who resented needing to. What replaced it was worse to fight: a Glass Path stance that gave up a fraction of its reach to buy a half-beat of recovery, which meant every one of Cael's reads arrived correct and a half-beat too slow to be worth anything.

He had won it in the eighth exchange, by a margin thin enough that he had gone back to the log that night and written the honest version rather than the flattering one.

*Margin: one exchange. Possibly none — Wray's call on the sixth was defensible either way and went to me. Wind-adjacent, four deployments across eight exchanges, which is one over my working ceiling and I felt it: the fourth burst landed a half-inch short of where I'd placed it and the recovery beat ran long. Hip strain, leading side, three days. Gain: the fourth burst is what bought the eighth exchange, so the price was correct, but I want it on the page that I paid over my rate to win and that a better opponent than Edran would have made me pay it twice.*

Edran had put a hand out afterward, which was new, and had said: "Third one someday." Not a courtesy. Cael had watched him say it, and had read it in the shoulders as much as the face, and had filed it under promises rather than pleasantries.

*Week twenty-one: Quenna.*

She had found him in the yard at the end, as she had found him at the end of everything, and had said one sentence.

"Told you it would be soon."

That was the whole of it. She had not said the paragraph underneath it — that she had made the biggest professional bet of her career on a boy nobody could classify and had watched it pay out into an institution four times Greyvane's size taking him on a documented legal basis she had spent two years wishing she could have offered him herself. She had not said it and he had not asked her to, and he had understood, walking away, that the sentence had cost her more than the paragraph would have.

*Week twenty-two: Prynn.*

She had come to the gate, which nobody had ever seen Prynn do, and had pressed a bound sheaf into his hands without preamble. Fifty-odd pages, hand-copied, in an archivist's hand that had not gotten smaller in sixty years: an index of registry-law citations, cross-referenced three ways, every entry carrying its source compilation and the shelf-mark where the source compilation actually lived.

"You'll want this," she had said. "The next archive will be bigger and worse-organized. Big ones always are. They hire for cataloguing and promote for administration and the second thing eats the first inside a decade."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, use it." She had already been turning back toward her door. Then, without turning around: "The shelf gap is still there. It'll be there when you're forty."

---

The road down from Greyvane had taken four days and there had never been fewer than two of them.

He had counted, to pass the time, because the alternative was thinking about the road and there were only so many hours a person could spend thinking about a road. The rotations had been open — not concealed, not even lightly disguised, and he understood after the first morning that the openness was the point. A watcher who hides is trying to collect information. A watcher who does not is trying to produce behavior.

*Day one: two, both mounted, changed once at the second waystation. Day two: three — the mounted pair plus one on the coach with us, grey coat, no attempt to sit anywhere but where he could see all four of us. Day three: two, new faces. Day four: two, and one of day two's. Total distinct faces across four days: seven. Total distinct rotations: five. Standing coverage: two minimum, three at road junctions and inn stops. Nobody spoke to us. Nobody needed to.*

*Gain to them, from doing it this way: I know I'm watched, which is meant to change how I behave. Price to them: I now have seven faces, five rotation patterns, and a working estimate of what the Compact is willing to spend on a fifteen-year-old — which is two men and a horse-change every eighty miles, indefinitely. That's a real budget. Somebody signs for it every month.*

*Note: they're not hiding, and they're also not stopping. Both of those are decisions. I'd like to know who made the second one.*

Brom had asked, on the third afternoon, whether it bothered him.

"Less than it should," Cael had said, and had thought about it further, because Brom asked questions that deserved second attempts. "It's a known quantity. I can see them, I can count them, I know what they cost. The thing that would bother me is if the count went to zero and I couldn't work out why."

"That'd mean they stopped."

"Or that they got better."

---

Ostrand was the largest place any of them had walked into since Ardenmere, and it was a different animal from Ardenmere entirely.

Ardenmere had been a city with a district the maps declined to describe. Ostrand was a city that had been drawn on maps carefully, repeatedly, by people with a financial interest in its accuracy. It sat at the last navigable bend of the Ost, which meant everything coming down out of the hill country had to be broken out of carts and put into boats here, or the reverse, and a town that exists because two kinds of transport meet at it develops a particular set of muscles. Cael read them off the approach without meaning to: warehousing along the whole east bank, six long sheds and a seventh going up; a crane gantry at the public wharf that four men were working and a second, private one further down that had a company mark on it; a customs house with its own dock and its own steps, positioned so that nothing could reach the public wharf without passing it.

The bridge was stone, five spans, with a toll house at the near end. Two coppers for a person on foot, four for a laden handcart, a schedule painted on a board with nine categories on it, which meant somebody had been arguing about the categories for a long time. Brom paid for all four of them before anyone could object, which was a thing Brom did roughly once a week and never discussed.

"How many people live here?" Lira asked, on the bridge, watching a barge work its way up against the current with two men on the sweeps and a boy on the tiller.

"Twenty thousand, near enough," Karis said. "It's in the academy's own prospectus, so it's probably rounded up. Fourteen guild lodges, three of them with their own halls. Two registry offices — a district office and a stations office, which are not the same thing, and I intend to find out the difference within a fortnight."

"Why does that matter?"

"Because one of them files things about Cael and the other doesn't, and I'd like to know which."

Cael looked at her sideways. "You've been in this city eleven minutes."

"I've been in this city eleven minutes and I read the prospectus in Greyvane in the spring." She said it without any self-satisfaction at all, which left him nothing to push back against. "There's a tournament in it, by the way. Continental. Every three years. I'll get to that when we're not on a bridge."

The road up the far bluff was properly made — cut, drained, surfaced, with passing bays for carts at the two switchbacks, which told Cael that this road carried loads and did so on a schedule. Halfway up, a wagon came down past them with four barrels and a driver who nodded at them without curiosity, and Cael understood from the nod that arriving students walking up this hill were not, at Halcenvane, an event.

The wall at the top was not old. It had been built in the same generation as everything behind it, faced stone over rubble core, four and a half feet — a wall that marked a boundary rather than defended one. The gate had two leaves and stood open, and beside it there was a lodge with glass in the window and a man inside it with a ledger.

Cael had walked up to a gate with a list on it once before, and had felt something in his chest he had not had a category for. He braced for it and found, this time, something quieter and more complicated in its place: not relief. Something closer to the feeling of a machine engaging.

"Names?"

"Cael. Lira. Brom. Karis Dellenmoor."

The clerk ran a finger down the page and stopped and did not look up.

"Four of you. Karis Dellenmoor, standard enrollment, research standing pending. Brom, standard enrollment, Blade cohort assessment on the ninth. Lira, re-certification transfer, Copper bracket, ladder registration by the fourteenth." He turned the page. "Cael. Assay-provision enrollment. Registrar's office directly — she's expecting you, and she's blocked the whole morning, so don't be leisurely about the walk."

He looked up then, at all four of them together, with the mild professional impatience of a man with a dozen more arrivals to process before noon.

"Second quadrangle for residence, the porter has your door assignments. Assessment wing is the low building with its own wall; don't cut through the Crown yard when a bout's called, they'll shout at you. Welcome to Halcenvane."

Four names. Four lines. Four tracks that had all been decided in advance, in writing, by people who had never met any of them, on the strength of documents.

Cael walked through the gate and did not stop in it, and noticed himself not stopping, and filed that too.

---

Intake ran four desks in a hall built for twelve.

That was the first inversion, and it landed harder than he had expected. The Greyvane intake room had been somebody's converted study, three desks and a bench and a window. Halcenvane's was a purpose-built hall a hundred feet long with a coffered ceiling and a rank of high windows down the west side, and on an ordinary intake morning it ran four desks out of twelve, with the other eight standing shrouded, because the building had been sized for the institution Halcenvane intended to become rather than the one it currently was. Somebody had built ambition into the joinery.

Cael counted arrivals while they queued: thirty-one that morning, by his tally, in the ninety minutes before his own name was called. Greyvane had taken forty-three students in his whole first year.

Brom's desk took nine minutes. Certification, transfer record, two seasons of documented circuit results and two terms of documented academy standing, stamp, tag. The clerk did not look at his papers so much as confirm the shape of them. Nine minutes, and Brom came away turning the tag over in his fingers with the expression of a man who had now been the uncomplicated case twice and had still not gotten used to it.

"Ninth," he said. "Blade cohort assessment. That's four days."

"You're not Blade Path," Lira said.

"No, but that's who Rooke runs, and Rooke runs the fast ones, and I've been told by three separate people since the bridge that if I want to find out how slow I am I should go and stand in front of his cohort." He put the tag in his pocket. "So I'm going to go and find out how slow I am."

Karis's desk took twenty minutes, most of which was the research-standing annex, and she came away with three forms she had not been expecting and an expression of undisguised greed.

"They have a library committee," she said. "With a *charter*. Which means the terms of archive access are written down, which means they can be read, which means they can be argued with." She looked around the hall as though it had personally impressed her. "Greyvane had Prynn. Prynn was better than any charter. But Prynn was one woman and a shelf and no appeal."

Lira's desk took fifty minutes, and Cael watched most of it, because Lira standing at an administrative table with her arms folded was a thing he had learned to keep an eye on.

Her file had traveled ahead of her — the Fenmark record, the acknowledgment of prior standing she had signed a year and a half ago, and, appended, the thing Greyvane had managed to produce for her and had never had the standing to make anyone act on: a priority-review flag on her formal classification, entered by an academy that had watched a Copper-tier practitioner beat Iron-equivalent opposition for two terms and had said so, in writing, on a form.

The Halcenvane clerk read the flag, and did something no Greyvane clerk had ever done: she nodded at it, made a note in a margin, and said, "That transfers. It goes to the registry's own queue, not ours."

"And what does their queue do?"

"Moves." The clerk did not look up. "Slowly. But it moves — we've had three flagged reassessments come back in the last two years, so it isn't a drawer they throw things in. In the interim your ladder placement runs off your formal tier, which is Copper."

Lira's jaw did the thing it did.

"That's the bracket."

"That's the bracket."

"Even though the flag exists."

"The flag says the tier might be wrong. It doesn't say the tier *is* wrong; only an Arbiter evaluation says that." The clerk finally looked up, and there was nothing unkind in her face at all, which somehow made it worse. "I've filed nine of these. I've never once thought the system moved fast enough. Copper bracket, registration by the fourteenth, and I'd get your name on that ladder early because the standings do get read by people who matter."

Lira signed. She did not say anything on the way back over, and when Cael caught her eye she shook her head very slightly, which meant *not now, and not because I'm angry at you.*

---

Bracken's office was on the assessment wing's ground floor, behind a door with a brass plate, and she had cleared the desk before he arrived, which he registered before he registered her.

Registrar Bracken was somewhere in her forties, narrow-faced, with ink on the first two fingers of her right hand and a filing system visible through the open door of the room behind her that made Cael want, immediately and improperly, to go and look at it. There were four stacks on the desk and each stack had a slip on top of it with a number written in the small upright hand he had read twenty-two letters in.

"Sit down. You've read the letters?"

"All of them."

"Good, that saves us ten minutes." She pulled the first stack toward her. "I'm going to talk while I work. Stop me when something is wrong, and I mean anything — a date, a place name, the spelling of your grandfather's name. This document is going to be read by people whose entire professional method is finding the one line that doesn't hold, and I would rather find it now, in this room, than have it found for me in eighteen months by a man in grey."

She worked through it. Ninety minutes, and Cael spent all of them in the strange condition of watching a system function correctly on his behalf, which was not a sensation he had a stored comparison for.

The first stack was provenance: the assay clause's text, in three separate pre-standardization compilations, each with its shelf-mark and each with a certified copy attached. The second stack was the negative case — the three repeal schedules, the two consolidations, and Bracken's own tabulated demonstration that the clause appeared on none of them, with an accompanying note in her hand explaining what conclusion the absence supported and, in the next sentence, what conclusion it did not.

"That second note is the important one," she said. "Never let a document claim more than it proves. A file that overreaches by one sentence is a file that can be attacked on that sentence and then dismissed entirely."

"Karis says something close to that."

"Karis Dellenmoor writes better letters than most of the counsel I've dealt with and she is, I understand, seventeen. I've made my peace with it." She squared the stack. "Third."

The third stack was Greyvane. The adjudication transcript, complete, all sessions, all exhibits — a public record now, and Bracken had it bound and tabbed like something she intended to use rather than something she intended to survive. The ruling itself sat on top with two lines underscored.

"This is your foundation and it is also your problem," she said. "It establishes that custom is not code — which is your friend, because the assay clause is code and its retirement was custom. It also establishes you publicly, in a document anyone can buy a copy of for six silver marks, as the most interesting enrollment question on the continent. Both of those are permanent. You don't get one without the other."

"I understood that when I argued it."

"Most people say they understood a thing when they mean they'd been told it." She glanced up, briefly, and something in her face moved and then stopped moving. "Fourth stack."

The fourth stack was the enrollment itself, and it was thin.

"The provision creates a standing," Bracken said. "Not an exemption, not a waiver, not a provisional anything. A standing — a category, with its own name, its own entry requirement, and its own ongoing obligation. Read it before you sign it, and then read it again, because the obligation is real and it does not get smaller."

He read it.

*Enrollment record. Halcenvane Academy, chartered.*

*Category: assay-provision practitioner: evaluation by demonstration per charter clause, standing basis.*

*Entry requirement: satisfied by demonstration before the assessment office, this term.*

*Ongoing requirement: full evaluation by demonstration before the panel of the assessment office, each semester, without exception. Compact observation rights attach.*

He read it three times.

He was aware of doing it, and aware of why, because he had done exactly this once before at a smaller table in a smaller room with a colder cup of tea beside him, reading a line that had said *unclassified observer* and the words *has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment* beneath it, and had read them three times not to understand them but to make himself accept that he was going to sign them anyway.

That form had been defensible. Quenna had been right about that, and the panel at the hearing had ultimately been right about it too, in the narrow way that mattered. Every word on it had held up to scrutiny exactly as written. And every person at that table — Quenna, Naveth, the clerk, Cael himself — had known that the words held up by describing something other than the truth, and had agreed, without saying so, to let them.

This one said what he was.

Not what he could do; the document made no claim about that, and he understood that the absence of the claim was the whole engineering of it. It said what his relationship to the institution was. He was a person whose capability could not be read off a registry entry and therefore had to be shown, in a room, to people with instruments, on a schedule, forever. That was accurate. That was, as far as he could tell, the most accurate sentence anyone had ever written about him in an official hand, and it had taken a seventeen-year-old eleven weeks and a registrar twenty-two letters and a chancellor's counsel and a public adjudication record to make it exist.

"Something wrong?" Bracken said.

"No." He set the page down flat. "It's the first one that's true."

Bracken did not respond to that immediately. She finished squaring the fourth stack, and then she said, in exactly the register she had used for shelf-marks: "It will be inspected. Not if — when, and probably inside two years. I want you to hold that fact next to the other one and not let go of either."

"I intend to."

"Good. Sign in both places. The second signature is the acknowledgment of the ongoing requirement, and I want it separate from the first, because a person who signs the standing and the obligation in one motion can later claim he only read one of them."

He signed in both places.

His handwriting was careful — the same hand he used for the fragment log, the hand that had learned somewhere back in Denvash that permanence deserved precision. He watched the ink dry on the second signature and found, sitting there in a registrar's office on a bluff above a river he had first seen four hours earlier, that his chest had done something he did not have a good name for and that his eyes had gone briefly and inconveniently hot, and that neither of those things was going to be permitted to happen in front of Registrar Bracken.

She had, in any case, already turned to her filing cabinet, which he suspected — with no evidence and complete confidence — was deliberate.

"Ninety-four minutes," she said, from inside the drawer. "I'd budgeted a hundred and twenty. Your tag's on the corner of the desk. Assessment office will schedule your entry demonstration; Magister Gault runs that panel and he is not warm, and I would encourage you not to read anything into it, because he isn't warm to anyone and it has never once affected a finding."

"Registrar."

"Mm."

"Twenty-two letters," Cael said. "Why?"

Bracken shut the drawer. For the first time in ninety-four minutes she stopped moving entirely.

"Because I have kept this academy's enrollment records for twelve years," she said, "and in twelve years I have never once been asked to file a document that required me to be *good at my job* in order to be true. Every other record in that cabinet survives because nobody looks at it." She turned back to her desk. "Yours will be looked at by an inspector. That's not a burden. That's the first interesting thing that's happened to my filing system since I built it. Go and find your residence."

---

The four of them found a wall, because they always found a wall.

This one ran along the bluff's edge behind the second quadrangle, waist-high, with a drop below it to the terraced slope and the river beyond, and the whole of Ostrand laid out on the far bank going gold at the edges as the light came down. Lamps were being lit along the wharf a mile off and half a mile below. A bell rang somewhere behind them, four strokes, for some hour of the institution's day none of them had learned yet.

Nobody said very much for a while. It had been a long four days.

"I've been assigned a research carrel," Karis said eventually, in the tone of a woman announcing a birth. "With a *number*. Carrel eleven."

"Is eleven good?" Brom asked.

"Eleven is a place where I can leave things overnight." She was already writing. "Greyvane had a table."

Lira had her elbows on the wall and her chin on her hands, looking at nothing. "Copper bracket," she said, to the river.

"I know."

"Two terms of beating Iron-equivalents. A flag on my file that an entire academy signed. A registry queue that *moves*, apparently, at three a year." She exhaled through her nose. "And I get sorted by the number, same as always. The number's the only thing anybody sorts by."

"They said the flag transfers."

"The flag transfers into a queue." She straightened up. "I'm not sulking. I want that on the record — I got a door today that Greyvane couldn't have opened and I know exactly what it cost to open it. I'm just going to be extremely annoying about the bracket for approximately one season, and then I'm going to win the thing, and then we'll see whether the queue moves faster for people whose names are in the standings."

Brom considered this. "That's a plan."

"That's a grudge with a schedule. Same thing, if you keep the schedule."

Cael got the Power Log out.

He had bought the current binder in Greyvane's second term and it was a third full, and the habit of opening it in front of these three had stopped being a decision somewhere in the last year and become simply what he did at the end of things. Lira glanced over without pretending she wasn't. Brom did not look, which was its own form of paying attention. Karis kept writing in her own book and would ask to read his later, and he would say yes, because that had been the arrangement since the day she'd asked for it.

He wrote it plainly, because plain was the only register that could carry something this size without wobbling.

*Enrolled.*

*Second time the word's been used about me. First time it's been accurate.*

He looked at the two lines a while. Below them, in the same hand:

*Karis spent five months making one sentence true. Bracken spent twenty-two letters checking it would hold. Withrow's counsel put a seal on it and Greyvane's provost read that letter twice and looked like a man watching somebody else afford a thing he'd wanted. The sentence cost more than anything I own.*

*I intend to be worth the sentence.*

And beneath that, because the log was for the whole truth and not the comfortable part of it, and because he had learned at fifteen that an entry which recorded only the gain was half an entry:

*Ongoing requirement: every semester, in a room, before a panel, with the Compact entitled to watch. Forever, or for as long as I'm here. That's the price. It's the first price anybody's ever quoted me in advance and in writing.*

*I'd have paid more.*

He closed the binder. The light went off the water and the lamps on the far bank finished coming up in their long uneven line, and behind and above them, somewhere in the middle of the bluff's careful architecture, a yard that seated eleven hundred people sat empty in the dark, waiting for its season to open.

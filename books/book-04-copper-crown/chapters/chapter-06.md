# Chapter 6 — Counted

The thing that finally started it was not suspicion. It was bookkeeping.

Cael had been keeping a glance count since his ninth day at Halcenvane, and he had started it for a reason that had nothing to do with anyone watching him: he'd wanted to know how long the fame was going to last.

At Greyvane, the staring had run about six weeks and then decayed, and he had watched the decay happen and had not measured it, and had regretted not measuring it within a month of arriving here, because here the staring was worse and he had no curve to compare it against. So he had built the crudest instrument he owned. Every time he crossed the long lecture-complex corridor between the second bell and the third — the same corridor, the same hour, four days a week, roughly ninety people in transit — he counted the number who marked his passage. A turned head, a held glance, a nudge to a neighbor. Anything that constituted a decision to look.

Day nine: thirty-one of ninety-one.
Day eleven: twenty-nine of eighty-eight.
Day fourteen: twenty-six of ninety-four.

Roughly one in three, sliding slowly downward, which was a decay curve and would give him a half-life in another month. The number itself was not interesting. What was interesting was that he now had a baseline for what it cost to be looked at in this building, and a baseline is an instrument, and an instrument measures whatever you put in front of it, including things you were not looking for.

On day sixteen he ran the corridor count, wrote *24 of 89* in the margin, and then, at the bottom of the page, without any plan behind it, wrote a second number, because he'd noticed himself noticing it and the notebook's oldest rule was that anything he noticed himself noticing went on the page before he decided whether it mattered.

*TA, assessment office: 0 of 7.*

Seven encounters. Seven days. And not once — not a glance, not a half-glance, not the flick of attention a person gives a door opening — had the man in Gault's office looked at him.

---

He did not do anything about it for two days, which he was later mildly proud of.

One instance was an accident. Two was a coincidence. Three was structure. It was the oldest rule in the notebook, older than most of what surrounded it, and he had broken it exactly once in three years and had underlined the loss it bought him so he would keep not breaking it. Seven instances cleared the threshold with room to spare — except that the rule was about *tells*, about a shoulder-drop repeating across four watched bouts, and what he had here was not a tell. It was an absence. And he had no idea whether the three-instance rule even applied to absences, because he had never in his life tried to build a plan on something that wasn't there.

So he did the honest thing, which was to widen the sample before touching the hypothesis.

On day nineteen he stopped counting only the corridor and started counting everywhere, and by the end of that week he had a number that no amount of squinting made ordinary.

Thirty-one encounters. Corridors, the assessment wing, the yard at the second bell, the gallery rail at hall three, the covered walk between quadrangles, the queue at the residence board on the morning the floor assignments went up. Thirty-one occasions on which he and the teaching assistant from Gault's office had occupied the same space at the same time, close enough for a glance to be free.

Zero glances.

He sat with that on the edge of his cot with the notebook open and did the arithmetic three ways, because the first thing to do with an impossible number is check whether it is actually impossible.

Baseline: roughly one in three people in this building marked him. Call the staff rate lower — staff had seen enrollees before and were not impressed by any of them — so call it one in six. One in six, thirty-one times, meant an expected count somewhere around five. Getting zero from thirty-one draws at one in six was not unheard of; unlikely things happened, and a man who forgot that ended up seeing pattern in weather.

But there was a second number, and the second number was the one that broke it.

He was not measuring the general population. He was measuring a man who worked in the office that processed his enrollment, whose desk held the calendar his evaluations were written on, who had personally entered Cael's floor allocation into the scheduling ledger and read the reverse-side authorizations top to bottom before doing it. Every other member of the assessment wing's staff — four of them, he'd counted — looked at him routinely, and two of them looked at him with open curiosity, because they had all read the same paper about the same hearing and worked in the office where the outcome lived.

The whole institution's attention pointed at him. One person in it had a professional reason to look and did not.

That was not an unlikely draw. That was a man doing something.

---

He built a new instrument, because the old one didn't fit.

The compound gaze was for reading people, and reading people required them to give you something to read — rhythm under load, weight before commitment, breath under strain, the disagreement between the three that meant strain or lying. It was a method built entirely out of what a body leaked. And this body leaked nothing, on seven separate examinations, which he had now performed at increasing depth and increasing shame, because the seventh had involved standing at the assessment wing's copying table for four minutes discussing a filing date he already knew the answer to.

Level rhythm. Settled weight. Slow, ordinary breath. Total agreement between all three. A man who copied paper, being a man who copied paper.

So he stopped trying to read the man and started recording the room.

The new pages did not look like anything else in the notebook. No columns for confirmed, probable, discarded. Instead he drew the campus — badly, four times, until the fifth version held — and gridded it, and every entry after that was three things and no more: a time, a position on the grid, and what the position could see.

The four failures were worth as much as the fifth, and he kept them rather than tearing them out, on the standing principle that a discarded method written down as discarded could not sneak back in disguised as a fresh idea. The first map had been drawn to scale, which had taken an evening and had been useless, because what he needed was not distance but *lines* — two rooms forty feet apart with a wall between them were further from each other than two rooms at opposite ends of a landing, and a scale drawing insisted on saying the opposite. The second had recorded who else was present at each sighting, on the theory that the man might be watching somebody other than him, and had produced four pages of names and no signal at all; he had killed it on day three and had been careful to write down *killed, no signal* rather than simply stopping, because an abandoned line of inquiry that nobody had formally closed had a way of reopening itself at two in the morning. The third had tried to log the man's route between sightings — which had failed for the simple and slightly humiliating reason that observing a route required following someone, and following someone in a building where one in three people turned to look at you was not a thing Cael could do for six consecutive paces. The fourth had been the first to give up on the man entirely and record only the campus, and had been right in principle and wrong in execution: he'd gridded by room, and the answer, when it came, had not lived in rooms. It had lived in rails, landings, stair-heads, and the third body of a cluster — in the places between rooms, which is where a person stands when standing is the work.

*Day 21, second bell. Gallery rail, hall three, north end, four bodies in from the stair. Sees: hall three floor entire, hall one's east doors, the covered walk.*

*Day 21, fifth bell. Crown yard, upper tier, west, third body in a cluster of six. Sees: bout floor, both stair heads, the assessment wing's approach.*

*Day 22, second bell. Lecture complex, second landing, against the inner rail with the traffic between him and the stair. Sees: the long corridor its whole length.*

It took him four days to see it, and when he saw it he went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather coming up off the river.

He had been drawing the wrong overlay. He had been asking *where is he*, and the answer was always *somewhere reasonable, doing his job*, because a teaching assistant with an armful of paper had a legitimate reason to be in any corridor in the building at any hour of the day. That was the beauty of the thing and he'd been staring straight through it.

The right question was the one he'd been asking about fighters for three years and had somehow not thought to ask about a room. Not *where is he*. **Where would I stand?**

So on the fifth version of the map he drew a second layer in a different ink: for every hour of his own routine, the position on the campus from which the largest amount of that hour was visible. It was a solvable problem. He had solved harder ones about sightlines in the Ironyard's fourteen-lamp lighting rig. Hall three at the second bell was best watched from the north end of the gallery rail, four bodies in from the stair, because the stair itself blocked the floor's western third from anywhere closer. The Crown yard at the fifth bell was best watched from the upper west tier, because the lower tiers looked across the floor and the upper looked *down* it and also held both stair heads. The lecture corridor was best watched from the second landing's inner rail, because the outer rail put your back to the traffic and the inner rail put the traffic between you and everyone's line of sight while leaving the corridor open below.

He laid the two layers over each other.

They matched. Not roughly. Not most of the time.

Nineteen recorded positions across five days, and on nineteen of nineteen the teaching assistant from Gault's office had been standing on the optimum.

---

He tested it, because a pattern you have not tried to break is a pattern you have decided to believe.

The first test was the crudest and he expected nothing from it. He changed his training hour — asked Brom to swap the hall-three slot for the later one, which Brom agreed to without asking why, because Brom had long ago sorted Cael's requests into *has a reason* and *will explain eventually* and had never once found the sorting wrong. The change went through the assessment office in the ordinary way, on paper, two days ahead.

Useless as a test. The change went through the assessment office. He had handed the man the answer himself.

The second test was better. He did not change the paper. He simply arrived at hall three forty minutes early on a day when the schedule said otherwise, sat on the bench inside the doors, and watched the gallery rail.

The gallery rail was empty for thirty-one minutes.

At the thirty-second minute a man came along it with an armful of forms, going somewhere, and stopped at the north end four bodies in from the stair — except there were no bodies, because the gallery was empty, and so he did not stop there at all. He carried straight through and out the far door without breaking stride, and Cael understood, watching him not stop, that he had just learned more than the previous three weeks had taught him.

The position had been unavailable. Not physically — the rail was free, a man could stand on it. Unavailable in the other sense. There was nobody there to stand among. And rather than stand on a good sightline alone and be a shape at a rail in an empty gallery, the man had discarded the position entirely and walked on.

Cael wrote that down on the bench with his hands not quite steady, in four words that he would still be turning over a year later:

*He needs a crowd.*

Which meant the concealment was not passive. Which meant it had requirements, and constraints, and failure conditions, and a person operating a thing with requirements and constraints and failure conditions is not incidentally standing somewhere. He is *working*.

The third test he ran twice, because he wanted to be sure and because he was, by then, honestly a little afraid of being right.

At the fifth bell on the following day he stood at the Crown yard's lower rail with his back to the upper west tier, and used the yard's brass session-lamp — the tall one on the north post, polished weekly, the surface of it giving back the whole upper tier in a smeared brown curve — and watched the reflection for eleven minutes while appearing to watch a Copper-bracket practice bout.

Third body in a cluster of five. Upper west tier. He never looked down.

Not once. Not when a Force declaration cracked off the floor and every other head in the yard turned toward the noise. Not when the bout's second exchange put a body onto the boards hard enough to be heard in the lecture complex. Not in eleven minutes, in a yard where the only interesting thing was the floor, and Cael was standing on the floor's edge.

He ran it again two days later from a different angle with a different pretext. Same result.

That night he wrote the sentence the whole month had been assembling itself toward, and it came out on the first attempt, which almost never happened.

*Nobody avoids me that consistently by accident. Avoidance at that precision is aim.*

---

He had, he realized, three grades of monitoring in his notebook now, and he had lived through all of them, and the comparison was the most useful thing he owned.

The first grade was Havel — a junior assessor in Ardenmere with four years' service, who had followed him for a season and had looked at him constantly, and had looked *away* badly, and had twice written in a notebook while standing where Cael could see him do it. Cael had understood at the time that the man was not incompetent. He had understood, later and better, what the tells had actually been made of: Havel had not been sure he was allowed to be doing it. Monitoring that is uncertain of its own authority leaks everywhere, because the officer is watching two things — the subject, and his own standing — and a divided attention shows.

The second grade was what came after the hearing. Rotations, at Greyvane and on the road and now in Ostrand, at the ferry landing and the bluff road's foot: two men, changing every four hours, entirely open about it. He had counted them for eleven days on the walk south to pass the time and had stopped counting because the number never changed. That was monitoring that was sure of itself and had money behind it, and its whole method was to be seen. It produced almost nothing — he had worked that out on the road, and it had been an oddly comforting piece of arithmetic — because a watcher you can see is a watcher whose report you are writing for him.

The third grade was a teaching assistant with an armful of forms who had stood on the optimal sightline nineteen times out of nineteen and had never once let his eye touch the subject.

*What the difference is,* he wrote, *is that the first two were watching me. This one is watching the room I'm in, and I'm the reason the room is worth watching, and those are not the same activity. Havel wanted to see what I did. The rotations wanted me to know they could see. This one wants the shape of my week, and he is getting it, because the shape of my week is a public document and he is standing where it's legible.*

*Note, for honesty: he is better at his job than I have been at mine. It took me twenty-six days. He has had all twenty-six.*

---

He put it to the other three on a Sixth-day evening in the common room, with the door shut and the ladder brackets three days from posting and the whole quadrangle loud enough downstairs that the room's own quiet was not conspicuous.

He gave it to them the way he gave them everything now: the data first, the conclusion last, so that they could disagree with the conclusion without having to fight the data on the way. The glance count and its baseline. The thirty-one encounters and the zero. The two-layer map and the nineteen for nineteen. The empty gallery and the man who had walked on rather than stand alone on a good sightline. The brass lamp and the eleven minutes.

Karis had her notebook out before he was three sentences in and stopped writing halfway through the map, which he had learned meant she'd gotten ahead of him and was waiting for him to catch up so she could check her work against his.

Lira did not sit down at all.

"So we go to Withrow," she said, when he finished.

"That's one option."

"It's the *first* option." She had her arms folded, which was Lira thinking, and her weight on the ball of one foot, which was Lira ready to move, and the two together meant she'd already decided and was going to argue anyway because they'd agreed a long time ago that she would. "He's a Compact officer sitting in the office that holds your evaluation calendar. He's reading your paper. He's inside the one thing at this academy that's actually keeping you enrolled. Withrow accepted the provision knowing there'd be Compact attention — fine. I don't think she signed up for Compact attention with a *desk*."

"Probably not."

"So we tell her, she throws him out, and he's gone."

"And then what," said Karis.

She said it without heat, still looking at the map, and it landed harder for that.

Lira turned. "Then he's gone. That's the point of the sentence."

"Then a man we have identified, whose position we can predict to a stair-head, whose failure conditions we have already partly characterized" — Karis tapped the four words on the bench page, *he needs a crowd* — "is replaced by a man we have not identified, and cannot predict, and know nothing about, and who will have been briefed on exactly why his predecessor was removed and will therefore be better at the thing his predecessor got caught doing." She looked up. "You would be trading an instrument you can read for an instrument you can't find. I don't say that as a preference. I say it as arithmetic."

"He's *spying* on Cael."

"Yes. He was always going to be. The Compact has been watching him continuously for two years, and Cael has known it for two years, and the only variable that has ever actually changed is how well." Karis set her pen down, which she did when she wanted the room to hear something in a register she rarely used. "Lira. I want him out too. I want him out enough that I have to be careful with my own reasoning about it, which is why I am being this careful with it out loud. And every version I run comes back the same way. Exposure buys us a week of feeling better and costs us the only visibility we have ever had into the apparatus."

Lira looked at her for a long moment and did not concede, because Lira did not concede. She sat down on the arm of the chair, which was the same thing.

"Brom," Cael said.

Brom had been quiet through all of it, sprawled the way he sprawled after a Rooke session, one shoulder wrapped, working a bruised knuckle with his thumb. He'd been listening. Brom was always listening; people had been mistaking the sprawl for absence since he was fourteen and he'd never once corrected them.

"We know where their eyes are," he said. "First time ever. Why would we give that back?"

Nobody answered that, because there wasn't an answer to it, which was the entire quality Brom brought to a table and had brought since Ardenmere: an ability to find the one load-bearing sentence in an argument and set it down where everyone could see it holding the weight.

"Then it's settled," Cael said.

"It's not settled, it's *outvoted*," Lira said. "I want that on your page."

"It's going on my page." He was already writing it. "Word for word."

---

The decision itself took another hour, because deciding not to act was the easy part and deciding what to do instead was not.

They agreed on three things, and Cael wrote all three down, because agreements that lived only in a room came apart in six weeks and agreements that lived on a page did not.

The first: no confrontation. Nobody was to look at the man, speak to him beyond the ordinary business of an academy, or in any way alter their conduct in his presence. Karis pointed out — correctly, and to Lira's visible irritation — that this was the hardest of the three and would be broken accidentally by all of them, and that the correct response to breaking it was to report the break to the others rather than to try to smooth it over.

The second: the boring truth. This one was Cael's, and it took him two attempts to explain properly, because the shape of it was counterintuitive until you'd sat with it.

"He's going to file reports," he said. "The reports are going to say what he sees. So the question isn't whether he reports on me. It's what's in the report." He was drawing on the map's margin without meaning to, a small grid. "For two years I've been building a version of myself that's flat on paper. Supervised training. Documented capability. Consistency across trials. Nothing the record doesn't already carry. That's not a disguise — that's just what I actually do, four days a week, and I do it because it's how you get good, and the fact that it also reads flat in a report is the best accident of my life." He looked up. "So we change nothing. We are exactly as boring as we already are, on purpose, forever. He watches, he files, and everything he files is true, and none of it is *news*, and the apparatus reads it and learns that the situation is stable. That's the best product I could possibly get out of a man in that office, and it costs me nothing, because I was going to do it anyway."

Karis wrote that down verbatim. He watched her do it and did not ask what for.

The third took no discussion at all.

"And I study him," Cael said.

He had already started, and all three of them knew it, and Lira's mouth did something that was not entirely a scowl.

"Shadow Path," she said. "You've never charted one."

"Nobody's ever charted one. Not properly. You can't — the Path's whole function is not being available for charting, so the only people who get to watch one work are the people it's being worked on, and by the time they know, it's over." He closed the map page and set his hand flat on it. "I'm being worked on right now. I'm going to be worked on for months. That's the single best observational position anybody's ever going to hand me, and it was handed to me by the people who want to take my enrollment away, and I would very much like to make them regret the generosity."

"That," said Brom, from the chair, "is the meanest thing you've ever said."

"It's not mean. It's just accounting."

"It's accounting with a *tone*." Brom sounded, on balance, pleased.

---

He wrote the entry that night in the Power Log rather than the observation notebook, and hesitated for a moment over which book it belonged in before deciding, because the distinction had never once been arbitrary and he was not going to start now.

The observation notebook held other people. The Power Log held his own architecture — what he could do, what it cost, where it failed.

Counter-observation was not something a stranger did. It was something he did. It had prices — he had already logged the first of them, that afternoon, honestly, after a supervised session in hall three that had been the worst training hour he'd had since arriving. Three weeks of running a positional survey across an entire campus had cost him exactly what three weeks of intensive watching had always cost him, and he had known it would and had done it anyway: the low buzz behind the eyes that ran back into the skull, the attention arriving at the floor already half-spent. He had deployed the Wind-adjacent framework twice against a supervised drill and it had answered both times, clean, the way it always answered — hips dropping, the held half-breath, the half-body-width of displacement his legs had no honest right to produce, and then the landing beat, locked, half a breath of pure committed stillness at the end that a real opponent would someday learn to wait for. Both bursts had bought exactly what they always bought. The hip had complained down its leading edge until evening, which was the standing rent. The Iron-adjacent surface read had been the one that suffered — it was the quietest of the five and the one that ran on the same fuel as the watching, and by the session's end it had gone soft on him, giving him pressure information a full beat late, which in a real bout would have been a beat too late to use.

*Cost of the survey: the surface read degrades when the attention budget is spent. Confirmed twice now. It's the same fuel. I have been paying for the counting out of the one fragment that can't be paid for out of anything else.*

*Gain: I know where the Compact's eyes are, to the stair-head, on a campus of four hundred people.*

*Worth it. But it isn't free, and I'm writing the price down now rather than after the session where it matters, because after is how people end up believing things were free.*

And under that, the entry itself:

*Shadow Path, Bronze, fifteen years' craft if he's a day. The Compact finally sent someone worth watching. So I'm watching.*

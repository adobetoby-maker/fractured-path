# Chapter 11 — Omission

The apparatus in hall three's north bay was called a resistance post, and Cael had priced it in his first fortnight as he priced everything, and had concluded that it was the best-designed object at Halcenvane and the one most likely to hurt somebody.

It was a padded column, chest-high, set into a floor plate, with a graduated core that could be wound to any of nine settings. A Force practitioner declared against it and the core took the delivered force and converted it into rotation against a calibrated drag, and a dial on the far side gave a number, and the number went in a ledger, and over a term you got a curve of a practitioner's delivery that no instructor's eye could have produced.

Its virtue was that it accepted force. That was also its whole risk, and the risk was printed on a brass plate bolted to the plinth in letters a quarter-inch high, which Cael had read on his first day and had thought about approximately once a week since:

*THE CORE MUST BE FREE BEFORE DECLARATION. CHECK THE PIN.*

Because a Force declaration is a container. That was the thing the eleven Paths had in common and the thing that the yard's crowds never quite grasped: a Path does not make force out of nothing. It gathers, assembles, and delivers, and the delivering is a controlled release of something that has already been built, and a builder who has finished building has a full container in his hands whether the world is ready to receive it or not. Wind released into air. Ember released into a channel. Force released into a target — and if there is no target, or if the target refuses, then the container is still full and the release is still happening and the force goes somewhere, and the somewhere is chosen by geometry rather than by anyone's intention.

Cael had written all of that down in his fourth week, on the Force Path pages, and had underlined the last clause.

---

It was a supervised session on a Third-day at the sixth bell, in the last week before the fifteenth ladder session, and there were nine people in hall three.

Cael had the north bay's floor allocation and was working through the fifth of six drills he had built for himself and had gotten the Ash Path instructor to countersign, because everything he did on a Halcenvane floor had to be countersigned by somebody, which was a provision requirement and which he had stopped resenting in about the third week when he had realized that a countersigned drill was also a documented drill and that documentation was, for him, structural armor.

The instructor — the one he had had in the standing eight for a fortnight in the autumn, a broad quiet man who taught Ash Path to first-years and supervised general floors because he liked the floors — was at the south end with two second-years and a rope frame.

And in the north bay, at the resistance post, was a Force Path third-year named Jask, working through his own term curve at the seventh setting.

Cael had a page on him. He had a page on everybody in the bracket and Jask was in the bracket, at Copper Rank 5, sixteenth in the standings, a wide-shouldered young man with an honest and rather slow delivery and a habit of over-committing in the third quarter of any session because he got competitive with his own dial.

*Over-commits when tired,* the page said. *Reads his own numbers between declarations, which is a mistake — a man watching his own dial is a man fighting the dial.*

It was, Cael would think later, an entirely accurate page. It had simply been written for the wrong purpose. He had written it to beat the man and it had, in the end, been the reason he was in the right place.

---

The pin failed at the fourth declaration of Jask's third set.

Cael did not see it fail. Nobody saw it fail; that was the finding in the safety citation eleven days later, and the citation named a fatigue fracture in a retaining pin that had been in service nine years and had been inspected in the autumn by a man who had done the inspection correctly. What Cael saw was the consequence, and he saw it about a third of a second before it happened, which was the entire margin the afternoon contained.

He saw it because of the sound.

The core's drag made a noise as it turned — a low even grind, four seconds long, that dropped in pitch as the rotation bled off. He had heard it perhaps two hundred times that term from twelve feet away while doing something else. On the fourth declaration of the third set the noise did not start.

The pin had sheared, and the sheared pin had jammed the core against its housing, and the core was not turning, and the container Jask had spent four seconds assembling was arriving into a column that had stopped being an apparatus and had become a wall.

Cael's Iron-adjacent surface read was the fragment that answered.

That was the thing he would write about first that night, because it was the item that had actually done the work and because it had done it in the one condition where it was best. The surface read gave him incoming pressure as *shape* — a density in the air ahead of a strike, half a second early, a bloom of gathering that arrived as information rather than as sight. It had been running slow for two months while the counting project ate his attention budget, and had come back to full inside four days of easing off, and he had eased off eleven days ago because Karis's finding had made him decide to be rested.

It came in on time. It came in a half-second early and it came in *wrong*, which was the useful part: the shape it handed him was not the shape a resistance post's absorbed delivery makes. It was the shape of a container with a closed exit, folding back along its own line, and the fold was coming out of the post's near face at about the height of a person's chest, into a bay where a third-year was standing eighteen inches from the plinth with his weight forward and his hands still committed.

*Not enough time to move him. Not enough time to shout.*

He had, at a generous estimate, a third of a second, and he spent it on two things.

The first was position, and the position was the part that would be talked about for a week by people who had not seen it and would be described in the incident report in eleven words.

He was six feet from Jask's left shoulder, standing at the bay's edge with a training staff in one hand, and he needed to be at Jask's left hip inside the space between one heartbeat and the same heartbeat, and the Wind-adjacent framework was the only tool on the continent that could do it.

It went the way it always went, in the hips: the drop and the loosening, the floor giving him the half-inch it kept in reserve, the breath stopping as though a door had shut on it. Hollow-boned. Weightless in the way that had frightened him for a year and now merely felt like a room he knew. A half-body-width of sideways travel his legs had no honest right to produce — except it was not sideways this time, it was forward and across, into rather than away from, which was a use he had made of it perhaps eleven times in three years and every one of those times had been a mistake.

He arrived at Jask's hip with his own left forearm coming up and his breath locked and the landing beat about to happen underneath him.

The second thing he spent the third of a second on was the Compression-adjacent, and he did not choose it. It arrived on contact, as it always arrived, which was the whole difference between it and the Pressure-adjacent and the reason he had reached for it without deciding to at least four hundred times.

The force came off the post's near face in a single flat sheet.

Cael took it on the left forearm at the point where the fold was thinnest and had the least distance to travel, which was not a thing he had time to work out and which he had, in fact, worked out weeks ago on a page about an apparatus he had priced as dangerous. The Compression-adjacent did what it did: the incoming stopped being one arriving thing and became a spreading one, out through the forearm, across the shoulder, down the flank, into the floor plate through both heels. His weight went into the boards. The boards, being sprung wood over stone, gave and gave the force back, which was the wrong direction, and about a third of what he had spread came back up into him.

And the rest of it went past.

That was the whole of the thing, and it was the only part that mattered: the sheet of force that had been travelling toward a third-year's sternum went instead into a forearm, across a torso, down into a floor plate, and what came off the far side of it went wide and low, into the bay's padded wall, at hip height, four feet to Jask's left.

Then the landing beat happened, and Cael, who had used the Wind-adjacent framework forward and across into a live release, spent a half-breath of locked commitment standing inside the geometry of the thing he had just redirected, unable to change direction if the building had come down.

Nothing else came. It had been one container and the container was empty.

---

Jask sat down on the floor plate. He did not fall; he sat, all at once, as a person does when their legs have received information their head has not yet had.

"What," he said.

Cael was on one knee with his left arm not doing anything he had asked of it. He got his breath back first, which took a moment, because the framework's half-breath and the Compression's half-breath had come out of the same set of lungs inside a third of a second and his chest had opinions about that.

He looked at the post. The dial read nothing. The core had not moved. There was a bright shear across the pin housing that had not been there four seconds ago.

"Pin's gone," he said. "Don't touch it."

The instructor arrived at a dead run from the south end, which took him about five seconds, and Cael would note afterward that the man had crossed a training hall in five seconds and had still arrived four and a half seconds after everything was over, and that this was not a criticism of the instructor but a fact about the speed of events.

The next twenty minutes were procedure, and Halcenvane's procedure was good.

The hall was cleared of the north bay. The instructor put a hand on Jask's shoulder and asked him four questions in order — his name, the setting, whether anything hurt, whether anything felt wrong that did not hurt — and Cael, watching, recognized a checklist and approved of it. Two second-years were sent, one for the floor warden and one for the assessment wing's duty clerk. Nobody touched the post. Somebody found the brass plate's instruction and read it aloud unnecessarily.

The floor warden came, looked at the housing for a long moment, and said a word Cael had not heard from a member of Halcenvane's staff before.

"Nine years in service," she said, to nobody. "Autumn inspection passed. That's a fatigue shear, that is, and it'll be in every post in this building."

She took the whole north bay out of use on the spot, which Cael learned three days later had cost the academy the use of four resistance posts across two halls for eleven days and had produced, at the end of it, a replacement schedule for pins in every apparatus on campus. Rooke had apparently argued for the schedule in faculty session and Bracken had found the money in a fortnight, and Cael, hearing about it, had written down that the safest thing that had happened all term had happened because a pin broke in front of nine people rather than in front of one.

---

Jask found him afterward at the hall's north door, having been checked over and released and told to sit down for an hour, and stood there working out what to say for long enough that Cael began building an exit.

"You were six feet away," Jask said.

"About that."

"How did you get there?"

There it was, in the ordinary form, from an ordinary person, forty minutes after an ordinary accident.

Cael had answered the question, in one shape or another, perhaps two hundred times in three years, and had a doctrine for it, and the doctrine was that the true answer stated flatly and incompletely beats any invented answer, every time, forever. Invented answers require memory. True incomplete ones require nothing.

"I heard the core not start," he said. "It makes a noise for four seconds and it didn't make it. So I had about a third of a second more than you did, and I spent it moving."

Jask thought about that.

"That's not how anybody moves," he said.

"No," Cael agreed. "I'm quick off a read. I've been working on reads since I was twelve because I've never had anything else, and I've got about two decent bursts in me a day and I spent one of them on you." He shifted his left arm, which had begun to do the thing his left arm did about a minute after a Compression deployment, and which was going to go on doing it a great deal longer than usual today. "Don't make it more than it is. You'd have been badly hurt and now you're not, and the reason is a broken pin nobody could have seen and a noise I happened to know the length of."

"Right," said Jask, who plainly did not believe the last third of that and had decided to accept it anyway, which was the entire purpose of a true incomplete answer.

He put out a hand. Cael shook it with his right.

"You're the assay one," Jask said.

"I'm the assay one."

"Huh," said Jask, and went to sit down for an hour.

---

The paperwork ran three days and Cael read all of it, because he read everything with his name on it.

The floor warden's incident report ran to four pages and named the pin, the housing, the setting, the nine-year service interval, and the autumn inspection, and it recommended the campus-wide replacement schedule in its final paragraph in language so dry that Cael copied two sentences of it into the observation notebook as a model.

His own part appeared once, in the second page's narrative sequence, and it read in full:

*Enrollee (assay provision) present at bay edge; moved to intervene; received the release on a guard arm; no injury reported. Third-year not struck.*

Eleven words for the intervention and five for the outcome. He read it three times and found nothing in it that was false and nothing in it that was anything.

The instructor's supplementary note said he had acted correctly and promptly. The assessment wing's duty clerk logged a floor incident with no evaluable content. Gault's office issued the safety citation on the eleventh day, unsigned by Gault because citations were the floor warden's instrument, and the citation attached a replacement schedule.

Nobody asked him a single question about it. That was the part he sat with longest.

Because there had been nine people in hall three. Two second-years at the rope frame with their backs to the north bay. The Ash Path instructor at forty feet, moving toward it. Jask, who had been looking at his own dial. Three other enrollees at the south bays. A floor porter in the doorway. And an assessment-office teaching assistant restacking session forms at the west wall, eleven meters away, with a clear line.

Of those nine, seven had seen a noise and a shout and a young man on the floor.

Jask had seen somebody arrive who had been six feet away, and had asked the ordinary question, and had accepted an ordinary answer, and would tell the story eleven times over a fortnight in a version that got better each time and less accurate.

And one person in that hall had watched a fifteen-year-old with a documented Copper-adjacent baseline cross six feet inside a third of a second and take a jammed Force release on a forearm and put it into a floor.

Cael wrote his log entry that night with his left hand doing its shake, and the shake made the writing untidy, and he did not care.

*Wind-adjacent, one deployment, forward-and-across, which is the use I've written down as a mistake eleven times. Price: the held half-breath, the landing beat, the hip. And the extra price of using it forward: the landing beat put me inside the release's geometry instead of outside it, which today did not matter and on any other day would have been how I died. Write that as the actual finding: the framework has no directional preference and I have been treating it as though it does, because every safe use of it in three years has been away from something.*

*Compression-adjacent, one deployment, unplanned, on contact. Price: half a breath, no telegraph, and a forearm that has gone past ache into a kind of deep humming wrongness in the bone that I have not had before and do not have a word for. It has been four hours. It is not improving on the schedule it usually improves on. Note that as data, not as complaint: the sheet was heavier than the drop-frame's heaviest weight by a margin I can't estimate, and the fragment held, and holding cost more than nine repetitions at the middle weight cost this morning.*

*Failure mode found: the floor gave. Sprung wood over stone returns about a third of what you put into it. I have been spreading force into Halcenvane floors all term and getting a third of it back and never once noticed, because at drop-frame weights a third of it is nothing. At today's weight a third of it was most of what I'm feeling now. If I ever do this on stone I'll be better off. If I ever do it on anything softer I'll be worse. Add it to the page.*

*Gain: Jask is walking around.*

*Usefulness: I would do it again and I would do it the same way and I want that written down before I write the rest.*

He stopped there for a while.

*The rest is this. There were nine people in that hall and eight of them do not have the vocabulary for what happened. The ninth was standing at the west wall with an armful of forms.*

*He had a clear line. He was at eleven meters, which is inside every range where his positional habit means he was looking at the whole bay because the whole bay was the interesting part of a room he was in. He has watched me train under supervision for eleven weeks and has a better baseline for what I do on a floor than Gault does, because Gault has six trials and he has eleven weeks.*

*So he knows. Not what I am — nobody gets that from one afternoon. But he knows that what the file says I can do and what I did in that bay are not the same size, and that is exactly and precisely the thing he was sent here to observe, and he observed it, and I watched him not look at it for the whole twenty minutes afterward.*

*It goes in a report. It has to go in a report. That's not a failure of his and it isn't a betrayal; it's the job, and I have known it was the job since the empty gallery, and I built the boring-truth doctrine for exactly this — so that the reports would be true and dull. This one will be true and it will not be dull.*

*So the question is what happens after it goes up. Escalation, probably. A second officer. An exception report — his brief will have a category for it, everybody's does. Rotations doubled at the bluff road. Possibly a letter to Withrow, which would be the worst of the available outcomes because it would put the provision in front of somebody's desk in the same paragraph as the word* undisclosed.

*I can't stop any of it. I can prepare for it, and I am going to, starting tomorrow. Karis gets the documentary side. Lira and Brom get told tonight.*

*And I am going to watch the rotations at the ferry landing and the bluff road's foot, every day, because that is the only instrument I have that reads the apparatus from outside. If nothing changes down there, nothing went up. If something changes, I'll know within four days.*

*That's the plan.*

He closed the log, and did not sleep well, and his forearm hummed in the bone until the second morning.

---

Seln drafted the exception report on the evening of the same day, at his own table, in his own room, in the fourth handwriting, with the window shut.

He had the incident's material in front of him in three forms, which was two more than most officers ever got. The floor warden's draft, because the assessment wing copied everything. The duty clerk's log. And his own observation, which was the only one of the three that had been made by somebody who understood what he was looking at.

He wrote the header. He wrote the date. He wrote the reference number of the assignment brief.

Then he sat with the pen not touching the paper for a while, which was not deliberation, because there was nothing to deliberate. He had known the content of this report at the moment the force went into the floor plate. He had stood at the west wall with a stack of session forms against his chest and had watched a boy with a documented Copper-adjacent baseline cross six feet of hall in less time than the release took to leave the post, put a jammed Force delivery from a seventh-setting resistance core onto a forearm, and put it into the ground, and had understood every part of it, because he had spent fifteen years being the person in the room who understood what he was looking at.

Two components at least. A displacement he could not account for by any Path he had charted, in a direction that made no sense for a body that size. And an absorption that a Bronze-tier Iron Skin practitioner would have been proud of and would have needed to see coming.

Reportable. Escalation-grade. Exception category two — *observed capability materially inconsistent with the subject's documented baseline* — which was the category the whole assignment had been constructed to fill, and which he had filled, in fifteen years, eleven times, and had been right on all eleven.

He wrote:

*Subject assisted in a training-floor equipment incident, hall three, north bay, sixth bell, Third-day. Retaining pin failure in a resistance post caused an obstructed release during a Force Path enrollee's fourth declaration of a third set at the seventh setting. Subject was present at the bay edge and intervened. The Force Path enrollee was not struck. No injuries. Academy floor warden has taken four posts out of service pending a campus-wide pin replacement schedule; documentation attached. Response consistent with documented baseline.*

He read it back.

It was, sentence by sentence, true. The subject had assisted. The pin had failed. Nobody had been struck. There had been no injuries. The floor warden had taken the posts out of service and the schedule was attached and would, in fact, be the part of the report that an analyst three grades up would find useful, because analysts liked institutional weather and there was no institutional weather like an academy discovering a fault in nine years of apparatus a fortnight before an inspection nobody had announced yet.

And the last sentence was the one that would have to survive audit, and he had built it the way you build a load-bearing wall.

*Response consistent with documented baseline.*

He turned it over the way he would have turned over another man's sentence if it had come across his desk in the years he had spent auditing product, which had been two of them, early, and had taught him more about the trade than the thirteen since.

An auditor reads for three things. The first is contradiction: does any sentence in the file argue with any other sentence in the file. This one did not. The subject's baseline document, filed by the academy's assessment office and copied into the monitoring product in the second week, recorded a Copper-adjacent practitioner with non-standard architecture, consistent execution, an honest four-repetition ceiling and a capability to receive force on a plate at a drop-frame's heaviest setting. A man reading that baseline and then reading *response consistent with documented baseline* against a floor incident in which nobody was hurt would find nothing to catch on. The baseline had force reception in it. The report said the response was consistent with the baseline. The two documents agreed.

The second thing an auditor reads for is *shape*: has the officer written more than the event requires, or less. Officers who omit tend to omit by writing short, because a frightened man's instinct is to give the machine as little as possible to hold, and short reports on interesting days are the single most legible tell in the trade. So he had written it long. Five sentences of apparatus, one of outcome, one of institutional consequence, and the attachment. It read as the product of a man with good access who had been in the room and had bothered.

The third thing an auditor reads for is what the officer *chose to find interesting*, because that is where a man's attention lives and a man's attention is the only part of him he cannot fully govern. So the report was interested in the pin. It was interested in the nine-year service interval and the passed autumn inspection and the four posts out of service and the replacement schedule and the money Bracken would have to find. It was interested, in short, in exactly the things a covered officer inside an academy's assessment wing had unique access to and would naturally be interested in, and it was interested in them at length, and any auditor at any grade would read the report and conclude that its author had been standing in a hall on a day something went wrong and had done the useful thing, which was to record the institution's response.

The interest was real. That was the part that made it work, and it was the part no untrained man ever understood about the craft: an omission survives audit because everything around it is genuine. He *was* interested in the pin. He had thought about the pin for an hour. A fatigue shear in a nine-year housing a fortnight before a charter inspection was the best piece of institutional weather he had recorded all term.

The capability data was not in the report.

He did not think about that in a sentence. He noticed the shape of the report without it — the way a man notices, in a room he has furnished himself, that a chair is not where it was — and he found the shape sound, and he sealed it.

Then he addressed the outer wrapper, and stood, and put his coat on, and walked it down to the Ostrand courier post himself, because a man who sends his post through an academy's own bag is a man whose post an academy can read. It was two and a half miles down the bluff road and back and it took him an hour and ten minutes, and it was raining for the last third of it, and he had made the same walk four times since the term opened and had never once minded it.

He came back up at the eleventh bell and let himself into the assessment wing with the duty key, because his working papers were there and he had a session ledger to total before the morning. He totalled it. He got the column right, which he did four times in five and was careful to do only four times in five.

Then he pulled open the drawer where the month's product carbons lived — the schedule extracts, the associates list, the copies of every sheet he had sent south since the first of the term — and looked at it.

The wing was entirely quiet. Somewhere below, the river was doing what the river did.

He filed the report. Then he sat for a while with the file drawer open, not filing anything else.

# Chapter 13 — The Operation

The fire-watch posted its closure list in the covered walk every morning at the first bell, on a quarter-sheet of the cheapest paper the academy bought, and as far as Cael could establish in four months of looking, nobody in the building read it but him.

It was not a document with any information in it. It was an accounting of lamp oil. Halcenvane burned oil in every hall it kept open past the last bell, and the fire-watch — two men and a boy, working a circuit of the bluff from the residence quadrangles out to the assessment wing and back — logged each building's closing hour so that the bursar could argue with the chandler about the invoice at the end of every month. Hall three, last bell. Lecture complex, last bell. Library, second hour, which was the librarians and always had been. Records hall, closed at last bell.

He read it every morning because Crown season had filled the bluff with people who did not live on it.

That was the season's texture and it had crept up on all of them. Guild factors up from Ostrand for the finals-track afternoons, with Withrow beside them and her hand on the machine. Two academies' worth of instructors down for the Iron bracket, on the polite fiction of a methods exchange and the actual business of watching Ephram. Endowment families, a river-trade house's whole junior office in matched coats, Ostrand's newssheets printing the standings on their third page for the first time in eleven years.

Four hundred people on the bluff had become five hundred and forty at the season's midpoint, and a hundred and forty of them slept somewhere else. A building's population was the denominator of everything else he measured, and a denominator that moved was a denominator worth watching.

So on a Third-day morning in the covered walk, with the season's crowd going past him toward the refectory, he read the fire-watch's quarter-sheet and found the records hall listed at the fourth hour.

He read it twice, which was his whole reaction. The fourth hour was five hours past the last bell, in a building that held no sessions, no lectures, and no reason on the calendar for anyone to be in it. The library's second-hour entry had a standing explanation. The records hall's fourth-hour entry had none, and it was there again the next morning — Fourth-day, fourth hour, in the fire-watch boy's laborious round hand.

Twice was a pattern. Once was a clerk falling asleep over a ledger.

He did not go to Bracken. He went to Karis, in the library, at the carrel she had colonized in her second week and had since filled with founding-era documentation in an arrangement that looked like chaos and was, he had learned not to say out loud, a filing system with about nine dimensions in it.

She read the two quarter-sheets — he had taken them off the board, which was theft of a document with no information in it, and he had done it anyway — set them side by side, and said nothing for a moment.

"You're a night behind," she said.

"Behind what?"

"Bracken flagged it yesterday morning." She pushed her chair back. "I was going to tell you at supper, and now I don't have to feel clever about it, so thank you for that. The hall's inner door has a lock that logs — it's a Compact-standard fitting, it's in every chartered records office on the continent, and it stamps a plate every time the bolt throws. Bracken reads the plate on Fifth-days because reading it more often than that would suggest he expected something. He read it early this week because the fire-watch list was odd." She glanced at him. "He reads the fire-watch list too."

"Good," said Cael, meaning it entirely. "What did the plate say?"

"Four throws across two nights. Two in, two out. Nothing forced, nothing broken, nothing missing." She said the last word with the precision she gave to words that were about to do work. "Nothing missing, Cael. Whoever it is didn't take anything. They opened the enrollment section, and they read, and they closed it, and they came back and did it again."

He sat down on the carrel's other stool without being asked.

"Which section of enrollment."

"The provision files." She had her hands flat on the table, and she kept them there, which he had come to recognize as Karis holding still on purpose. "Eleven of them, going back nineteen years. Nine are dead-file. The two live ones are a Silver-tier woman on a medical provision, and yours."

"And the order they were read in."

"Bracken can't get that off a lock plate."

"No," Cael agreed. "But he can get it off the drawers, because a drawer that's been opened sits proud of one that hasn't, and Bracken is exactly the sort of man to notice that and then not mention it because it sounds unserious."

Karis gave him the expression she kept for moments when he arrived somewhere ahead of the evidence and turned out to be right, which she had told him once she found genuinely annoying and had never once let stop her.

"He noticed it," she said. "The dead files sat flush. The medical provision sat flush. Yours was proud, both mornings."

Cael put the two quarter-sheets into his coat pocket and did the arithmetic he had been doing since the covered walk, which came out the same the third time as the first.

Somebody had come into a chartered records office twice, past a lock that stamped a plate, and had spent two nights reading a document with his name on it, and had taken nothing at all — because a document you copy is a document you have, and a document you steal is a document somebody knows you have.

"Who else has this?"

"Bracken filed it to the chancellor's office and copied the assessment wing, because half the provision paperwork lives on Gault's side of the building." Karis paused. "It went across a desk in the assessment office at about the ninth bell yesterday morning."

Neither of them said anything for a second.

"Right," said Cael.

---

He wrote the decision down before he made it, in the observation notebook rather than the log, because a thing you write becomes a thing you can be held to: *Going out. Purpose is observation only. I am not going to interfere with a records intrusion, and if I am seen by anyone official this costs me my enrollment, which is the only true thing anybody has ever written about me. Priced. Going anyway.*

The last four words sat on the page looking exactly as bad as they were, and he left them.

The records hall stood at the eastern end of the administrative range, a squat two-storey block with the enrollment section on the ground floor and the charter archive above it. Three ways in: the main door onto the courtyard, which the fire-watch passed four times a night; the covered walk's inner door, which was the one with the plate; and a service stair on the east face that ran from a yard door up to the archive and had, at its foot, a half-landing with a window and no lamp.

Cael took the covered walk's upper gallery, which overlooked the courtyard and the main door and was, at the third hour, entirely empty and entirely dark.

He was there for forty minutes before he understood that he was the second-best-positioned person on the bluff.

It was not a sighting. He never once saw the man that night. What he had instead was a subtraction — four months of an instrument that measured where attention went, working better in the dark than it had ever worked in daylight: the fire-watch's lantern coming along the range at the third hour, the boy's head turning at every doorway, the older man's not turning at all, because thirty years of the same circuit had taught him which doorways were worth a head-turn.

At the second doorway of the administrative range — the shallow one beside the records hall's main door, three feet deep, with a stone bench in it that nobody had sat on in Cael's four months — the boy's head turned, and the older man's head turned, and both of them looked at the bench.

And neither of them stopped.

Cael sat with that for the better part of an hour, then went back to the residence at the fifth hour by the long way round.

*Two men looked at an occupied doorway and walked on,* he wrote up in the morning. *That is not invisibility. That is the thing I measured in the covered walk in month two, running at a rate I have not seen it run at before: attention lands, finds nothing worth a second glance, and releases. He is not preventing the look. He is bankrupting it.*

*Second note, and this is the one I want on the page: he had the best position on that range and I had the second-best. He should have wanted the second-best too — it is where another man watches from, and another man inside his operation is a variable he cannot price. He had four hundred minutes to take it off me.*

*He left it for me. I don't know what that is yet. I know it isn't an accident, because nothing that man does at the third hour is an accident.*

---

The second night he changed the terms, and he did it with his feet, because there was nothing else available.

He had built the intruder's window that afternoon in the library, out of the same instrument and the same arithmetic he had used on the ladder's climb-rates, and had then checked it twice looking for the error and failed to find one. An intruder did not want an empty building. An empty building was a place where one set of footsteps was an event. What an intruder wanted was the thinnest survivable traffic — enough movement that a door opening was ordinary, few enough people that nobody was standing in the corridor when it did.

The administrative range had that window between the third and fourth hours, for a reason nobody had designed: the library's second-hour closing put four or five librarians and their late readers down the covered walk at about the third hour, in ones and twos, unpredictably, for perhaps fifty minutes. Then nothing until the kitchen's fifth-hour boys.

And the service stair on the east face was the only one of the three ways in that a person could use inside that window without crossing the covered walk itself.

So on the second night Cael did not take the gallery. He took the service stair's half-landing — the dark one, the one with the window and no lamp — at the second hour, and sat down on the fourth step with his back to the wall and waited, and did not look for the man at all.

He was making an argument, in the only language available: *I know when, and I know which door, and here is my evidence, which is that I am sitting on it.*

At some point past the third hour the argument was answered.

He did not hear the door below him. He did not hear anything. What happened was that the quality of the stairwell changed — the flat, unmistakable, entirely unprovable sense of a room becoming occupied — and Cael, who had spent four months writing that sensation up as an instrument and had never once been on the receiving end of it, felt every hair on his forearms stand up at once.

Nothing else happened for a full minute. Then, below him, in the dark, a man moved eight or nine feet across a stone floor and made a sound at the eighth foot — one small scuff, deliberate, the sound of somebody choosing to be a person in a room rather than a shape in one.

Cael did not move and did not speak.

The man went up past him, within four feet on the half-landing's outer edge, and Cael got him for a fraction of a second against the window's grey — a shoulder, a stack of something under one arm, nothing of a face — and then he was on the upper flight and the stairwell was ordinary again and Cael's heart was going like a bird in a box.

He wrote it up at the fifth hour, sitting on the floor of his room with his back against the bed frame because the chair felt like too much furniture.

*He took the upper flight. He left the lower door and the half-landing to me — again — and this time he did it after finding me sitting on them, which means it wasn't a gift the first time either. It's an allocation. He has a two-man problem and one of the men isn't his, and he has solved it by giving that man a sector.*

*He also scuffed his foot. There is no other person in this building he needed to tell he was there, and there is no version of that man who makes an involuntary noise on a stone floor. I have been sitting with what the scuff was for two hours and I have an answer I don't especially like: courtesy. He came within four feet of somebody sitting in the dark in a building where being found ends his enrollment, and he told him so, because the alternative was letting him find out by being touched.*

*Third note. Tonight I saw the second component, and I have been building the study wrong for four months.*

*Everything I have charted about this Path came from a man standing still. Standing still, it taxes the look. Moving, it does something else — a different thing, not a stronger version of the same thing. He crossed a lit corridor below me and I did not hear a footfall, and then he chose to make one. So it is not that he moves silently. He moves inside the building's own noise. The stair's fourth step complains under everybody; he did not use the fourth step. The library traffic was going past the courtyard end; he moved while it went past. Every increment of that man's movement is folded into a movement the building was already making.*

*Two components. One buys the eye. One buys the ear and the interval. They are not the same tool, and I would bet the pen Hesk hasn't sent me yet that they don't cost the same.*

---

The third night began ordinarily and stayed ordinary for two hours and then did not.

Cael was on the half-landing at the second hour with his back to the wall and his hands flat on his knees, which he had found the night before was the only posture that kept him from fidgeting. The season's last library readers went down the covered walk at the third hour in their ones and twos. The fire-watch came along the range and looked at the shallow doorway and walked on.

At perhaps a quarter past the third hour, the yard door at the bottom of the stair opened.

It opened well. That was Cael's first thought and it stayed his assessment through everything that followed: whoever came through it lifted the door on its hinges as it swung, which is a thing you only do if you have opened that class of door before and been unhappy with the noise. The man came in and stood for a count of twenty without moving, which was correct, and then went up the lower flight at the pace of somebody who had timed the flight in advance.

He passed within four feet of Cael and did not see him, because Cael was sitting in the one part of the half-landing the window's grey did not reach, and because he was not looking for a person on a stair. He was looking at the top of it, which is where a records office is.

Cael got a great deal in that four feet. Middle years. Not big. A satchel worn front-slung and strapped, which is how you wear a satchel you intend to run with. Gloves. A smell of lamp oil and something sharper, ink or spirit, which meant copying kit, which meant he had come to make a copy and had spent two nights deciding it was worth the risk of a third.

The man went up. Cael counted his own pulse and got to about four hundred.

Then the stairwell changed again.

It came from above this time, not below, which Cael had not expected and which cost him half a second of pure animal wrongness before he pinned it: the man had not come in from the yard. He had been in the building already, on the upper floor, in the dark, for some length of time that Cael had no way to measure and did not want to.

What happened next took eleven seconds and Cael saw perhaps four of them.

There was no sound of movement. There was a change in the light on the upper flight's wall — a lamp unshuttered, the intruder's own, because he had reached the door and needed to see the lock. In that light, at the top of the flight, in the doorway the intruder had come through and already passed, there was a man.

He had not been there. Cael had been looking at that doorway for two hours with an instrument he had built and tested and trusted, and there had been nothing in it, and now it had a man in it, and the man was entirely ordinary — no size, no bearing, an assessment-office coat, hands loose at his sides. Cael understood it in a rush that went down his spine like cold water: the art was not to arrive unseen. It was to have *already arrived*, so that the arrival was never an event anyone's eye had to process.

The intruder turned around.

He did it fast, and he got his hand into the satchel, and then he stopped, which surprised Cael more than anything else that night.

"That's a copying kit," the man in the doorway said. His voice was flat, mid-register, and pitched at exactly the volume of two people discussing a form. "It'll be in the incident record either way. If your hand comes out of that bag holding anything else, it stops being a records matter and starts being an assault on academy staff, and the difference for you is about nine years."

There was a pause of perhaps three seconds.

The intruder's hand came out of the bag empty.

"Sit down on the step," the man said. "Not that one. The one behind you." And then, in the identical register, as though it were the next line on the same form: "Bracken is in the courtyard with the chancellor's counsel and two of the fire-watch. They came up four minutes ago. You'd have heard them if you'd been listening to the building instead of the door."

Below, in the dark, on the fourth step of the lower flight, Cael reached.

---

He had written the terms out three weeks before, in the common room, with four people watching him do it, and had read them back so many times since that he did not need to remember them so much as fail to forget them.

He was not going to aim it at the man in cold blood. But the operation had been aimed at him — at his file, at his name, at the one sentence in the building that was true about him — for four months, by a professional, on instruction; there was a version of the line where that counted and a version where it did not, and he had chosen the version that counted and signed his own name under it in advance. *Write it down before, not after. That's the whole difference between me and the system.*

So he did not reach on instinct. He reached on the fourth step of a stone stair with his enrollment in the balance and a man he owed nine kinds of debt standing eleven feet above him, and he knew exactly what he was doing while he did it, and the knowing was the price and he paid it.

He had done the reach once before, at Greyvane, mid-exchange, with Karis's ignition point going up in front of him. It had felt then like turning a handle he had spent a year and a half not knowing was there. It felt now like turning the same handle in a much larger door.

And then the quiet came, the way it had come six times in his life, from the inside out — the sound of the stairwell going away, the light on the wall going flat, the whole eleven seconds above him subtracted at a stroke.

He lost the operation. Entirely. Three seconds, four, in which the most consequential thing he had ever been permitted to watch went on above him and he was not there for it, and he understood while it was happening that this was the bill and that he would be angry about it later.

Then the settling.

It did not go to his legs, where Wind-adjacent had coiled since a boarding house in Ardenmere. Not under the ribs where Pressure-adjacent sat its stone-weight, not across the skin where Iron-adjacent lay like a garment, not into the sternum where Karis's ignition point had sat banked and hot for eight months.

It went *outward*.

That was the only word he had for it, then or later. It settled into the half-metre of air around him — the space a person occupies and never thinks about, the boundary where a body stops and a room starts — and for one long moment that boundary had a setting on it, the way a lamp has a wick you can raise or lower. He could feel the whole stairwell as a field of attention with directions in it: the intruder's, pinned to the doorway; the other man's, spread flat across the room in a way he had no vocabulary for at all; and the thin scatter of the four people in the courtyard below, none of it pointed anywhere near him.

Then it collapsed, and he was a fifteen-year-old on a cold step in the dark with his heart hammering and his hands shaking on his knees, and the notice arrived.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Shadow-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: Bronze.
Note: presence-suppression component; movement-masking component.
Contact-to-short range.
Acquisition: directed. Engagement: adversarial, non-combat.
```

He read it, and then he read it again, and then he stopped being able to do anything else for a while.

*Tier equivalent: Bronze.*

Five notices in his life had carried that line, and five notices had said *unknown*. He had built a whole private theory on the word — that the mechanism could name a type and could not rate one, that rating belonged to somebody else's instrument and not to this one — and the theory had just been deleted by a single word in a flat administrative hand. It could rate. It had been able to rate the entire time. It had looked at Lira's Wind Path and Feryn's Pressure and Brom's Iron Skin and Reydan's compression and Karis's ignition and had declined, five times, to say a number, and tonight it had said one.

*Note: presence-suppression component; movement-masking component.*

Two. Every note line he had ever received had named one thing. The Iron-adjacent had said *surface-awareness component* and left him a year of work to find out what that meant. This one named two components, separately, with a semicolon in it, in the order he had charted them in his own notebook on two consecutive nights — which was either the least important thing about the notice or something he would be thinking about for a very long time.

*Acquisition: directed. Engagement: adversarial, non-combat.*

He looked at that line longest.

He had made an argument in a common room three weeks ago, out loud, to three people with every right to talk him out of it: that the mechanism's condition had never actually been violence; that in a year and a half of evidence what it required was *earnestness* — a person genuinely engaged against him, spending something real, meaning it — and that combat had only ever been the commonest way for a person to be that. Karis had said the conditions were met. Lira had said she distrusted how comfortable the sentence felt. Brom had said the thing that settled it. And Cael had written the line and had privately, in the part of himself he did not put on paper, expected to be wrong.

The architecture had just agreed with him. In writing. In a field it had never used before.

He sat on the fourth step with his hands shaking and thought, very clearly: *every other one of these arrived while somebody was hitting me.*

Lira had been hitting him. Feryn had been hitting him hard enough that it took a week to breathe properly. Brom had spent three mornings hitting him for science. Reydan had put him on a floor in front of six hundred people. Karis had thrown an ignition point at him under panel supervision with a bout's stakes on it.

Tonight he had sat still in the dark and taken a piece of a man out of the air at eleven feet, and the ledger had told him more than it had ever told him for any of the ones that cost him blood.

He had nowhere to put that, on a stone step, at the third hour, with his enrollment eleven feet from being over. So he did the only honest thing available, which was to notice that he did not like it, and to refuse to decide yet whether the not-liking was a finding or a flinch.

Above him, the man in the doorway said something short about the satchel and the intruder answered, and the stairwell filled with lamplight as somebody came up from the courtyard with a proper lantern, and Cael heard Bracken's voice — precise, entirely awake at the third hour — say, "Don't touch the kit. Counsel wants it as it lies."

Nobody came down the stairs.

That was the part he thought about afterward, more than the notice. The man had stood in that doorway for the whole eleven seconds and for however many minutes before it; the doorway looked down the flight; the flight had a half-landing on it that a professional had allocated to a second man on two consecutive nights. He knew exactly where Cael was. He had known for three nights.

He did not once look down the stairs.

---

Cael tried it in the stairwell, four minutes later, because he was fifteen and because the alternative was sitting still with what had just happened.

The corridor above had gone busy — counsel, Bracken, the fire-watch, a great deal of low talk about who would write what — and the lantern light was coming down the flight in a long wedge that ended two steps below him. He was going to have to leave, and leaving meant crossing the yard door's mouth while four people stood in the courtyard.

So he reached for the thing he had owned for four minutes and told it to make him not worth looking at.

Nothing happened. Not a partial thing, not a flicker: nothing, in the flat and total way a rope goes slack when the far end isn't tied to anything. He tried again, harder, with the whole trained apparatus of aiming he brought to the Wind-adjacent burst — the organized demand, the sharp timing — and got a spike of pressure behind both eyes that he recognized instantly and with real dismay, because it was the currency the surface read spent and the currency the compound gaze spent, and he had been running all three for three nights on about nine hours of sleep.

He got up, holding the wall, went down the stair, and made himself walk out of the yard door at an ordinary pace with his hands out of his pockets.

The younger of the two fire-watch men, standing at the courtyard's corner with the lantern, looked directly at him.

"You all right, lad?"

"Couldn't sleep. Heard the noise."

"Go on, then. It's nothing." The man's attention was already returning to the door, where counsel had appeared. "Records business."

Cael went on.

He wrote the failure into the observation notebook standing up in the covered walk, because it was going into the ledger tonight and not in the morning, when he might have been kinder about it.

*First attempt: total. Not a weak deployment — nothing at all, twice, followed by the attention-headache at a rate the Iron-adjacent charges for six breaths of reading. Then the one man in the courtyard whose job is looking at doorways looked straight at me and spoke to me, which in four months on this bluff has happened exactly never.*

*I aimed it. I felt myself do the aiming. Brom told me two years ago that the surface read would never once be aimed, and it took me four months to hear him. I have just spent four minutes proving I never learned the general case.*

*Working hypothesis, hour one, to be tested and probably discarded: the whole thing runs on not being the person who is watching. Everything else I own runs on attention pointed outward like a lamp. This one may require me to put the lamp down — and if that's right, the two components fight each other in my hands, because folding into a building's movement means reading the building's movement, and reading is the lamp, and the lamp is the thing this Path exists to switch off.*

*If that's right it is going to take a year.*

---

Karis found him in the covered walk before he reached the residence, coming the other way with her coat over her nightclothes and her hair in a state he had never seen it in, which told him more about the hour than any bell had.

"Bracken sent for me," she said. "The intruded file is mine as much as anybody's — I built the documentation, I get to see what somebody wanted with it." She fell into step, and did not ask what he was doing in the administrative range at the third hour, then or ever, which he noticed and put away to be grateful for later. "His name's Jessup. Records-broker out of the lower city — it's an actual trade, apparently, six of them in Ostrand, and they don't steal documents. They read them and sell what's in them. Bracken says the profession is older than the academies and half of it is legal."

"What did he want?"

"The provision text. Not your assessment record, not Gault's baseline — the *enrollment basis*. The clause, the citations, Bracken's verification chain." She let out a breath. "He was copying the argument, Cael. Somebody paid a man to find out exactly how the assay provision was built."

Cael walked about ten paces with that.

"The tasking?"

"Anonymous. Written, paid in advance, delivered through a factor's post box that closed the week it was used. No client name in the bag, and Jessup says — for whatever a records-broker's word is worth at the fourth hour — that he never had one and never asks." She hesitated, and then said the rest, because she always said the rest. "The assessment office filed a note into the incident record. I read it over Bracken's shoulder before counsel took the folder, and I'd rather you had it from me than worked it out. *Pattern is consistent with private interest. Not Compact pattern.* And under that: *Client unidentified; no further inference available on present material.*"

Cael stopped walking.

"That's a good note."

"It's a very good note," Karis said. "Written by somebody who could have given them four paragraphs of theory and gave them what he could prove instead. I have read a great many bad notes this year."

They went the rest of the way without talking. At the residence stair she stopped with her hand on the rail and looked at him properly for the first time, and whatever she found in his face made her mouth go thin.

"It happened," she said.

"Yes."

"Tonight?"

"About twenty minutes ago."

Karis closed her eyes briefly. "Right. Then tomorrow you're not training, and the day after that you're not training, and on the third day I'm going to write down every number I can get off you, and you're going to answer honestly even when the honest answer is embarrassing." She opened her eyes. "That's the covenant. That's all of it, actually — that's the entire covenant in three sentences and I'm astonished it took us a year to get it that short."

"Karis."

"Go to bed."

---

He wrote the entry before dawn, sitting at the window with the shutter half-open and the river doing its business below the bluff, in handwriting that was worse than usual and that he decided not to be embarrassed about, because a hand that shakes is data too.

*Sixth fragment. Bronze — the notice finally said a tier out loud, like the architecture decided I'd earned a number. Acquired from a man who doesn't know, in defense of a file with my name on it, during an operation he let me watch. I drew the line in advance and I stayed on my side of it. It cost anyway. Write that down too.*

He read it back, and then added the rest, because the rest was the part he would want in a year.

*The cost, itemized, so I can't round it down later: three seconds of the operation, gone — I lost the thing I was there to see at the exact moment it happened, which is the worst currency I have and I spent it without being asked. The attention-headache, immediate and large. Two total failures inside four minutes. And a debt I have no mechanism for paying, to the only person in this building who has never once made me pay for anything.*

*Five times before, somebody was hitting me. Tonight nobody touched me and the ledger told me more than it has ever told me. I made an argument three weeks ago about what this thing actually requires, and tonight the architecture agreed with the argument in a field it has never used before, and I am going to be honest on this page and say I do not like being agreed with by something I cannot ask a question.*

*A system that confirms you is a system that was listening.*

*I don't know what that means. Filed.*

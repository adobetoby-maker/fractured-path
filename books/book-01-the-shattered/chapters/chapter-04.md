# Chapter 4 — Exit

He woke before the alarm he hadn't set, in the grey light that comes before a city fully commits to morning, and lay still for a moment doing the thing he always did on important days: cataloguing.

Ceiling. Water stain, northeast corner. The bag by the door, packed the night before, sitting there with the patience of an object waiting to be used for its purpose. The sound of Hesk already awake in the kitchen — the workshop-quiet clink of a kettle being filled, not banged, the sound of a man being deliberately unhurried about something he didn't want to rush.

He lay there a moment longer than the cataloguing required, listening to the small domestic noises of a house he wasn't going to hear again for a long time, maybe ever quite this way — the creak of the second floorboard from the kitchen door, the tap running and stopping, a cabinet closing with the soft, careful click Hesk used for that cabinet specifically because the latch had gone slightly loose two winters ago and a firm close made it rattle. He knew this house's sounds the way he knew the streets outside it: completely, invisibly, the kind of knowledge you only notice you have once you're about to lose the chance to use it.

Cael was fourteen. He had been fourteen for a matter of days, technically — Kindling had a way of making a birthday feel like it had happened twice, once biologically and once administratively, and only one of the two had come with paperwork.

He got up. Dressing took longer than it should have — not because there was much to decide, he owned exactly enough clothes to fit in one bag and had already packed all but what he was putting on, but because his hands were doing the small, deliberate work of a person trying to make an ordinary task last slightly longer than it needed to.

He went to the kitchen, where Hesk had made the kind of breakfast that wasn't really about the food — bread from the corner bakery, the good preserves that usually got saved for occasions, tea made carefully instead of efficiently, poured and left to steep the full recommended time rather than the abbreviated version they usually settled for on ordinary mornings. Neither of them said anything about what the breakfast meant. They didn't need to. It sat between them on the table the same way the money pouch and the leather notebook would sit between them later, at the depot — a fact expressed through an object instead of a sentence, because some facts went down easier that way.

"Merchant road runs east from the transit depot," Hesk said, over tea, in the tone of someone delivering practical information because practical information was easier to deliver than the other kind. "Two days on foot if you keep a steady pace, less if you catch a cart willing to take a fare partway. Ardenmere's gate opens at dawn and closes at full dark. Don't arrive after dark on your first night — find a waystation instead."

"I read the road notes at the record office."

"I know you did. I'm telling you anyway." A small, dry almost-smile. "Some things are better heard than read."

"Is that going in the notebook? Under things you tell me that you know I already know?"

"It might be." Hesk didn't quite smile, but something in his face loosened, briefly, the way it did on the rare mornings when the workshop was quiet and he had nothing urgent to fix. "There's a version of this conversation where I say all the practical things and none of the other things, because the practical things are easier and I've had more practice with them. I'm aware that's what I'm doing. I'm doing it anyway, because the practical things are also true and you'll need them on the road whether or not I manage the rest."

"You don't have to manage the rest."

"I know." He looked down at his tea. "I'm going to try anyway."

Cael ate the bread. It tasted, he thought, exactly the way it always tasted, which felt like it should have been wrong somehow — that the bread hadn't changed even though everything around it had. He found himself chewing more slowly than usual, the way you slow down toward the end of something you've decided, without quite admitting it to yourself, that you want to last.

---

They walked to the transit depot together, which was not strictly necessary — Cael knew the way, had known it since before he could remember not knowing it — but which neither of them suggested skipping.

The Outer District in the early morning had its own texture, one Cael had absorbed so completely over fourteen years that he'd never really looked at it directly until now, when he was about to stop being able to take it for granted.

The bakery on the corner had its side door open, ovens going, the smell of the morning's first bread carrying half a block the way it always did. The baker — a broad, quiet woman named Ressa who had sold Hesk bread for as long as Cael had been alive — was out front sweeping the step. She looked up as they passed. Her eyes went to the bag on Cael's shoulder, then to his face, and something in her expression softened into a kind of understanding that didn't need explaining.

"Safe travels," she said. Just that. Not a question about where, not a comment about why. She had clearly heard something — everyone in a four-block radius had probably heard something, information moved through the Outer District the way water moved through a canal system, finding every low point — and she had decided, on her own, that the correct response was two words and nothing more.

"Thank you," Cael said.

She nodded and went back to sweeping.

Three doors down, a man Cael had known since he was eight — Garrik, a retired Copper-tier who fixed carts for a living and had once spent an entire afternoon teaching Cael how a wheel bearing worked because Cael had asked and Garrik had liked being asked — was out front of his shop, and did not look up as they passed. Did not, in the deliberate way of someone choosing not to.

Cael noted this without saying anything. He filed it in the growing category of things he was learning to expect: some people met a hard thing directly, and some people found it easier to look somewhere else, and neither reaction was really about him. It cost something to understand that so young. He understood it anyway.

They passed the community hall, where the reference room he'd spent so many afternoons in sat dark behind its shuttered windows, not open yet at this hour. He thought, briefly, of the historical classification summary on its shelf, the four-line entry that had started all of this weeks before it had a right to start, and felt a strange gratitude toward the room itself — toward the fact that the information had been findable at all, that Denvash, for all it was taking from him this morning, had at least never actively hidden the truth from a boy determined enough to go looking for it.

Two streets on, a woman he half-recognized — someone's aunt, a face from the market rather than a name he'd ever learned — nodded at Hesk the way neighbors nodded at each other, then caught sight of Cael's bag and the grimness Hesk was carrying beneath his composure, and her nod slowed, softened, turned into something closer to a held breath. She didn't say anything either. Nobody, it seemed, had the correct words for this, and Cael was beginning to understand that this wasn't a failure of the neighborhood so much as an honest admission that no correct words existed.

The light at six in the morning, on this street, came in low and gold and caught the dust the bakery's ovens threw into the air, and Cael thought, with the clarity of someone cataloguing a thing they knew they wouldn't see again for a long time, *I am going to remember exactly this. This exact color of light.*

He was right. He would. He would think of it three years later, in a city he couldn't yet imagine, and the memory would arrive with the same low gold light and the same bread-smell and the same ache, undiminished by distance, that this morning had never really belonged to anyone but him.

---

The transit depot was busier than the residential streets — merchants loading carts, a family with too much luggage arguing quietly about whether they'd brought too much luggage, two Bronze-tier practitioners in guild colors moving through the crowd with the unhurried confidence of people who never had to wait for anything.

Hesk walked him to the edge of the depot, where the merchant road picked up and the city's maintained cobblestone gave way to packed dirt.

They stood there for a moment. Cael had imagined this moment several different ways over the past two days, and none of the versions he'd imagined had prepared him for how ordinary it actually felt — not smaller than expected, exactly, but quieter. Less like a scene and more like just another moment, the kind that turns out to matter more in memory than it did while it was happening.

Around them, the depot kept moving — a cart driver arguing amiably with a toll-taker over a discrepancy that would probably resolve itself the same way it resolved itself every week, the two Bronze-tier practitioners disappearing into the crowd toward whatever unhurried business had brought them here, a child chasing a loose chicken with the total focus only a child could bring to a chicken. Cael found the ordinariness of it almost offensive — that the depot didn't know, that the whole city didn't know, that this morning was different from every other morning that had ever happened here. Then, a beat later, he found it steadying instead, the same way the ordinary street sounds outside the kitchen window had steadied him three days before. The world was going to keep doing what it did regardless of what he was about to do. There was something almost generous in that.

"You have the money," Hesk said. Not a question. A checklist, spoken aloud, the way he checked structural calculations twice even when he was certain of them.

"Yes."

"The notebook, unopened until the road."

"Yes."

"Ardenmere. The Unranked District. Find your feet before you find anything else."

"I remember."

"Don't trust the first person who's kind to you completely. Don't distrust them completely either. Watch, the way you already watch everything, and let the watching tell you which way to lean." Hesk paused, and something in his shoulders shifted, the way they shifted in the workshop when a repair was more delicate than he'd first assessed. "I know I'm repeating myself. I've had two days to say everything I wanted to say and I've discovered there isn't a version of two days that's enough."

"You could have another two."

"I could. And then another two after that, and I'd still be standing here finding new things I hadn't said, because that's not actually a problem two more days solves." He almost smiled — the same small, dry almost-smile from breakfast, worn thinner now by the effort of holding it in place. "At some point I have to trust that what you're carrying is enough. Not because it's complete. Because it's what there is, and you're capable of building on incomplete things. You've been doing it your whole life."

Cael didn't have an answer for that, so he didn't try to find one.

Hesk nodded, once, as if Cael's silence had answered him anyway. He looked, for a moment, like he was going to say something else — something bigger, something that matched the size of what was actually happening — and then, visibly, decided against it, in favor of something smaller and truer.

"Write to me," he said. "Even if there's nothing to say. Especially then."

"I will."

Hesk reached out and put a hand on Cael's shoulder — not a hug, not quite, but close enough to one that the distinction didn't matter much. His grip was firmer than Cael expected, the grip of a man who worked with his hands and had never learned to make that strength gentler than it needed to be, and Cael understood, in that grip, something he wouldn't have believed if Hesk had simply said it out loud: that his grandfather was, underneath the careful composure, holding on exactly as hard as Cael was.

"Whatever you find out there," Hesk said. "Whatever you turn out to be able to do. You were worth raising before any of it, and you'll still be worth it no matter what it turns out to be. I need you to actually believe that, not just hear it."

"I believe you believe it."

"That's not quite the same thing."

"It's what I've got this morning." Cael's voice caught, briefly, on the last word, and he let it, because pretending otherwise in front of the one person who'd never once asked him to perform anything felt, suddenly, like a waste of the little time left to spend honestly.

Something in Hesk's face broke open, just slightly, just for a second — not tears, Hesk didn't do that, not where Cael had ever seen — but a kind of visible cost, the expression of a man doing something that was costing him more than his composure was designed to show. "It's enough," he said, quietly. "It's more than enough. Go on, then, before I decide it isn't."

Cael did not trust his voice enough to answer that directly. "I'll write," he said again, which wasn't the same thing, but which Hesk seemed to understand as close enough.

Hesk let go. Stepped back. Cael picked up his bag, settled the strap across his shoulder — the same shoulder Hesk had just held — and made himself turn toward the road before he lost the nerve the turning required.

Cael went.

---

He looked back once, from far enough down the road that Hesk was a shape rather than a person — a shape standing at the edge of the depot, not moving, watching. Cael raised a hand. The shape raised one back.

Then he turned east and didn't look back again, because looking back a second time would have made it harder, and he needed it to stay possible.

---

At the district gate — the actual boundary marker between Denvash's Outer District and the open road beyond it, a low stone arch with a guard post beside it that mostly existed to check merchant cargo manifests — a gate-guard flagged him down out of habit rather than suspicion.

"Registration," the guard said, bored, already reaching for the next cart's paperwork in his mind.

Cael handed over the document. The guard glanced at it the way he'd glanced at a hundred documents that morning — and then didn't.

His eyes stopped on the classification line. Cael watched it happen: the half-second where a person's face goes from processing routine information to processing something it wasn't expecting, and has to decide, in that half-second, what to do about it.

The guard's face didn't do anything dramatic. It did something smaller and, Cael thought, more honest — a kind of quiet withdrawal, the posture of someone deciding that the fastest way through an uncomfortable situation was to make it as short as possible.

"Go on," the guard said, and handed the document back without meeting Cael's eyes, and waved him through the gate faster than he'd waved through the cart in front of him.

Cael walked through. He didn't say anything. He didn't look back at the guard either, though he wanted to — wanted to see whether the man's face had already resumed its ordinary boredom, whether the whole exchange had already stopped mattering to anyone but Cael the moment his back was turned. He suspected it had. That was, in its own way, the most useful part of the whole encounter: he was going to matter enormously to himself for a long time yet, and to almost everyone else, for exactly as long as it took them to finish the sentence.

Twenty paces past the gate, on the open road, with the city wall receding behind him, he took out his notebook and wrote it down, because it was information and information went in the notebook regardless of how it made him feel.

*Gate guard, Denvash Outer gate. Saw [SHATTERED]. Didn't ask questions. Waved me through faster than routine.*

*People don't engage with what they're afraid of. They get you through the interaction as quickly as possible so they don't have to keep looking at it.*

*This is useful. Fear that wants to look away is fear that isn't paying close attention. Remember this.*

*Corollary, added a moment later, because it felt important enough to earn its own line: this will not always be true. Somewhere, eventually, I am going to meet the kind of fear that does the opposite — that looks harder, not away. I don't know yet what that looks like. I should.*

He closed the notebook and kept walking, the city wall shrinking behind him until a bend in the road took it out of sight entirely, and Denvash, for the first time in his fourteen years, became a place he had left rather than a place he was standing in.

---

The merchant road east ran through farmland for the first several hours — fields, low stone walls, the occasional farmhouse with smoke already going from the morning fire. Traffic was light: a few carts heading the opposite direction into Denvash, a family walking with a hand cart, a pair of merchants on horseback who passed him without a glance.

Walking alone, he discovered quickly, was a different kind of activity than walking with a destination inside a city he knew. In Denvash, walking had always been a way of moving through something already understood — streets with names, distances with known ends. Out here, the road simply continued, indifferent, and his own footsteps became the only reliable rhythm in a landscape that otherwise offered nothing but the occasional bird call and the distant, patient work of someone tending a field he'd never see up close.

He noticed things anyway, because noticing things was the one skill he trusted himself to have regardless of circumstance. The way morning mist sat in the low points between fields, burning off unevenly as the sun climbed, leaving some hollows still ghosted with white long after the higher ground had cleared. A farmer, two fields over, who raised a hand in greeting without breaking the rhythm of whatever work he was doing — a gesture Cael returned, feeling, for the length of the exchange, unexpectedly less alone than the empty road had been making him feel. A stray dog that fell into step beside him for the better part of an hour, matching his pace with the loose-limbed indifference of an animal that had adopted the road itself rather than any one traveler on it, and then peeled off toward a farmhouse without ceremony, the way it had arrived.

By midmorning his legs had begun the dull complaint of muscles doing more sustained work than they were used to — not pain exactly, just an insistent awareness of themselves that he hadn't had to think about in the city, where distances were shorter and broken up by things to look at. He adjusted his pace, the way Hesk's road notes had suggested, into something sustainable rather than urgent. There was no reason to hurry. Ardenmere would still be there in two days whether he arrived exhausted or merely tired.

He walked for most of the morning before he stopped, at a low wall beside the road, to eat some of the bread Hesk had packed and to do the thing he'd been telling himself he'd wait for the road to do properly.

He took out the leather-bound book.

It was smaller than he'd expected — pocket-sized, the leather soft with handling, the cord tie worn from being opened and closed many times over what had clearly been years. He untied it and opened to the first page.

Hesk's handwriting. Not the careful, deliberate hand he used for structural calculations in the workshop — a rougher hand, written in different inks that suggested different sessions across a long stretch of time, some entries clearly older than others.

*If you're reading this, it happened. I'm sorry I couldn't make it not happen. I can't undo the classification. What I can do is give you what I've been able to learn — from watching, mostly, because there was no one to ask directly, and asking directly tends to draw attention you don't want yet.*

*First: I don't know what [SHATTERED] actually means, and I want to be honest with you about that before I tell you anything else, because everything after this sentence is a guess dressed up as advice. What I believe, for whatever a belief is worth against a word like that: the name was chosen by people who were frightened, and fear naming a thing doesn't make the fear correct. It might mean broken. It might mean something outside the map entirely — not off it, just not yet drawn. I don't know which. I've had years to sit with not knowing, and I still don't have a better answer than that. Anyone who tells you they're certain is telling you more than they actually know.*

*Second: your body is going to learn before your mind does. This isn't poetry, it's the most practical thing I can tell you. Whatever you can do, it won't arrive as a declaration in a formatted box the way it does for standard Paths. It will arrive as instinct — a moment where you do something before you've decided to do it, and only afterward do you understand what just happened. When that happens, don't perform it for an audience. Watch it. Learn what your body already knows before you try to explain it to anyone else, including yourself.*

*Third: don't perform weakness, and don't perform strength. Perform competence. People read confidence as a threat and helplessness as an opportunity. Competence, quiet and undramatic, is the only posture that buys you room to actually figure out what you are before someone else decides for you.*

Cael read the first entry twice, then a third time, sitting very still on the low wall with the fields going gold-green around him. He had expected, somewhere underneath the parts of himself that hadn't examined the expectation closely, that Hesk's notebook would tell him something — that a man who'd spent years preparing for this would have arrived, eventually, at an answer. Instead he'd been handed a more honest thing than an answer: a grown man's admission that he didn't have one, offered without apology for the not-knowing, only for the years of silence that had preceded it. It was, Cael thought, somehow more useful than certainty would have been. Certainty could be wrong. This, at least, was true.

He turned the page. There was more — years of it, entries dated across what must have been more than a decade, ink shifting from a younger, looser hand to something steadier, the handwriting itself a kind of record of the years the words had been collected across.

*Fourth,* read an entry dated, by the ink's fading, considerably later than the first three: *if the abilities do come, they will not come labeled. You will not be told what you have or where it came from. This is the part I have the least practical advice for, because I have never watched it happen to someone and I am working from theory rather than observation. My best guess: pay attention to what you find yourself able to do only under real pressure, not in practice. Whatever this is, I suspect it does not show itself for an audience of one, calm and unthreatened. It shows itself when something forces it to.*

*Fifth, and this one matters more than the others, so read it twice: you are allowed to be afraid and competent at the same time. I have watched people convince themselves those two things are opposites, that fear is proof you're not ready. It isn't. Fear that keeps you paying attention is a tool, not a flaw. The only fear worth worrying about is the kind that makes you stop watching. Yours won't. I've known you fourteen years. You don't know how to stop watching. It's the thing about you I'm least worried will fail you.*

Cael sat with that last entry longer than any of the others. He thought about the eleven seconds in the certification office, the clinical way he'd catalogued his own unsteady hands the morning the expulsion notice arrived, the archive entry he'd copied word for word rather than letting himself simply feel it. Hesk was right, and being right about it didn't make it feel less strange to have named — that the watching, the noting, the constant careful cataloguing of everything including his own reactions to things, wasn't a symptom of something wrong with him. It was, according to the one person whose judgment he trusted completely, the exact tool he was going to need.

He thought about the archive entry — three dead within weeks, one unaccounted for — and about this notebook, built quietly over years by a man who had seen enough of the pattern to prepare for it without ever being sure he'd need to.

He thought: *I am not starting from nothing. I'm starting from this.*

It helped. Not completely. But it helped, the way that true things helped more than comforting ones, even when the true thing was *I don't know.*

He tied the notebook closed, put it back in the bag, and got up, because Ardenmere was still a day and a half east and the afternoon wasn't going to last forever, and there was, he was beginning to understand, a quiet kind of comfort in simply continuing to walk.

# Chapter 14 — The Summons

He read Hesk's notebook first — not for new information, since he'd read it enough times by now to recite most of it, but because rereading it in moments like this had become a kind of grounding ritual, a way of hearing his grandfather's voice when the actual man was two days' road away and unreachable in the time he had.

*Don't perform weakness. Don't perform strength. Perform competence.*

He read it standing, then sitting, then lying on his cot with the lamp turned low enough to save the oil, and by the third pass he wasn't really reading anymore so much as listening — the way you listen to a piece of music you know well enough that your attention drifts from the notes themselves to the shape they make together. Hesk's handwriting had a particular economy to it, sentences trimmed of anything that wasn't load-bearing, and Cael had come to understand, these past weeks, that the economy wasn't a stylistic accident. It was the same instinct that made Hesk good at structural calculations: say the true thing in as few words as it actually required, and trust the reader to supply the weight themselves.

He read his own log next, front to back, the tactical entries and the private ones both, looking for anything he'd already learned that might be useful now. Renn's telegraphed strikes. Dessa's transition windows. The cumulative shape of five weeks spent turning strangers into information before they ever became opponents. He found, mostly, confirmation of something he already suspected: that his best tool, in almost every situation he'd faced since Weaver's Row, was patience applied to information other people hadn't bothered to gather. Nobody outworked him at watching. He didn't know yet whether that would matter against something as large and slow-moving as the Compact — but it was the only tool in the bag, and a fight, he'd learned in the circle enough times now, was rarely won by wishing for a different tool than the one you'd brought.

The other cots in the room held their usual sleepers, breathing in the unhurried rhythm of people who didn't have forty-eight hours ticking down somewhere behind their eyes. He envied them, briefly, in the abstract way you envy anyone whose problems aren't currently trying to have you physically retrieved by an organization with the resources to make that happen. Then he set the envy aside, because envy wasn't information, and information was the only currency he had.

He decided, sitting with both notebooks open on his cot in the lamplight, that the summons deserved the same treatment as Dessa's guard — not confronted head-on, where his disadvantage was total, but studied for the gap nobody had thought to close. Coss had a procedure. Procedures had edges. He had spent five weeks learning that almost everything with edges had a seam somewhere, if you were patient enough and bored enough and, frankly, scared enough to go looking properly.

---

He spent the first several hours of his forty-eight at the Ardenmere public registry records — a reference archive he hadn't had reason to use before now, working through a section entirely new to him: compliance procedure, non-standard classifications, the exact regulatory language governing what the Compact could and couldn't require of someone in his position.

The archivist on duty — a thin, unbothered man named Halden who took one look at the urgency in a fourteen-year-old showing up alone at opening hour and asked no questions about it — pointed him toward the correct shelving section without being asked twice, which Cael appreciated more than the man probably knew. He pulled down three separate volumes: the district's general compliance handbook, a supplementary volume on non-standard classifications that hadn't been re-copied in at least a decade judging by the brittleness of its binding, and a cross-reference index that existed, as far as Cael could tell, mostly to make the other two volumes harder to use correctly unless you already knew what you were looking for.

It was dense, official, deliberately unfriendly to a casual reader — exactly the kind of material that discouraged most people from ever finding what he was looking for, written in the bureaucratic register that seemed designed to make even simple facts require three readings to extract. He read anyway, methodically, the same way he'd mapped Renn's tells and Dessa's transition windows, looking for the seam. He built a working list as he went, the same instinct that had filled pages of the log with opponents' patterns now turned on paragraphs and clauses instead: *this section defines standard evaluation. This section defines who standard evaluation applies to. This section doesn't define anything — it just points to two other sections, which is either laziness or the kind of laziness that hides something if you're patient enough to follow it.*

He followed it.

Two hours in, he'd found nothing but confirmation of what he already feared: the summons process, as written in the primary compliance handbook, was airtight, standard, and applied, on its face, to every classification the Compact recognized. He nearly closed the volume there. He didn't, because closing it there would have meant admitting he'd run out of road, and he wasn't ready to make that admission with over forty hours still left on the clock.

He found it roughly three hours in, in a cross-reference between two sections that, read separately, gave no indication of connecting to each other at all — a footnote in the compliance handbook that referred him, in the smallest print on the page, to a statute number in the non-standard classifications volume, which itself referred back to a definition buried two appendices deep in the same brittle supplementary text he'd almost dismissed as irrelevant an hour before.

*Compliance evaluations,* the primary section read, *require that the subject's classification be recognized within the standard Compact evaluation framework, permitting assessment against established tier and Path benchmarks.*

*[SHATTERED],* the historical classification appendix noted, in the same dry, deprecated-language register as the entry he'd already copied into his notebook, *is not recognized within the standard Compact evaluation framework. Non-standard classifications of this type require senior evaluation authorization rather than standard district-level compliance procedure.*

He read both passages three times, checking and rechecking that he'd understood the logic correctly, because the logic, if he had understood it correctly, was genuinely useful: a standard compliance evaluation — the kind Coss's summons had ordered — required a classification the standard framework could actually evaluate. [SHATTERED] wasn't in that framework. Therefore, by the regulation's own language, the compliance evaluation Coss had summoned him to could not, procedurally, proceed as written.

It wasn't a way out. He understood that clearly, even in the moment of finding it — it was a delay, a procedural obstruction that would buy time rather than resolve anything. But time, he had learned since Weaver's Row, was the single most valuable resource available to someone with no other leverage at all.

He sat with the two open volumes for a long moment before doing anything else, not quite trusting the discovery yet. He'd been wrong about smaller things before — had thought he understood a fighter's pattern only to have it collapse under him in the third exchange, had thought a source was reliable only to find the detail didn't hold up under a second look. He made himself read the two passages a fourth time, slower, out loud under his breath in the archive's hush, the way Hesk read a load calculation before signing off on it. The logic held. It held on the fourth reading exactly as cleanly as it had on the first, which was, he was starting to understand, the actual difference between a real find and a hopeful one.

Something in his chest loosened, fractionally — not relief, not yet, but the quiet satisfaction of a hypothesis surviving contact with scrutiny. He allowed himself exactly one moment of feeling it before returning to the work, because the finding, however real, was only half of what the next day and a half required.

He copied both passages, word for word, citation numbers included, into his log, along with the shelf location and volume number in case he needed to point someone else to the same source. He copied them twice, actually — once quickly, to have the information secured, and a second time more carefully, in the neat handwriting he reserved for things that mattered, because he had a feeling, even then, that this particular page of the log was going to matter more than most.

Halden, on his way out, glanced at the two volumes stacked neatly for reshelving and raised an eyebrow, the closest thing to a question the man had asked all afternoon. "Find what you needed?"

"I think so."

"Most people who come in here looking that satisfied are wrong." Halden said it without malice, an observation offered the way a craftsman offers a caution about a tool. "Come back if you're not."

Cael thanked him and walked out into the early evening with the careful optimism of someone who intended to prove the exception rather than the rule.

---

He spent the second day preparing — not the argument itself, which he'd already found, but the presentation of it. He wrote it out twice, refining the language each time, aiming for something that would read, to a Compact official, as precise and procedurally grounded rather than as a fourteen-year-old's improvised defense.

The first draft, reread the next morning with slightly fresher eyes, embarrassed him a little — too many sentences that circled back to justify themselves, too much of his own uncertainty leaking through in places where the regulation should have been left to speak on its own. He rewrote it, cutting anything that sounded like pleading, keeping only the citation, the logic, and the single sentence of context a reader would need to follow both. The second draft was shorter than the first by nearly half, and better for it — another thing, he noted, that Hesk's notebook had told him without quite saying it directly: *say the true thing in as few words as it actually needs.*

He showed the final draft to Lira in the late afternoon, in the boarding house courtyard where they'd taken to sparring some mornings when the Cinder House yard was crowded. She read it with the same focused attention she brought to studying an opponent's tape, if the circuit had kept tape, which it didn't — the same stillness, the same slow, careful re-reading of anything she wasn't immediately sure about.

"This citation," she said, tapping a line partway down the page. "You're stating it like a fact. It is a fact. But the sentence around it sounds like you're bracing for someone to argue with you."

"Because someone is going to argue with me."

"Sure. But you don't write the argument for them in advance. You state the fact, flat, like it's already settled, and you let *him* do the work of finding a counter — which he won't, because there isn't one, but that's not something you demonstrate by sounding nervous about it." She turned the page toward him, tracing the offending sentence with one finger. "Cut this whole clause. Just — 'Compliance evaluations require recognized classification. [SHATTERED] is not recognized. Therefore standard procedure does not apply.' Three sentences. Nothing to argue with because there's nothing extra to grab onto."

He made the cut. It was better immediately, the way a fight became better the moment you stopped explaining your own strategy mid-exchange and just executed it.

"You want this to read like you're citing regulation because you know it, not because you're scared," she said, handing the page back for a final look. "Confidence changes how people receive the same information. Doesn't change the information. Just changes whether they fight you on it out of habit."

"I am scared."

"I know. Doesn't mean it needs to be on the page." She sat back, studying him with something that wasn't quite the sparring-focus of a minute before — something a little softer, a little more direct. "This is good, Cael. Genuinely good work. I've read Compact paperwork before, back before the academy. This reads like it was written by someone twice your age who's done this a hundred times, not someone who found the regulation yesterday in a dusty archive with an archivist watching him work."

"Halden's not nervous."

"Everyone in a records office is a little nervous. It's the job." She smiled, briefly, the quick smile she allowed herself when she'd landed something she was pleased with, and then it was gone, replaced by the steadier, more careful expression she wore when she meant the next thing seriously. "You're going to be fine tomorrow. Not because I'm being kind. Because I read the page, and the page is right."

He appeared at the district office at the appointed hour, on time, the way he was early to almost everything, carrying both notebooks and the written procedural challenge, folded carefully into the front pages of the log.

The waiting room was smaller and shabbier than Weaver's Row had been — a single bench, a window that didn't quite close all the way, a district notice board on the opposite wall carrying the same kind of official language he'd spent two days learning to read past rather than around. He sat, and made himself sit still, which was harder than it should have been. He had done the work. He had checked the logic four times. He knew, with as much certainty as anything in his life since the Kindling had allowed him, that the citation was correct.

None of that made the waiting easier. He'd learned, in the circuit, that the moment before an exchange was often worse than the exchange itself — the body understood, somewhere beneath conscious thought, that anticipation cost more than action, and refused to be reasoned out of the cost no matter how solid the plan underneath it. He took out his notebook, not to write anything, just to hold something familiar in his hands, and reread the two cited passages one final time, silently, lips barely moving, the way he imagined a fighter might run through a combination one last time before the match was called.

A clerk he didn't recognize eventually opened the inner door and said his name, and he stood, folded the notebook away, and walked in with the deliberate steadiness of someone who had decided, somewhere between the boarding house and here, that whatever happened next, he was going to walk into it rather than be pulled.

---

Warden Coss received him in the same cramped office he'd been working from for two days, and Cael watched, with the same attention he brought to reading anyone, as Coss's expression moved from routine bureaucratic neutrality to something more alert the moment Cael produced the written document rather than simply appearing to answer questions.

"What's this?"

"A procedural objection," Cael said, and handed it over.

Coss read it. Cael watched him read it twice, the second time slower, his expression shifting from mild irritation — the irritation of a man whose straightforward task had just become less straightforward — to something closer to genuine surprise, and, underneath that, an emotion Cael hadn't expected and took a moment to correctly identify: reluctant, professional respect.

"You found this yourself," Coss said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Yes."

"In the district archive."

"Yes."

Coss set the document down and looked at Cael properly, for the first time since he'd entered — not as a compliance subject, Cael understood, but as a specific, unexpected variable in a file that had already been behaving strangely before this moment arrived. He picked the document back up almost immediately, as if needing a second look to confirm the first hadn't been a trick of poor light, and read it through once more from the top, this time murmuring the citation numbers under his breath, checking them, Cael realized, against something he already knew — the actual text of the regulation, held in his own memory well enough to verify a stranger's citation without needing to consult the source himself.

"I've conducted eleven years of compliance evaluations," Coss said, slowly, setting the page down a final time and folding his hands over it as though physically holding the problem in place while he worked out what to do with it. "I have never once had a subject present a procedural challenge to the summons itself. Most people don't know the regulation exists. Most people who do know it exists don't understand it well enough to cite it correctly." He tapped the document. "You've cited it correctly. Statute number, subsection, cross-reference to the appendix — all of it, exactly as written. Do you understand how unusual that is?"

"I had two days and a reason to be thorough."

"Two days." Coss repeated it, something flickering behind his eyes that might have been disbelief, might have been the beginning of recalculating an assumption he'd been carrying since he opened the file that morning. "It took our own regional training office the better part of a week to compile a study guide covering half of what's in this document, and that was written by people whose entire job is cross-referencing compliance statutes for a living. You found it in a district archive, alone, in forty-eight hours, while presumably also eating and sleeping."

"Mostly not sleeping."

Something that wasn't quite a laugh moved across Coss's face — there and gone, suppressed almost as fast as it arrived, the reaction of a man who had decided a long time ago that a certain kind of professional distance was safer to maintain and was finding it, in this specific case, harder to hold than usual.

"Is it wrong?" Cael asked, into the pause.

Coss was quiet for a long moment — the specific quiet of a man checking his own certainty before committing to a statement he couldn't easily walk back, running the argument again in his head the way Cael had run it a fourth time in the archive the day before. "No," he said, finally, with the flat honesty of someone who valued procedural accuracy over convenience even when the accuracy inconvenienced him personally. "It's not wrong. Standard compliance procedure genuinely doesn't apply to your classification. Which means I can't run the evaluation I was sent here to run." He exhaled, and for just a moment, the professional composure slipped enough to show something that looked, to Cael, almost like frustration directed at the situation rather than at him specifically. "This is going to be a very unpopular report to file."

"Why unpopular? You're following the regulation."

"Because the people who requested this sweep didn't want a procedural delay. They wanted a resolved file. I don't know what resolved means to them — closed favorably, closed unfavorably, I genuinely don't know — but a fourteen-year-old citing statute at me and forcing a six-week postponement is not going to read, to whoever's above me, as resolution of anything." Coss said this with the candor of a man who had already decided, somewhere in the last several minutes, that pretending otherwise to Cael would cost him more credibility than it bought him. "I file accurate reports. That's the job. Today's report is going to say a fourteen-year-old outmaneuvered eleven years of my training with two days in an archive. I don't enjoy writing that sentence. I'm going to write it anyway, because it's true, and because the alternative is worse."

"What happens now?"

"Now I stamp this closed-pending and file for senior evaluation authorization, which is the process the regulation actually requires for your classification." Coss picked up a stamp from his desk, considered it a moment — a pause just long enough that Cael understood he was weighing something beyond the mechanical act of stamping a page — and applied it to the summons with more force than the action strictly required. "Senior evaluation authorization takes time. Regional office has to review the file, assign a qualified evaluator, schedule travel out to a district this far from the capital. Six weeks, typically. Possibly more, if the evaluator's calendar is already full, which it usually is."

Six weeks. Cael did the arithmetic instantly, the same way he calculated timing windows in a fight, running the number forward against everything he already knew he wanted to accomplish with it. Six weeks was not resolution. Six weeks was room to move — training, circuit bouts, whatever else he could build into the gap before someone with real authority arrived to close it.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it, despite understanding that Coss's compliance with the regulation wasn't kindness, exactly — it was the same professional precision Coss had clearly built his entire career around, applied without exception even to a case he'd probably rather not have complicated further. It occurred to him, watching Coss file the stamped summons into a folder with the same careful economy he brought to everything, that this was itself a kind of lesson, one he hadn't expected to receive from a Compact official: that doing the job correctly, even when correctness was inconvenient, was its own form of integrity, distinct from and sometimes opposed to what the job's actual sponsors wanted done.

Coss studied him a moment longer, something unreadable behind his eyes — the look, Cael thought, of a man deciding whether to say something that wasn't strictly part of the job. "Can I ask you something," he said, finally, "that isn't part of this evaluation?"

"You can ask."

"What are you going to do with six weeks?"

Cael considered how much to say, and decided, in the end, on something close to the whole truth, because Coss had earned at least that much by being more honest than his position required. "Get stronger. Build a reason for people to notice if I disappear."

Something crossed Coss's face — not quite approval, not quite concern, something adjacent to both. "That's either very smart or very dangerous. Possibly both, the way most smart things end up being once you look at them long enough." He closed the folder. "Don't thank me yet," he added, standing, signaling the meeting's end in the specific way officials signal things without needing to say so directly. "You've bought six weeks. I'd use every one of them, if I were you, to figure out what you're actually going to do with the classification you have, because whoever authorized the sweep that put me in this district in the first place isn't going to be satisfied with a procedural delay. They want something resolved. I don't know what. I'd start finding out, if it were me — because I can buy you time, Cael. I can't buy you an answer to whatever question they're actually asking. Only you can find that."

It was, Cael understood, walking out past the desk with its ink-stained surface and its slightly wobbling leg, the most useful thing anyone in the Compact had ever said to him — not a threat, not reassurance, just an honest, professionally offered piece of information from a man who had no particular reason to offer it beyond his own evident discomfort with a file that kept refusing to behave the way files were supposed to behave.

---

Cael walked out of the district office into the Ardenmere afternoon and stood for a moment on the step, letting the ordinary sounds of the street settle back around him — a cart, a vendor's call, someone laughing two doors down about something that had nothing to do with him, the grounding indifference of a district that had no idea what had just happened inside that cramped office and would have gone on exactly the same regardless.

He thought about Hesk, two days' road away, who would want to know this the moment a letter could reach him. He thought about the archive entry, four names, three dead within weeks — and about the fact that he had just done something none of those four, as far as the record showed, had apparently thought or been able to try: he had read the rules closely enough to find the gap they'd left unclosed, and he had used it.

It wasn't victory. He understood that clearly enough not to let the relief curdle into something less useful. It was six weeks, bought with two days of reading and a fourteen-year-old's stubborn refusal to accept the first answer he was given. But six weeks, spent correctly, could become something larger than six weeks — a reputation, a rating, a reason for an entire district to notice if the Compact tried to make him disappear quietly.

He thought, too, about Coss's question — the one that wasn't part of the evaluation. *What are you going to do with six weeks?* He'd given an honest answer, but standing here now, with the office behind him and the ordinary street in front of him, he found the answer had more underneath it than he'd said out loud. Getting stronger wasn't just strategy. It was the only response available to him that didn't involve waiting for someone else to decide what he was. The registry had tried to define him with one word and a deprecated classification. The Compact had tried to define him with a summons and a sweep he hadn't asked for. Even Hesk, gently, carefully, had tried to define him with provisions and warnings, born entirely of love and still, in their way, another set of edges drawn around him by someone else's hand.

The circuit didn't care what the registry said. Lira had told him that weeks ago, and he'd understood it then as permission. He understood it now as something closer to instruction: if nobody else was going to hand him a definition worth trusting, he'd have to build one himself, one demonstrated fight at a time, in a place where the only classification that mattered was the one he could actually prove.

He went, without quite deciding to, directly to the Cinder House circuit board, where the notices sat exactly where they always sat, offering exactly the kind of opportunity he now intended to make full use of.

Six weeks. He intended to make every one of them count.

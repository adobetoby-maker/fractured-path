# Chapter 1 — The Day Before

The thing about Kindling was that everyone had a different word for what it felt like.

Cael had collected eleven.

*Sunlight,* said the dried-fruit seller at the Outer Market.

*A door opening,* said the gate guard on Merchant Row.

*Like remembering something you never knew,* said the older girl three streets over.

*I don't know how to describe it,* said Hesk.

Cael had written them all down in the notebook he kept for things that mattered, but he had only starred the last one.

Tomorrow, he would find out for himself.

That was the problem.

---

Kindling happened the year you turned fourteen, ready or not. The city scheduled the certification appointment a year in advance, which meant Cael had spent approximately three hundred and sixty-four mornings trying not to think about this one.

This was not, it turned out, a sustainable strategy.

He ate breakfast while Hesk read the city notice sheet and morning light cut sideways through the kitchen window. The house smelled like bread, soldering flux, and the machine oil Hesk used in the workshop out back.

Cael wrote in his notebook and did not think about tomorrow.

He thought about it continuously.

"You planning to wear a hole through that page?" Hesk asked.

Cael looked down. He had been tracing the same line with the end of his pencil.

"Possibly."

Hesk folded the notice sheet. "Useful strategy?"

"No."

"Good to know."

Neither of them mentioned the appointment.

That was worse.

Cael finished the bread, shut the notebook, and went outside because the house had become too small to contain the thing they were both not saying.

---

The Outer District of Denvash ran along the city's interior wall like a footnote. Cael had grown up in its northern quadrant among transit depots, repair shops, market stalls, and people whose Path rankings were low enough that the city considered them useful mainly in aggregate.

The Inner District wall made the arrangement plain. It was taller than it needed to be, better maintained on the other side, and guarded by people whose armor alone cost more than most Outer District families saw in a year.

Cael had a notebook entry on this. Five pages.

He walked north toward the market with no destination because moving was easier than waiting.

Fourteen carts between the house and Merchant Row. Six vendors already selling. Three still setting up. One argument over the weight of a delivery that sounded familiar enough to be recreational.

Counting things quieted his thoughts.

Mostly.

He was passing the certification office on Weaver's Row when he saw Alis Trent standing outside with her mother.

Alis had Kindled three days ago. Her mother was crying. Alis was not.

She held out her certification document before Cael could ask.

"Tide Path. Copper, Rank 1."

"Good healing potential," he said.

"My brother got Blade Path."

"Also good."

"He's Silver Rank 3 now."

Cael considered lying politely and decided against it.

"You're going to have more options in two years than you think you do right now."

Alis stared at him. "You sound like a motivational pamphlet."

"I'm paraphrasing my grandfather."

"Is he right?"

Hesk was right about most things, generally because he'd been wrong often enough earlier in life to develop a strong dislike of certainty without evidence.

"Usually."

Alis glanced back at the document.

"Tomorrow's yours."

"Yes."

"Are you worried?"

Cael respected direct questions.

"Yes."

She nodded. "Me too. Before." Then, quieter: "It's better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted."

Cael opened the notebook.

*Better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted.*

He starred it.

"Congratulations," he said.

"Good luck tomorrow."

He walked on.

---

Joren's result had become neighborhood news for a different reason.

For a year, Joren had predicted Gold. Then Silver. Then, as the appointment got closer, "at least Bronze." He had done this with such confidence that half the northern quadrant had begun treating his Kindling as a public experiment.

Stone Path. Copper, Rank 1.

Not bad. Not even close to bad. Stone rewarded patience, and Joren had more of that than people noticed underneath the bravado.

But it wasn't what he'd promised himself.

Cael had seen him walking home after certification. Joren hadn't looked devastated. He had looked like someone working very hard not to look devastated, which was different, and harder.

That bothered Cael more than the result itself.

He bought a flatbread with cracked pepper and salt and stood at the edge of the market eating it while people moved around him.

Alis had said *better when it's done.*

Joren had been certain.

And then there was the registry entry Cael had found three months ago under *non-standard designations and legacy classifications.*

*[SHATTERED]: Classification indicating fractured Path architecture, historically associated with unstable practice and practitioner safety risk. Currently deprecated. Four (4) instances recorded in the history of the Compact registry.*

Four.

Not four percent.

Four people.

Cael had not copied that entry into his notebook.

He had copied almost everything else.

---

Hesk found him on the rooftop that afternoon.

Cael had been there for two hours. The notebook had mostly served as something to hold.

Hesk came through the access hatch carrying two cups of tea and sat beside him on the low southern wall. From there, the rooftops of the Outer District ran toward the Inner District boundary, where the wall rose too high against the afternoon sky.

They sat without speaking.

Below them, the large grey dog that belonged to no one Cael had ever identified barked at a cart and was ignored.

"Joren seems better," Hesk said.

"He is."

"He'll be all right."

"Yeah. Stone probably suits him better than Blade anyway."

Hesk made the sound he made when he had something specific to say and was deciding when to place it.

Cael waited.

"You're not Joren," Hesk said.

"No."

"Whatever happens tomorrow—"

"Hesk."

He stopped.

Then: "We'll figure it out."

Cael looked at him. "You already looked into it."

Hesk was quiet.

That was answer enough.

"You talked to someone."

"The district record office. Procedurally."

"About non-standard classifications."

"Yes."

Cael felt something in his chest tighten, not because he was surprised, exactly, but because suspicion was easier to carry than confirmation.

"How long have you been thinking about this?"

"A while."

"Since my preliminary assessment?"

"Before that."

Cael turned toward him fully.

"What do you know?"

Hesk looked out over the district.

"Your preliminary assessment showed irregular coupling patterns. The assessor called it uncommon variation. She wasn't wrong." He paused. "But there are different kinds of uncommon variation."

Cael's notebook lay open in his hands. He wasn't writing.

"You've seen it before," he said.

Hesk's eyes shifted toward him.

A fraction too late.

"Once," Hesk said.

"Who?"

Hesk shook his head. Not refusal. Delay.

"Tomorrow first. Then the rest."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It isn't."

Cael hated that Hesk could say things like that without becoming easier to argue with.

"You should have told me."

"There was nothing certain to tell."

"I've been worried about it alone."

Hesk looked down at his tea.

"Yes," he said. "I know. I'm sorry about that."

The apology landed harder than a defense would have.

Below them, the grey dog crossed the intersection with the unhurried confidence of an animal that had never once considered the possibility that the street might not belong to it.

"If it's what you think it might be," Cael said, "what happens?"

"Then we figure it out."

"That's what you said before."

"Because it's still true."

Hesk finally looked at him.

"Whatever the certification says tomorrow, Cael, it does not get the final word on what you become."

Cael stared at him.

Hesk rarely said things that sounded like philosophy. When he did, Cael paid attention.

Years earlier, in the workshop, Hesk had told him never to trust a number he couldn't trace back to where it came from. He had been talking about a failed housing bracket.

Cael had written the sentence down anyway.

Now he opened the notebook and added another beneath it.

*The certification does not get the final word.*

He considered starring it.

He didn't.

Not yet.

"Tea's cold," Hesk said after a while.

"You let it get cold."

"I did."

He stood. "Come down when you're ready. I'll start dinner."

Hesk left him there without asking when ready would be.

That was one of the things Cael loved about him.

---

That night Cael tried to sleep.

He lasted twenty-three minutes.

He got up, opened the notebook, and turned to page seven.

Eleven descriptions.

Sunlight.

A door opening.

Remembering something you never knew.

*I don't know how to describe it.*

Outside, Denvash settled into its night noises: distant cart wheels, a closing shutter, someone laughing two streets over, the faint mechanical hum from the transit yards.

Tomorrow the city would put him in a chair, run the assessment, and tell him what Path he had.

Or didn't.

Cael looked at the blank space beneath the eleven descriptions.

For the first time all day, he stopped trying not to think about it.

Then he wrote one more line.

*Ask again tomorrow.*

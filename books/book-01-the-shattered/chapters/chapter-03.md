# Chapter 3 — The Record

The expulsion notice came on the second morning, delivered by a courier in the grey-and-brass of the city administration, who knocked once, waited exactly long enough to be polite, and handed the document to Hesk with the trained blankness of someone who delivered documents like this often enough to have stopped reading them.

Cael watched the exchange from the kitchen table through the open front room — the courier's satchel, worn at the strap in a way that suggested a great many mornings just like this one; the practiced motion of Hesk's signature, unhurried, on the receipt slip; the courier's eyes moving once, briefly, to Cael's face over Hesk's shoulder, and then away, the way you look at a thing you've been told about and don't quite know how to look at directly. The courier left without another word. His boots were quiet on the front step going down, quieter than they'd been coming up, which struck Cael as the kind of thing that shouldn't have meant anything and did anyway.

Hesk signed for it. The courier left. Hesk brought it to the kitchen table, where Cael was eating breakfast without much interest in the eating part, and set it down between them without opening it. He didn't sit immediately. He stood over the table for a moment, looking at the sealed document the way he sometimes looked at a piece of instrument housing before deciding where the first cut needed to go — assessing, not hesitating, though from the outside the two could look identical.

"You should be the one to read it," Hesk said.

Cael looked at the seal for a moment before breaking it — a small, official pressing in dark wax, the Compact's balance-and-hand insignia stamped into it with a precision that suggested a machine had done the pressing rather than a person. He thought, absurdly, of the certification office's brass plate, the same institutional care applied to something that was about to change his life, as though the seriousness of the document could be measured by how carefully its wax had been worked. He broke it anyway. There was no version of the morning where he didn't.

Cael opened it.

The language was administrative in the way that administrative language achieved distance from its own content — long sentences, passive constructions, a structure designed to be technically true while emotionally inert. He read it twice. The second time was to make sure he'd understood it correctly, because the first time had felt like reading it through water.

*By authority of the Denvash City Administration, acting under Compact Registry Statute 14, Section 9 (Non-Standard Classification, Residential Provisions):*

*The bearer of Registration 41-7843-V, classified [SHATTERED] as of this date, is hereby notified that residential access above Unranked District designation is revoked effective immediately. The bearer retains legal access to Unranked-designated residence.*

*Note: the registered residence at 14 Fen Street, Outer District, Denvash, is held under the standing of an Iron-tier practitioner (registration 22-1190-H). Continued cohabitation by a [SHATTERED]-classified individual may affect the standing-holder's guild status pending Compact review. The standing-holder is advised to consider residential arrangements accordingly.*

*This notice does not constitute a criminal or civil finding against the bearer. It constitutes a residential and administrative provision only.*

He read the last sentence a third time. *This notice does not constitute a criminal or civil finding against the bearer.* It was, he thought, a strange thing to specify, unless a great many people needed to be told that in order to believe it. He noted this. He did not write it down yet — his hands weren't steady enough to write, which he also noted, clinically, the way he noted most things about himself when the noting was easier than the feeling.

He set the document on the table and looked at it from a small distance, the way you look at a wound before deciding whether it needs immediate attention or just time. The paper was good stock, heavier than the certification form had been, with a watermark he could see when the light caught it at an angle — a small, official flourish that struck him as almost funny, that someone had thought to make the paper *nice* for a document doing this job. He thought about the courier's careful blankness, the practiced non-reading of a page he must have read a hundred times before delivering it a hundred more. He wondered whether the courier had a script for reactions he wasn't allowed to have, the way Pellin apparently had one, the way Cael himself was building one, morning by morning, without anyone teaching him how.

"They're not expelling me," he said. "They're expelling you. From having me."

Hesk read the document over his shoulder. His face did the thing it had done in the certification office — went still in the prepared way. "That's the mechanism," he said. "It's designed that way. Easier to make a family choose than to be seen forcing a fourteen-year-old out directly."

"Convenient."

"Very." Hesk set the document down. "It's not a request, Cael. Don't spend energy being angry at the choice being offered. There isn't one."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway."

Cael looked at the document again — at the line about Hesk's guild status, the *pending Compact review* that sat there doing exactly the work it was designed to do. He thought about the years Hesk had spent building a reputation as a competent, quiet, reliable Iron-tier instrument-maker. He thought about how easily a document like this could put that at risk over something Cael hadn't chosen and couldn't have prevented.

"You could refuse," he said. It wasn't really a suggestion. It was a thing he needed to say out loud so he could hear how it sounded, and it sounded, out loud, exactly as impossible as he'd expected.

"I could," Hesk agreed. "And in three months my guild registration gets reviewed, and in six months I lose workshop access, and in a year we're both in the Unranked District with no income and no standing and no way to help you that isn't worse than the alternative." He said this without heat. It was arithmetic, not argument. "This isn't a fight I win by refusing. It's a fight I win by making sure you land somewhere with resources."

"I don't want you losing anything for me."

"I'm not losing anything for you." Hesk's voice was steady in a way that made the sentence land harder than a raised one would have. "I'm doing exactly what I decided to do a long time ago, which is whatever it takes. This is the *whatever.* Don't make it smaller than it is by apologizing for it."

Cael didn't have an answer for that. He put the document down and looked at his hands, which had steadied enough to be useful again.

They sat with it a while longer, the document between them on the table, neither of them reaching to move it. Outside, the ordinary sounds of the street continued without any awareness that something in the house had just changed shape — a cart, a vendor's call, the clatter of the bakery's morning delivery being unloaded three doors down. Cael found the ordinariness of it almost unbearable and then, a moment later, strangely steadying, the way it was steadying to know the sun would still rise the next day regardless of what the day contained.

"What happens to the workshop," Cael asked, eventually, "if your standing actually gets reviewed."

"Nothing happens to the workshop today. Possibly not for months. The Compact moves slowly when moving slowly serves them, which is most of the time." Hesk's voice had leveled back into its usual register, the one he used for structural calculations and difficult truths alike. "I'm not going to pretend there's no risk. There is. I've decided the risk is mine to carry, and I've decided that a long time before this morning."

"When?"

"When I took you in." He said it simply, without weight, as though it were the least remarkable fact in the room. "You don't raise a child and reserve the right to change your mind about the cost later. That's not raising. That's something else, and I didn't do something else."

---

He spent part of that afternoon at the district record office, which was three streets from the house and which he had visited before, in the abstract, theoretical way of someone researching a thing that might never apply to him. It applied to him now.

The record office was a squat, practical building, older than the certification office and considerably less concerned with appearances — the stonework was original, unrestored, and the front steps had worn into a shallow curve from what must have been centuries of feet. It smelled the way archives always smelled, in Cael's admittedly limited experience: paper and dust and the faint mineral tang of ink that had dried decades ago and had no intention of fading further. He'd been here twice before, both times for the ordinary reasons a curious fourteen-year-old had for visiting a public record office — a school inquiry, once, and the afternoon six months ago when he'd first gone looking for anything the community hall's reference section didn't have. Both visits had felt like research. This one didn't.

The clerk on duty recognized the registration number when Cael gave it — a flicker, quickly suppressed, professional — and directed him to the historical classification archive without being asked, which told Cael something about how quickly information moved through a system that was supposed to be discreet. The clerk was young, younger than Cael expected for the position, and for a moment something almost sympathetic crossed her face before she caught it and smoothed it back into the neutrality her job required. She didn't ask why he wanted the archive. She didn't need to.

The archive was a single room with shelving that went floor to ceiling and a research table under a window, the kind of window that let in light without much warmth, angled north in the way archive windows always seemed to be angled, as though sunlight itself might damage something if it fell too directly. Dust moved visibly in the light, slow and unbothered, and Cael watched it for a moment before making himself stop watching it and start working. The historical classification summary — the same document he'd found a fragment of at the community hall — was here in its full form, bound, official, and considerably more detailed than the four-line entry he'd already memorized. The binding was old enough that the spine cracked when he opened it, a small sound in the quiet room that felt, for a half-second, unreasonably loud.

He found the [SHATTERED] section. It took longer than expected, because it was filed under a subheading — *Deprecated and Non-Standard Designations, Pre-Compact Era Holdovers* — that suggested the classification predated the current system entirely, which was itself new information.

The entry was longer here. Four paragraphs instead of four lines.

*[SHATTERED]: A classification applied when Arbiter evaluation identifies practitioner potential inconsistent with standard Path architecture. The designation reflects fractured or incomplete coupling between practitioner and the Path framework, historically associated with unpredictable ability manifestation and elevated practitioner risk.*

*Historical incidence: four (4) instances recorded in the entire history of the Compact registry.*

He stopped there for a moment. Four. He had known the number in the abstract, the way you know a fact you've read once and set aside. He had not, until this exact moment, converted it into four actual people who had stood where he'd stood and heard what he'd heard.

He kept reading.

*Of the four (4) recorded instances, disposition is as follows: three (3) practitioners are recorded as deceased within weeks of classification, cause of death listed as "practice-related incident" in Compact records. One (1) practitioner's disposition is recorded as unknown, last registry update dated 32 years prior to the present record.*

He read it again. Then a third time, because the sentence was doing something he needed a moment to fully receive: three of four dead within weeks. One unaccounted for. Not a policy. Not a warning. A record of what had actually happened to the only other people who had ever stood in his position.

*"Practice-related incident"* was doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and he noted, with the same detachment he'd applied to his own unsteady hands that morning, that he did not currently have the information to know whether that phrase meant *died attempting to use an ability they didn't understand* or *died because someone made sure of it.* He suspected, without evidence yet, that it might not matter which — that the distinction between accident and consequence got blurry fast when the person doing the recording had an interest in which one was believed.

He copied the entry into his notebook, word for word, in handwriting that was neater than it needed to be, which was its own kind of tell.

He sat with the closed volume for a while afterward, not reading, just sitting, in the stillness of the archive room with its slow-moving dust and its window full of thin northern light. He thought about the clerk outside, young and briefly sympathetic before catching herself. He thought about how many people, over however many decades this archive had stood, had sat in this exact chair reading about something that applied to someone they loved, and how the room gave no sign of remembering any of them — just shelving, and dust, and the patient indifference of paper that outlasted everyone who ever needed it.

He returned the volume to its place on the shelf, careful to set it exactly as he'd found it, and walked back out through the record office's front doors into a street that looked, infuriatingly, exactly the same as it had two hours before. The vendors were still calling out prices. A cart was still creaking past with a load of something that smelled like cut lumber. The ordinary world had, once again, declined to notice that something in his had shifted.

He did not, on the walk home, decide how he felt about it. He decided, instead, on something more useful: that he would not be the fifth entry in this record, and that deciding this in the abstract, on a walk home from a records office, was easy in a way that staying alive to make it true would not be.

---

Hesk was in the workshop when Cael got home, doing something methodical with a set of small tools and a housing he'd half-assembled. He looked up when Cael came in, read something in his face, and set the tools down.

"You went to the archive."

"Yes."

"You found the full entry."

"Yes." Cael sat on the workshop stool across from him — the one he'd sat on since he was young enough that his feet didn't reach the floor. "Three dead within weeks. One unaccounted for."

Hesk was quiet for a moment. Then: "I know."

"You knew before."

"Yes."

"You didn't tell me."

"No." Hesk met his eyes without flinching from it. "I told you what you needed to make decisions with. I didn't tell you what would only make you afraid without helping you do anything about it. I'm not going to pretend that was a neutral choice. It wasn't. I made a judgment, and you're allowed to think it was the wrong one."

Cael sat with that. He found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he didn't think it was the wrong one — not entirely. He thought it was a choice made by someone trying to hold two things at once: his responsibility to prepare Cael for what was coming, and his instinct to protect him from carrying more weight than the moment required. Those two things didn't fit together cleanly. They rarely did.

He watched Hesk's hands while he thought about it — still moving through the half-assembled housing, unhurried, precise, a craftsman's hands that had clearly done this exact motion thousands of times and could apparently keep doing it even while the rest of the man carried a conversation that mattered considerably more than the instrument on the bench. Cael had always found that steadiness reassuring. He found it reassuring now too, though it took him a moment to notice that the reassurance and the anger could exist in the same breath without canceling each other out.

"I need you to tell me things now," Cael said. "As they come. Not managed. Not timed for when you think I can handle them."

"All right." Hesk held his gaze. "Then I need to tell you something now, while we're being direct about it."

"Okay."

"The fourth record — the unaccounted-for one. Thirty-two years ago." He paused, choosing the next words with the care of someone who had rehearsed this moment without knowing exactly when it would arrive. "I was twenty-two. I was doing a two-year stint with the Compact's regional logistics division — not a fighting posting, just supply routing, the kind of assignment they give competent Iron-tiers who don't ask questions. I was there when that file was opened. I saw the beginning of it. I didn't see the end."

Cael went very still. "What happened to them?"

"I don't know," Hesk said, and it had the weight of a true answer that had cost something to give. "That's not evasion. I mean I genuinely don't know. They were reassigned — pulled from regional oversight into something higher, something I didn't have clearance for. The file went quiet. I asked, twice, and was told it wasn't my concern, in the flat tone that means *stop asking.*" He exhaled. "I left the division eighteen months later. I've wondered about it for thirty-two years. I don't have an answer. I have a name I've never told anyone, and a promise I made to myself the day I mustered out, and I think you should know both exist, even though I can't finish the story for you yet."

"Will you tell me the name?"

"When you're older. Not because I don't trust you — because a name like that is a thread, and I want you strong enough to pull it without it pulling you first." He held up a hand before Cael could argue. "That's not a managed timeline. That's a real condition. When you meet it, you'll know."

Cael wrote none of this down, not yet, not in front of him. He held it instead, turning it over the way he turned over the third exchange in the Ardenmere circuit match on the radio — a piece of information whose shape mattered more than its content, right now.

"Okay," he said, finally. "I believe you."

Hesk nodded, once, and picked his tools back up, and for a while they sat in the workshop together not talking, which was, as it had always been, a way of saying several things at once.

---

That evening, after the light had gone out of the window and Hesk had gone quiet over his book, Cael packed.

It didn't take long. He didn't own much — clothes, the notebook collection going back three years, a few tools he'd made himself in the workshop that were more sentimental than useful, and now the historical archive entry, copied in his own hand, folded into the current notebook's back pages.

He held each notebook for a moment before packing it, an inventory of a kind he hadn't planned to take. The first one, from three years ago, had a cracked spine and a cover gone soft from handling — mostly childish observations, in retrospect, though he'd been proud of them at the time. The second carried him through the year he'd first started noticing the gap between the Outer District and the Inner one, the five-page entry on the wall's grading now feeling less like an essay and more like a prophecy he hadn't known he was writing. The third and current one held eleven descriptions of Kindling and, now, a copied registry entry about four people he'd never met and would probably never stop thinking about. He packed them in order, oldest on the bottom, the way you'd pack anything you intended to keep adding to for a long time yet.

Hesk came in as he was finishing and set three things on the bed beside the bag, one at a time, with the ceremony of someone who had been planning this moment for longer than he'd admit.

The first was a pouch of money — more than Cael expected, counted out in the small, worn denominations that suggested it had been saved specifically for this rather than pulled from general funds.

The second was a small, hand-bound book, the leather worn soft at the corners, closed with a simple cord tie. Cael reached for it. Hesk didn't stop him, but didn't let go right away either.

"Not tonight," he said. "Read it on the road. It'll make more sense with distance between you and this house."

Cael nodded and set it in the bag without opening it.

The third thing was not an object. Hesk sat on the edge of the bed, in the posture of a man about to say something he'd rehearsed and was going to say plainly anyway.

"Ardenmere," he said. "Two days east on the merchant road. The Unranked District there is bigger than anything you've seen — big enough that nobody checks registration at the district gate. It's not perfect. It's not safe in the way a home is safe. But it's a place a [SHATTERED] classification doesn't announce itself, and that buys you room to figure out what you're actually dealing with before anyone with authority over you gets a vote in it."

"You've been there."

"Once. A long time ago." He didn't elaborate, and Cael, mindful of the conversation in the workshop, didn't push. Some threads needed pulling later. "Go there first. Find your feet. Everything else, you'll figure out as it comes — same as everyone does. You're not behind, Cael. You're early. There's a difference."

Cael looked at the bag — money, notebook, tools, a leather-bound book he wasn't allowed to open yet — and thought that it was a strange kind of complete: everything he owned that mattered, and none of it explained what he was actually going to become.

"What was Ardenmere like," he asked, "when you were there?"

Hesk was quiet long enough that Cael thought the question had drifted somewhere he wasn't going to follow it. "Loud," he said, finally. "Bigger than I expected. I was younger than you'd think, and I didn't understand half of what I was looking at." A pause, careful, the same care he'd used in the workshop that afternoon. "I understand more of it now, looking back, than I did standing in it. That's most places, I think. You don't get the shape of somewhere until you're far enough away to see the whole thing at once."

"Will I understand it while I'm there, or only after?"

"Both, probably. At different speeds, for different parts of it." Hesk almost smiled — the particular almost-smile that meant he found the question fair even though he didn't have a clean answer for it. "You've always understood things faster than most people your age. That's not going to stop being true just because the thing in front of you is bigger than anything you've had to understand before."

Cael turned this over. It was, he thought, the closest thing to reassurance Hesk had offered all day, delivered the way Hesk delivered most things — sideways, embedded in an answer to a different question, trusting Cael to find it rather than handing it to him directly.

"I'll write," he said.

"You'd better." Hesk's voice caught, very slightly, on the second word, and he covered it by standing and busying himself with the window latch, which didn't need attention. "Get some sleep. Long walk tomorrow."

Cael lay awake a while after the lamp went out, listening to the house settle around him one last time — the creak of the floorboard outside Hesk's door, the canal gate sighing somewhere north, the quality of silence a home has the night before you stop living in it.

He thought about the four names in the archive record, three dead and one unaccounted for. He thought about a promise Hesk had made at twenty-two and kept for thirty-two years without ever being able to finish it. He thought about the leather-bound book waiting in his bag, unopened, a document Hesk had spent years writing on the chance it might one day be needed and would rather have been wrong about. He thought about the money pouch, counted out in small denominations that meant it had been saved a little at a time, over what he suspected had been a long stretch of ordinary mornings that hadn't looked, from the outside, like anything was being prepared for.

He thought about all of it the way he thought about most things that mattered too much to feel all at once — piece by piece, cataloguing rather than absorbing, because absorbing it whole, tonight, in this bed, in this house he wasn't going to wake up in again, felt like more than he had room for.

He thought: *I am not going to be an entry in someone else's cold record.*

It was not a comforting thought. It was, however, a useful one, and useful had gotten him further than comforting so far. He turned onto his side, facing the window, watching the faint edge of light where the shutter didn't quite meet the frame, and let his mind empty itself the way it did on the rare nights he actually managed to sleep before an important day rather than despite one.

He slept.

# Chapter 20 — Coss Again

Coss found him at the food market, three weeks into the quiet stretch, on an ordinary morning that had given no warning it would turn into anything else. Cael was doing the same errand-and-observe circuit he'd kept up since his first week in Ardenmere — bread, the dried fruit stall, a slow pass through the stalls to note anything that had shifted since yesterday — when a familiar shape resolved out of the crowd's ordinary movement and sat down across from him at a narrow public table without asking.

No summons this time. No district office, no ledger, no careful bureaucratic staging. Just a man in plain clothes rather than his usual Compact-issued coat, sitting across from a fourteen-year-old buying bread, in a way that told Cael, before a single word passed between them, that this visit had been chosen and arranged deliberately rather than simply happening to occur.

He read the setting the way he read an opponent's opening stance — because plain clothes and a public table were themselves information, a choice that traded the intimidation of officialdom for something Coss apparently wanted more: deniability, or perhaps simply the chance to speak as a person rather than as a title.

"This isn't official," Coss said, before Cael could ask, confirming the read. "I want to be clear about that up front, because I think you've earned enough honesty from me that I'm not going to dress this up as something it isn't."

"Okay." Cael set down the bread, giving Coss his full attention, the same attention he gave a fight once it had stopped being preliminary positioning and started being the thing itself.

"The legal review is going to take a while. Months, like Ilsev told you. But I want you to understand something she may not have said as directly, because it's not really her job to say it: the outcome isn't actually in doubt, whatever the paperwork ends up taking to get there. The Compact doesn't want [SHATTERED] practitioners fighting in unsanctioned circuits. That's not a legal question. That's a policy one, and policy questions don't get decided by the kind of procedural arguments you're good at."

This was, Cael understood, a real threat, delivered without any of the theatrical weight threats usually carried — Coss's voice stayed level throughout, almost gentle, the tone of someone doing an unpleasant job as kindly as the job allowed. He filed the observation the way he filed everything: not the content alone, but the manner of its delivery, because manner was often the more honest half of any message.

"You sound like you disagree with the policy," Cael said, testing the edges of the statement rather than accepting it whole.

Coss's mouth twitched, something that wasn't quite a smile. "I've stopped having opinions about policy a long time ago. It's not a useful habit in this job. What I have opinions about is procedure, and procedure says a policy decision that hasn't formally been made yet shouldn't be treated as inevitable. I'm telling you anyway, because I think pretending otherwise would be doing you a disservice you haven't earned."

Cael turned the answer over, checking it the way he'd check an opponent's stated intention against the actual mechanics of their stance. "That's a careful way to avoid the question."

"It's the honest way to avoid the question. There's a difference, even if it doesn't always feel like one from the outside." Coss met his eyes without flinching from the observation. "I could tell you I think the policy's wrong. It wouldn't change anything except how you feel about the next ten minutes, and I'd rather spend those ten minutes usefully than comfortably."

"Fair enough."

"You say that like it costs you nothing to concede a point. Most people your age don't concede points that easily, in my experience — they either dig in or they fold entirely. You do neither. You just note it and move to the next question." He said this with something that might have been the beginning of genuine curiosity, the same reassessment Cael had watched cross Renn's face, and Dessa's, and Feryn's, weeks and months apart — the look of someone recalibrating what they'd assumed they understood.

"Why are you telling me this?" Cael asked. "You could just let the review run its course and let the outcome surprise me."

"Because I'd like this to be simple," Coss said. "You leave Ardenmere voluntarily, before the review concludes, and I file a closure report that doesn't require anyone above me to get involved. Nobody has to do anything messy. You get to choose where you go next instead of having it chosen for you."

"Where would I go?"

"Somewhere small. Somewhere the circuit doesn't operate, so there's nothing for the Compact to object to in the first place." Coss said this without embellishment, the flat honesty of a man who'd thought through the offer carefully before making it. "I know it's not what you want to hear. I'm not offering it because it's what you want. I'm offering it because it's the version of this that doesn't get worse for you."

Cael turned this over, the same careful consideration he applied to any offer that came wrapped in someone else's certainty about what was best for him. "And the legal review, if I leave?"

"I can make it disappear. A closure report ends the file's active status. Nobody keeps chasing a question that's already been resolved by the subject relocating voluntarily."

"You've thought about this carefully."

"I've had weeks to think about it. I don't like the shape of your file, Cael. I haven't liked it since the first afternoon I read it in Denvash, before I ever set foot here. A thin file on an unusual case is either nothing, or it's exactly the kind of nothing someone worked hard to arrange. I've spent eleven years learning to tell the difference, and I don't like which one this is starting to look like." He said this evenly, without drama, the flatness of a man who had decided, somewhere along the way, that plain language served the moment better than any performance of concern would have.

"What would you do? If you were me."

Coss considered the question longer than Cael expected, turning his own untouched cup slowly on the table. "I don't know," he admitted, finally. "I've asked myself that more than once since our first meeting, and I don't have a clean answer. I know what the safe path looks like on paper. I'm not sure it's actually safe, underneath the paper. That's the honest version, and it's not much comfort, I know."

"And the flag Ilsev found? The one neither of you recognize?"

Coss went still — not dramatically, just a small stillness that Cael had learned, over months of reading people's tells for a living, meant something real had just been touched. He didn't answer immediately, and the silence stretched a beat longer than it should have for a question he should have had a ready response to.

"I don't know anything about a flag," he said, finally, and it had the particular texture of a true statement that was nonetheless incomplete — the kind of honesty that answered exactly the question asked and nothing more.

"That's not really an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Coss met his eyes, and something in his expression, for just a moment, looked less like a Compact agent delivering a message and more like a man genuinely troubled by a gap in his own understanding. "I've been doing this eleven years, Cael. I know what standard procedure looks like better than almost anyone in the regional office. What's happening around your file isn't standard. I don't know what it is. I know I don't like not knowing, and I know that not-knowing is exactly why I'm sitting here trying to talk you into leaving quietly, rather than waiting to find out what happens if you don't."

---

Cael sat with this for a long moment, the market's ordinary noise continuing around them, entirely indifferent to the weight of the conversation happening at one small table within it.

"I appreciate you being honest with me," he said, finally. "Even the parts you can't finish."

"That's more grace than I probably deserve, given what I'm here to ask you to do." Coss looked genuinely uncomfortable for the first time since sitting down. "I'm not going to pretend this is a request without consequences either way. If you stay, and the review concludes the way policy suggests it will, the retrieval that follows won't be gentle. I won't be the one who handles it — that gets escalated past my level once policy's actually decided. I don't know who handles it after that. I know I'd rather you weren't there to find out."

"Think about it," Coss said, standing, the conversation apparently at its natural end. "That's all I'm asking. You don't have to answer today."

He left without further ceremony, and Cael sat alone at the table for a while, the bread he'd bought going cold and unimportant in front of him, running the conversation back through his mind the way he ran a fight back through the log — looking for the tell that mattered most, the moment where the surface explanation had cracked and something truer had shown underneath it.

He found it in the stillness. The pause before *I don't know anything about a flag.* A career bureaucrat, eleven years of practiced composure, caught flat-footed by a direct question about something he genuinely didn't understand — and troubled enough by that not-knowing to sit down, off the record, with a fourteen-year-old and try to talk him into leaving before anyone had to find out what the not-knowing actually concealed.

He sat with the bread going cold for a long while, replaying the conversation twice more, the way he'd learned to review a fight he'd lost — not to relitigate it, but to extract everything useful before the memory of it softened into something less precise. He wrote nothing down yet. Some things needed a second pass, spoken aloud to someone he trusted, before they were ready for the permanence of the log.

---

He found Lira at the practitioners' quarter that evening, finishing a drill in the day's last light, and waited at the edge of the yard until she'd wound down rather than interrupting mid-motion — a small courtesy that had become, over the months, an unspoken rule between them.

"You've got a face on," she said, wiping sweat from her forehead with the back of her wrist, studying him with the particular attention she gave any expression she didn't immediately recognize on him. "Something happened."

"Coss found me. At the market."

That got her full attention, the drill entirely forgotten. "Tell me everything. Don't summarize it for me — I want the actual words, not your version of them. You edit things when you're worried about how I'll take them, and I'd rather have the raw version."

He told her everything — the offer, delivered plainly and without theatrical weight; the warning about policy rather than law; the flag, and the stillness that had answered his question about it — and she listened with the focused, unhurried attention she brought to any problem worth taking seriously, not interrupting once, though he could see the tactical calculation running behind her eyes the entire time, the same expression she wore reading an opponent mid-fight.

"He's not lying to you," she said, when he'd finished. "Not about the parts he's telling you, anyway. He genuinely doesn't know what's going on with your file. That's what scares me more than if he did."

"Because if he doesn't know, and he's the person actually assigned to my case—"

"Then whoever does know is operating above the level of the people who are supposed to be handling this. Which means it's not really a Compact bureaucracy problem anymore. It's something else, wearing the bureaucracy as a costume." She said this slowly, working it out as she spoke, the same methodical unpacking Cael usually did with a fight. "He doesn't want you gone, Cael. Not really — you can hear it in how he's talking about this, all the ways he keeps trying to soften an offer he clearly doesn't love making. Someone else wants you gone. He's just the one delivering the message, because he's the one with a name and a face and a title that makes the message look official instead of like what it actually is."

Cael thought about this for a long moment, and found that she'd arrived, faster and more directly than he had, at precisely the conclusion his own careful unpacking had been circling toward without quite landing on it.

"How did you get there so fast?"

"Because I'm not the one it's happening to." She said this simply, without any weight of accusation in it. "It's easier to see the shape of a thing clearly when you're not standing inside it. That's most of what I'm actually useful for, half the time — not better tactics than you, just a different vantage point on the same fight."

"That's not a small thing to be useful for."

"I know. I've decided to stop being modest about it." She almost smiled, then let the seriousness of the moment reclaim her expression. "Cael. I need you to actually hear the rest of what I said, not just the part that confirms what you already suspected. If this is above Coss's level — above Ilsev's, probably, given she flagged the same gap and didn't have an answer either — then it's not something you can out-argue with a well-cited regulation. You've beaten two Compact officials at their own procedure. That method has a ceiling, and I think we might be getting close to it."

"So what do I do instead?"

"I don't know yet either. That's the honest answer." She was quiet for a moment, turning it over the way she turned over an unfamiliar opponent's opening stance. "But I know what you don't do. You don't leave. Leaving doesn't make you safer — it just means whatever this is gets to happen somewhere you have no district, no Vell, no ledger, no me. At least here you have people who'd notice."

"I'm not leaving," he said, finally, the decision arriving with more certainty than he'd expected once he actually said it out loud.

"I didn't think you would." She said this without surprise, already moving toward the practical question underneath the decision. "So what do we actually do with the time between now and whenever this stops being patient?"

"I don't know yet. I need to think about it properly, not just react to it."

"Good. React later. Think first. That's the whole method that's gotten you this far." She bumped his shoulder with hers, the same easy gesture she'd offered weeks before, grounding rather than dismissing the weight of what they'd just worked through together. "Whatever it is, we'll figure it out. Same as always."

They sat together for a while after that, not talking, the practitioners' quarter settling into evening around them — the last of the training-yard sounds fading, someone's cookfire starting up two streets over, the unhurried quiet of a district that had learned to end its days without urgency. Cael found, in the silence, that he didn't need to fill it with tactics or theories. Some silences were just companionship, and this was one of them.

"Can I say something that isn't tactical," Lira said, eventually, breaking the quiet herself.

"Always."

"I'm glad you told me straight away this time. Not after you'd already sat on it alone for two weeks deciding what it meant, the way you did with the fragment. Just — straight away, raw, before either of us had a plan." She looked at him, something warmer than her usual directness showing through. "It's a better way to carry hard things. Together, from the start, instead of you carrying it alone first and handing me the finished shape of it."

"I'm learning."

"I know. It's one of the things I like most about you — you actually learn, instead of just apologizing and doing the same thing again." She stood, offering him a hand up, the earlier tension of the day finally, fully dissolved. "Come on. It's getting dark, and thinking properly is easier with food in you. We can carry this a while longer over dinner."

---

Dinner was quiet, unhurried, neither of them in a rush to return to the weight of the conversation now that it had been carried properly rather than alone. They talked, for a while, about nothing that mattered — a fighter Lira wanted to spar against next week, a rumor about a new bakery opening near the practitioners' quarter, the absurdity of one of Torvin's other boarders who'd apparently tried to sneak a stray cat into the shared room and been caught within the hour. It was, Cael thought, exactly the kind of ordinary evening he'd learned to value more since the quiet week — proof that a hard conversation didn't have to consume everything around it, that a life could hold both weight and lightness in the same few hours without either one canceling out the other.

He walked her back to her own room afterward, the district settled into its evening rhythm around them, and stood for a moment at her door before heading back to Torvin's himself.

"Thank you," he said. "For today. All of it."

"You don't have to thank me for being a friend, Cael. That's not a transaction." She studied him a moment, something gentler than her usual directness in her expression. "Get some sleep. Whatever's coming, it'll still be coming tomorrow, and you'll think better after rest than you will exhausted."

He walked back to Torvin's alone, the district's late sounds carrying around him — voices, distant music, the wheezing man's boarding house presumably already settling into its usual nighttime quiet. Alone with the log, Cael wrote the entry that would sit with him longest, the one that named, precisely, the shape of the thing he'd been circling since Weaver's Row without quite having language for it until Lira had handed it to him at dinner:

*He doesn't want me gone. Someone else wants me gone. He's delivering the message.*

He read it back once, twice, the same careful checking he applied to anything he suspected might be more important than its length suggested.

*I don't know who "someone else" is yet. I don't know what they actually want, beyond me being somewhere I'm not currently standing. I know the shape of the pressure is changing — not procedural anymore, not something I can out-read the way I outread Coss's summons or Ilsev's counter-procedure. Something colder, moving through channels neither of them fully understand.*

*I'm not leaving. I decided that faster than I expected to, and I trust the decision more for how fast it arrived.*

*Lira said something today I want to remember properly: it's easier to see a thing clearly when you're not standing inside it. I think that's true of more than just this. I think it's part of why the two of us keep working, month after month — she sees what I miss because she's not carrying the same weight I am, and I see what she misses for the same reason in reverse. Neither of us is complete on our own. I don't think that's a weakness. I think it might be the actual design.*

*Whatever's coming, I'd rather meet it standing somewhere I chose than running toward somewhere I didn't. And I'd rather meet it with her a step away than face it entirely alone, the way I might have tried to if Weaver's Row had happened to a version of me who hadn't yet learned that some things are actually easier to carry in company.*

He closed the notebook, banked the lamp, and lay in the dark listening to the boarding house's familiar sounds settle around him, the unhurried quiet of a place that had, somewhere in the last several months, become something closer to home than he'd expected any place after Denvash to feel. Whatever waited beyond the edges of Coss's careful warning, he intended to meet it rested, and steady, and exactly where he'd chosen to stand.

# Chapter 16 — What the Notebook Says

The two weeks after the fragment notice were, on the surface, unremarkable — Cael woke, trained, fought when the circuit offered something worth entering, ate, wrote, slept, and did it again the next day, the same rhythm he'd built over the past two months, worn smooth by repetition into something that no longer required deciding.

Underneath the surface, he was building a second life entirely, one that lived almost exclusively in the back pages of the log, where the private section had grown from a scattering of uncertain paragraphs into something closer to an actual working theory — incomplete, contradictory in places, revised almost weekly as new evidence forced him to throw out conclusions he'd been confident about a week before, but a theory nonetheless, the first one he'd managed to build about himself since the Arbiter had gone dark and left him with nothing but a word and a silence.

He wrote, on the first night after he'd practiced the borrowed Wind-adjacent step enough times to stop feeling clumsy doing it:

*I think I'm borrowing something. Not taking it — I don't have the whole thing, not the way Lira has her own Path, complete and declared and hers to draw on whenever she wants it. What I have is closer to the shape of how it moves. When I need it, under the right kind of pressure, the shape works. When I try to use it deliberately, slowly, outside a fight, it works too, but clumsily — like tracing a letter I've watched someone else write a thousand times, close enough to be recognizable, not close enough to be mistaken for the original hand.*

He wrote, two nights later, after a session where he'd tried and failed to identify any conscious signal that preceded the borrowed movement arriving:

*Does Lira notice that I'm using her patterns? I don't think so. I'm not using them exactly — I'm using something that's mine that looks like them, the way a student's handwriting looks like their teacher's without being a forgery of it. But I don't actually know that's true. I'm inferring it from the fact that she hasn't said anything, and she says something about almost everything. Absence of comment isn't the same as absence of noticing. I need to be honest with myself about that distinction, because I want the first explanation to be true badly enough that I might be choosing it without enough evidence.*

He wrote, a week after that, in a hand slightly less careful than usual, the words coming faster than his usual deliberate pace:

*I need more examples. One might be coincidence, even with the notice confirming it wasn't. Two is a pattern, but a pattern of what — of watching translating into borrowing, or of something broader that watching just happens to be the trigger for? I don't know yet whether the mechanism is "I watch someone closely enough and something transfers" or "watching is only ever the first step, and there's a second condition I haven't identified yet that has to be present too." I need a second source. Not Lira again — a different person, a different Path, watched with the same intensity, to see if the transfer happens the same way or differently or not at all.*

This last entry sat with him longer than the others, because it implied something he hadn't fully let himself consider until he'd written it down: that if this was a general mechanism rather than something unique to Lira, then everyone he'd ever watched closely — Renn, Dessa, Sarel, Talis, the woman doing stance drills in the practitioners' quarter on his first full day in Ardenmere — might have left something behind in him, waiting, unconfirmed, for the right pressure to surface it. The thought was, depending on the hour he had it, either the most exciting idea he'd had since Weaver's Row or the most unsettling one, and some nights it managed to be both at once.

He decided, three days after writing it, that a hypothesis untested was just an interesting sentence, and went looking for a second source.

He chose Kestrel — a fighter he hadn't fought yet, but had watched twice from the crowd, whose Force Path had a signature he'd been quietly cataloguing without any real purpose beyond the general habit of cataloguing everything. He spent an entire circuit day doing nothing but watching Kestrel train, close enough to note the rhythm of his breathing between combinations, far enough back not to be noticed doing it, and that evening, alone in the courtyard behind Torvin's, tried to summon whatever the watching might have left behind the way he'd learned to summon the Wind-adjacent step — reaching for the same texture of possibility that had answered him after Lira.

Nothing came. Not the clean absence of the Arbiter itself, which had a particular finality to it, but a different kind of nothing — an unfinished quality, like reaching for a word that was almost on the tip of his tongue and refusing to arrive.

He tried again the following week, after a second full day of deliberate watching. Still nothing usable — though this time, in the middle of a sparring session with Lira two days later, his stance shifted for half a heartbeat into something that wasn't quite Wind-adjacent and wasn't quite anything he recognized either, gone before he could examine it, too brief to be sure he hadn't imagined it entirely.

*Inconclusive,* he wrote, that night, with the discipline of someone refusing to let hope inflate an ambiguous result into a confirmed one. *Either two days of watching isn't enough — Lira took months, not days, and I may have simply skipped the accumulation phase and expected results too early — or Kestrel's Force Path doesn't transfer the way Lira's Wind Path does, for reasons I don't understand yet. Or the half-second shift in the sparring session was real and I need more data before I can call it anything. I don't like inconclusive results. I'm going to have to get better at tolerating them, because I suspect this entire subject is going to produce a great many of them before it produces anything certain.*

---

Lira noticed the change in him before he said anything about its cause, which did not surprise him, because Lira noticed most things.

"You've been somewhere else the last few weeks," she said, one morning, midway through a drill she'd clearly stopped paying full attention to in order to say this. "Not distracted exactly. More like you're running two conversations at once and only giving me half of one of them."

"I've been thinking about something."

"I gathered that much on my own." She studied him, arms crossed, the patient attention she brought to a problem she'd decided was worth waiting out rather than forcing. "You going to tell me, or is this a Cael-shaped mystery I have to solve myself, the way you solved the notice board?"

He'd rehearsed this moment more than once, lying awake, and every version he'd rehearsed had sounded either too small or too strange out loud. He tried anyway, because two weeks of not telling her had started to feel less like patience and more like a debt accumulating interest.

"I think I've been picking things up. From watching people. Not just information — actual capability. Small pieces of it." He watched her face carefully as he said it, the way he watched everything, cataloguing the sequence of her reaction in real time: surprise first, quick and genuine; then something more considering, the recalibration he'd seen from her before, the day he first told her what he was; then, underneath both of those, something that looked almost like relief, though he wasn't certain enough of the read to trust it yet.

"That's what happened during the Dessa fight," she said, slowly, working it out loud rather than asking. "And the sparring session, months ago. The footwork that wasn't yours."

"Yes."

"Whose is it? Whose have you been picking up?"

This was the part he'd rehearsed most and trusted least. "Some of it's yours," he said, plainly, because plain was the only version that felt honest. "I don't know how much. I don't fully understand the mechanism. But I've had a confirmed fragment for two weeks now, and it's Wind-adjacent, and you're the person whose movement I've watched most closely, for longest, of anyone in Ardenmere."

He waited for her reaction with an unfamiliar vulnerability — not fear exactly, closer to the sensation of showing someone unfinished work and being unable to predict whether they'd see the potential in it or only the roughness.

Lira was quiet for a long moment, turning it over with the same careful attention she gave a new opponent's tell. "Okay," she said, finally. "That's — genuinely strange, Cael. I want to be clear that I think that's strange, in case you were worried I'd pretend it wasn't." A pause. "But it doesn't feel bad. I thought it might, hearing it, and it doesn't. It feels like — I don't know. Like something of mine did some good in the world without my permission, and I'm finding out about it after the fact instead of before, which is a strange way to learn you've been generous, but not an unwelcome one."

"You're not upset."

"Should I be?"

"I don't know. I've been assuming you would be, which is part of why I waited so long to tell you."

Lira considered this, and something in her expression softened into the specific directness that had made their first conversation, months ago, the most honest one he'd had about his classification with anyone outside Hesk. "I spent four months at an academy that punished me for demonstrating a better technique than the one they wanted taught," she said. "I know exactly what it feels like to have something of yours treated as a threat instead of a contribution. I'm not going to do that to you. Especially not for something you didn't even choose — you didn't steal this from me, Cael. You just spent months paying more attention to me than anyone ever has, and something about you is built to turn attention like that into something usable. That's not theft. I don't fully understand what it is. But it's not that."

He didn't have an immediate answer for this. He filed it instead, the way he filed the things too large to respond to properly in the moment, and let the silence between them settle into something comfortable rather than strained.

"Thank you," he said, eventually, which felt inadequate and was, he'd decided, the correct size of inadequate for a thing this large.

"Don't thank me. I didn't do anything except not be unreasonable about it, which is a genuinely low bar and I'm slightly insulted you expected me to clear it with difficulty." But she was almost smiling when she said it, and something in her posture had eased that he hadn't quite registered as tense until it wasn't anymore.

"Can I ask you something," she said, after a moment, in a different register — less certain than her usual directness, which was rare enough that it caught his attention immediately.

"Of course."

"Does it feel like anything? Having it. Using it." She was watching him carefully now, the way she watched an opponent's first exchange, gathering information before committing to a read. "I ask because I've spent my whole life having a Path that's just — mine. Declared, certain, no question about where it came from or what it costs to use. I don't actually know what it's like to have something that arrived a different way."

Cael considered the question with the same seriousness she'd asked it. "It feels borrowed," he said, finally. "Not in a bad way. More like — you know how a coat that's actually yours fits differently than one you've borrowed, even if it's the right size? It's like that, except the coat is starting to fit better than it should, given how recently I got it. I don't know if that's because it's becoming more mine, or because I'm getting better at wearing borrowed things generally. I don't have enough data yet to tell the difference."

"That's either a very good metaphor or a very strange one."

"Can't it be both?"

"Everything with you can be both. I've stopped being surprised by it." She said this with real fondness, the kind that had crept into her voice gradually over the months without either of them announcing when it had arrived, and the training resumed a few minutes later with a new, unspoken ease between them that Cael noticed and didn't write down, because some things were true enough to keep rather than record.

---

The high-profile bout came up two days later, delivered by Lira with the barely-contained excitement she reserved for genuinely good news.

"There's a match being arranged," she said, finding him at the notice board — his notice board, though he'd stopped thinking of it that way months ago, once it had become simply a place he checked rather than a place he'd discovered. "Bigger than anything you've fought. A Bronze-tier practitioner, from outside the district, looking for interesting opponents to test himself against before some official evaluation of his own. High-purse exhibition. Circuit points on top of the coin, which matters more than the coin does, honestly, given where you're trying to get to."

"Who's arranging it?"

"Vell, mostly, through channels she doesn't usually bother explaining to anyone below assessed-Copper — which tells you something about how seriously she's started taking you." Lira leaned against the board, watching his face for the reaction she was clearly expecting and, from her expression, not entirely getting. "Word about you has been moving outside Ardenmere for weeks now, apparently. Circuit gossip travels faster than either of us gave it credit for. This is the first time it's turned into an actual opportunity instead of just talk."

"You think I should enter."

"I think it's exactly the kind of fight that changes what people believe about you faster than four more Copper-tier wins would. I also think it's genuinely dangerous, and I'm not going to pretend the second thing doesn't matter just because the first thing is exciting." She sat on the low wall beside the board, the same wall, he realized, where he'd once watched her train through a stance sequence before either of them had said a word to each other. "Bronze-tier is a real gap. Not an unbridgeable one — you've closed bigger gaps than people expected already — but real. I want you to go in with a plan, not with momentum."

"What's his name?"

"Feryn. Bronze Rank 2. Nobody local's fought him — he's not from Ardenmere, he's passing through on some circuit-adjacent tour of his own, testing himself against unfamiliar opponents before an evaluation of his own next season. Vell says his Path does something with force and pressure, sustained rather than sharp — not a cutting style, a crushing one." She said this with the care of someone relaying secondhand information honestly rather than dressing it up, careful not to promise more certainty than she actually had. "That's most of what anyone knows. He's good enough that people who've watched him spar describe it more like weather than fighting."

"That's not very much to build a plan from."

"No. It's not." She didn't apologize for the gap, which he appreciated — false reassurance would have been worse than an honest shortfall. "Which is exactly why I think three weeks matters more here than it did for Dessa or Corbin. You're not walking in with two weeks of observed bouts this time. You're walking in with secondhand descriptions and whatever you can learn about Pressure-adjacent Paths generally, from anyone in this district who's ever fought one."

"How long until the match is set?"

"Three weeks, if you want it. Less, if you don't need that long."

Cael thought about this the way he thought about everything worth thinking about carefully — turning it over, checking it from angles he hadn't considered yet, resisting the pull toward an answer that felt good rather than an answer that was actually sound. Three weeks was enough time to watch the opponent, if the opponent fought publicly anywhere in that window. It was not enough time to build the kind of exhaustive pattern-map he'd built for Dessa, for Renn, for anyone he'd genuinely understood before facing them.

"Who else is entering, that I could watch fight in the meantime?"

Lira blinked, then laughed — a short, surprised sound, more delighted than mocking. "That's it? That's your whole response to 'do you want to fight a Bronze-tier professional in three weeks'? Not 'am I ready,' not 'is this too soon' — just, who can I study first?"

"Is that not the correct question?"

"It's exactly the correct question. It's just not the question most fourteen-year-olds ask." She was still smiling, but something more serious had settled underneath it. "You're fourteen, Cael. I don't say that to talk you out of it. I say it because I want you to actually hear it, once, out loud, before you commit — because you have a genuinely bad habit of treating your own age like a fact that doesn't apply to the calculation, and it does apply. It just doesn't have to be the deciding factor."

"So I need three more weeks."

"You need three more weeks, and you need to spend every one of them the way you spent the last six — which, for the record, is the only reason I'm not talking you out of this entirely." She stood, brushing off her trousers, already moving toward the practitioners' quarter with the brisk energy she brought to a problem she'd decided to help solve rather than merely observe. "Come on. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly. First thing: I want a list of every fighter within two days' travel who's ever gone three exchanges with a Bronze-tier opponent and survived. We start there."

---

The list, once they'd built it, was shorter than either of them had hoped and more useful than Cael had expected. Three names, gathered over two days of careful, unhurried questions asked around the practitioners' quarter, the registry-free market, and once, awkwardly, of Vell herself, who answered with more patience than her usual manner suggested she had to spare.

The first name led nowhere — a fighter who'd left Ardenmere for the coast six months prior, according to everyone who remembered him, and couldn't be reached in any useful timeframe. The second name belonged to a woman who'd survived a Bronze-tier bout by losing decisively in the first exchange and had, by her own cheerful admission, learned nothing from the experience worth passing on except "don't." The third name was the one that mattered: a retired Copper-tier instructor two districts over who'd genuinely studied Pressure-adjacent Paths as a specialty, and who agreed, after Lira's persistent and eventually successful negotiating, to spend an afternoon with Cael in exchange for a modest fee and what Cael suspected was simple curiosity about the [SHATTERED] boy people had started talking about.

The afternoon was, in Cael's estimation, worth considerably more than the fee. The instructor — a spare, precise woman named Corvane who spoke about technique the way Hesk spoke about structural calculations, with an economy that assumed the listener could fill in what wasn't said — walked him through the general shape of how Pressure Paths generated and released force: not a single strike but a building, sustained application, harder to read because it didn't have the single clean tell of a Blade or Force practitioner's committed swing.

"The mistake everyone makes against Pressure," Corvane told him, sketching a rough diagram in the dirt with a stick, "is watching for the moment it releases. By the time you see the release, you're already inside its effective range. You have to watch the buildup instead — the specific tension in how they hold ground before they commit to generating anything at all. It's slower to read, but it gives you more warning, if you catch it early enough to matter."

Cael copied the diagram into his notebook, added three pages of notes to the log that evening, and read Corvane's description back to himself so many times over the following days that it started to feel less like secondhand information and more like something he'd learned firsthand — which was, he understood, the entire point of the exercise, and exactly the kind of patient, unglamorous preparation that had gotten him this far in the first place.

---

He wrote a letter to Hesk that night as well, shorter than his usual letters but more direct than most of them, the kind of directness that came easier now than it had months ago, back when every honest sentence about his own condition had felt like it cost something to produce.

*Told Lira everything. All of it — the fragment, where I think it came from, the whole strange shape of it. She didn't flinch. I keep waiting for someone to flinch, the way half of Denvash flinched, and I'm starting to think that's a Denvash problem, not a universal one. There's a fight coming in three weeks, bigger than anything I've done yet, against someone genuinely dangerous. I'm not going to pretend I'm not nervous about it. I'm also not going to pretend I'm not looking forward to finding out what I actually am when the stakes are large enough to make the answer matter. Write when you can. I think about the workshop more than I expected to.*

He sealed it, set it aside for the morning post, and turned, finally, to the private entry that had been forming in his head all evening — the one that mattered less for its content than for the simple fact of writing it down, the way some thoughts only became fully real once they existed outside his own head.

That night, Cael added a final entry to the private section of the log before turning to the tactical work the coming weeks would require — the growing list of names, the questions still needing answers, the specific, disciplined optimism of someone who had learned, the hard way, that hope without preparation behind it was just another word for wishing.

*Three weeks. A Bronze-tier opponent. Lira knows what I am now, both parts of it — the classification and the thing growing underneath the classification that doesn't have a name yet — and she didn't flinch from either one. I keep expecting the people who matter to me to eventually run out of patience with how strange this all is. She hasn't yet. Hesk hasn't yet. I'm starting to wonder if the running-out-of-patience part was something I invented to protect myself from a disappointment that was never actually coming, the way you brace for a hit that isn't thrown.*

*Either way. Three weeks. Work to do.*

He closed the notebook, and for the first time in longer than he could easily remember, fell asleep without needing to argue himself into it first.

# Chapter 2 — Kindling

The certification office on Weaver's Row opened at eight in the morning. Cael arrived at seven forty-five, because being early was not something he could stop doing and he had long since stopped trying.

The building was institutional — grey stone facing, the Compact Registry seal above the door, windows that let in light without committing to it. It had been a dye-house at some point, and the faint smell of whatever the process had left in the walls mixed with the newer smell of official paper and something close to machine oil that Cael couldn't place and didn't investigate further.

There were three other people in the waiting area: two girls who appeared to be twins, both his age, sitting close together and not talking; and a boy of about fourteen with the slightly glazed expression of someone who had been thinking about this for so long that the actual event had become difficult to access. He was holding a registration slip and rereading it without reading it.

The waiting area had benches and a counter with a small window behind which an official was doing paperwork. The window had a number dispenser. Cael took a number. He was seven. The sisters were five and six. The glazed boy was eight.

He sat down and opened his notebook to a blank page. He had nothing in particular to write. He wrote the date and the time and then left the rest of the page empty, which was not his usual practice and which he noted and did not examine too closely.

There was a clock on the wall above the counter, the kind with a face designed for reading at a distance, and Cael found himself watching it more than he watched anything else in the room. Not because he needed to know the time. Because it was the one object in the waiting area doing something predictable — moving forward, steadily, without opinions about anyone in the room — and predictable was, at that moment, in short supply.

The twins whispered to each other, occasionally, in the low register of people who'd agreed in advance not to be loud about being nervous. One of them kept turning a bracelet around her wrist — three rotations, pause, three rotations — a habit so precise Cael suspected she didn't know she was doing it. He didn't write it down. He filed it, the way he filed most things, in the part of his memory that didn't need paper.

The glazed boy sat very still, which Cael understood, on some level, to be its own kind of motion — the taut stillness of someone holding very carefully onto a version of themselves they weren't sure would survive the next twenty minutes.

The sisters went in together, which was apparently allowed if both were Kindling the same week. They came back out twelve minutes later, each holding a certification document, with expressions that were very close to identical — a combination of relief and the look people have when something significant has happened and they haven't processed it yet. One of them was crying, quietly, in the unbothered way of someone crying from relief rather than grief. Their mother, who had been waiting outside, met them at the door with an expression that answered a question Cael hadn't known he was asking: *this is what it looks like when it goes the way you hoped.*

The glazed boy went next. He came out after eight minutes with a Force Path certification and the look of someone who had expected Force Path and was trying to feel things about it now that it was official. He paused at the doorway for a moment, glanced back at the empty examination room as if checking it was real, and then left without acknowledging anyone else in the waiting area — not rudely, just entirely elsewhere, the way people get when a thing they've spent a year imagining finally becomes a thing that already happened.

Cael was alone in the waiting area for the first time that morning. He looked at the clock. He looked at the number dispenser. He looked at his own hands, which were, he noted clinically, not quite steady, and made a decision to stop noting things about his own hands.

Cael was called in.

---

The examination room was smaller than he'd expected — a desk, two chairs, a filing cabinet, and in the center of the room, a plain circle inlaid in the floor, worn smooth by fourteen years' worth of feet standing exactly there. The walls held nothing decorative: a framed copy of the Compact Registry charter, a shelf of identical grey folders arranged by some system Cael couldn't guess at, and a single window that let in the same committed-but-not-quite light as every other window in the building. It smelled, up close, more strongly of the old dye-house than the waiting area had — a mineral, slightly bitter note underneath the paper and ink, the kind of smell that had probably been in the walls longer than the certification office had existed.

The certification officer was a woman of approximately fifty, with close-cropped hair going to grey and the manner of someone who had administered the same procedure so many times that she had reduced it to its essential parts. Efficient. Not unkind. Her name plate said WARDEN-ADJUNCT PELLIN. She did not look up immediately when he entered, finishing a line of writing first, and Cael understood this not as rudeness but as its own kind of courtesy — she was giving him a few extra seconds to arrive fully in the room before she required anything of him.

"Name and registration," she said.

"Caelen Hesk-ward. Registration 41-7843-V." He had memorized the number the previous week.

She found the file, confirmed the information, made a mark. "Your Kindling assessment. Standard process: your Arbiter will resolve here, in front of the registry ledger." She tapped a flat brass plate set into the desk beside her — an unremarkable object, the kind of thing Cael wouldn't have looked at twice. "I record what it tells us. The process takes between three and fifteen minutes. Any questions before we begin?"

"What happens after fifteen minutes?"

She looked at him. "It doesn't take fifteen minutes."

"But if it did."

A brief pause. "Extended evaluations are rare. They simply mean the process is being thorough." She said this with the neutrality of someone who had been trained to say exactly this sentence.

"How rare?"

"Extremely." She gestured toward the circle in the floor. "If you're ready."

He was ready. He had been ready for a year and also completely unprepared and these two things were, apparently, compatible.

He'd read the procedural summary enough times to recite it: *the practitioner stands within the marked circle. No external device is required — every practitioner's Arbiter is their own, dormant since birth, and Kindling is the moment it wakes.* Nobody had ever been able to tell him what that would actually feel like, only that it would.

He stepped into the circle.

Something woke.

It didn't arrive from outside him — that was the strangeness of it, the thing none of the eleven descriptions in his notebook had prepared him for. It gathered from somewhere behind his sternum, a small point of light he felt more than saw, resolving slowly into a shape only he could perceive — hovering somewhere just past the edge of his vision no matter which way he turned his eyes, the way a held breath sits somewhere you can't quite point to. Pellin, three feet away, showed no sign of seeing anything at all. Whatever this was, it belonged entirely to him.

The sigil made a sound that was less like a sound and more like the memory of a sound — a resonance, brief, that he felt at the base of his sternum and did not hear with his ears.

Then it was quiet.

He counted. He had not intended to count but he found himself counting, the way you count during something uncomfortable without deciding to.

One. Two. Three.

He watched the certification officer's face from the corner of his eye, because the certification officer's face was information. Her attention wasn't on him — it was on the brass plate at her elbow, which she'd explained would light the moment his Arbiter's result reached the registry.

He was aware, distantly, of his own heartbeat, of the pressure of standing exactly where the procedural summary had told him to stand, of the light gathering somewhere he couldn't quite look at directly no matter how he angled his eyes. He thought, absurdly, of the eleven descriptions in his notebook — *sunlight, a door opening, remembering something you never knew* — and understood, even before the eleventh second arrived, that none of them were going to make his own list.

Four. Five.

Her expression was pleasant and patient and the particular type of vacant that professionals adopt when they are doing something routine and do not want to project boredom at the person they are working with.

Six. Seven.

The pleasant patience shifted, slightly, toward something that was not exactly attention but adjacent to it. The plate at her elbow, Cael understood without looking at it directly, had not lit when it should have.

Eight.

She sat up straighter. This was small — half an inch, maybe — and not dramatic, but it was real, and Cael noticed it the way he noticed things that were small and real.

Nine.

Her hand moved to the filing cabinet drawer, not quite opening it, resting on the handle.

Ten. Eleven.

He had been counting for eleven seconds. He was quite certain it had been eleven seconds.

Then the Arbiter spoke.

The word it spoke was not a word in the conventional sense — it was more like language stripped of everything except its structure, the shape of a designation without the warmth of how it was normally delivered. He had heard others describe receiving their Path classification as a sound like a door opening, like music, like suddenly remembering something forgotten. He had heard someone describe it as the word arriving in your chest before your ears.

What Cael heard was a word that sounded like it was arriving from somewhere very far away — clear and complete and absolutely certain, the way a geological fact was certain.

**[SHATTERED].**

The brass plate at Pellin's elbow lit, finally, eleven seconds late, the registry text resolving into the same word Cael had just heard — and the certification officer's face did not change. This was, Cael thought in the half-second before everything else, extremely professional of her. Her face did not change and her hand on the filing cabinet drawer opened it and she extracted a different form — one from further back in the file, a tab on it that indicated it was used infrequently — and she began to fill it out with the same efficiency she had applied to everything else.

Her voice, when she spoke, was entirely level.

"Your classification has resolved," she said. "Please wait a moment."

She did not say what the classification was. This was not because she was being unkind — he understood this even then, in that room, with eleven seconds of Arbiter silence sitting in his chest — but because the word had already arrived. They both knew it. Saying it again would have been for form's sake, and she was not a form-sake person.

She finished filling out the form. She picked up the telephone receiver on her desk and dialed three digits. She waited. She said: "Pellin at Weaver's Row. Non-standard result." A pause. "Yes. I'll proceed." She hung up.

"There's a secondary consultation room," she said to Cael. "I'll need you to wait there. It won't be long."

He followed her down a short corridor to a room that contained two chairs and a window looking out at a grey interior courtyard. The window had been left open slightly. Outside, there was a tree that had grown at an angle, as if it had started one direction and then reconsidered. The corridor itself was longer than he'd expected for a building of this size, lined with more of the identical grey folders he'd noticed in the examination room, and he wondered, walking it, how many of those folders held a story that had gone the way this morning had gone, and how many held the ordinary kind, the kind that ended with a certificate and a walk home and nothing more complicated than that.

She left. The door closed. He heard her footsteps retreat down the corridor, unhurried, the same pace she'd used walking him there.

He sat. He looked at the tree. He counted how many branches were growing toward the light and how many had given up on that and grown sideways instead. Eleven toward the light, he decided, though he wasn't entirely confident in the count and didn't feel like doing it twice to check. The room was quiet the way a room built for waiting is quiet — no clock this time, nothing to measure the minutes against except his own attention, which had never felt less reliable.

He was fourteen years old. He had, forty seconds ago, received a classification that the Compact Registry described as deprecated and associated with practitioner safety risk — one of only four instances recorded in the registry's entire history.

He felt, specifically, like the tree. Like something that had started in one direction and was now figuring out whether there was another one.

---

He waited for thirty-seven minutes. He knew this because he counted those too, though less systematically.

At the thirty-seven-minute mark, the door opened and Hesk came in.

His grandfather's face was completely still. Not closed — Hesk never closed his expression the way some people did, pulling a wall up — but still, the way water is still before something comes through it. He had been preparing this stillness for a long time. Cael did not know how long until later, but he could see, even then, that the stillness was prepared and not spontaneous.

Hesk sat in the other chair.

He looked at Cael for a moment. Then he said: "All right."

"All right," Cael agreed.

"You're not hurt."

"No."

Hesk nodded. He was looking at Cael the way he looked at instrument housings he'd opened up in the workshop — assessing for stress fractures, testing load-bearing points. Not clinically. With care that expressed itself through precision.

"The officer told you the classification," he said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"What did it say?"

Cael said the word. He had not planned to say it so simply, but it came out as what it was — a word, one word, specific. He watched Hesk receive it.

"Okay," Hesk said. Then: "You've read the registry entry."

"Yes."

"How much of it."

"Enough."

Hesk was quiet for a moment. He was not, Cael thought, surprised. Not the way you're surprised when something unexpected happens. He was the way you are when something you prepared for arrives and preparation turns out not to help as much as you thought it would.

"There are things I should have told you," Hesk said.

"Yes." Cael looked at the tree through the window. "When did you know?"

"Before your preliminary assessment." A pause. "There were signs. Smaller ones. Before that."

"How much before."

Hesk didn't answer immediately. When he did, he said: "A few years." Then: "I'm sorry. I made a choice that seemed correct. It may have been wrong."

Cael let that sit a moment, watching the tree outside the window bend slightly in a wind he couldn't feel from inside the room. "A few years," he repeated. "You've been watching me for a few years, and you never once —"

"Never once told you," Hesk agreed, before Cael could finish it himself. "No. That's fair to be angry about. I'd rather you be angry about it directly than carry it quietly, if it's all the same to you."

"I'm not — " Cael stopped, because he wasn't entirely sure what he was, other than tired, and full, in a way that didn't have room left over for anger even if he'd wanted it. "I don't know what I am right now."

"That's a reasonable place to be," Hesk said. "You don't have to sort it today."

The choice — Cael could reconstruct it from here, now, in this room: don't tell Cael it might be [SHATTERED] so he doesn't carry that weight into a Kindling that might resolve normally. Keep the preparation internal. Keep the worry separate. Don't make something real before it is.

He understood the logic. He did not feel good about the logic, but he understood it.

"Tell me now," Cael said. "What you know."

Hesk told him.

What he said in that room: that he didn't know, for certain, what [SHATTERED] meant — nobody living did, not really, and anyone who told Cael otherwise with confidence was telling him more than they actually knew. What he believed, for whatever a belief was worth against a word like that: that the name was registry language, chosen by people who'd been frightened of what they didn't understand, and that fear naming a thing didn't make the fear correct. It might mean broken. It might mean something the system simply had no word for yet. He didn't know which. He wasn't going to pretend to Cael that he did.

What he said: that the abilities would come, were probably already beginning, but would not arrive in the way of a standard Path — no declaration, no tier-progression, no clear advancement markers. Whatever Cael could do, he would have to find by doing, not by being told.

What he said: that there were practical problems. Registry problems. The Compact kept records. The record would follow Cael, and people who held certain positions in the Compact would eventually see it, and the response to that would depend on who they were.

What he did not say, in that room: that the last four times [SHATTERED] had appeared in the registry, three of the practitioners had not outlived the documentation by more than a few weeks. This was a thing Cael would find in the historical records eventually. Hesk knew this. He chose to give Cael the practical information first and let him find the rest in his own time.

This was a second choice Cael did not know how to evaluate yet.

"Don't let anyone see you move," Hesk said, quietly, at the end. "Not until you know what you're doing."

"I don't know what that means."

"You will." He said it the way he said the structural load calculations in the workshop: not performance, just accuracy. He had observed something. He was reporting it. "Your body is going to learn before your mind does. That's the nature of it. When it happens — when you find yourself doing something you didn't decide to do — don't show it to anyone. Not yet."

Cael looked at him. "Has that already happened?"

Hesk met his eyes. "Has it?"

He thought about it. The notebook, filled with observations he hadn't consciously decided to start keeping. The close attention he paid to how people moved, the third exchange in the circuit match, the dropped stance — things he had been cataloguing without knowing he was looking for them.

"I don't know," he said. "Possibly."

Hesk nodded. "Then start paying attention to what you don't decide."

---

The walk home was the same walk it had always been — Weaver's Row to the main throughway, north along the outer district canal, left at the corner where the vendor sold transit schedules to cities Cael had never been to. The morning had gotten warmer. People were going about their business with the indifference of a district where everyone was doing something that required their attention.

Two blocks from home, they passed Alis.

She was on the other side of the street. She looked up, the way people look up when they register familiar movement without consciously scanning. She saw Cael. Something in her expression shifted — not pity, which would have been worse — but something more like *I see what happened*, which was harder to know what to do with.

She raised one hand. A minimal acknowledgment. Not waving. Just: *I see you.*

He raised his in return.

He did not know yet what exactly she was seeing. He assumed she had some information — Kindling results moved through neighborhoods — but not specific information. He assumed what she saw was a fourteen-year-old walking home from his Kindling with his grandfather, on the wrong side of whatever she had hoped for. He assumed she was extending the same courtesy he had extended to her: acknowledgment without demand.

He thought about what she had said on Weaver's Row. *Better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted.*

He added a note in his notebook that evening: *She was not wrong. It is better when it's done. What it does not feel like is done.*

---

That night, Hesk made tea.

He didn't make dinner — or rather, dinner happened at some point, something from the cold box, and neither of them particularly attended to it. The tea was what mattered. The tea was the thing that happened while they sat in the kitchen and the evening came down and the light changed, the ritual of it more important than anything either of them said while it steeped. Hesk made it the careful way tonight, the way he usually reserved for the handful of evenings a year that seemed to call for it — measuring the leaves properly instead of guessing, waiting the full time instead of the abbreviated version they used on ordinary nights. Cael watched him do it and understood, without being told, that this was Hesk's version of marking the occasion. Neither of them was going to say the word *goodbye* tonight, not really, not with the actual leaving still a day away. But the tea knew what was happening even if the conversation didn't.

Hesk told him about the expulsion notice — that it would likely arrive within two to three days, registered post, the standard procedure for non-standard classifications. He told him that his own standing would be affected by Cael's continued residence, which Cael had read and had not needed to be told, but being told was different from reading.

"Ardenmere," Hesk said, eventually.

"You mentioned it." Cael had looked it up in the public reference section six months ago, when he was trying to figure out what his options might be, depending on what happened. "The Unranked District is large."

"Large enough to disappear into, if you need to."

"I don't want to disappear."

"No." Hesk looked at his tea. "That's not what I mean. I mean large enough that your registration won't be checked at the gate. Anywhere the Compact has active enforcement, they'll pull your registry on entry. Ardenmere's Unranked District is — unofficial. They tolerate it because it keeps certain kinds of practitioners contained."

"Or they think they do."

Hesk almost smiled. "Yes."

Cael wrote *Ardenmere* in his notebook. He wrote *Unranked District — large, no gate registry checks.* He wrote *Hesk knows things about this that he hasn't explained yet* and then crossed that out because writing it felt like accusation and he didn't mean it as accusation. He meant it as a note: there was more to learn, and Hesk was not the only source of it.

He looked up from the notebook to find Hesk watching him write, an expression on his face that Cael couldn't fully place — something between pride and worry, the two so tangled together that separating them seemed pointless. "You're already cataloguing it," Hesk said. "Good. That's the right instinct. Write down everything, even the things that don't seem important yet. You won't always know which ones are important until later, and by then you'll wish you'd written them down."

"Is that in the notebook you're giving me?"

"Some version of it." A faint, tired smile. "I've had a long time to think about what I'd tell you, if it came to this. Turns out most of it fits on not very many pages."

"I'll give you some things before you go," Hesk said. "Practical things. A notebook." He paused. "I've been working on it for a while."

"I have a notebook."

"A different one." He was not looking at Cael. "Something I should have given you when there was time to explain it properly. We'll have the morning."

Cael looked at his hands. Then at the window, which reflected the kitchen's lamp-light back at them in the way of windows after dark.

"Hesk."

"Yes."

"Whatever you've been preparing for — the provisions you looked into, the notes you wrote — it helped." He said this because it was true, and also because the alternative was to say that it hadn't, which would have been easier and less accurate. "I'm not — I'm not telling you it was right. I'm telling you it helped."

Hesk was quiet for long enough that the evening sounds from outside — a cart on the pavement, a dog, the mechanical sigh of the canal gate somewhere to the north — filled the room between them.

"All right," he said, finally. "Get some sleep."

Cael went to bed.

He lay in the dark and looked at the ceiling — the same ceiling, the water stain in the northeast corner, the same tired off-white. He had come home. The house was the same. He was not the same in a way he couldn't fully articulate yet.

He thought about the Arbiter's silence — eleven seconds of evaluation that had produced a word nobody had expected, or that one person had expected and had kept separate. He thought about what Hesk had said: *your body is going to learn before your mind does.*

He thought: what is it learning?

He fell asleep before he had an answer. He would not have an answer for a long time. But the question had arrived, and that was, he would come to understand, how it started.

# Chapter 10 — The Weight of [SHATTERED]

He waited four days before he tried it — the thing he'd been avoiding since the certification office, the thing he'd told himself he wasn't ready for and had slowly stopped believing.

Four days was long enough that he'd started noticing the shape of his own avoidance, the way you notice a limp only once you've been favoring the leg for a while without admitting it. He'd told himself, on day one, that he needed to settle into Ardenmere first — find his feet, in Hesk's phrase, before he went looking for anything else. On day two, the excuse had been the summons anxiety he hadn't yet developed, the general newness of everything, the fact that he hadn't slept well since the district gate. By day three the excuses had run out and been replaced by something closer to the truth: he was afraid of the answer, whichever answer it turned out to be. Afraid of nothing happening. More afraid, in some ways he hadn't fully examined, of something happening that he didn't understand.

He waited until Torvin's boarding house was quiet, past midnight, the other four cots in his room occupied by their usual sleepers — the wheezing man nearest the door, the two brothers who talked in their sleep and never remembered it, the young woman who'd arrived the week before and kept her boots on even in bed, as if she expected to need to leave in a hurry — and went to the small shared courtyard behind the building where he could be alone without needing to explain why.

The courtyard was little more than a gap between buildings that someone, long ago, had decided to leave unbuilt: packed dirt, a low wall on two sides, a single scraggly tree that had clearly given up trying to grow much taller and settled for growing wide instead. At this hour it belonged entirely to him — no laundry lines, no children, no one boiling water over a low fire the way the courtyard sometimes hosted during the day. Just the cold, and the sound of the district settling into its late hours, and the particular quality of dark that came from being somewhere with no lamps at all, only whatever light leaked from shuttered windows above.

He stood still in the middle of the packed dirt, in roughly the spot he'd chosen because it felt, arbitrarily, like the center of something. He thought about the marked circle in the certification office, about the sensation of something gathering behind his sternum, resolving into a shape only he could perceive. He tried to summon it the way — he assumed — other practitioners could summon a status check on their own Arbiter, the way Lira had once described checking hers between fights, casually, the way you'd check the time.

Nothing happened.

He adjusted his stance, which was a foolish thing to do, since nothing about the Kindling had required a particular stance, but foolish or not it felt like the kind of thing his body wanted to try, the same instinct that made you press harder on a locked door instead of trying the handle a different way. He closed his eyes. He thought about the eleven seconds in the examination room, tried to reconstruct the exact texture of that resonance at his sternum, the way it had gathered before it spoke.

Nothing.

He tried again, more deliberately this time, reaching for the same internal sensation he remembered from the certification office — the resonance at his sternum, the point of light that had gathered and grown before speaking. He held the reach longer than felt reasonable, the way you hold your breath past the point of comfort just to see how long you can manage it, daring his own body to prove him wrong about what wasn't there.

Nothing. Not silence exactly — silence would have implied something was there, declining to answer, the way Pellin's brass plate had waited a beat before lighting. This was closer to absence. A door he couldn't find the shape of, let alone knock on. He tried a third time, and a fourth, adjusting nothing about his approach because he had nothing to adjust — no technique to refine, no posture to correct, nothing but the same reach into the same dark, over and over, hoping repetition might succeed where the first attempt hadn't.

It didn't. Somewhere past the fifth or sixth attempt he stopped counting, which was, for him, its own kind of admission.

He stood in the dark courtyard for a long time after that, not trying anymore, just standing, letting the cold settle into his hands and a quiet dread settle into a place his usual detachment couldn't quite reach. He thought about Lira's easy description of checking her Arbiter — *like checking a ledger* — and about how casually she'd said it, the way you describe something so ordinary you've forgotten it's a privilege. He thought about the four names in the historical archive, about Hesk's careful non-answers, about the marked circle in the certification office and the eleven seconds that had started all of it.

He did not cry. He noted, with the same clinical detachment he applied to most things about himself, that he wanted to, and that the wanting was itself useful information, filed away with everything else he was learning to observe rather than simply feel. Eventually he went back to bed, cold through to the bone, and lay awake a while longer before sleep found him, uneasy and thin.

He dreamed, in the fractured, unmemorable way of dreams that don't survive contact with waking, of the certification office — not the classification itself, but the marked circle on the floor, worn smooth by fourteen years of feet that weren't his. In the dream he stood in it and nothing happened, the same nothing he'd just spent an hour failing to overcome, except in the dream the nothing had a texture, almost a weight, like standing in a doorway that led somewhere and refused, specifically, to open for him and no one else. He woke once in the night, briefly, disoriented, and lay there long enough to remember where he was — Ardenmere, Torvin's, the fifth cot by the window — before sleep pulled him back under.

He woke for good with the room already grey with early light, the wheezing man across from him snoring through the last stretch of his night, and lay still for a moment doing the arithmetic he did most mornings now: what he'd learned, what he still didn't know, what the day in front of him was likely to require. The courtyard sat behind him now as a fact rather than an open question — he had tried, thoroughly, honestly, and the door hadn't opened, and there was a kind of clarity in that even though the clarity wasn't the one he'd wanted. He got up before the others stirred, dressed quietly, and went to find Lira at the yard, because standing still with the question hadn't worked and he suspected, correctly as it turned out, that moving might do more good than stillness ever would.

---

He asked Lira the next morning, carefully, framing it as curiosity rather than what it actually was.

They were at the Cinder House yard early, ahead of even their usual first-light start, mist still sitting low over the packed dirt and the crows not yet arrived to work the previous night's scraps. Cael had slept badly, unsurprisingly, and had turned the question over so many times on the walk there that by the time he actually asked it, it came out more abruptly than he'd intended, cutting across whatever Lira had been about to say about the morning's drills.

"What does it look like when you check your Arbiter?"

She was midway through stretching, unbothered by the question in the way of someone who genuinely hasn't registered anything strange in it yet. "Like checking a ledger, more or less. You reach for it — it's hard to describe if you haven't done it, it's not really a physical motion, more like remembering something on purpose — and it opens. Shows you your current declaration, your rank, sometimes a note if there's been a recent shift in standing. Takes a few seconds. Why, are you thinking about what your evaluation's going to look like once it un-sticks itself?" She said this lightly, still stretching, still entirely unaware of the ground she was standing on.

"Does it always respond?"

"Of course. It always—" She stopped mid-sentence, the way a person stops when a casual question turns out to have a less casual reason behind it. She lowered the leg she'd been stretching and looked at him properly, her whole posture changing in the space of that one interrupted word — not alarm exactly, but a fast, total recalibration, the same one she brought to a fight when an opponent did something she hadn't trained against. "Why are you asking?"

He'd known, on some level, that this conversation would arrive eventually. He hadn't expected it to arrive because he'd initiated it, sloppily, at six in the morning, with insufficient preparation for what came next. He'd imagined, in the version of this conversation he'd rehearsed lying awake, that he would find some careful, oblique way into it — a question two steps removed from the actual question, something he could retreat from if her reaction went wrong. Instead he'd walked straight at it, with none of the patience he usually spent weeks building before he trusted himself to move.

"What does it look like when it doesn't respond?" he asked, instead of answering her question directly.

"It doesn't. It always responds. Unless—" And there it was: the sentence stopping again, this time because she'd arrived somewhere she hadn't expected to arrive, and Cael watched her do the thing he'd done in the archive three weeks ago — convert an abstract classification into a real, present fact standing in front of her.

"You know what I am," Cael said. It wasn't quite a question.

"[SHATTERED]." She said the word carefully, the way you handle something you've heard about but never actually held. "I looked it up. After the first day. I wanted to know what I'd walked up to at that notice board." She studied him, and there was nothing in her expression that resembled the reactions he'd catalogued from Denvash — no flinch, no careful blankness, no performed sympathy. Just a direct, considering attention. "You don't get an Arbiter you can check. That's what it means, isn't it? Not a bad Path. No Path the system recognizes at all."

"Yes."

"That's why you don't know things everyone else just — knows. Tier, rank, whether you're improving." She exhaled, processing. "I check mine most mornings, honestly, without thinking about it. It's like glancing at the sky to see what the weather's doing. I never once considered what it would be like to reach for that and just — not have it there. That's actually worse than I thought it meant."

"Is it."

"I don't know. Is it?" She wasn't performing the question — she genuinely wanted to know, the same directness that had made their first conversation the most honest one he'd had about his classification with anyone outside Hesk.

He thought about it, honestly, for the first time putting words to something he'd been carrying without examining. "It's worse in the sense that I don't have a map. Everyone else gets told, roughly, what they are and where they stand. I get nothing. I have to build the map myself, from evidence, without knowing if I'm even reading the evidence correctly, and there's no one to check my work against." He paused. "It's better in a sense too, though. Nobody can tell me what my ceiling is. Because nobody knows. Including me. There's no Arbiter sitting somewhere with a number already decided, waiting for me to catch up to it."

"That's either very freeing or completely terrifying."

"Both. At the same time. Most hours of most days."

Lira considered this with the seriousness she brought to genuine problems rather than performed ones, sitting with it for a moment, turning it over the way she turned over a fight afterward, looking for the part of it she hadn't fully understood yet. "Can I ask something that might be a stupid question?"

"You've never asked me a stupid question."

"There's a first time for everything." She picked at a loose thread on her sleeve, not quite looking at him, which he'd learned was her version of choosing words carefully. "When you were in the certification office. When it happened. Did it feel like anything was actually *wrong* with you? Or did it just feel like — nothing. Like static. Like a door not opening."

He thought about it honestly, the way her question deserved. "Neither, exactly. It felt complete. That's the strange part. It didn't feel like a malfunction. It felt like an answer, just not one anybody had a category for." He paused. "Nothing about it felt broken from the inside. Everyone else's reaction is what made it feel that way."

"That's not a stupid question, by the way. I take it back — that might be the most useful thing you've told me about it." She looked at him properly. "Because that's the thing that actually matters, isn't it? Not what the registry thinks it means. What it feels like from where you're standing."

"I don't fully know yet. That's most of what this is — trying to find out from the inside what everyone else insists on defining from the outside."

"Then we're doing the same thing," she said, "just with different starting points." She said it simply, without making more of it than it was, and somehow that made it land harder than if she'd tried.

---

She asked, eventually, what the registry notice had said — the expulsion document, the residential provisions, the language he'd read three times over water in the kitchen in Denvash. He hesitated before answering, not because he didn't trust her with it, but because handing someone a document like that felt like handing them a piece of the actual weight, and he'd carried it alone long enough that the carrying had become a habit he wasn't sure how to set down.

"You don't have to show me," she said, reading the hesitation correctly, as she seemed to read most things. "I'm not asking because I think I'm owed it."

"I know." He got it out of his bag anyway, folded into the back of his current notebook where it had lived since Denvash, the creases gone soft from being opened and closed more times than the document strictly required. "I think I want to. I just haven't shown it to anyone who wasn't already there when it arrived."

He gave it to her.

She read it in full, without comment, her expression giving away nothing until she reached the end — and then, before handing it back, she read it a second time, slower, the way Cael himself had reread it in the kitchen, the way you reread something you want to be sure you haven't misunderstood out of some private hope that you had.

"This is designed to make you disappear without anyone having to be the one who made you disappear," she said. "That's — I've read a lot of bureaucratic language. This is unusually careful. Someone thought hard about how to write this so nobody involved feels responsible for it." She tapped the line about Hesk's guild standing. "This part especially. They didn't order him to make you leave. They just made staying cost him something exact, and let him do the math himself. That's not an accident. That's someone who's done this before and knows exactly how much pressure a sentence needs to carry to do the job without ever sounding like a threat."

"That's what Hesk said. More or less."

"He's not wrong." She handed the document back and, for a moment, looked older than her fifteen years — not tired exactly, but weighted by something that Cael recognized, having carried a version of it himself since Weaver's Row. "The circuit doesn't care what the registry says," she said, finally. "That's not me being kind. It's just true. Vell doesn't check registrations. The Ledger doesn't care about tier classifications the Compact assigns. Out here, you're whatever you can demonstrate, and nothing else about you is anyone's business unless you make it their business."

Cael turned this over. Of everything anyone had told him since Weaver's Row — Hesk's careful honesty, the archive's cold statistics, the gate guard's averted eyes — this was, he realized, the single most useful sentence he'd been given. Not because it changed the registry, or the Compact, or the four names in the historical record. Because it named, precisely, the one space where none of that mattered.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it more completely than the two words could carry.

Lira studied him a moment, then did something unexpected — she reached over and, briefly, without ceremony, put her hand on his shoulder, the same shoulder Renn had bruised days before. Not a hug. Something more practical and, in its way, more significant: an acknowledgment that didn't need to be bigger than it was to mean what it meant.

"Come on," she said, stepping back, all business again. "You're still trying to check an Arbiter that isn't going to answer. Let's go do something that'll actually tell you what you're made of."

The walk to the Cinder House yard took the usual ten minutes, and neither of them filled it with talk — a comfortable quiet rather than an awkward one, the silence of two people who'd already said the thing that needed saying and didn't feel any pressure to say more just to fill the space. The morning market was starting to wake around them, stalls opening, the first vendors calling out prices to a street that wasn't quite listening yet. Cael found, walking through it, that the district looked slightly different than it had an hour before — not changed, exactly, just seen by someone who was carrying a little less alone than he had been at breakfast.

---

They trained through the morning, and it was, Cael understood partway through it, deliberately different from their usual sessions — not gentler, exactly, Lira didn't seem to know how to be gentle in a way that would have insulted them both, but shaped differently, aimed less at sharpening any one technique and more at simply putting his body through enough demanding, structured motion that his mind didn't have room left over to keep circling the courtyard and the dark and the door with no shape.

"Again," she said, after a combination he'd fumbled the footwork on. "Slower this time. You're thinking about your feet instead of trusting them."

"I am thinking about my feet. You told me to."

"I told you to think about your feet three weeks ago. You're allowed to stop now." She reset her stance, waiting. "That's the whole trick, by the way, in case nobody's told you. Eventually you stop thinking about the thing and your body just does it, and the day that happens is usually the day you stop being bad at it."

"Is that how it worked for you?"

"More or less. Took about a year, with an actual instructor and an actual training schedule and none of the — " she gestured vaguely at the world in general, at Ardenmere, at the whole shape of the past four months, "— complications you're currently dealing with. You're doing it faster than I did, for what that's worth. Doesn't mean it's easy. Just means you're either very talented or very motivated, and probably some of both."

They went through the combination again, and again, until his feet stopped needing his permission to move correctly, and there was something genuinely useful in that — not the technique itself, though the technique was useful too, but the absorbing focus the drilling demanded, a kind of thinking that left no room for anything else. Somewhere around the twentieth repetition, sweat starting to sting his eyes, he stopped noticing the counting entirely, which was, he understood only afterward, the actual point of the exercise — not the footwork itself but the mercy of a task that asked for his whole attention and gave him, in exchange, an hour where the Arbiter and the courtyard and the four names in the archive simply weren't the loudest thing in the room.

He understood, working through it, part of why Lira trained as often and as hard as she did. It wasn't only ambition. It was also, probably, its own kind of courtyard — a place to stand and reach for something and not have to think, for an hour at a time, about everything reaching couldn't fix.

Near the end of the session, breathing hard, sweat cooling in the morning air, she caught his eye across the yard.

"Better?"

He considered the question honestly, the way her questions generally required. "Yes. Not solved. Better."

"That's usually all training actually does," she said. "I wouldn't trust anyone who promised you more than that."

---

He ate dinner that evening at Torvin's communal kitchen table, alone — Lira had a second sparring commitment across the district, something informal with a Blade Path fighter she'd been meaning to test herself against — and found the solitude less heavy than it usually was. He turned the day over while he ate, cataloguing it the way he catalogued everything, and found himself returning, again and again, not to the failed attempts in the courtyard but to the weight of Lira's hand on his shoulder, brief and unceremonious and entirely without performance.

Later, in the narrow privacy of his cot, the other four sleepers settling into their usual rhythms around him, he took out his private log — the back section, the one that had started as a handful of uncertain lines and had grown, over the past weeks, into something closer to a running account of a self he was still assembling in real time.

Cael's private log, that evening, carried a new entry in the back section — shorter than the others, but the one he'd return to, months later, as the moment he'd stopped being entirely alone with the classification.

*Tried to reach the Arbiter tonight. Nothing. Tried it enough times that I stopped counting, which for me is saying something. I don't think it's coming back the way Lira's does — a ledger you check, a status you read. I think whatever this is, it doesn't work that way, and I need to stop expecting it to.*

*Lira knows. Has known since the first day, apparently, and never said anything until I asked. She didn't flinch when I finally did. She didn't perform anything — no careful blankness, no sympathy arranged into the right shape. She just adjusted, the way she adjusts to a new pattern mid-exchange, and kept going. I don't think I understood, until today, how much weight I'd been carrying just from not knowing whether she'd do that.*

*She asked me something today that nobody else has asked — not "what does the registry say you are" but "what did it actually feel like, from where you were standing." I didn't have a complete answer. I'm not sure I have one yet. But the question itself did something. It moved the whole problem an inch, from a thing that's being done to me, to a thing I get to have an opinion about.*

*The circuit doesn't care what the registry says. I'm going to build whatever this turns out to be inside that fact, because it's the only solid ground I've been offered since Weaver's Row. Everything else — the archive, the four names, Hesk's careful non-answers, the eleven seconds in that room — has been a version of the world telling me what I am. The circuit is the first place that's asked me to show it instead.*

*Hesk gave me a notebook and told me my body would learn before my mind did. He was right about that. What he couldn't tell me — because he didn't know, and I don't think anyone does — is what happens once my mind starts catching up. I think that's coming. I think it's already started, in small pieces, in sparring sessions and circuit exchanges I don't fully understand yet. I don't know what it means. I know it's not nothing.*

*More data. That's all I actually have, most days. More data, and, now, someone else who knows and hasn't looked away.*

He closed the notebook and set it on the small shelf beside his cot, in the same spot Hesk's leather-bound book occupied, the two of them sitting together like a matched pair — one voice from before, one voice figuring itself out in real time. He lay back, listening to the room's familiar sounds settle around him: the wheezing man nearest the door, the brothers murmuring something neither of them would remember by morning, the boots-in-bed woman shifting once and going still.

For the first time in three weeks, he slept without the particular vigilance he hadn't realized he'd been carrying every night since Denvash — the low, constant hum of being the only person who knew the whole shape of what he was. It wasn't gone. It was, for one night, shared, and that turned out to be enough of a difference to let him rest.

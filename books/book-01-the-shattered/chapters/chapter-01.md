# Chapter 1 — The Day Before

The thing about Kindling was that everyone had a different word for what it felt like.

Cael had collected eleven. He kept a notebook for things that mattered, and the descriptions were on page seven, listed in order of who had given them.

*Sunlight,* said the dried-fruit seller at the Outer Market, who had an Ember Path and eleven years of certainty about it. She had said this while handing him a bag of dates and not looking at him, the way people described important things when they weren't sure how much attention the listener was paying.

*A door opening,* said the gate guard on Merchant Row, who had a Copper-tier Shield Path and enough time on his hands to explain it to a boy who kept asking questions. He had used the phrase twice, as if repetition would make it land.

*Like remembering something you never knew,* said the older girl three streets over whose Kindling had been two years ago and who considered herself an expert on the experience in the way of people who have had a thing happen to them once and have since constructed a complete theory of it.

*I don't know how to describe it,* said Hesk.

Cael had written them all down, but he had only starred the last one.

---

It was the morning of the day before his Kindling. There was no version of this where he got to opt out or ask for more time — Kindling happened to you the year you turned fourteen, whether you were ready or not, and the only thing anyone actually scheduled was the certification appointment that formalized it, booked by the city a year in advance to manage the volume of fourteen-year-olds the district produced annually. He had known his appointment date for a year, and he had spent approximately the last three hundred and sixty-four mornings trying not to think about what this morning meant.

This was not, it turned out, a sustainable strategy.

He ate breakfast in the kitchen while Hesk read the city notice sheet and the light came through the east-facing window at the angle it only managed for about twenty minutes in the morning, clear and sideways and brief. The house smelled like the workshop in the back—soldering flux and the machine oil Hesk used on instrument housings—and underneath that the smell of the bread Hesk had bought from the bakery on the corner, which left its side door open when the ovens were going and let the smell carry for half a block.

Cael ate the bread and wrote in his notebook and did not think about tomorrow.

He thought about it continuously.

---

The Outer District of Denvash ran along the city's interior wall like a footnote—wider in the northern quadrant where the transit depots were, narrower in the east where the district pressed up against the inner wall and the street access became complicated. Cael had grown up in the northern quadrant, which meant he knew the transit depots and the Outer Market and the community hall and the rhythms of a district that served mostly as the place where people who weren't yet ranked enough to matter lived until they were.

Some of them never got there. That was not something the city authority said directly, but it was something the city authority's architecture communicated clearly through the placement of the Inner District gate and the height of the wall between them and the grade of the road maintenance on the Outer District's side of the divide.

Cael had a notebook entry on this, too. It was five pages long.

He went out after breakfast because the house felt small in a way it didn't usually feel, and walked north toward the market with no particular destination in mind, which was a thing he did when he needed to be moving without having to explain why he was moving. The morning was cold enough for his breath to show. The streets in the northern quadrant had vendors out even this early—dried goods and tool parts and a man who sold hand-copied transit schedules for cities Cael had never been to, which seemed like optimistic commerce for the Outer District but which the man appeared to have been conducting profitably for several years.

He counted things as he walked, the way he did when his own thoughts felt too loud to trust. Fourteen carts between the house and the Row. Six vendors already selling, three still setting up, one arguing with a supplier about the weight of a delivery in a tone that suggested this was a recurring argument they both privately enjoyed. A woman he didn't recognize was chalking the day's prices onto a slate outside a spice stall, and the chalk squeaked in a way that set his teeth slightly on edge, and he noted that too, because noting things was easier than sitting with the one thing he wasn't ready to note directly.

He was passing the certification office on Weaver's Row when he saw Alis.

Alis Trent lived two streets over and had turned fourteen and Kindled three days ago. She was standing outside the certification office with her mother, who was crying, which Cael registered and then carefully did not look at because other people's crying was not his business. Alis herself was not crying. She was holding a certification document and reading it with the expression of someone who was still parsing what the words meant, which was fair, because Kindling classifications tended to be phrased in official registry language that was not designed for immediate comprehension.

She looked up when she heard him.

"Tide Path," she said. "Copper, Rank 1."

"Good healing potential," Cael said, because this was true.

"My brother got Blade Path."

"Also good."

"He's Silver Rank 3 now."

There was a space after this sentence into which Cael fit several possible responses, none of which felt adequate. Tide Path was a legitimate Path with real advancement potential and more value in the market than most people gave it credit for. Tide Path Copper Rank 1 at fourteen was not, however, the same thing as a Silver-tier brother, and pretending otherwise would have been insulting to someone who was clearly intelligent enough to understand the comparison she was making.

"You're going to have more options in two years than you think you do right now," Cael said.

Alis looked at him. "You sound like a motivational pamphlet."

"I'm paraphrasing my grandfather."

"Is he right?"

Cael considered this. Hesk was right about most things, the way of someone who had been wrong about enough things earlier in life to have calibrated toward accuracy. "Usually," he said.

Alis looked back at her document. Her mother had stopped crying and was now talking to the certification officer through the door, which was the appropriate next step in the process—filing the family notification form, updating the residential registry, beginning the Path guild enrollment inquiry if applicable. Cael knew this because he had read the certification office's procedural summary three months ago when he'd found it in the community hall's reference section.

"Tomorrow's yours," Alis said.

"Yes."

"Are you worried?"

This was a direct question, which he respected. "Yes."

She nodded, once, like someone confirming a thing they already knew. "Me too," she said. "Before. Three days ago." She looked at the document again. "It's—I don't know. It's better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted."

Cael wrote this in his notebook, standing there on Weaver's Row in the cold morning air with the certification office's windows casting rectangles of light on the pavement. *Better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted.* He starred it.

"Congratulations," he said. "On the Path."

Alis almost smiled. "Thanks. Good luck tomorrow."

He walked the rest of the way to the market without a destination, which was more or less the point.

---

He knew Joren's result because the whole northern quadrant knew Joren's result. Joren had been making predictions about his Kindling for approximately a year, in increasingly precise terms, and the neighborhood had been following his certainty with the close attention people pay to certainty when they suspect it might be wrong.

It had been wrong.

Stone Path, Copper Rank 1. Not a bad Path—Cael had looked up Stone Path the day after Joren's Kindling, because he looked up everything, and Stone Path had solid advancement potential and good synergy with defensive specializations at the higher tiers. It was the kind of Path that rewarded patience, which Joren had in abundance underneath the bravado. In ten years, if he worked at it, Joren could be Bronze-tier with a Stone Path specialization and a legitimate guild placement, and that was a life, a real one, with access to more of the city and more options than most people in the Outer District ever got.

But it was not Gold. It was not Silver. It was the beginning of something rather than the arrival at something, and Joren, who had spent a year telling everyone he would arrive, had to figure out how to begin instead.

Cael had watched him walking home from the certification office two days ago. He had not looked devastated. He had looked like someone working very hard on not looking devastated, which was different, and harder, and something Cael recognized because he had practiced it himself, though not for this reason.

He bought a flatbread from the market—the stall in the south corner that did them with cracked pepper and salt, which was the best available option in the Outer Market's morning selection—and ate it standing at the edge of the district's small open space, where three streets converged and there was technically room for a tree, though the tree had been removed at some point before Cael could remember it and what remained was a slightly larger area of pavement than the surrounding streets offered.

He thought about Alis, who had said *better when it's done.*

He thought about Joren, who had been so certain.

He thought about the list of Path types in the Compact Registry's public summary, which he had read thoroughly and which he had made notes on, and the entry that had appeared near the bottom of the historical classification section under the heading *non-standard designations and legacy classifications.* The entry was brief—four lines, official language, deliberately unremarkable—and it had stayed in his memory with the stubborn insistence of things that seem more significant than they should.

*[SHATTERED]: Classification indicating fractured Path architecture, historically associated with unstable practice and practitioner safety risk. Currently deprecated. Four (4) instances recorded in the history of the Compact registry.*

Four. Not a percentage, not a statistic diluted across a registry of hundreds of thousands — four actual people, in the entire recorded history of the system, who had stood where he might stand tomorrow and heard whatever there was to hear.

He had not written this entry in his notebook. He didn't want to make it more real than it already was.

He walked home a longer way than the one he'd taken out, past the community hall where the reference collection lived, past the workshop of a boot-mender who'd known him since he was small enough to be handed off between adults without being consulted about it, past the low wall where he and Joren used to sit as children and argue about which Path they'd get, back when getting one at all had still felt like the only question that mattered. Joren had wanted Blade Path since he was seven. Cael, at seven, had wanted whatever would let him build things that lasted, and had never quite settled on which Path that was supposed to be, and had stopped, somewhere around eleven, wanting anything specific at all, which he now understood, walking past the wall with his hands in his pockets, might have been less a virtue and more a kind of premonition he hadn't known how to read.

---

Hesk found him on the rooftop in the afternoon, which was where Cael went when he needed sight lines, which was what they called it when he didn't want to explain what he was actually doing.

He'd been up there for two hours. The notebook had mostly served as something to hold.

Hesk came up through the rooftop access with two cups of tea, which was a thing he did when he had something to say and was deciding how to say it. He sat next to Cael on the low wall at the roof's southern edge, where the view opened up over the northern quadrant's rooftops toward the Inner District's boundary wall, which was taller than it needed to be and had been built, Cael suspected, to be taller than it needed to be.

They sat for a while in the comfortable silence that comes from enough years of it. Below them the afternoon traffic moved through the streets—carts and practitioners and children running between houses and the large grey dog that lived somewhere on this block and could never quite be located despite the fact that it appeared regularly and vocally on the street below.

"Joren seems better," Hesk said.

"He is better. He's working on it."

"He'll be all right."

"Yeah." Cael looked at his notebook. "He's got the right temperament for Stone Path, actually. Patient. Doesn't get frustrated by resistance."

Hesk made the sound that preceded something he was deciding whether to say. Cael had grown up with that sound and could distinguish between at least four varieties of it. This one meant *I have something specific and I'm choosing when to put it down.*

He waited.

"You're not Joren," Hesk said.

"No."

"Whatever happens tomorrow—"

"Hesk."

He stopped. Then: "—we'll figure it out."

Cael looked at him. Hesk was looking at the street below, which was either a deliberate choice to give Cael room to respond without an audience, or it was just Hesk's way of being careful, which were sometimes the same thing.

"I know," Cael said.

Hesk nodded. He picked up his tea. Below, the grey dog barked at something and was silenced by someone's window opening and then closing.

"I talked to the district record office," Hesk said.

Cael kept his expression even. "About."

"About non-standard classifications. Procedurally. What the process is."

He meant what the process was for practitioners whose Kindling produced something the standard system couldn't handle. Cael had researched this. He knew what the standard process was.

"And?" he said.

"It's manageable," Hesk said. "Whatever the result is. There are provisions."

Cael wrote *there are provisions* in his notebook. He was not sure yet what it meant, but Hesk did not say things without meaning them, and the fact that he had researched this meant he had been thinking about it for long enough to want answers. Which meant he had been thinking about it for a while.

"How long have you been thinking about this?" Cael said.

Hesk's expression stayed level. "A while."

"Since my preliminary assessment."

"Before that."

Cael looked at him. Hesk looked at the street. The grey dog reappeared and crossed the intersection below them with the deliberate unconcern of an animal that knew its territory.

"You should have told me," Cael said. Not angry. Just accurate.

"There was nothing to tell until there was something to tell."

"I've been worried about it."

"I know."

"Alone."

Hesk was quiet for long enough that Cael thought he might not answer. Then: "Yes. I know. I'm sorry about that." A pause. "I didn't want to make something real before it was."

Cael turned this over. It was exactly the kind of thing he might have done, if their positions were reversed. This was not entirely comforting.

"What do you know?" he said. "Actually know. About what my assessment indicated."

Hesk was quiet again. Longer this time. 

"Your preliminary assessment showed irregular coupling patterns," he said finally. "The assessor called it uncommon variation. She wasn't wrong to call it that. But there are different kinds of uncommon variation." He paused. "The kind yours showed—I've seen it before. Once. A long time ago."

Cael's notebook was open in his hands but he wasn't writing anything. "Who."

Hesk shook his head. Not *I won't tell you* but *it's not the right story for right now.* "It'll be clearer tomorrow," he said. "One way or another."

"And if it's—"

"Then we figure it out." He said this with the weight of something that had been decided rather than something that was a possibility. "I mean that, Cael. Whatever it is."

Cael believed him. He believed him in the way he believed the structural load calculations Hesk did in the workshop—not because he was required to but because the math was right and Hesk had always shown his work. Hesk had taught him that, years ago, without meaning it as a lesson: *never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from.* He'd said it about a housing bracket that had failed a stress test, and Cael had filed it away the way he filed most things Hesk said, not realizing until much later how many parts of his life the sentence would end up applying to.

He didn't say this. He wrote it in his notebook instead, and they sat until the light changed, the grey dog making one more circuit of the block below, the afternoon carts thinning toward evening, the hour when the Outer District's noise softened into something closer to a held breath.

"Tea's cold," Hesk said, eventually, looking at his cup as though it had personally disappointed him.

"You let it get cold."

"I did." He didn't move to refill it. "Come down when you're ready. I'll start dinner."

He left Cael on the rooftop a while longer, which Cael understood as its own kind of gift — not being asked to perform readiness before he actually felt it.

---

Dinner was rice with whatever was left in the cold box from the week, which Hesk made with the efficiency of someone who had lived alone for a long time before Cael arrived and still thought of cooking as a problem to be solved rather than an art form. It was reliably adequate. Tonight Cael ate without tasting it very much, which was not unusual when his attention was elsewhere and which Hesk did not comment on.

Hesk ate the way he did most things — steadily, without hurry, pausing occasionally to look at something across the room that wasn't actually there, the middle-distance stare of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to do out loud. Cael recognized the expression. He'd seen it on Hesk's face over broken instrument housings, over unpaid invoices, over the two occasions in Cael's memory when the workshop had gone quiet for an entire week because a commission had fallen through. It was the face of a problem being worked, patiently, without any guarantee of a solution.

Hesk put the small radio on, which he did sometimes in the evenings. The program was Path athletics commentary—a regional circuit match that had happened two days ago, being discussed with the enthusiasm of commentators who had been enthusiastic about regional Path athletics for long enough to have opinions about everything. Cael listened because listening was easier than thinking, and because the athletics commentary was useful in the way that watching people who knew what they were doing was always useful.

"The Ardenmere match," Cael said. "The Wind Path practitioner in the quarter-finals."

"Third exchange," Hesk said, without looking up.

"She did something I couldn't track from the radio coverage."

"She dropped her stance before the commitment." Hesk's expression was neutral. "Counter-intuitive. It bought her the angle."

Cael looked at him. "How do you know that?"

"I watched the community hall broadcast." A pause. "On Tuesday."

Cael had not known Hesk went to the community hall on Tuesdays. He had not asked. He wrote *ask Hesk about Tuesdays* in the margin of his notebook and underlined it twice.

The commentator on the radio was explaining, at length, the Wind Path practitioner's advancement potential. Cael half-listened and thought about the third exchange. The dropped stance before the commitment. Counter-intuitive. What was she seeing that made the counter-intuitive move correct?

He wrote it down. He would think about it later. Later was when he did his best thinking, which was inconvenient because later was when he was supposed to be sleeping.

After dinner Hesk cleared the table and Cael washed the dishes, which was the arrangement, unchanged since Cael had been tall enough to reach the basin without a stool. Then Hesk sat in his chair with his book and Cael sat at the other end of the table with his notebook and they occupied the same room in silence, which was the best kind of evening. Outside, the district's evening noise had its own shape — a door somewhere down the block opening and closing, a cart's wheels on stone growing faint, the grey dog announcing something to nobody in particular before losing interest in it.

He was trying to write a coherent account of what he knew about Kindling and what would happen tomorrow. He kept getting stuck.

The Compact Registry's historical classification records were public information, technically. The parts relevant to his concern were not in the section available at the Outer District's public record office. He had found the *[SHATTERED]* entry in a supplemental registry summary at the community hall's reference collection, which held more documents than the official summary of its holdings suggested. He had also found, in a footnote of that same supplemental summary, the sentence: *practitioners presenting non-standard classifications at Kindling are advised to report to the nearest Compact district office within 48 hours for standard compliance processing.*

He had looked up what standard compliance processing involved.

He had written this down and then crossed it out, which was not his usual practice. He crossed it out because writing it down made it real in a way he wasn't ready for, and crossing it out was a compromise position.

Hesk put his book down at half past nine.

"Go to bed," he said.

"I'm fine."

"You've been writing the same paragraph for thirty minutes."

Cael looked at his notebook. He had, in fact, been writing the same paragraph for thirty minutes, or variations of it. He closed the notebook.

"Hesk."

"Yes."

"The thing you saw before. The irregular coupling pattern. The person you mentioned." He stopped. "Did they—how did it go for them?"

Hesk was quiet long enough that Cael thought this was one of the things he wasn't going to answer. Then: "It was complicated," he said. "I know how it started. I don't know how it ended — I've spent longer than you've been alive not knowing the rest of it." Another pause. "Go to bed."

Cael went to bed.

---

He wasn't sure what woke him. The house was quiet—Hesk had gone to bed an hour after him, which he knew from the particular quality of the silence once the reading lamp in the other room went dark. The street outside had the 2 a.m. stillness of a district where most people worked early morning shifts.

He lay in the dark and looked at the ceiling.

Tomorrow the Arbiter would come. It would evaluate him. It would give him a classification.

Eleven descriptions. *Sunlight. A door opening. Like remembering something you never knew. I don't know how to describe it.*

He thought about Alis: *better when it's done, even if it's not what you wanted.* He thought about Joren, who had been so certain, walking home with an expression he was working on not showing. He thought about the Wind Path practitioner and the dropped stance and the counter-intuitive move that bought her the angle, and what she was seeing in that moment that made the wrong move right.

He thought about Hesk, who had researched the provisions. Who had seen it before, once, a long time ago. Who had said: *I know how it started. I don't know how it ended.* — and had carried not knowing for longer than Cael had been alive, and was still here, still steady, still making tea.

He got his notebook from the shelf beside the bed and wrote, in the dark: *whatever it is, we figure it out.*

He had written this before. In different words, in different notebooks, in the margins of other things. He kept writing it because it kept being the truest thing he knew.

He put the notebook back. He lay down. The ceiling was the same ceiling it had always been—water stain in the northeast corner from a winter four years ago, the same tired off-white that every ceiling in the Outer District seemed to default to eventually.

He was allowed to be worried. Worrying was not the same as being sure something would go wrong. It was just paying attention to the possibility.

He was, at 2 a.m. with the certification office waiting in seven hours, finding this marginally convincing.

He thought about the eleven descriptions again, turning them over one more time the way he'd turn over a coin he was deciding whether to spend. Sunlight. A door opening. Like remembering something you never knew. He tried, briefly and without much hope, to imagine which one might turn out to be his — whether tomorrow would feel like warmth, or opening, or memory — and found that his imagination, usually reliable for this kind of exercise, simply refused to produce an answer. It was possible, he thought, that some things couldn't be imagined in advance. That was probably the whole point of them.

Somewhere below, the house made its small settling sounds — a joint in the old timber, the tick of the cooling stove in the kitchen, the sounds a home makes when it thinks nobody's listening. Cael had grown up inside those sounds the way other people grew up inside a language, fluent in a way he'd never had to think about until tonight, when every ordinary thing about the house had started to feel worth cataloguing, in case tomorrow changed what he was allowed to call it.

He closed his eyes. He thought, not for the first time, that the worst part was the waiting. The event, whatever it was, would have the decency to be specific. The waiting was all possibility, which was harder, because possibility included everything.

He slept eventually, without dreaming about anything in particular, which was the best outcome he could have reasonably hoped for.

# Chapter 15 — Six Weeks

Six weeks was not forever. Cael understood this with the same clarity he understood most numbers — as a fact with edges, not a feeling with room to negotiate — and he spent the first evening of it doing what he did with any deadline: breaking it into pieces small enough to actually use.

Six weeks. Forty-two days. If the senior evaluation and whatever came after it were going to treat his standing in Ardenmere as a variable worth weighing, then the only useful thing he could do with the time was make that variable as heavy as possible before anyone came to weigh it.

"You want to fight more," Lira said, when he laid it out for her on the boarding house steps that evening, notebook open on his knee with a rough count already sketched in the margin. "Not train more. Fight more. Actual bouts, actual Ledger entries."

"Six weeks is enough time to move from unrated to something the circuit actually has a word for. If I use it."

"It's also enough time to get seriously hurt if you rush the wrong match." She said this without discouraging him, exactly — more like she was making sure the risk was priced correctly before he committed to spending it. "You've got five fights logged. One win. That's a start, not a foundation."

"So help me build the foundation faster."

Lira studied him for a long moment, the same evaluating look she still gave him sometimes, even now, months into knowing him — not suspicion, just the ongoing project of understanding exactly what kind of person she'd decided to spend her mornings training. "Okay," she said, finally. "But we do it my way. Faster doesn't mean reckless. It means efficient. There's a difference, and I've watched enough circuit fighters die not knowing it."

---

What "her way" meant, over the following six weeks, was a schedule more demanding than anything Cael had kept since Weaver's Row — training every morning before the market woke, circuit bouts arranged with a frequency Vell raised an eyebrow at but didn't refuse, and evenings spent in the log, converting each fight into data before the memory of it went soft.

He fought six times in the six weeks. He won four of them.

The first win, three days after the six-week clock started, came against a Copper Rank 2 whose Path Cael had mapped from two prior observed bouts — a clean, methodical victory that felt, compared to the desperate patience of the Dessa fight, almost easy, and which he distrusted precisely because it felt easy.

"Don't get comfortable," Lira told him that night, correctly reading his suspicion of his own success. "Easy fights happen. They don't mean the hard ones stopped existing."

The second win came harder — an opponent who'd clearly heard about him by then, circuit gossip having done exactly what circuit gossip did, and who came in with a plan for neutralizing whatever Cael was supposed to be capable of. The plan didn't account for the fact that Cael, watching the opponent's own preparation from the crowd two days before, had built a counter-plan first. He won in the fifth exchange, closer than he'd have liked, but a win.

He lost twice.

The Bronze Rank 1 came in the third week — a woman named Sarel, Blade Path like Renn but a full tier beyond him in every way that mattered, and Cael had known, standing across the circle from her, that he was outmatched before Vell had even finished calling the start. He fought anyway, because Lira's rule about efficiency hadn't included a clause about avoiding fights he couldn't win, only fights he couldn't survive, and this, she'd judged, was survivable.

It was not comfortable. Sarel's speed made his usual method — watch, wait, let the pattern resolve before committing — a genuine liability rather than an asset, because by the time a pattern in her movement had resolved clearly enough to act on, she'd already moved twice more. He adapted mid-fight, the way Lira kept telling him he needed to learn to do, abandoning the plan he'd built from watching her two prior bouts and reacting instead to what was actually happening in front of him, exchange by exchange, with no time to think it through first.

He lost in the sixth exchange, cleanly, no shame in it — Sarel herself said as much afterward, crouching down to his eye level with the specific respect of someone who didn't extend it lightly. "You adjusted," she told him. "Most people your rating just keep running the same plan into the ground until it stops working entirely. You noticed it stopped working after two exchanges instead of six. That's rare." She didn't offer to fight him again, and didn't need to; Cael understood, without being told, that this was the kind of respect that didn't require repetition to mean something.

He wrote the loss into the log that night at greater length than any win: *Watching isn't enough against speed that outpaces the watching. I need a second method for opponents I can't out-observe — something faster than pattern-recognition, something closer to what Lira does when she improvises. I don't have it yet. I don't know if watching alone will ever get me there, or if this is a different skill entirely.*

The second loss, a week later, was smaller and stranger — a Copper Rank 4 named Talis, Stone Path like Dessa but younger, less patient, whose ability turned out to have a secondary effect Cael hadn't catalogued from the crowd: a faint, almost imperceptible vibration in the ground itself that telegraphed nothing about the coming strike but gave away, half a beat early, exactly where Talis intended to plant his weight next. Cael had been watching hands and shoulders, the way he watched everyone. He hadn't been watching the ground. He lost in the fourth exchange to a strike he never saw building, because he'd been looking in the wrong place entirely.

*This is the kind of loss that should embarrass me and doesn't,* he wrote afterward. *It taught me something specific: I have a method, and the method has blind spots, and the blind spots aren't going to announce themselves. I have to go looking for them on purpose, in every opponent, instead of assuming the same three or four tells cover everyone. Talis's ground-vibration tell is the first thing I've catalogued that didn't come from a hand or a shoulder. I doubt it'll be the last kind of tell I haven't thought to look for yet.*

He wrote both losses into the log with the same clinical thoroughness he applied to everything, no self-pity in the entries, just data: *what I missed, why I missed it, what watching more carefully next time would need to catch.*

The third win came in the fifth week, against a Copper Rank 3 named Dellin whose Force Path he'd mapped over three separate observed bouts, and it went almost exactly the way he'd planned it — the rarest kind of satisfaction, watching a plan survive contact with an actual opponent instead of needing to be rebuilt mid-fight. Dellin, afterward, asked him fewer questions than most opponents did, just looked at him for a long moment and said, "You knew what I was going to do before I did it," which wasn't quite accurate — Cael hadn't known, he'd calculated a probability high enough to act on — but which he didn't correct, because the distinction wouldn't have meant anything to either of them in that moment.

The fourth win, in the final week, was the one that actually changed how the district talked about him, though Cael didn't understand why until Lira explained it afterward. The opponent was a Copper Rank 3 named Corbin, a circuit veteran with nearly forty fights logged, respected rather than feared, the kind of fighter newer circuit-watchers used as a measuring stick for anyone they were trying to evaluate. Beating a fighter like that wasn't just a win. It was a data point other people could use.

"That's the fight people are going to remember," Lira told him, walking back to Torvin's afterward, the evening market noise settling around them. "Not because it was your hardest. Sarel was harder. Not because it was your cleanest — Dellin was cleaner. Because Corbin is someone everyone already has an opinion about, and now your name's attached to an opinion people already trust."

"That seems like an inefficient way to build a reputation."

"It's the only way reputations actually work. Nobody trusts a new fact. They trust a new fact attached to an old one they already believed." She said this with the weary authority of someone who'd learned it the hard way, at an academy that had decided her own new fact — a better technique, demonstrated at the wrong moment — wasn't worth trusting at all. "You want to be believed faster than four months should reasonably allow? Beat the fighters everyone already respects. It's not fair. It's also not going to change, so you might as well use it."

He filed that observation the way he filed everything useful — not in the tactical section, which was for opponents' bodies, but in the growing section at the back where he kept the things that were true about the world itself.

---

"Assessed-Copper," Vell told him, at the end of the sixth week, with the specific flat satisfaction of someone confirming a fact rather than offering praise. "Unofficial designation. Means the circuit thinks you perform at roughly Copper Rank 5-6, regardless of what your registry says you are, which is nothing." She made a mark in her ledger, the same mechanical handwriting she used for everything, and did not look up while she spoke, which Cael had learned by now was simply how she talked about things that mattered to her. "It's not a real rank. Nobody outside this district would recognize it. Take it to a guild office in Denvash and they'd laugh you out the door, or worse, ask you to explain where it came from. But inside this district, it's the difference between 'unrated kid who might get lucky' and 'fighter people plan around.' That's worth something."

"Is it worth anything to you?"

Vell looked up at that, something almost amused crossing her weathered face. "I don't rate fighters because I like some of them better than others. I rate them because an accurate ledger is the only thing standing between this circuit and it tearing itself apart over disagreements about who's actually good. Whether I like you is a separate question from whether the ledger's honest about what you can do." A pause, and then, in a slightly different register, quieter: "For what it's worth. I do."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. You did the fighting." She looked at him a moment longer, then went back to the ledger, the conversation apparently over as far as she was concerned. Then, without looking up again: "Six weeks well spent. Whatever's coming for you next, you didn't waste the time you had. Not everyone manages that."

---

The training itself, underneath the fight count, had changed shape over the six weeks in ways Cael was only starting to notice.

He and Lira had settled into a rhythm that felt less like instructor and student — which was, he realized somewhere around week three, not quite what it had ever technically been, since she was fifteen and self-taught in her own right and had never once claimed authority she didn't have — and more like two people solving the same problem from different angles, comparing notes, correcting each other in both directions.

"You're doing the thing where you plan four moves ahead and then get surprised when the opponent doesn't cooperate with your plan," she told him, one morning in the fourth week, after a sparring exchange that had gone visibly wrong for him. "You're not wrong to plan. You're wrong to be married to the plan once the fight tells you it's outdated."

"You improvise. I pattern. We've established this."

"We've established it about forty times now, and you keep nodding like it's new information and then doing the exact same thing in the next exchange." She wasn't annoyed, exactly — more amused at her own persistence than at his stubbornness, the fondness of someone who'd decided a slow project was still worth finishing. "Try something for me. Next exchange, the moment I do anything you didn't predict, I want you to say the word 'gone' out loud. Not think it. Say it. Out loud, every time, until throwing the plan away stops needing a word to make it happen."

It felt absurd the first few times — announcing, mid-exchange, that his own strategy had just failed, the word coming out clipped and self-conscious while Lira grinned at him from across the yard. By the twentieth repetition it had stopped feeling absurd and started feeling like a genuine tool, a small verbal hinge his body could use to let go of a plan before his pride talked him into defending it past its usefulness. By the fortieth, he'd stopped needing to say it at all — the letting-go had become fast enough to happen without the word, which was, Lira told him with visible satisfaction, "the whole point of making you say it out loud in the first place. You don't keep the training wheels. You keep what the training wheels taught your legs."

Other mornings taught him other things, less dramatically. She corrected his footwork by demonstrating the wrong version of it first — exaggerating his own mistake back at him until he saw, from the outside, exactly what she was seeing from across the yard, which turned out to be a faster teacher than simple correction ever was. He, in turn, started narrating what he was watching during her sparring — not to instruct her, since she didn't need instructing, but because saying his observations out loud sometimes surfaced patterns even he hadn't consciously registered until the words left his mouth, and Lira, to his surprise, found this useful too.

"You notice things about my own fighting that I don't notice," she told him one morning, genuinely startled by an observation he'd made about a habit she hadn't known she had — a tendency to over-commit slightly on her dominant side when tired, something that had apparently cost her at least one match at her old academy without her ever understanding why. "That's — Cael, that's actually valuable. That's not just you being strange about watching people. That's a real skill."

"I know it's a real skill. I just didn't know it had a use beyond fighting."

"Everything has a use beyond fighting eventually. That's sort of the whole secret nobody tells you when you start." She said this lightly, but there was something underneath it that felt more considered than the tone let on — the weight of someone who'd learned it the expensive way and was choosing, deliberately, to hand it over cheap.

He tried it again. It went better, marginally, the kind of marginal improvement that meant real progress rather than a lucky exchange — and this, he was beginning to understand, was the actual shape of what they were building together: not one person teaching the other, but two incomplete methods slowly correcting each other's blind spots, month after month, morning after morning.

He didn't say this to her directly. He wrote it in the log instead, in the private section, because some things were true enough to need writing down before he trusted himself to say them out loud.

*Lira makes me better at the things I'm bad at. I don't know yet if I do the same for her, or if I just make her patient with a slower person. I hope it's the first thing. I think it might be. I'm not sure I've earned the right to be certain about it yet.*

---

The fragment notice arrived on the thirty-ninth night of the six weeks, and it did not arrive the way he expected anything to arrive, because he had, by then, stopped consciously expecting anything at all.

He was reviewing the day's log entries by lamplight, the boarding house quiet around him, the wheezing man nearest the door snoring through his usual rhythm, when something shifted at the edge of his perception — not a sound, not quite a sensation, closer to the internal quiet that precedes a thought arriving fully formed, the way a solution to a problem sometimes surfaces whole after you've stopped actively working on it.

He knew, with a certainty that arrived before any explanation for the certainty did, that something had just changed.

He set down the log. He sat very still, the way he'd learned to sit still in the certification office months ago, and waited.

It came the way the classification word had come — internal, private, visible only to him, structured in a way that felt less like language and more like the shape language would take if it had been stripped down to only the parts that mattered:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

He read it — or perceived it, or whatever the correct verb was for something that arrived without eyes being involved — three times, the way he'd read the historical archive entry three times, checking that he'd understood correctly before he let himself believe it.

*Wind-adjacent.*

He knew immediately what it was and where it had come from, with the same clean certainty that had told him something had changed in the first place. Months of morning sparring. Months of watching Lira move — not admiringly, not distractedly, but with the same cumulative attention he brought to everything he was trying to understand, applied for longer and more consistently to her than to anyone else in his life since Hesk. The instinct that had dropped him into her exact stance months ago, the one neither of them had been able to explain. The moment in the Dessa fight when something that wasn't quite his own footwork had put him in position to win. He'd been collecting this, apparently, the whole time, without a name for what he was collecting, and now, thirty-nine nights into a six-week reprieve, it had a name — or at least a designation, which was not quite the same thing, but was more than he'd had yesterday.

He sat with the notebook open on his knee for a long time, not writing anything, just holding the fact of it.

He thought about telling Lira. He got as far as imagining the conversation — *I think I have something of yours, except it isn't really yours anymore, it's mine, except it came from watching you, which makes it feel like it's still partly yours* — and found he didn't have a version of that sentence that didn't sound, out loud, like something between a confession and an accusation, neither of which was accurate, and both of which risked making something that felt private and still-forming into something he'd have to defend before he understood it himself.

*Not yet,* he wrote, finally, in the private section of the log. *Not because I'm hiding it. Because I don't know what it is well enough to explain it to anyone else, and I'd rather understand it first than have her help me understand it and always wonder, later, how much of the understanding was actually mine.*

He read that sentence back and wasn't entirely sure it was honest. He left it anyway, because honesty about his own uncertainty felt more important, right now, than pretending to a clarity he didn't have.

He tried, in the hour that followed, to understand the thing itself rather than just its origin. He closed his eyes and reached for it the way he'd reached for his own dormant Arbiter in the courtyard weeks ago, half expecting the same absence to answer him back. It wasn't absence this time. It was there, faint and strange, a texture underneath his own sense of his body that hadn't existed the week before — not a second set of instructions exactly, more like a possibility his muscles now knew about that they hadn't known about yesterday. He stood, carefully, in the small space between his cot and the wall, and tried a single slow step the way Lira stepped into a Wind-adjacent evasion, the specific drop of the hip that started it.

It worked. Not well — clumsy, over-deliberate, nothing like the effortless version he'd borrowed without meaning to during the Dessa fight — but it worked, on purpose this time, for the first time, a thing he had chosen to do rather than a thing that had simply happened to him under pressure. He stood there afterward, breathing harder than the small motion should have justified, turning the fact over like a stone he'd found and didn't yet know the weight of.

*I can do it on purpose now,* he wrote. *Badly. Slowly. But on purpose. That's different from before, and I think it might be the actual thing that matters — not that I have it, but that it's starting to answer when I ask instead of only showing up when I don't.*

He thought, for a long moment, about waking Lira to show her — she slept two streets over now, in a room of her own that she'd finally saved enough to afford, a small independence she was visibly proud of — and decided against it, not from secrecy but from a simpler instinct: some things deserved to be practiced alone first, clumsily, in private, before they were ready to be shown to anyone whose good opinion he cared about. He would tell her. Not tonight. Soon, once the clumsy version had a little more grace in it, once he had something worth showing rather than something worth merely confessing.

---

He wrote to Hesk that same night, a shorter letter than usual, because some things didn't compress well into a page and he'd rather say less than say it badly.

*Six weeks bought. Used all of them. Assessed-Copper now, whatever that's worth outside Ardenmere, which is probably nothing and inside Ardenmere is apparently something. Something happened tonight I don't have words for yet. Your notebook said my body would learn before my mind did. I think my mind just started catching up, a little. I'll tell you properly once I understand it myself. Miss you. C.*

He read the letter over once before sealing it, decided it was true even where it was incomplete, and set it aside for the morning post.

He lay awake a while after that, the wheezing man's breathing settling into its usual rhythm across the room, the district's late sounds carrying up through the window the way they always did — voices, distant music, the restless quiet he'd stopped finding unfamiliar months ago.

Six weeks, spent. A fragment, acquired, unexplained. A senior evaluation, coming.

He thought about the eleven seconds in the certification office, the word that had arrived from somewhere very far away and changed the shape of everything that came after it. He thought that whatever was coming next — whoever the senior evaluator turned out to be, whatever counter-procedure they arrived with — he was, for the first time since Weaver's Row, meeting it as someone who had actually built something in the time he'd been given, rather than someone simply waiting to find out what would be taken from him next.

It wasn't confidence, exactly. It was closer to the specific, hard-won steadiness of someone who had learned, the slow way, that the waiting was survivable if you filled it with work.

He slept, eventually, and didn't dream of anything he remembered in the morning, which he'd come to understand, by now, was itself a kind of rest.

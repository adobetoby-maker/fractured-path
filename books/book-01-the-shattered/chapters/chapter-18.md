# Chapter 18 — The Senior Evaluation

The six-week deadline Coss had granted him had come and gone weeks before — swallowed, without either of them remarking on it, by the fragment's arrival and the long, careful work of preparing for Feryn — and the senior evaluator's summons finally arrived on an ordinary Tuesday well after that, which Cael understood, reading it a second time, was simply how regional offices moved: not fast, not always on the edge of their own deadlines, but eventually, and without warning once they actually got around to it.

He spent the night before her arrival the way he'd spent the night before his Kindling, and the night before his first summons meeting with Coss: reviewing everything he had, not because he expected to find something new in a final read, but because the reviewing itself had become a kind of ritual, a way of arriving at a difficult morning already steady rather than still scrambling. He read the definitions section of the compliance handbook one more time, aloud, in a whisper, the way Hesk checked a load calculation before signing off on it. He read his own procedural challenge from weeks before, the one that had bought him the original reprieve, and tried to imagine what a more sophisticated opponent than Coss might do with the same regulation.

He found the temporary non-standard category on his own, two hours before dawn, buried in a cross-reference he'd skimmed past on his first pass through the handbook and never fully returned to. He read it three times, the way he read anything that might matter, and understood, with a cold clarity that kept him awake past the point his body wanted to sleep, that it was exactly the kind of argument a genuinely careful evaluator would reach for — and that he needed an answer to it before anyone arrived with the question, not after.

He built the answer before dawn, checked it twice against the regulation's own definitions section, and copied it, clean and precise, into the front pages of the log, ready to be spoken rather than read from, the way Lira had taught him confidence needed to sound.

Lira found him at first light, already awake, and didn't ask why. She simply sat beside him on the boarding house steps, offered him half of whatever she'd brought for breakfast, and let him talk through the argument out loud one final time, catching two phrasings that sounded more defensive than the content warranted and helping him trim them into something flatter, more certain.

"You're ready," she said, when he'd run through it a third time without stumbling. "I mean that as a fact, not encouragement. You're ready."

"I hope so."

"Hope's not the operative word anymore. You did the work. Go do the thing the work was for."

She was nothing like Coss. Where Coss had carried the weary competence of a man doing an unglamorous job thoroughly, the senior evaluator — her name plate read ASSESSOR ILSEV — carried herself with the unhurried precision of someone who had never once needed to raise her voice to get a room's full attention. She was perhaps forty, sharp-eyed, and she had, Cael understood within the first two minutes of the meeting, already read his procedural challenge from weeks before and prepared a response to it.

"Your objection to the standard compliance framework was correct," she said, without preamble, once they were seated in the same cramped district office Coss had used. "[SHATTERED] genuinely isn't recognized within it. I'm not here to relitigate that. I'm here because the regulation you cited also specifies what happens next, and I don't think you've read that part as carefully."

Cael had, in fact, read that part carefully, multiple times, in the weeks since his first visit to the archive. He said nothing yet, letting her make her case first, the same patience he brought to reading an opponent's opening exchange.

"Non-standard classifications outside the framework require senior evaluation authorization," Ilsev continued. "That's what's happening now. But the regulation also grants the senior evaluator discretion to apply a temporary non-standard category when the primary classification can't be assessed by ordinary means — a category that carries its own compliance pathway. One that requires physical presence at a Compact assessment facility, not a district-office conversation."

This was, Cael recognized immediately, considerably more sophisticated than Coss's straightforward summons had been — a genuine counter-procedure, not an oversight he could simply point at and expect resolved. He felt something tighten in his chest, a cold clarity that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with the alertness a real opponent produced, the kind he'd learned to recognize across months of circuit fights against people who actually knew what they were doing.

He had, however, prepared as well, and some part of him — the part that had spent the small hours before dawn finding this exact argument before anyone arrived to make it — felt something almost like relief underneath the alertness. This was the version of the confrontation he'd rehearsed. He simply had to trust the rehearsal now, the way he trusted a plan built from two weeks of watching an opponent train.

He let Ilsev finish laying out the temporary category in full, watching her the way he watched a fighter's opening exchange — not for weakness yet, just for information, the rhythm of how she built an argument before he answered it. She was thorough. She cited the correct section number without needing to check it, laid out the compliance pathway's requirements in the exact order the regulation specified them, and paused, at the end, with the composed patience of someone entirely confident she'd just closed every door he might have hoped to find open.

"The temporary non-standard category requires that the practitioner not already carry an existing formal classification," he said, keeping his voice level, the way Lira had coached him to sound certain rather than defensive. "[SHATTERED] is a formal classification. It's recorded in the historical registry under that exact designation, with a specific incidence count and a specific procedural history. The temporary category exists for practitioners the Arbiter genuinely can't classify at all — an Arbiter failure, not an unusual result. Mine wasn't a failure. It resolved. It just resolved to something outside the standard framework's vocabulary. Those are different situations, and the regulation's own language distinguishes between them in the definitions section, three pages before the section you're citing."

Ilsev was quiet for a long moment, and Cael watched her face do something he hadn't expected — not the flicker of professional irritation Coss had shown weeks ago, but something closer to genuine surprise, followed by a slow, careful reassessment. She reached for the compliance handbook she'd brought with her, opened it without needing to search for the page, and read the definitions section herself, silently, her lips moving very slightly the way a careful reader's sometimes did when they wanted to be certain of exactly what a sentence said rather than what they remembered it saying.

The silence stretched long enough that Cael found himself doing the arithmetic he always did in a fight when an exchange ran longer than expected — counting, not out of anxiety exactly, but out of the same habit that had counted eleven seconds in a certification office months ago, a way of anchoring himself to something concrete while the outcome remained genuinely uncertain.

She looked up before he reached twenty.

"You've read the definitions section," she said.

"I've read the whole compliance handbook. Twice."

"At fourteen."

"I had six weeks and a good reason."

Ilsev almost smiled at that — the first crack in her otherwise composed expression since she'd walked in. "You're right," she said, after a moment, with the flat honesty of someone who valued procedural accuracy over the convenience of being correct. "The temporary category doesn't apply. I hadn't encountered this edge case before — most non-standard classifications that reach senior evaluation are genuine Arbiter failures, not resolved-but-unrecognized results. Your case is rarer than the regulation was written to anticipate cleanly."

"What happens now?"

"Now I file for legal review, which is slower and more thorough than either the standard compliance track or the temporary category — potentially months, not weeks. It requires an actual determination on how the regulation should treat a case like yours, which nobody currently sitting in an office has ever had to make." She made a note in her own file, unhurried. "I'll tell you honestly: I don't know what that determination will conclude. I've never seen this exact question asked before."

---

Coss, when he learned the outcome, was audibly frustrated in a way Cael hadn't seen from him during their first, brief encounter.

"Months," he said, to Ilsev, in the district office's outer room, apparently not caring that Cael could hear him through the thin wall. "This was supposed to resolve. Regional wanted resolution. I told them six weeks would be enough time to close this cleanly, and now I'm going back to them with a timeline that stretches into next season."

"Regional will get a correct resolution," Ilsev said, evenly, unbothered by his frustration. "Just not a fast one. I'm not going to misapply a regulation to satisfy someone's timeline, Warden Coss. You of all people should understand why. You're the one who flagged this file's documentation as inconsistent in the first place. Did you expect a genuinely irregular case to resolve on a genuinely ordinary schedule?"

"I expected it to resolve. Full stop. Not to become the kind of case people ask questions about for the next six months."

"Then perhaps you should have hoped for a subject less thorough than this one," Ilsev said, and there was, underneath the flat procedural calm, something that might almost have been dry amusement. "I've read his objection twice, Warden Coss. It's better constructed than most objections I receive from licensed advocates. That's not a failure of the process. That's the process working exactly as it's designed to, against a case it was never built to anticipate."

Cael, waiting in the hallway, caught only fragments of what followed, but the tone was unmistakable: Ilsev, quietly, professionally immovable; Coss, frustrated but unable to argue the procedural point, because the procedural point was, as far as Cael's own careful reading had confirmed, correct. He found, listening through the thin wall, that he felt something close to sympathy for Coss underneath the satisfaction of having won the exchange — a man caught between an instruction he couldn't fully explain and a procedure he couldn't honestly circumvent, doing an unglamorous job as thoroughly as the job allowed him to.

Ilsev found him before she left, her expression back to its composed neutrality, though something more considered lingered underneath it. She sat across from him in the same cramped office, unhurried, the way she seemed to approach everything, and set her own working file down on the desk between them rather than holding it close the way most Compact officials Cael had encountered tended to.

"I don't often get outmaneuvered by a compliance subject," she said. "I want you to understand that I'm not upset about it. I became an evaluator because I care more about getting the regulation right than about closing files quickly, and most of the people who cross my desk never bother to test that principle. You made me do my job better today. That's rare enough that I'm choosing to be grateful for it instead of annoyed, which is not, I'll admit, always my first instinct."

"I appreciate you saying that."

"I mean it. I've been doing this work for fifteen years. I can count on one hand the number of times a subject has actually improved the quality of my own reasoning instead of just consuming my time." She studied him for a moment, something almost curious in her professional gaze. "Where did you learn to argue like that? Not the regulation itself — I mean the structure of it. The way you built the objection, cited the definitions section before I could reach for it myself. That's not something most fourteen-year-olds have occasion to practice."

Cael considered the question honestly. "I had a summons before this one. A district-level compliance meeting, with Warden Coss. I found a procedural gap that bought me six weeks. I spent most of those six weeks reading, because reading was the only tool I had that nobody could take away from me." He paused. "And I've spent the last several months learning to read people the way I read regulations. Watching for the tell before the strike. I think the two things ended up teaching each other."

"That's a genuinely unusual education," Ilsev said, "and an effective one, from what I've seen of it today." She made a small note in her own file, unhurried, and then set the pen down entirely, the gesture of someone shifting from official business into something more personal. "What should I expect, while the legal review is pending?"

"Nothing, officially. You're not required to appear anywhere, not required to change anything about your circumstances, not subject to any compliance action until the review concludes." She said this plainly, the recitation of someone who'd delivered the same reassurance to other subjects before, though something in her tone suggested she understood it would land differently on him than it usually did. "The review itself moves through regional legal counsel — they'll examine the regulation's language, likely consult the historical case files on the four prior [SHATTERED] classifications for precedent, though I'll tell you honestly that precedent won't help them much. None of those four cases produced anything resembling a legal determination. They produced outcomes, and outcomes aren't the same thing as reasoning anyone can build on."

Cael absorbed this, filing the mention of the four prior cases without visible reaction, though something in his chest tightened at the reminder — three dead within weeks, one unaccounted for, and now a fifth case that the system itself apparently had no framework for resolving cleanly.

"There's one more thing," Ilsev said, and her voice shifted register entirely, dropping the careful officialdom of the last several minutes into something quieter, more private. "I noted something while preparing your file that I don't fully understand, and I want to be honest with you about that rather than pretend I have more clarity than I actually do. I've been doing this long enough to know the difference between telling a subject something reassuring and telling them something true, and I'd rather give you the second one, even when it's incomplete."

"Okay."

"There's a flag on your evaluation record. Not one I requested. Not one I recognize the origin of, and I've checked — I have access to authorization trails most field agents don't, and this one doesn't trace to any office I can identify. It activated the moment I began applying standard procedures to your case, as though the system itself objects to your classification being handled through ordinary channels at all." She held his gaze for a long moment, the steadiness of someone delivering something difficult without softening it into something smaller than it actually was. "I don't know what it means. I don't know if it's benign — some artifact of the classification's rarity, a flag that exists for every unusual case and simply hasn't been documented properly — or something else entirely. I'm noting it in my report regardless, because pretending I didn't see it would be worse than admitting I can't explain it. That's not a comfortable position for me to be in, professionally. I imagine it's considerably less comfortable for you."

Cael filed this away with the same careful attention he gave everything that didn't yet fit into an explanation, turning it over the way he turned over an opponent's unfamiliar tell — noted, catalogued, not yet understood, but real. "Thank you for telling me."

"Don't thank me yet. I don't know if it's good news or bad news. I just know it's true, and you deserve the truth even when I can't finish the sentence for you." She stood, gathering her file, and paused at the door, looking back at him with an expression that had shed the last of its professional distance. "For what it's worth — whatever this turns out to be, you've handled every piece of it that's actually been within your control better than most adults I've evaluated. That won't fix the parts outside your control. But it's not nothing." She nodded once, the closest thing to a farewell gesture she offered, and left.

---

Cael walked straight from the district office to the circuit notice board, the same instinct that had carried him there after his first procedural win with Coss, and entered a bout scheduled for the following week before he'd fully processed the morning's events. It was, he recognized even as he did it, less a tactical decision than a physical one — his body needing to convert the morning's tension into something it knew how to metabolize, the way it always had since Weaver's Row.

Lira found him there, notebook in hand, staring at a notice he'd already read twice without absorbing a word of it.

"How did it go?" she asked, though her expression suggested she'd already guessed most of the shape of it from the fact that he was still standing, unescorted, in the middle of the district.

He told her everything — the temporary category, the argument he'd built before dawn, Ilsev's genuine surprise, the exchange with Coss he'd overheard through the thin wall, and finally, the flag. She listened with the same focused stillness she brought to a fight's opening exchange, absorbing rather than reacting, saving her response until she had the whole shape of it.

"A flag neither of them can explain," she said, once he'd finished. "That's twice now. Ilsev's flag, and whatever triggered the sweep months ago. That's not a coincidence pattern anymore. That's an actual thread."

"I know. I don't know what to do with a thread I can't see the other end of."

"You don't do anything with it yet. You just hold it, carefully, until it's ready to be pulled." She said this with the same patience she'd been teaching him for months, applied now to a problem considerably larger than a sparring exchange. "You're good at holding things carefully. You've been doing it since the day I met you."

They walked back toward Torvin's together, the market's ordinary evening business continuing around them, entirely indifferent to the quiet weight of what Cael was carrying.

That evening, he wrote it all into the log — the exchange with Ilsev, the unrecognized flag, the months of uncertain waiting now stretching ahead of him — and found, underneath the uncertainty, something steadier than he expected: not relief, exactly, but the confidence of someone who had tested his own preparation against a genuinely sophisticated opponent and found it, again, sufficient.

*Months now, not weeks. I don't know what the legal review will conclude. I don't fully understand the flag Ilsev mentioned. But I understand something else, which feels more useful right now: every time someone in the Compact has tried to move against me using their actual procedures, correctly applied, I've been able to meet it. Not because I'm exceptional. Because I did the reading nobody expected a fourteen-year-old to do, and did it thoroughly enough that "thorough" turned out to matter more than "official."*

*I don't know what happens when someone stops using their actual procedures. I hope I don't find out for a while yet.*

He wrote a shorter letter to Hesk that same night, describing the day in broad strokes — the counter-procedure, Ilsev's honesty, the flag she couldn't explain — and found, reading it back before sealing it, that he'd left out most of the fear the day had actually contained, keeping only the parts that were true and steady rather than the parts that were simply true. He decided, after a moment's consideration, to leave it that way. Some things Hesk deserved to know in full. Some things could wait for a conversation that happened across a table instead of across a page, where a worried expression could be answered directly instead of imagined into something worse than it was.

He thought, before sleep finally took him, about the four names in the historical archive — three dead within weeks, one unaccounted for — and about the fact that he was now, by any honest accounting, further into his own case than any of them had gotten, at least as far as the written record showed. He didn't know if that meant anything. He didn't know if the four of them had simply run out of time before they could do what he'd done today, or if something else entirely had ended their cases before procedure ever had the chance to matter.

He added one final line before closing the notebook, smaller than the others, the kind of sentence that didn't need elaboration to carry its full weight: *I'm still here. That's not nothing either.*

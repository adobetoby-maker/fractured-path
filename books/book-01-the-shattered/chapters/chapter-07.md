# Chapter 7 — The Rules

He woke before his own internal alarm, the one that had gotten reliably earlier every day since Weaver's Row, as if some part of him had decided that sleeping late was a luxury that belonged to a version of his life he no longer had access to. The room was still dark, the other four cots still occupied by their usual sleepers, and he lay still for a moment doing the small accounting he'd started doing most mornings now: what day it was, how long he'd been in Ardenmere, what he'd learned yesterday that he hadn't known the day before.

Six days. He'd been in Ardenmere six days, which felt, depending on which part of himself he asked, like either much longer or barely any time at all.

He dressed in the dark, careful not to wake the wheezing man nearest the door, and went down through the quiet boarding house to the street, where the district was only beginning to stir — a baker two doors down already had her ovens going, the smell reaching him before he'd gone a full block, and a handful of early risers moved through the grey light with the unhurried purpose of people who did this every morning and had stopped needing to think about it.

He'd asked Lira, the evening before, where he should meet her, and she'd named the Cinder House without elaboration, as though the name alone should mean something to him. It didn't, not yet, but he'd found it easily enough by asking two people on his walk — the district's geography had started, gradually, to organize itself in his head the way Denvash's had, streets connecting to other streets in a pattern his feet were learning faster than his conscious mind could map it.

The Cinder House itself turned out to be a public house on a corner two streets from the practitioners' quarter, its sign a faded painting of something that might once have been a hearth and was now mostly just a shape, weathered past recognition. Its front room was still shuttered at this hour, chairs stacked, the whole building holding the stillness of a place that came alive only at certain hours of the day and treated the rest as an inconvenience to be waited out.

The yard behind it was where the life actually happened, he'd been told, and he found his way around the building's side to it, arriving at exactly the hour Lira had named — a habit he suspected he'd never fully break, arriving early enough to be early and then waiting the extra few minutes so he wouldn't be early enough to seem eager.

The Cinder House yard, before eight in the morning, was mostly quiet — a packed-dirt square behind a public house that did brisk business once the fights started but sat empty and unremarkable in the early hours, the way a stage looks strange without an audience. A few crows worked the edges of the yard where scraps from the previous night's crowd had ended up, unbothered by anything moving at Cael's pace.

Lira was already there when Cael arrived, working through a drill against a wooden post wrapped in worn canvas — short, controlled strikes, footwork that looked less like fighting and more like a conversation she was having with the ground. She didn't stop when he came in, just called out without turning:

"You're on time. I'm mildly impressed."

"You said not to be late."

"People say a lot of things they don't expect to be listened to." She finished the combination, exhaled, and turned to face him properly, faintly damp with the effort of someone who'd been at this for a while already. There was a rhythm to how she moved even now, between strikes — nothing wasted, nothing performed for an audience that consisted, at present, of one fourteen-year-old and several disinterested crows. "Sit. Watch first, like I said. I'll explain the circuit while I work."

He sat on an overturned crate at the yard's edge and took out his notebook, which made Lira laugh — a short, genuine sound.

"You're going to take notes on me training."

"Is that a problem?"

"No. It's the most Cael thing you've done so far, and I've known you for one day." She went back to the post. "All right. The circuit. Ask me things as they come up — you'll understand it faster that way than if I just talk at you."

She started with the post again, a longer combination this time, and talked between strikes in short bursts, the sentences landing in the gaps like she'd practiced explaining things this way before — to herself, maybe, or to someone else who'd once stood exactly where Cael was standing.

"First thing you need to understand," she said, "is that the circuit isn't a place. People say 'the circuit' like it's one building with a sign out front, and it isn't. It's more like — a habit a few hundred people all decided to have at the same time, in the same district, and then kept having long enough that it turned into something with rules."

"Who decided the rules?"

"Nobody. That's the part that's hard to explain to someone who grew up somewhere with an actual authority handing down actual regulations." She caught the post, held the position a beat, released. "Nobody sat down and wrote a founding document. It built itself, the way anything builds itself when enough people need the same thing and none of them can get it anywhere official."

"What thing?"

"A place to find out what they're actually made of." She said this without irony, without the performance he'd half-expected from someone selling him on something. "Guild sanctioning tells you what a controlled, safety-regulated version of you can do against another controlled, safety-regulated version of someone else. The circuit tells you what you can actually do, against someone who actually means it, with nothing softening the answer. Some people can't live without knowing that. I'm one of them. You might be too. We'll find out."

---

What she explained, over the following hour, between combinations and the occasional break to correct his understanding of something he'd gotten slightly wrong:

The circuit was not one thing but a loose confederation of fighting venues across the Unranked District, all operating under a shared, unwritten governance that had developed over years without anyone formally deciding to build it. Bouts were arranged between fighters or their representatives, agreed on terms, and witnessed by a neutral party called a Ledger-keeper — someone trusted by all sides to record what happened honestly, because the entire system's survival depended on a currency more valuable than money: reputation for fairness.

"There's no guild enforcement," Lira said. "No Compact oversight. If someone cheats, or someone dies who wasn't supposed to, the circuit doesn't come back to that venue. Ever. That's the whole enforcement mechanism. Everyone's incentive is the same: keep it clean enough to keep existing."

"No-kill rule," Cael said, writing.

"Not a rule exactly — nobody wrote it down anywhere. A fact everyone agrees to act like is a rule, which turns out to work about as well as an actual rule, as long as everyone keeps believing in it." She caught the post with a final strike and turned to face him fully. "Abilities are unrestricted. Whatever your Path can do, you can do it in the circuit — that's part of the appeal. Guild-sanctioned matches have restrictions, safety protocols, weight classes. The circuit has: don't kill anyone, and try not to embarrass the Ledger-keeper who vouched for you."

"What happens if you break it?"

"You don't fight in Ardenmere again. Possibly anywhere, depending how bad it was and how far the story travels." She shrugged. "It's happened. Not often. The system's held together by everyone being more afraid of losing this than they are of losing a fight."

"Give me an example."

She considered this, wiping her palms on her trousers, buying herself a moment. "There was a fighter — before my time, I only know this secondhand — Bronze-tier, Ash Path, who kept pushing past the point where his opponent's Ledger-keeper called the match. Twice. Warnings both times. Third time, he put someone in a state that needed a healer brought in from outside the district, which is its own kind of expensive and its own kind of visible. No venue in Ardenmere would host him after that. Last I heard he was fighting three towns over, under a different name, for a fraction of the purse he used to draw here." She shrugged again. "Reputation doesn't travel as fast as people think. But it travels."

"And the Ledger-keeper who called the match — the one he ignored twice?"

"Vell." Lira said the name like it was self-explanatory, which, Cael was beginning to understand, most things about the circuit's actual governance turned out to be, once you'd been in the district long enough to have absorbed them without noticing. "She runs the Cinder House matches. You'll meet her tomorrow, if you go through with this."

---

He asked about the ratings — the Copper, Bronze, Silver-equivalents he'd seen posted alongside names on the notice board.

"Circuit rating isn't Compact rating," Lira explained. "The circuit doesn't care what the Arbiter said about you. It cares what you've actually done. You fight, the Ledger-keeper records the result, and over time your performance builds a rating that has nothing to do with your registry classification. A lot of people fight here specifically because their official tier doesn't reflect what they can actually do — failed evaluations, guild rejects, people who never Kindled at all and just turned out to be dangerous anyway."

"So I could enter."

"You could enter as unrated. Everyone starts unrated — it just usually doesn't stay that way for long, one direction or the other." She studied him for a moment. "You'd be fighting people who've been doing this for years, with no idea what you can do, including you. That's not a small thing to walk into."

"I know."

"I don't think you do, yet. But you will." She said this without malice — an observation, not a warning meant to discourage him. "That's kind of the whole reason people come to the circuit in the first place. It's the fastest way to find out."

"How does the rating actually get calculated? If it's not tied to a Path declaration."

"Vell's judgment, mostly, informed by results. It's not a formula — or if it is, nobody's ever explained it to me in a way that sounded like a formula and not just 'I've watched a lot of fights and I have opinions.'" Lira picked up a water skin from beside the post, drank, offered it to him. He shook his head. "Win convincingly enough, often enough, against opponents who are themselves rated, and your rating climbs. Lose badly enough, enough times, and it doesn't. There's more nuance than that — how you lose matters, sometimes more than whether you win — but that's the shape of it."

"How you lose matters?"

"Ask anyone who's watched you get carried out of the circle unconscious after refusing to tap versus someone who reads the moment and taps at the right time. One of those people gets more fights offered to them afterward. It's not the one you'd guess, if you're thinking about it the way the Compact would want you to think about it." She smiled, briefly, at something private. "Circuit doesn't reward stubbornness for its own sake. It rewards being interesting to watch and hard to predict. Those aren't the same as being the strongest person in the yard, though it doesn't hurt to be that too."

"What's your rating?"

"Assessed-Copper, high end of it. Which sounds unimpressive until you understand that most people my age with my training gap haven't cracked assessed-Copper at all, let alone the high end." She said this without false modesty and without inflating it either, the same flat accuracy she brought to describing her own Path. "I lost my first four fights here. Badly, some of them. I was better trained than almost anyone I fought and I still lost, because knowing a technique in a controlled academy setting and knowing it against someone who's trying to actually hurt you turn out to be two different skills, and nobody tells you that until you find out the hard way."

"What changed?"

"I stopped fighting the way my academy taught me to fight and started fighting the way that actually worked for what I could do. Sounds obvious. Took me four losses to actually believe it." She considered him for a moment, something calculating behind the consideration. "You're going to have an advantage there, actually. You don't have an academy style to unlearn. Whatever you build, you're building from nothing, which is slower at first and faster later, once it's built. I had years of good training to strip out before I could see clearly. You just have to see clearly."

"That's a strange kind of advantage."

"Most of the good ones are." She picked the water skin back up, drank again, capped it. "All right. Enough exposition. You want to actually watch some fights, or should I keep talking at you until the crowd shows up on its own?"

---

They talked through the morning about specifics — how bouts were structured (exchanges, not rounds; a fight ended when the Ledger-keeper called it, based on incapacitation, submission, or a fighter's own judgment that continuing wasn't worth it), how the betting economy worked (Lira had opinions about this that suggested she'd both profited and lost money from it in roughly equal measure, and a specific grudge against a bookmaker named Marrow who she claimed shaved odds in a way that wasn't technically dishonest but wasn't exactly honest either), and how a fighter built a reputation worth something.

"It's not just wins," she said, toward the end of the morning, finally letting the post rest and dropping onto the crate beside his. "A fighter with a boring style who wins every fight draws smaller crowds than a fighter with an interesting style who loses half of theirs. The purses follow the crowds. So does the respect, for what that's worth out here, which is more than you'd think."

"That seems like it rewards recklessness."

"Sometimes. Mostly it rewards being legible — having a shape people can recognize and root for or against. You'll figure out your shape eventually. Everyone does, whether they mean to or not."

By early afternoon the yard had begun to fill — not the packed, roaring crowd Cael imagined an actual match night would draw, but enough people, drifting in from the surrounding streets on the strength of word that two scheduled bouts would run that afternoon, that the empty stage from the morning had become, gradually and without any single dramatic moment, an actual venue.

Cael found a spot at the edge, on an overturned barrel that gave him a clean line of sight to the circle Vell — he didn't know her name yet, not this early, just recognized her by the ledger she carried and the authority with which people made room for her — had chalked into the packed dirt an hour before.

The first bout was between two Copper-tier practitioners: a lean, quick young man with a Blade Path, and an older woman with a grappling style Cael didn't yet have a name for, built around closing distance fast and staying inside an opponent's reach where a blade extension lost most of its advantage.

He opened his notebook and started writing before the fight had even begun, cataloguing what he could observe in the stillness before the first exchange — the Blade fighter's stance, weight forward, the angle of his bladed forearm held low and slightly behind him, ready to whip forward rather than lead; the grappler's stance, lower, weight centered, hands open rather than fisted, the posture of someone whose whole plan depended on getting close enough that striking distance stopped mattering.

Vell called the start with a single word Cael didn't quite catch over the crowd's noise settling.

The grappler moved first, which surprised him — he'd expected the faster fighter to open, not the one whose entire strategy required closing a gap under pressure. She crossed the distance in two steps, weight low, and the Blade fighter's response was immediate: a short, controlled cut aimed at her leading shoulder, meant to open a wound that would make the closing-distance strategy considerably more painful to repeat.

She took the hit. Cael watched her decide to take it, in the half-second before it landed — a small adjustment in her shoulder, angling to absorb rather than avoid, trading a cut for the two feet of distance it would have cost her to dodge properly. It was, he understood even before he'd finished writing it down, a genuinely intelligent trade: the cut was shallow, controlled, the kind of wound a Blade Path fighter delivered when they were testing rather than finishing, and she was inside his guard a half-second later, both hands closing on his extended arm before he could retract it.

The second exchange lasted four seconds and ended the fight. She used his own committed extension against him, turning the blade arm's own leverage into a joint lock that put him on the ground before he'd finished processing that his advantage had become a liability. Vell called it clean, decisive, and the crowd's reaction — a scattered mix of groans from people who'd bet on speed and appreciative murmurs from people who'd recognized what they'd just watched — told Cael something about how differently a room full of experienced observers read a fight compared to how he might have read it, a month ago, before any of this.

He wrote for a full minute after the bout ended, not wanting to lose the shape of it: *she let herself get hit on purpose. The wound she took to close distance was smaller than the wound she'd have needed to trade for the same distance later, once he'd adjusted to her approach. She understood the trade before he understood there was one to make.*

The second bout ran longer — two Bronze-tier fighters, more evenly matched, the exchanges longer and more tactical, both of them clearly experienced enough that neither offered the kind of clean openings the first bout's loser had given away. Cael filled four pages of his notebook watching it: the tells before named techniques, the moments where fatigue changed decision-making somewhere around the sixth exchange, the way both fighters adjusted their strategy mid-fight based on what they'd learned about each other in the earlier rounds, discarding approaches that weren't working with a speed that suggested real experience rather than natural talent alone.

By the end, he had more written about the two fights than he'd written about anything since Weaver's Row — pages of tells and openings and granular observations that felt, even as he wrote them, like they were building toward something he didn't have a name for yet.

Lira watched him watching. At one point she leaned over, close enough that he caught the effort-and-dust smell of someone who'd been training all morning. "You're not watching it like a spectator."

"How am I watching it?"

"Like you're going to need this information later. Like it's homework instead of entertainment." She didn't say anything else about it, but he noticed she watched him a little more closely after that, the way she'd watch an opponent she was starting to take seriously rather than a curiosity she'd decided to be kind to.

---

Before they parted for the evening, Lira walked with him as far as the crossroads where they'd first met, the notice board behind them lit orange by the setting sun. She stopped there, arms crossed, studying him with the same appraising look she'd given him on his first morning in the district.

"You're going to put your name forward," she said. Not a question.

"Probably."

"Not probably. You've already decided. You decided sometime during the second bout, I watched it happen — you stopped writing for about ten seconds and just watched, and when you started writing again it was a different kind of writing." She said this with the flat satisfaction of someone who enjoyed being right about people. "I'm not telling you that to be clever. I'm telling you because you should know what you look like when you've made a decision, since you clearly don't."

"Is that useful information?"

"Enormously. It means you can't bluff me, which is either going to be very convenient or very inconvenient depending on what you end up needing from me." She pushed off from where she'd been leaning. "Vell runs matches most afternoons at the Cinder House. If you're serious, go find her tomorrow morning, tell her you want to fight unrated, and let her match you against someone. She'll ask who's vouching for you."

"Do I need someone vouching for me?"

"Not strictly. It helps. New unrated fighters with nobody speaking for them get matched more conservatively — safer opponents, lower stakes, because nobody knows if they're going to fold in one exchange or actually last." Something in her expression shifted, a decision arriving the way her decisions seemed to arrive, quickly and without visible deliberation. "I'll vouch for you, if you want it. I don't do that for everyone. Mostly because most people don't spend eleven minutes reading a notice board like it's a puzzle they're determined to solve."

He hadn't expected the offer, and it took him a half-second longer than it should have to answer, which she clearly noticed and, for once, didn't comment on.

"I'd want that," he said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. If you embarrass me out there, I reserve the right to deny ever having met you." The corner of her mouth moved, not quite a smile, close enough to one. "Tomorrow, then. Cinder House, mid-morning. Bring the notebook. You're going to want it after, even if you don't think you will."

She left him at the crossroads and cut off toward whatever she called home in the district, and Cael watched her go for a moment before turning the other direction, toward Torvin's, the evening settling gold and then grey around him the way it did most days here, a color he was beginning to associate less with Denvash's mornings and more with this unfamiliar place that was, in small increments he was only starting to notice, becoming familiar anyway.

---

That evening, back at Torvin's boarding house, Cael reviewed his notes by lamplight — pages of observations, organized in a system he'd started developing without quite deciding to: opponent, Path, tells, timing windows, what worked and what didn't. He read back through the day's entries twice, adding small clarifications in the margins where his handwriting had outrun his thinking, the way it sometimes did when he was trying to capture something before it faded.

He thought about Hesk's notebook, open in his bag beside this one — *Your body is going to learn before your mind does* — and found himself turning the sentence over with a new kind of attention now that he'd spent a full day watching bodies do exactly the kind of learning Hesk seemed to be describing. He hadn't understood, reading it on the road out of Denvash, how literally he was meant to take it. He was beginning to.

He thought about Lira's certainty that entering the circuit was the fastest way to find out what he could do, and about the fear underneath that certainty — that finding out might cost more than he was prepared to pay, that the circuit's no-kill rule was a fact everyone agreed to act like was true rather than a guarantee, that he was fourteen years old and about to volunteer to be hit by strangers who had spent years learning how to hit people effectively.

He thought, too, about the grappler in the first bout — the deliberate calculation of taking a smaller wound now to avoid a larger one later — and understood, turning it over, that this was closer to the actual shape of what he was about to do than anything Lira had said outright. Not recklessness. Not even courage, exactly, though he suspected it would look like courage from the outside. Just a trade, made with open eyes, because the alternative — staying safe, staying unknown, staying exactly as uncertain about himself as he'd been since Weaver's Row — was a worse trade in the long run, even if it felt, most days, like the safer one.

He wrote, at the bottom of the day's notes: *I don't know what I am yet. But I know how to watch. That's not nothing. That might be where this starts.*

Underneath it, after a pause, he added one more line, smaller, in the private hand he used for things he wasn't fully ready to examine: *Lira offered to vouch for me. I don't fully understand yet what that costs her, if it costs her anything. I should find out before I let her spend it.*

He closed the notebook, put out the lamp, and lay awake a while listening to the district settle around him — voices from the street below, a distant sound that might have been music or might have been an argument, the wheezing man across the room already asleep, the texture of a place that never fully went quiet, not the way Denvash's Outer District had gone quiet after dark, but a different, restless kind of quiet that he was starting to find, against his own expectations, almost restful.

Tomorrow, Lira had said, if he still wanted to, he could put his name forward for an unrated bout.

He thought he did want to. He wasn't entirely sure that wanting to was the same as being ready. He suspected, lying there in the dark with both notebooks within reach on the floor beside his cot, that nobody ever really knew the difference until after — and that the only way to find out which one he was, tonight, was to be standing in that circle tomorrow afternoon, whatever came of it.

He slept eventually, more easily than he expected to, and dreamed, as far as he could remember in the morning, of nothing in particular — which he decided, turning it over with his tea before the sun was fully up, was itself a kind of answer.

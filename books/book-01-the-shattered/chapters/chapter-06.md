# Chapter 6 — Lira

The crossroads where the notice board stood was busier at this hour than it had been the first time he'd found it — carts moving through toward the practitioners' quarter, a woman selling grilled skewers from a cart with a smell that made his stomach remind him he'd skipped the midday meal, two boys chasing something small and fast between the legs of passersby with the joyful disregard for consequence that only applied to people young enough not to have learned yet what consequences cost.

Cael had come back to the board on purpose this time, not by accident the way he'd found it the first morning. He'd spent the intervening day mapping more of the district's geography — the boarding house to the food market, the food market to the practitioners' quarter, the practitioners' quarter back to this crossroads — and had discovered that returning to a place with a question already in mind changed how he read it. The notices hadn't changed. He had.

He was three notices deep into a second reading — *Fenrow circuit, undercard slots open, ask for Dava* — when a voice behind him said something that made the whole morning reorganize itself around a single fact he hadn't previously considered: that he was, himself, being read the same way he read everything else.

"You've been reading that board for eleven minutes."

Cael turned.

The girl behind him was perhaps a year older, dark-haired, dressed in clothes that had been good once and were now practical in the way of things worn hard by someone who didn't have the luxury of replacing them. She had a canvas wrap slung over one shoulder that he'd learn later held her training gear, and she stood with a loose, weight-forward readiness that he didn't yet have the vocabulary to identify as a fighter's habit but that registered, somewhere beneath conscious notice, as *different from how other people stand.* She was looking at him with an expression that managed to be both entirely unbothered and intensely focused at the same time, which he would come to learn was more or less her default setting.

"I wasn't counting," he said.

"I was." She nodded at the board. "Eleven minutes. Most people take about ninety seconds to figure out what that board actually is, and then either walk away because they're not interested or start asking around because they are. You did neither. You just kept reading it like it was a puzzle."

"It is a puzzle. It's advertising something without saying what it is."

"That's the point. It's not for people who need it explained." She tilted her head, appraising him with the unhurried thoroughness of someone deciding whether a new variable in her environment was worth further attention. Her eyes moved once, briefly, over the travel-worn state of his clothes, the bag he wasn't currently carrying but had clearly been carrying recently, the alertness of someone who hadn't fully unpacked yet, mentally or otherwise. "So which are you. Interested, or just cataloguing?"

"Is there a third option?"

"There's always a third option. Most people are too busy pretending there isn't." She stuck out a hand, which was a slightly more formal gesture than anything else about her suggested. "Lira."

"Cael." He shook it. Her grip was firm, calloused in a way that told him something about how she spent her mornings before he'd had time to ask.

"Just Cael?"

"Just Cael." It had, until recently, been Caelen Hesk-ward, formally, on documents. Out here, with no registry checking anything, he found he preferred the shorter version — his own, chosen rather than assigned, and the choosing itself had felt like the first genuinely voluntary decision he'd made since Weaver's Row.

Lira studied him a moment longer, then nodded, apparently satisfied with something, and leaned against the post beside the notice board with the ease of someone who had leaned against it many times before, in exactly that spot, for exactly this purpose.

"You're new," she said. "Not new-to-the-district new — new-new. Everything about you still moves like you're expecting the ground to be a specific shape and it keeps not being that shape."

"Is it that obvious?"

"Painfully." She said it without unkindness. "Took me about a month to stop doing it. You get used to a place having rules, even bad ones, and then you land somewhere the rules are just — suggestions, mostly, made up by whoever got there first and decided to enforce them. It's disorienting until it isn't."

"How long have you been here?"

"Four months." Something in her tone shifted, very slightly — not closing off exactly, but choosing its next words with more care than the conversation had needed so far. "Expelled from my academy. Insubordination, officially. Unofficially, I demonstrated a more efficient variant of a technique during a practical exam, and the examiner decided that was worse than a student who couldn't demonstrate anything at all."

"That seems like a strange thing to punish."

"It's not strange if you understand what the exam was actually testing." She said this with the flat precision of someone who had turned the injustice of it over in her mind enough times to have worn the edges smooth. "It wasn't testing whether I could do the technique. It was testing whether I could do it *the way I was told,* which is a different skill entirely, and one I turned out to be bad at."

"What was the technique?"

She considered whether to answer that, weighing it the way she seemed to weigh most things — quickly, but not carelessly. "A deflection sequence. Three-part, standard academy curriculum, the kind every Wind Path student learns in their second year and every instructor teaches exactly the same way because that's how it's always been taught. I found a version that skipped the middle part entirely — used the redirected momentum from the first deflection to power the third directly, instead of resetting between them the way the textbook says to. Faster. Used less energy. Worked better against a real opponent than the textbook version ever had, because a real opponent doesn't wait politely through your reset." A small, humorless smile. "The examiner called it reckless. Said I'd gotten lucky, that the technique 'as taught' existed for good pedagogical reasons I wasn't senior enough to understand yet, and that demonstrating an unapproved variant during a formal evaluation showed poor judgment regardless of whether it happened to work."

"Did you argue?"

"I asked her to let me demonstrate it again, against a second opponent, to prove it wasn't luck." Lira's voice stayed even, but something underneath it had gone very still, the stillness of an old argument that had never quite finished arguing itself out. "She said that wasn't the point. I said if it wasn't about whether the technique worked, what exactly was the exam testing. She didn't have a good answer to that. She had authority instead, which turned out to be sufficient." A short laugh, more air than humor. "So. Here I am. Circuit fighter, occasional problem-solver, professional evaluator of new arrivals who stand in front of notice boards for eleven minutes."

"Circuit fighter."

"Copper Rank 2, Wind Path. Fast, not strong, which means I win by not being where the hit lands rather than by surviving it if it does." She said this the way Cael imagined a craftsman described their tools — practical, unromantic, entirely without the performance he half-expected from someone who fought for money in an unregulated district. "Which brings me back to my original question. Interested, or just cataloguing?"

A boy jogged past them, close enough that Cael instinctively stepped back — Lira didn't move at all, some part of her already having registered and dismissed the boy as no threat before Cael's own reaction had finished. He noticed her noticing him notice this, and something in her expression flickered — filed away, not commented on.

Cael considered the honest answer, which was that he genuinely didn't know yet, and decided the honest answer was also the useful one.

"I don't know what I'd be entering as," he said. "I don't have a Path declaration."

Lira's eyebrows went up, very slightly — the first genuinely surprised expression she'd shown. "Everyone has a Path declaration. Even the ones who got a bad draw have *something.*"

"I don't."

"Copper Rank 0? That's not really a thing, but I've heard people use it loosely for—"

"I don't have a tier or a rank," Cael said. "I have a classification the registry doesn't have a category for."

Lira went quiet in a way that was different from her previous quiets — sharper, more focused, the attention of someone whose curiosity had just been handed something genuinely unfamiliar instead of merely new. She'd stopped leaning against the post. "That's not a thing I've heard of. And I've heard of a lot of things. This district collects unusual people. I've met a man who claims his Path only works underwater and a woman who swears hers only activates when she's lying, which — if true — makes her either the most useless or the most dangerous person in Ardenmere depending who's asking. I haven't heard of what you're describing."

"[SHATTERED]," Cael said. He'd decided, somewhere on the road east, that he was going to have to say the word out loud to people eventually, and that deciding how and when he said it — rather than having it said about him — was one of the few pieces of control available to him. Better to spend it here, on someone who'd already demonstrated she noticed things and didn't perform reactions she didn't feel, than to have it discovered sideways later, the way it had already been discovered by a gate guard's averted eyes and a neighbor's careful absence of eye contact.

Lira didn't flinch. She didn't do the thing the gate guard had done, or the thing half of Denvash had done in the two days between his Kindling and his leaving. She just looked at him for a long moment, recalibrating, the way Cael imagined she'd recalibrate a fighting stance mid-exchange when the opponent did something she hadn't trained against — not alarmed by the surprise, just genuinely, visibly processing it.

"Huh," she said, finally. "I don't actually know what that means."

"Neither do I, mostly."

"That's either the most interesting thing anyone's told me in four months, or a very elaborate way of avoiding the question about whether you can fight." A ghost of a smile. "Possibly both."

"Possibly both," Cael agreed.

"Can you fight?"

"I don't know. I've never tried to hurt anyone on purpose."

"That's not what I asked." She pushed off from the post, circling him slowly, the way he imagined she'd circle an opponent she was sizing up before the first exchange — not threatening, just assessing, with the unselfconscious thoroughness of someone whose profession required her to read bodies for information. She stopped at his shoulder, studying the way he held it, the faint stiffness that was still healing from nothing in particular, just the accumulated tension of the last week. "I asked if you can fight. Not if you've fought. Different question."

"I don't understand the difference."

"You will." She stopped circling and faced him directly, close enough now that he could see the evaluating quality of her attention up close — not unkind, just thorough, the look of someone who had learned early that underestimating people cost more than overestimating them. "Everyone who's any good at this started before they knew they'd started. You'll have moved a certain way, at some point, without deciding to — dodged something, caught something, reacted faster than you had any business reacting. Most people don't notice it happening to themselves. I notice it happening to other people, which is a slightly useful skill in a place like this."

A cart came around the crossroads too fast, the driver's attention on something behind him rather than in front, and for a half-second it looked like it was going to clip the grilled-skewer vendor's stall square on. Cael saw it a beat before it happened and had already started the useless motion of reaching out, too far away to matter.

Lira moved before he'd finished registering the danger. One step, low and economical, and she had the vendor by the shoulder and pulled her back out of the cart's path with a motion so unhurried it looked almost bored — the cart rattled through the space the woman had been standing in a half-second later, the driver shouting an apology over his shoulder that nobody bothered acknowledging.

The vendor swore, more startled than hurt, and went back to arranging her skewers as though nothing had happened, the unbothered resilience of someone who'd lived in a crowded district long enough that near-misses barely registered as events worth having feelings about.

Lira turned back to Cael, entirely unruffled, as though she hadn't just crossed six feet of open ground faster than he could track the motion.

"That," she said, "is what I mean."

"You didn't even look like you were trying."

"I wasn't. That's the whole point." She rolled her shoulder once, a habitual gesture rather than a necessary one. "Wind Path, at Copper tier, doesn't make me fast the way people imagine fast — it's not a burst, not really, not yet. It's more like the world gets slightly more negotiable around me for about a second at a time. I didn't decide to move. My body decided the cart was a problem before the rest of me finished agreeing." She studied him, something calculating behind the casual delivery. "That's the thing I keep trying to explain. You don't get good at this by deciding to be fast. You get good at it by paying attention to the moments your body already decided something without asking you first, and then learning to trust those moments instead of arguing with them."

Cael thought about Hesk's notebook, the line he hadn't stopped turning over since the road out of Denvash. *Your body is going to learn before your mind does.* He hadn't expected to see it demonstrated quite this plainly, quite this soon, by someone who'd known him for less than a quarter hour.

"How many times has that happened to you," he asked. "Before you noticed it happening."

"Dozens, probably, before I had a name for it. My old instructor called it 'the gap' — the space between what your training tells you to do and what your body actually does when there isn't time to consult the training." She said this with the fondness people reserve for a teacher they'd otherwise stopped thinking about. "Most people spend their whole time in that gap trying to close it — trying to make the trained response and the instinctive one match up perfectly, every time, so there's no daylight between them. I used to think that was the goal too." A small, private shrug. "I don't think that anymore. I think the gap is where the interesting stuff actually happens. You just have to survive enough passes through it to find out."

Cael thought about the third exchange in the Ardenmere circuit match, weeks ago now, listening on the radio with Hesk. *She dropped her stance before the commitment. Counter-intuitive. It bought her the angle.* He thought about how badly he'd wanted to understand what she'd been seeing in that moment, sitting in Hesk's kitchen with the radio's tinny commentary filling in half the picture and his own imagination filling in the rest.

He thought, too, though he didn't say it yet, about Hesk's notebook — *your body is going to learn before your mind does* — and wondered, for the first time with something more specific than abstract worry, whether it had already started and he simply hadn't been watching closely enough to catch himself doing it. He thought about the gate guard at Denvash's Outer wall, and the unplaceable quickness with which he'd stepped aside from the cart he hadn't consciously seen coming, two days into the road east. He hadn't written that down at the time. He was starting to wish he had.

"I don't know if it's happened yet," he said, honestly. "I'd like to find out."

"That's a better answer than most people give me." Lira was quiet a moment, studying him with the same attention she'd used earlier to size up the danger of the cart — fast, thorough, already three steps ahead of whatever conclusion she was walking toward. "Most people who don't know what they're made of either avoid finding out forever, or they walk straight into the circuit the first week and get carried out the first day, learning nothing except that it hurts. You're doing neither. You're doing the thing smart people do, which almost nobody actually does, because it requires admitting you don't already know the answer." She tilted her head. "That's rarer than talent, where I'm from."

"Where are you from?"

"Not here." She said it lightly enough that he understood, without needing it spelled out, that it was a door she wasn't opening today, and possibly not for a while — a mirror, he thought, of his own careful management of what he'd chosen to say and when. She seemed to reach a decision, the way she apparently reached most decisions — quickly, and without much visible deliberation once the decision point actually arrived. "Tell you what. I train most mornings, practitioners' quarter, the yard behind the Cinder House. Come watch. Don't fight anything yet — you don't know what you're working with, and walking into the circuit blind is a good way to find out the hard way instead of the useful way. Watch first. See what your body does when it's not being asked permission."

"Why would you offer that?"

Lira shrugged, an easy, unbothered gesture that Cael would later learn covered for a far more considered motive than it let on. "Because you're the most interesting thing that's walked into this district in four months, and I get bored easily, and boredom in a place like this is how people end up doing genuinely stupid things to entertain themselves. You're cheaper entertainment than most of the alternatives." A beat. "And because 'classification the registry doesn't have a category for' is exactly the kind of problem I like. I'm good at problems nobody's solved yet. Ask anyone who's fought me."

"That's not really an answer either."

"No," she agreed, entirely unbothered by being caught at it. "It's not. You'll get a real one eventually, if you stick around long enough to earn it." She started walking, then paused and looked back at him, the late-afternoon light catching an expression that was, for just a moment, less performed than everything else she'd shown him so far — something closer to genuine curiosity, unguarded, the vulnerability of someone who didn't let people see her actually wanting something very often. "Cinder House. Tomorrow morning, before the market gets loud. Don't be late. I don't wait for people twice."

"I'll be there."

"I know," she said, with the certainty of someone who had already decided the outcome of a question she'd only just asked, and walked off into the crowd without looking back, in the exact manner Cael had walked away from Hesk two days before — not because she didn't care to look, he suspected, but because she'd already decided what she needed to know.

---

He stood at the notice board a while longer after she'd gone, though he wasn't reading it anymore.

The crossroads had filled further while they'd talked — the grilled-skewer cart doing brisk business now, a small crowd gathered around a man demonstrating something with a set of weighted cords, the metal-capped ends catching the low sun each time he whipped them through a turn. The crowd made the low murmur of people watching something they didn't fully understand but recognized, instinctively, as skill — not a Path, Cael was fairly sure, just discipline applied long enough that it started to look like one, which was itself an interesting data point he filed away without quite deciding to. The general late-afternoon current of a district settling into its evening rhythm moved around him, unhurried, indifferent to the conversation he was still turning over. He watched it without really seeing it, replaying what had just happened the way he'd replay a page of his notebook, checking for what he'd learned and what he'd given away in exchange.

He thought about the word he'd said out loud for the first time to a stranger, and about the fact that saying it had cost him something and gained him something in almost equal measure — a genuine reaction, unmanaged, from someone who hadn't flinched and hadn't performed sympathy either, just recalibrated and kept talking, the way she'd apparently recalibrate anything unexpected the fight in front of her produced. It was, he realized, the first entirely honest conversation he'd had about his classification with anyone who wasn't Hesk, and it had gone better than he'd let himself hope any such conversation could go.

He thought about her certainty — *I know* — spoken with the flat confidence of someone who'd already filed him under a category she trusted, before she had any actual evidence he'd show up. He wasn't sure yet whether that certainty was a compliment or simply the way she moved through the world, deciding things quickly and adjusting only when proven wrong. He suspected, from the little he'd already seen, that it was probably both, and that the distinction wouldn't matter much once he knew her better.

He thought, too, about the difference between this conversation and every conversation he'd had about [SHATTERED] before it. In Denvash, the word had traveled ahead of him — people had known before he'd said anything, had already decided how to hold their faces before he was close enough to see them deciding it. Here, for the first time since Weaver's Row, he had gotten to choose the order of things: meet a person first, form some rough sense of who they were, and only then hand them the one fact capable of changing everything they thought they knew. It was a small thing, procedurally. It felt, standing in the emptying crossroads with the light going down, like the first real advantage he'd had since his Kindling — not power, not safety, just the simple, unglamorous ability to decide when a hard thing got said instead of having it said at him.

He thought about Hesk, too — not with the sharp ache of the first days on the road, but with something quieter, more settled, the ache of missing someone you trust rather than someone you're afraid for. He would write to him tonight, he decided. Not everything — not the classification conversation with a stranger at a notice board, not yet, that felt like something he wanted to hold a while longer before he handed it to anyone else, even Hesk. But something. *Found somewhere to sleep. Found the market. Found someone interesting,* maybe, which was true and also managed to say almost nothing at all, which felt, for a first letter home, like exactly the right amount to say.

He took out his notebook, found a blank page, and wrote: *Lira. Copper Rank 2, Wind Path. Circuit fighter. Notices things other people miss — including, apparently, me. Says everyone's body learns before their mind does. Offered to let me watch her train.*

He paused, then added one more line, because it felt true and because the notebook was, more than anything else, the place where he kept the things that felt true before he was ready to say them out loud.

*First person outside Denvash who's looked at [SHATTERED] like a question instead of a verdict.*

He closed the notebook and stood for a moment longer, watching the crossroads empty its afternoon crowd into the evening one, the light going long and gold across the packed dirt the way it had gone long and gold across a different street, in a different district, on a morning that already felt like it belonged to someone he used to be.

He found his way back to Torvin's boarding house through streets that were beginning, very slightly, to feel less like a maze and more like a place — a turn he now recognized without having to think about it, a baker's stall he'd learned sold the good bread only before midday, the angle a certain alley cut that saved him two minutes if he remembered to take it. Small things. The kind of things that accumulated, without ceremony, into the beginning of belonging somewhere.

He slept better than he had in three nights.

Tomorrow, he would go to the Cinder House. Tomorrow, he would start finding out what his body already knew.

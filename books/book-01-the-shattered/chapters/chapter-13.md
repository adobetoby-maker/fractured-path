# Chapter 13 — The Notice

Warden Coss had been a Compact field agent for eleven years, and in that time he had developed a professional philosophy he privately considered his most useful asset: most files were exactly as boring as they looked, and the ones that weren't usually announced themselves early, if you were paying attention.

He had built an entire career on paying attention. Not brilliantly — Coss did not consider himself a brilliant man, and had made peace with that assessment somewhere around year four, when he'd watched a genuinely brilliant colleague burn out chasing patterns that weren't there and get reassigned to records processing for his trouble. Brilliance found things that weren't there as often as it found things that were. Attention, applied consistently over years, found the real things eventually and rarely found the imaginary ones. Coss had chosen attention. It had served him well enough that he was, at forty-one, one of the more trusted mid-level field agents in the regional office, sent out for exactly the kind of assignment that required someone who wouldn't embellish a report or panic at an unusual result.

Cael Hesk-ward's file was not announcing itself in any of the usual ways. That was precisely what bothered him.

He sat in the Ardenmere district office — a cramped room with one window and a desk that had clearly belonged to someone else before him, judging by the ink stains and the persistent wobble in the left front leg — and read through the file for the fourth time since arriving. He had read case files thin and thick, urgent and routine, complete and criminally incomplete, and had developed over the years a working sense for what a file's shape usually said about the person it described. Thick files meant complicated lives, or complicated people, or both. Thin files usually meant simple ones — a citizen who'd done little to attract attention, moving through a life the Compact had no reason to document closely.

This file was thin in a way that didn't match its subject. A fourteen-year-old, classified [SHATTERED] — even a recent classification of that rarity should have generated, at minimum, a preliminary risk assessment from the certifying district, a residential compliance note establishing the household's standing implications, and some record of the guardian's guild-standing review being formally opened. Coss had seen two prior [SHATTERED] files in his career, both decades-old archival records he'd pulled once out of professional curiosity after hearing the classification mentioned at a training seminar, and both had run to dozens of pages within the first month of classification alone. This file had almost none of that. It had the bare registration, the expulsion notice, and a passive flag dated to Cael's arrival at Ardenmere's district gate — a flag that, Coss noted with the irritation of a man who valued procedural cleanliness, had somehow triggered a routine sweep request three weeks later without any of the standard justification paperwork that should have accompanied it.

He went through the missing pieces methodically, the way he'd learned to audit a file when something about it didn't sit right: no preliminary risk assessment, though the certifying district — Denvash — had a functioning compliance office that produced these routinely for standard classifications. No residential compliance note beyond the bare expulsion language, though the standing-holder's guild status was explicitly flagged as at risk in that same expulsion notice, which should have triggered an automatic cross-reference to the guardian's own file. No indication that anyone had actually opened a formal case file at all, in the administrative sense — just a registration entry, a notice, and a flag, floating without the connective tissue a case this unusual should have generated.

Someone had requested the sweep. The request had come through channels above Coss's clearance to trace clearly — routed through a regional authorization code he didn't recognize, attached to instructions vague enough that he suspected the person who'd written them either didn't know precisely what they wanted confirmed, or knew precisely and had chosen language that wouldn't commit them to it in writing. *Confirm location, status, and compliance.* He had read hundreds of assignment briefs in his career. Most of them told him, plainly, what the regional office was actually worried about. This one told him nothing, which was itself a kind of telling.

Coss was not a cruel man. He was, by his own accounting, a competent one, which was different, and which he'd found over eleven years to matter more in practice than the alternative virtues people liked to claim for themselves. He did his job. He filed accurate reports. He did not, as a rule, involve himself in speculation about motives above his pay grade, because speculation above your pay grade in the Compact was a good way to acquire problems you hadn't previously had — he'd watched that happen to colleagues too, ambitious ones who'd asked the wrong question of the wrong superior and found their careers quietly narrowed afterward, reassigned to postings that were technically lateral and functionally punitive.

But an eleven-year professional instinct was an eleven-year professional instinct, and his was telling him, quietly and persistently, that this file was thinner than it should be for reasons that had nothing to do with administrative sloppiness and everything to do with someone, somewhere, wanting it to stay that way.

He closed the file, made a note in his own working log — *file inconsistent with standard [SHATTERED] documentation practice; flag for follow-up upon return* — and, before starting the fieldwork itself, walked the short distance to Ardenmere's own small administrative post, on the theory that whoever had actually filed the sweep request locally might remember something the paperwork hadn't captured.

The post was staffed by a single overworked clerk named Halvern, a man perhaps ten years Coss's junior who managed district registrations, minor permit disputes, and — apparently, though it clearly wasn't his favorite part of the job — the occasional Compact liaison request that came down from regional. Halvern pulled the relevant log without much enthusiasm, licked a thumb, and paged through it while Coss waited.

"Sweep request," Halvern said, eventually, tapping an entry. "Came through about three weeks back. Not from us."

"Not from Ardenmere."

"Not from anyone in Ardenmere, no. We just get told to provide a body to walk the route and confirm nobody's setting fire to anything. Regional authorization code on it, not a name." He turned the log so Coss could see it — a single terse line, an authorization string Coss didn't recognize any better on paper than he had in his own file. "Happens maybe twice a year, usually for something obvious — a fugitive, a guild dispute gone physical. This one didn't say why. We don't usually ask why. Asking why tends to get you a longer form to fill out and no better answer."

"Who walked the route?"

"Local constable, one afternoon, few hours — we lent him a Compact pin for the walk, since regional wanted it to read as official rather than local, which tells you something about how much they cared how it looked and how little they cared who actually did the looking. Nothing came of it — that's what the follow-up note says, anyway." Halvern shrugged, already sliding the log back into its place on the shelf, his attention visibly returning to the stack of permit disputes that constituted the actual bulk of his working week. "For what it's worth, most of us out here figure whatever this is about, it's not really an Ardenmere problem. We just carry the message."

It wasn't much. It confirmed what the file had already suggested — an authorization from somewhere above local reach, arriving without explanation, executed without incident, and filed away by people who had learned, correctly, that asking questions about it wasn't worth the extra paperwork. Coss thanked Halvern, who barely looked up from the permit disputes, and went to begin the fieldwork proper with one additional, unwelcome certainty added to what he'd started the morning with: whoever wanted eyes on Cael Hesk-ward, they wanted it badly enough to spend a regional authorization on a district that, by every ordinary measure, didn't warrant one.

---

He spent two days in Ardenmere doing the actual work his assignment required, and the two days taught him more about how little his assignment's language actually anticipated than about the boy himself.

The boarding house came first. Torvin — a heavyset man with the flat manner of someone who had dealt with Compact officials before and found the experience uniformly unpleasant — answered his questions with the flat, technically-complete cooperation of a man who had learned, somewhere along the way, exactly how much information the law required him to volunteer and had calibrated his answers to that exact minimum.

"Cael Hesk-ward. Boy, fourteen, registered here?"

"Room's paid through the month."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I've got." Torvin didn't look up from the ledger he was pretending to work on. "You want to know if he's here, he's here. You want to know his business, ask him yourself. Not my job to have opinions about my tenants."

Coss had heard variations of this exact non-answer in a dozen boarding houses across a dozen districts over the years, and had long since stopped taking it personally. It wasn't hostility, exactly — it was the practiced neutrality of a district that had learned, collectively, that cooperating too enthusiastically with Compact officials tended to cost you business from everyone else who lived there. He'd tried, early in his career, to push past that neutrality with charm, then with mild pressure, then with the procedural leverage a Compact badge technically afforded him, and had learned each time that all three approaches produced the same result: a slightly worse version of the same non-answer, delivered with a slightly longer memory of having been pushed. He'd stopped pushing years ago. It cost him a small amount of information on visits like this one. It saved him a much larger amount of goodwill on the visits that came after, in districts where word of how a Compact agent behaved traveled faster than the agent himself ever could.

He confirmed the residential registration matched, confirmed the address fell within Unranked-designated status, and left without pressing further, because pressing further on a man like Torvin rarely produced information and reliably produced a longer memory of the visit than Coss wanted to leave behind.

The second task — flagged in his instructions with the careful, non-committal language that told him someone above his pay grade cared about the answer without wanting to be quoted asking for it — was confirming whether the subject appeared to be engaging in unsanctioned Path practice.

This last part was, on paper, straightforward. Coss walked the district across both days, the way he always did on an assignment like this — not asking direct questions, which tended to make people defensive and unreliable, but moving through markets and thoroughfares with the patient attentiveness that eleven years had trained into him, listening for names, watching for patterns, occasionally asking a vendor or a passerby a question phrased to sound like idle curiosity rather than official inquiry.

He learned things about Ardenmere's Unranked District in those two days that his procedural training hadn't prepared him for — that it functioned, in practice, as something closer to a self-governing town than a holding pen for the Compact's excess population, with its own economy, its own social hierarchies, its own methods of resolving disputes that had nothing to do with any authority Coss represented. He found this mildly unsettling in a way he couldn't fully articulate — the unease of a man realizing that a significant portion of the population he was nominally responsible for monitoring operated in a space his training simply hadn't equipped him to read.

He found nothing that directly implicated Cael in anything the Compact would formally classify as unsanctioned practice, because the entire premise of his inquiry rested on an assumption the Compact's procedural framework hadn't updated in years: that unsanctioned practice would take a form the Compact recognized as practice at all — a demonstrated Path ability, a declared technique, something with the shape of what the certification offices spent their days evaluating.

He did not think to ask about the circuit. He heard the word twice, in passing, from vendors discussing weekend plans in the offhand way people discussed entertainment they assumed everyone already understood the context of, and filed it, without examining it further, under the general category of local color — betting, gambling, the kind of informal recreation every district developed regardless of its official designation. It did not occur to him that "the circuit" might be a venue in the sense his training cared about, because the entire concept of unsanctioned, unregulated Path combat existing openly, in a district he was actively surveying, sat so far outside his procedural framework's assumptions that his attention simply slid past it, the way attention slides past anything it hasn't been trained to recognize as significant.

The irony, which Coss would not appreciate until considerably later, was precise and complete: the subject of his compliance investigation was, at that exact moment on his second afternoon in Ardenmere, three streets away in the Cinder House yard, engaging in exactly the unsanctioned practice Coss had been sent to rule out — in a venue his procedural framework hadn't equipped him to recognize as a venue at all.

---

On his second evening in Ardenmere, having exhausted the standard verification steps without producing anything that resolved the strange thinness of the file, Coss returned to his cramped temporary office and did the one thing his instructions actually required of him with any specificity: he prepared to summon the subject for formal compliance evaluation.

He sat with the blank summons form for longer than the task usually required. This was not indecision, exactly — Coss did not experience much of what he'd call indecision, having built his career on the discipline of acting once he'd gathered sufficient information rather than deliberating past the point of usefulness. It was something closer to a careful accounting of what he actually knew, laid out before committing it to a document that would outlive the moment of writing it.

What he knew: a fourteen-year-old, classified [SHATTERED], whose file was suspiciously incomplete in ways that suggested deliberate thinning rather than administrative neglect. A sweep request that had bypassed standard justification procedures, routed from somewhere above his clearance. Instructions vague enough to suggest the requesting party either didn't know what they wanted or didn't want it written down. Two days of district observation that had produced nothing concrete, because his procedural framework hadn't been built for a district that operated the way Ardenmere's Unranked quarter actually operated.

What he didn't know, and what bothered him more than anything the file actually contained, was *why.* A [SHATTERED] classification was rare enough to be genuinely unusual, and unusual things drew Compact attention as a matter of institutional habit — but the quality of attention this file had drawn, the careful incompleteness of its documentation, the routing that obscured rather than clarified its origin, suggested something more deliberate than ordinary bureaucratic curiosity. Coss had eleven years of experience distinguishing ordinary institutional thoroughness from something else, and this had the shape of something else.

He wrote the summons himself, by hand, in the careful, unembellished language the Compact's forms required — a summons for a compliance evaluation at the Ardenmere district office, forty-eight hours from delivery. He filled in the standard clauses without hesitation: subject identification, evaluation location, appearance window.

He paused at the final clause.

*Non-appearance to result in a formal compliance flag and, per standard procedure, a filing for physical retrieval.*

He had written this exact clause, in this exact language, perhaps a dozen times in eleven years — always for adults, always in contexts where "physical retrieval" meant something procedurally unpleasant but rarely genuinely dangerous: an escort to a mandatory evaluation, occasionally an uncomfortable conversation with a resistant subject, never, in his own direct experience, anything that had produced actual harm. He had never once, in eleven years, written it for a child.

He sat with the pen above the paper for a long moment, doing the mental accounting that had kept his career clean for over a decade: was there a version of this summons that omitted the clause, or softened it, that still satisfied the procedural requirement? He reviewed the regulation in his memory, the same way he'd reviewed hundreds of regulations over the years, and found, with the discomfort of a man whose professional integrity depended on following rules he sometimes disliked, that there wasn't. The clause was standard. Omitting it would be a procedural deviation he couldn't justify in writing, and Coss did not, as a rule, act on feelings he couldn't justify in writing — not because he lacked feelings about it, but because feelings without written justification were exactly the kind of thing that got careers quietly narrowed.

He finished the clause. He signed it. He did not enjoy any part of the process, and noted, with the same clinical attention he applied to everything else, that the not-enjoying was itself useful information — a data point about a case that kept producing more discomfort than its paperwork should have justified.

He left the summons with Torvin, who took it with the same flat unhelpfulness he'd offered everything else across the two days — a single glance at the seal, a single unreadable expression, and a wordless nod that Coss chose to interpret as delivery confirmation rather than pursue further.

He returned to his temporary office and filed his preliminary report — a careful, accurate document that recorded exactly what he'd observed and exactly what he hadn't, including a note he debated including and ultimately did: *Subject's file shows documentation gaps inconsistent with standard practice for this classification. Recommend review of original certifying district's compliance procedures.*

It was, he understood even as he filed it, a mildly risky note to include — the kind of observation that could be read as competent diligence or as an unwelcome question about someone else's paperwork, depending entirely on who read it and how they'd chosen to interpret vague instructions he had no visibility into. He filed it anyway. Whatever was happening with this case, above his clearance and beyond his ability to investigate directly, Coss had decided — in the specific, quiet way he decided most things — that he would rather be the agent whose reports were too thorough than the one whose reports had conveniently failed to notice something.

The report would, within the week, land on a desk considerably above his own, attached to a file that someone, somewhere, was still working very hard to keep exactly as thin as it had been when Coss first opened it.

---

Cael found the summons that night, propped against his cot by Torvin, who delivered it with a single unceremonious sentence — "Compact left this. Didn't like the look of it" — and then went back downstairs without further comment, leaving Cael alone with the sealed document and the specific quality of silence that settles over a room when everyone else in it is already asleep.

Cael read it once, standing, in the lamplight of the shared room, the other four cots occupied by their usual sleepers who paid him no attention.

He read it again, sitting down this time, because the second reading required steadier hands than the first, and he wanted to be certain he'd understood every clause correctly rather than reading it through the blur of his own rising alarm.

*Forty-eight hours. Compliance evaluation. Non-appearance results in a formal compliance flag and filing for physical retrieval.*

He read the phrase *physical retrieval* a third time, slowly, the way he read anything that mattered enough to deserve careful attention rather than quick comprehension. It was administrative language, the same careful, bloodless register as the expulsion notice — designed, he understood now with a clarity that felt almost clinical, to make an unpleasant thing sound like a filing procedure rather than what it actually meant: people, sent to take him somewhere, by force if the alternative wasn't offered.

He thought about Hesk's provisions, researched and never fully explained — *there are things I should have told you,* said in a room with a tree growing at an angle outside the window. He thought about the archive entry, four names, three dead within weeks, one unaccounted for after thirty-two years of silence. He thought about the market sighting, the silver insignia glimpsed across a crowded stall, five weeks after the district gate, a sweep that shouldn't have happened by standard procedure and had happened anyway.

He did not, this time, feel the specific, formless dread that had followed him since the Kindling — the kind that had kept him awake in Denvash, cataloguing a ceiling in the dark, waiting for a shape to resolve out of a fear he couldn't yet name. What he felt instead, sitting on the edge of his cot with the summons in his hands, was something closer to clarity — the particular, useful clarity that arrives once a vague and circling fear finally resolves into a document with a deadline on it, something with edges he could actually work against.

Forty-eight hours was a number. Numbers were things he knew how to work with. He had spent the last several weeks building an entire private discipline out of exactly this — taking a vague, frightening unknown and converting it, through patient, careful attention, into something with a shape he could study, map, and eventually answer.

He got out his notebook. Both of them — the log, thick now with weeks of observations, tactical entries, and the private section at the back where his real thoughts lived; and the one from Hesk, its leather cover soft from three weeks of near-constant handling, its pages carrying a decade of a grandfather's careful, unglamorous preparation for a day he'd hoped would never come.

He nearly went straight to work. What stopped him, notebook already open on his knee, was a small realization that arrived with the same clarity as the summons itself: he didn't have to do this part alone. He hadn't, in fact, done much of anything alone since Weaver's Row — not really, not once Lira had entered the picture — and some old habit from Denvash, from a life where he'd mostly solved his own problems quietly and told Hesk about them afterward, had almost let him forget that.

He found her at Torvin's side entrance twenty minutes later, having gone looking rather than waited for morning, and she took one look at his face in the doorway lamplight and didn't bother with a greeting.

"What happened."

He handed her the summons instead of explaining. She read it fast, the way she read most things, her expression staying carefully level right up until she reached the physical retrieval clause, where something in her jaw tightened in a way she didn't quite manage to hide.

"Forty-eight hours."

"Forty-eight hours."

"Okay." She handed it back, already thinking, the same visible shift into problem-solving mode he'd watched her make before a difficult bout — assessing, sorting, discarding options that wouldn't work fast enough to matter. "What do you actually know how to do about this."

"I don't know yet. I know where to look."

"The record office."

"The record office."

She nodded once, decisively, the particular nod that meant a decision had been reached and wasn't going to be revisited. "Then we start there in the morning, first thing, before the market crowds make the archive useless to work in. I'll come with you."

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to." She said it without heat, the same flat certainty she brought to most things she'd already decided. "I'm choosing to. There's a difference, and I'd like you to stop offering me the exit every time something gets difficult. It's starting to feel like you think I'll take it."

He didn't have a ready answer for that, and didn't try to manufacture one. "Okay," he said, instead, which seemed to be the correct response, because something in her shoulders eased slightly.

"Okay," she agreed. "Get some sleep. Not much of it, clearly, but some. You're no good to either of us reading regulation with a fogged head." She turned to go, then paused at the door, looking back at him with an expression that had dropped, briefly, all its usual careful composure. "For what it's worth — whatever this turns into, you're not doing it from nothing. You've got five weeks of proof that you're better at finding gaps than anyone gives you credit for. Find this one too."

She left before he could figure out how to answer that either. He sat back down on his cot, the summons and both notebooks arranged in front of him in the lamplight, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that her last sentence had settled something in his chest that all his own careful reasoning about clarity and edges hadn't quite managed to reach.

He opened the log to a fresh page and wrote the date at the top, and beneath it, the only line he needed to organize everything that came after: *Forty-eight hours. Find the seam.*

He had forty-eight hours. He intended to use every one of them.

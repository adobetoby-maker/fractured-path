# Chapter 18 — Reydan

Cael pieced most of the evening together afterward — from what Dace told him directly the next morning, and from the rest that reached him piecemeal over the following days, carried through the practitioners' quarter's usual current of gossip until enough of it had circulated to settle into something like a full account. It went, as near as he could later reconstruct it, something like this.

He arrived without announcement, the way genuinely dangerous people sometimes did — no letter ahead of him, no circuit contact vouching for his name, just a broad-shouldered stranger paying the standing-room fee at the Ironyard's entrance like anyone else off the street.

Dace clocked him first, the way Dace clocked everyone, cataloguing the stranger's bearing before he'd taken three steps inside: guild posture, the unmistakable economy of someone who'd been drilled since childhood into standing a certain way, sitting a certain way, watching a fight a certain way. Not circuit. Not local. Dace filed the observation and went back to his scheduling board, unbothered — strangers came through often enough that a single unfamiliar face rarely earned more than a glance, and this one hadn't done anything yet to earn a second.

He took a spot near the crowd's edge, close enough to see the floor clearly and far enough back that nobody would need to step around him, and settled there for the evening with the patience of a man who had nowhere else he particularly needed to be.

The Ironyard, that night, was running its ordinary Tuesday rhythm — the scheduling board thick with names, Vell's ledger open at her elbow, the crowd noise settling into the comfortable, familiar roar that meant nobody watching expected anything more than a decent evening's entertainment. Nothing about the room announced that it was about to matter more than any other Tuesday had in months. Rooms rarely did, Cael would have said, had anyone thought to ask him; the ones that turned out to matter almost never looked different going in.

The first bout was ordinary circuit fare: two Copper-tier regulars, a Stone Path grinder working his usual attrition game against a quicker Blade fighter chasing a losing record back toward even. It ended the way most Copper bouts did, cleanly, an opening that widened and then closed properly, and the crowd made the appreciative noise a tidy finish always earned. The stranger didn't make it with them. He watched the whole exchange without shifting his weight once, arms loosely crossed, his attention tracking footwork rather than strikes — the small adjustments a fighter made between exchanges rather than the exchanges themselves — and when the Blade fighter finally found her gap in the fourth minute, he simply noted it the way a man notes the time and waited for the next bout to start.

The second bout was uglier. A Force Path practitioner caught a Wind-adjacent opponent misjudging his distance badly in the third exchange, and the mistake cost the man a hard landing that sent him limping to the boundary rope under his own power but plainly worse for having tried it. Several people near the front looked away. The stranger didn't. He watched the fall happen with the same unhurried attention he'd given the clean finish twenty minutes earlier, nothing in his posture suggesting either enjoyment or discomfort — the stillness of someone who found a fighter's failure exactly as informative as a fighter's success, and who had long since stopped needing to feel one particular way or the other about which he happened to be watching. Not spectating. Assessing. It was, Cael would later recognize, once he'd learned to name it properly, exactly the kind of watching he himself did without thinking — the way a craftsman watches another craftsman's hands, filing away information nobody else in the room particularly needed.

By the second bout's end, a handful of people nearest him had started noticing him back. Not because he'd done anything — he hadn't moved, hadn't spoken, hadn't so much as shifted the angle of his crossed arms in twenty minutes — but because stillness of that quality was rare enough in a room built for noise that it drew attention on its own merits. An old practitioner two spots down murmured something to his neighbor about academy posture, guild money, the kind of half-formed guess the practitioners' quarter made about anyone who carried himself like that without announcing why. Nobody guessed close to the truth. Nobody had any particular reason to.

He spoke to nobody the entire evening except Dace, and even that exchange was brief — a handful of sentences, delivered quietly enough that the practitioners nearest the scheduling board only caught fragments of it before the stranger turned and left the way he'd come, unhurried, entirely unbothered by having drawn no attention at all beyond the kind that dissolved the moment he was out the door.

---

Reydan was twenty-two, Iron-tier Rank 8 — one rank below the Bronze threshold, and by formal standards the best opponent anyone at the Ironyard had faced in months, possibly longer. His Path was Pressure, but not the sustained-wave variant Cael had learned from Feryn the year before, the slow building force that gave an opponent time, however briefly, to read the danger coming. Reydan specialized in rapid burst compression instead: brief, extremely concentrated releases with almost no wind-up at all, and a punishingly short window in which to evade them.

By what eventually reached Ardenmere, he wasn't here for money. His background was professional, comfortable, the kind of fighter who could have spent his entire career inside sanctioned guild competition without ever needing an unsanctioned circuit's modest purse to survive on. He'd trained at a proper academy from age eleven, advanced cleanly through every formal evaluation his tier required, and built, by twenty-two, a reputation solid enough that most guild-track opponents at his level had either already fought him or actively arranged not to. That was, as it turned out, the actual problem. The challenge available inside sanctioned competition had dried up years before he was ready for it to. Guild matchmaking, careful and conservative by design, simply couldn't keep producing opponents worth his genuine attention once word of his skill had spread far enough to make people cautious around him.

The reputation had a shape by then, the kind concrete enough that people in guild circles used his name as its own kind of shorthand — not a threat exactly, more a data point, the sort of fact a promising young competitor's trainer might mention early and gently, before a tournament draw went up: mind who you might meet in the third round. Two academies in his home city had, according to the version of events that eventually reached Ardenmere secondhand, quietly restructured an annual invitational to keep him and their strongest prospect in separate brackets for three years running, an arrangement nobody involved would admit to out loud but which everybody in the regional circuit understood perfectly well. It wasn't cruelty on Reydan's part. He'd never gone looking for that kind of caution. It had simply accumulated around him the way caution accumulates around anyone consistently, unglamorously better than the room he's standing in, until the room learned to arrange itself around not finding that out directly.

The unsanctioned circuit produced a different category of opponent entirely — fighters the guild track's careful, reputation-protecting matchmaking would never have paired him against, because the guild track didn't know they existed, or didn't consider them worth the risk to a promising formal competitor's record. Reydan had discovered, somewhere in his early twenties, that this was exactly the kind of risk he actually wanted.

He'd tried this before, more than once, in more than one city — a rumor followed to its source, a modest circuit purse paid just to buy himself an evening's look at whoever the local talk was building up. Most of those trips ended the way most rumors did on close inspection: a genuinely talented Copper-tier fighter who'd simply never faced anyone good enough to reveal the ceiling on his own talent, impressive within his own small pond and unremarkable outside it. If Reydan resented those wasted trips, nothing in how he told it to Dace — the only person he spoke to all evening — suggested it. He'd apparently learned, over several of them, to tell within the first bout he watched whether a given circuit's best story was worth a second evening, and to leave quietly when it wasn't.

He'd heard about Cael the way most out-of-town interest eventually reached Ardenmere: practitioner to practitioner, city to city, a story passed hand to hand and embellished a little with each retelling until it arrived considerably larger than whatever had actually happened in the Ironyard the night it started. An unclassified fighter, formally nothing at all by the Compact's own accounting, beating ranked opponents clean. It was, by the time it reached Reydan two cities over, exactly the kind of story that made a fighter like him curious enough to travel.

One version of the story, the one that had actually reached him, claimed the boy had beaten a Bronze Rank 1 opponent while carrying no Path a certification office would recognize — an unclassified nothing, by the Compact's own accounting, doing something the Compact's own accounting said should have been impossible. Reydan had heard enough impossible things claimed about circuit fighters over the years to distrust the shape of a story like that on principle. He'd also learned, the harder way, that dismissing every impossible story on principle meant occasionally missing the one that turned out true, and that the cost of checking a story that failed to hold up was considerably smaller than the cost of ignoring one that didn't.

He'd wanted to see for himself before committing to anything, which was why he'd come and watched two ordinary bouts in silence rather than announcing his interest the moment he arrived. Ardenmere's story had survived two cities of retelling without collapsing into the ordinary shape those stories usually collapsed into by the time they reached him, and that alone had been enough to get him on the road; now he wanted to understand the venue, the crowd, the general caliber of what this circuit actually produced, before deciding whether the story matched the reality closely enough to be worth the rest of his time.

It apparently had.

When the second bout ended and the crowd's attention scattered back toward the notice board and the evening's remaining business, the stranger didn't leave with it. He crossed to where Dace kept his scheduling table, unhurried, threading the thinning crowd with the kind of economy that suggested he'd navigated crowded rooms full of people who mattered a great deal more than anyone here for most of his life. Dace looked up before the man had fully arrived, already reading him again — not with suspicion, just the habitual assessment of a man for whom every new arrival at his table was, potentially, either a problem or an opportunity, and who preferred to know which before the conversation started.

"I want to fight the unranked one," he told Dace, at the end of the evening, without elaboration, in the same unhurried tone he'd used to ask, twenty minutes earlier, where the exit was.

"He's Iron-equivalent now," Dace said. "Not unranked. He's earned an actual rating."

"I know. I want to fight him anyway."

"You've watched him fight."

"I've watched other people fight tonight. I haven't seen him yet." Something in Reydan's expression shifted, very slightly — the first crack in an otherwise entirely composed evening. "That's rather the point of the trip. I'd like to see for myself, not secondhand."

Dace read him for a long moment, the weighing attention he brought to every request that might reshape his careful scheduling — checking, Cael would later understand, not just whether Reydan was serious, but whether he was the kind of serious that made a match worth the disruption to everything else on the board. Whatever he saw settled the question quickly.

"Two weeks. Main floor."

"Thank you." Reydan turned to go, then paused, glancing back with the same unhurried composure he'd carried all evening. "Tell him I'm not here to embarrass anyone. I'm here because the story was interesting enough to travel for. I'd like it to stay that kind of fight."

He left without waiting for a response.

Dace stood at his table for a while after the stranger had gone, the scheduling board in front of him and a blank space where Reydan's match would eventually need to be written in. He'd matched plenty of visiting fighters against local names before, always with an eye on what it might cost the circuit if the visitor turned out to be exactly as good as he'd claimed and the local turned out not to be. This one felt different, and Dace, who trusted his own read of a room more than he trusted most things, let himself sit with that difference a long moment before he finally picked up his pen and made the first mark toward turning tonight's conversation into something official.

By the following morning, word of the exchange had already made its way through half the practitioners' quarter — the fast-moving current of circuit gossip that Cael had learned, over a year, to treat as a genuine information source in its own right.

---

Cael received the news from Dace directly, mid-morning, before the day's training had properly started. Dace found him at the alcove wall, delivered it in the same unhurried, matter-of-fact tone he used for every scheduling update regardless of its actual weight, and waited, watching, for whatever reaction Cael was going to have.

The alcove wall was where Cael did most of his early-morning thinking these days, worn smooth in places from a year of leaning against it while he ran through footwork or turned some half-formed observation over until it resolved into something usable. It wasn't a private spot, not really — anyone crossing the yard could see him there — but it had become private in practice, the kind of visible solitude the Ironyard's regulars had quietly learned to leave alone. Dace clearly understood that too, delivering the news there rather than pulling him aside somewhere more deliberately quiet, as if the ordinariness of the setting were meant to keep the moment itself ordinary a little longer.

Cael sat with it a moment before responding, running the bare facts through the same careful processing he brought to any piece of information that arrived without enough context yet to act on.

"What's his tier?"

"Iron-tier Rank 8."

A pause, longer this time. "When?"

"Two weeks. Main floor."

He turned the phrase over. The main floor, at capacity, for an Iron-tier outside opponent against an unclassified Iron-equivalent local. He'd fought on the main floor before — the Brom fight had been there, the ordinary weekly bouts that drew a genuine crowd — but this felt different in a way he couldn't yet name precisely, a weight in how Dace had said it that went beyond the words themselves. Iron Rank 8 sat one step below the Bronze threshold — nominally beneath Sarel, beneath Feryn, beneath Darrow Innes, the toughest names already in the log. The tier itself didn't trouble him; what troubled him was everything the tier couldn't measure. He'd beaten every one of those names by out-adapting them in real time, finding the seam in an opponent who was skilled but not exhaustively drilled to protect it. Reydan wasn't that kind of gap. Twelve years of formal training, against opponents whose entire profession had been making him better, was a different problem than talent outrunning preparation — and a burst-compression style built to punish exactly the read-and-adjust approach that had carried Cael this far was a worse one. He ran the comparison anyway, out of habit more than usefulness — Feryn, Sarel, Talis, Darrow Innes, every name in the log measured against a rank that didn't capture what actually made this one dangerous. The comparison told him less than he wanted it to. That, itself, was information.

"You could say no," Dace added, unprompted, something almost gentle in the offer. "Nobody's making you take this. Reydan wants it. That doesn't mean you have to give it to him."

"I know I could say no."

"But you won't."

"No."

Dace nodded, unsurprised, and left him to it — the practiced discretion of a man who'd learned, over years of running this place, exactly when a fighter needed room rather than conversation.

Cael stayed at the wall some time after Dace had gone, turning the two facts over without yet trying to build them into a plan — the tier, and the two weeks — because building too early, before he'd let the shape of the problem finish settling, had cost him clarity before. He'd learn everything he could stand to learn before the day was out. For now, he let himself simply hold it, the way he'd learned, over a year of exactly this kind of news, to hold anything that mattered too much to rush.

Training that morning went through the motions without much of his attention actually in it, which was, he knew, its own kind of information — evidence that some part of him had already started working the problem whether he'd given it permission to or not. He caught himself twice running through evasion footwork at half-speed, testing not the pattern itself but how it might need to change against something faster and shorter-committed than anything he'd drilled against before. Neither test told him anything useful yet. He filed the attempt anyway, the way he filed everything, on the theory that even a failed test was worth writing down.

---

The boarding house common room had settled into its usual evening rhythm by the time Cael brought it up — spoons and low conversation filling the room the way they did most nights, a pair of boarders arguing amiably over a card game near the door, nothing about the setting suggesting that anything unusual was about to be said. He waited until the meal was properly underway, and delivered it plainly, without preamble, the same way Dace had delivered it to him: the tier, the two weeks, the main floor.

Lira's reaction was immediate, no hesitation at all in the way she set down her cup.

"Iron Rank 8 is different."

"Yes."

"Different how? Walk me through it properly, don't just agree with the tone of the question."

Cael considered this honestly. "Different in that everything before this has been a gap I could close with preparation — watching, patterning, finding the seam. I don't know yet if this is that kind of gap or something larger. Reydan's spent his whole life being trained by people whose entire job was making him exactly this good. I've spent a year teaching myself out of necessity. Those aren't the same foundation, even if I've closed distance on plenty of foundations before."

Lira turned her cup slowly on the table, the way she did when she was working through a problem rather than simply reacting to one. "What do you actually know about him? Beyond the tier and the Path."

"Not much yet. Burst-compression, not the sustained kind Feryn used. Short wind-up. Professional background — trained young, trained properly. That's what Dace gave me, and what was already moving through the quarter by the time I got there this morning."

"That's a name and a warning, not a fighter." Brom said it without looking up from his own bowl, the flat economy he brought to most observations. "You'll need considerably more than that before two weeks is worth anything. And don't take comfort from the rank. He's under the Bronze line — under Innes, under Feryn, on paper. Innes and Feryn are gifted men who taught themselves. Reydan's had twelve years of people paid to make him better than that. The number doesn't measure the difference."

"I know. That's rather the point of the two weeks."

"Are you ready?"

"No. I have two weeks to get ready."

She studied him for a moment, the evaluating look that had become, over the past year, less about doubting him and more about calibrating exactly how hard she needed to push. "Two weeks isn't much, against Rank 8."

"It's what we have."

"Then we use every day of it properly. No wasted mornings." She was already, visibly, shifting into the mode she brought to genuinely dangerous preparation — the same focus she'd applied before the Brom fight, sharpened now by a year of additional experience and a clearer sense of exactly what Cael's method could and couldn't produce under real pressure. "We'll run mornings the way we ran them before that fight — full drills, nothing held back — and afternoons on whatever new information the day's turned up. If we're still guessing at his tells by the end of the first week, we adjust the split. We are not spending two weeks running the same schedule blind and simply hoping it works itself out."

"That's fair."

"How much of that do you want documented?" Brom asked. "In case something in the pattern needs to be checked back against, later."

"All of it," Lira said. "Same as we did before the Brom fight. If something works on day nine, I want to know exactly what we did on day nine, not a rough memory of it."

"And you eat properly the whole way through this time. Not just the last few days, once you remember you're tired." It wasn't quite a joke, though it came close to sounding like one — Lira had made the same demand before the Brom fight and been half-ignored for most of a week before Cael had actually started listening.

"I remember."

"You said that last time too."

Brom, from across the table, hadn't said anything yet, working through a private calculation of his own. When he finally spoke, it was with his usual economy. "I'll find practitioners who've fought him before. Someone in this district, or someone Dace knows through a contact somewhere else. Reydan's circuit tour didn't start here — there'll be a record of him somewhere, even if it's just secondhand impression rather than anything written down."

"How reliable is secondhand impression, against someone like this?" Lira asked.

"Depends who's remembering it. A loss tells you less than a loss from someone who kept his head clear enough during it to actually watch what beat him. I'll look for that kind first." Brom said it without much confidence he'd actually find one, only the plain intention to try.

"Good," Cael said. "I'll learn everything I can about burst-compression Pressure Path variants — what's publicly documented, and whatever I can piece together from anyone in the district who's fought one before, even outside a formal bout."

"That's not much of a plan yet," Lira said, though not unkindly.

"It's the start of one. We've built plans out of less."

"We have." She let that sit a moment, then added, quieter, "I want you to actually tell me when something isn't working. Not wait until the fifth day to mention a drill isn't landing, the way you did before Brom's fight. I'd rather adjust early than fix something on day twelve that we could have fixed on day three."

"I'll tell you."

"I know you'll mean to." It wasn't an accusation, only an old, familiar observation, delivered with enough warmth that it didn't need to be one. Cael let it stand without arguing, because arguing would only have proven her point rather than disproven it.

Brom set his spoon down, considering something before he said it. "Main floor, for an outside Iron-tier against you. That's not just Dace being generous with room. That's the circuit deciding this is worth watching." He said it plainly, without any particular weight attached, the way he delivered most observations that other people might have dressed up as bigger than they were. "Everyone in the district who pays attention to circuit gossip will hear about this fight before it happens, and everyone who watches it will remember whether you won or how you lost, for considerably longer than they'd remember an ordinary Tuesday bout."

"I know," Cael said.

"I'm not saying it to worry you. I'm saying it because it changes what preparing well actually means here. It's not only about winning. It's about what winning, or losing, does to everything you've been quietly building since the Innes fight." Brom picked the spoon back up, done with the point now that he'd made it. "You already know that. I just wanted it said, once, out loud, before we start."

Lira watched the exchange without adding to it, though something in her expression suggested she'd been thinking the same thing herself and simply hadn't found the moment to say it first.

They had a methodology, by now, built over months of exactly this kind of preparation — Lira's drilling, Brom's outside perspective, Cael's own patient accumulation of watched detail, the three of them working the same problem from three different angles until something usable emerged from the overlap. They used it now, the way they'd used it before every genuinely dangerous fight since the three of them had become a working unit, each of them already, before the meal had properly finished, turning private attention toward the two weeks ahead.

Two weeks was not a long time to close a gap this size, and none of them said so aloud, because saying it wouldn't have made the two weeks any longer. It was, instead, simply the shape of what came next: mornings starting earlier than usual and ending later, afternoons spent chasing down whatever fragment of information the district could offer about a fighter who'd built his reputation two cities over, evenings spent turning that day's findings into something Cael could actually use against a man who adapted, by every account Dace had passed along, faster than most opponents ever got the chance to plan for. It would not feel, on most of those days, like enough. It rarely did, this early in a stretch of preparation — Lira had told him as much before the Brom fight, and been right about it then too. What mattered, for now, was only that by the time the main floor filled again in two weeks, it would have to be.

Cael thought about Reydan's parting words to Dace as he lay awake later that night, turning them over the way he turned over everything that mattered too much to let go of easily. *I'd like it to stay that kind of fight.* A strange thing for a man to say before asking to fight someone he'd never met — not a threat, not quite a courtesy either, but something closer to a standard he was holding both of them to before either of them had earned the right to know if it would hold. Cael didn't yet know what kind of fight it would turn out to be. He knew only that he had two weeks to become the kind of fighter capable of finding out, and that two weeks, starting tomorrow, was no longer a comfortable amount of time to have.

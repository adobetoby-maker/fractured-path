# Chapter 11 — Third Exchange

He woke before the alarm he hadn't set, the way he always did on days that mattered, and spent the first several minutes of the morning doing the thing he always did before he let himself get out of bed: cataloguing.

Ceiling. The room's familiar shape, Lira's room still silent on the other side of the wall. His own body, run through in order, the inventory a year of circuit fighting had made automatic: legs rested and full, the deep reserve topped up by two easy days; the left wrist's old complaint quiet; breath slow and organized, four counts in, six out. The morning's quiet hadn't decided yet whether it belonged to nerves or readiness — he'd learned, across a year of mornings like this one, that the two feelings arrived wearing nearly identical clothes and the only real way to tell them apart was to get up and start moving.

He ran through the transition-window hypothesis one final time before breakfast, not because rehearsing it again would sharpen it any further — two weeks of watching had already given the theory whatever shape it was going to have — but because the rehearsal itself had become part of how he readied himself for anything that mattered. Third exchange. If the window existed at all, it would show itself there, when Brom was most settled into pattern and least braced for something to interrupt it.

He ran the other rehearsal too, the one he'd added this time and told no one about: the discipline plan for his own fragments. Wind-adjacent rationed — bursts only off a read, never off a reaction, because reactive bursts spent from the reserve and the reserve had to last four exchanges against a man who would spend nothing. Pressure-adjacent locked away entirely. He'd written the rule three days ago and underlined it: *no amplification against a hardened surface, no matter how good the opening looks. The better the opening looks, the more it will cost when it comes back.* He suspected, with the honesty he tried to apply to himself as rigorously as to opponents, that the rule would be hardest to keep at exactly the moment it mattered most — mid-fight, behind on the score, with a clean line to Brom's jaw and a year of trained instinct screaming to take it with everything available. That was why it was written down. Rules you only kept when they were easy weren't rules.

Lira caught him at it over breakfast, watching him mouth the plan silently to himself over a bowl of the boarding house's unremarkable morning grain. "You've been running that same sequence in your head since you woke up."

"It helps."

"It helps you. I'm not sure it helps the plan any." She said this with the fondness she reserved for teasing him about his more compulsive habits, the kind of comment that carried no real complaint underneath it. "Eat. You'll need it more than you'll need one more mental rehearsal."

He ate. It didn't quiet the familiar hum of a day he'd been building toward for two weeks, but it gave his hands something to do besides drum against the table, which was, he'd found, most of what breakfast was actually for on mornings like this one.

The rest of the morning passed as important mornings always seemed to pass — too slowly while he waited through it, and then, somehow, entirely gone before he'd finished bracing for the parts he'd actually meant to prepare for. He walked the market with Lira out of habit rather than need, bought nothing, watched the vendors set up their stalls with the useless attention of a mind that needed somewhere to put itself besides the fight. Brom didn't seek him out. Cael understood, without needing it explained, that this was deliberate — a fighter's professional courtesy, the two of them keeping their distance until the only conversation that mattered happened where it was supposed to happen, inside the circle.

He spent the early afternoon rereading his own notes on Brom one final time, less to learn anything new than to settle the shape of two weeks' work into something he could carry into the fight without needing to consult it again. The latency table. The breath signature — inhale held a half-beat long before the hardening. The heel-load platform. The coverage gap below the knee, still unexplained. By the time the light started going long and gold over the practitioners' quarter, he'd stopped rereading and simply sat with the notebook closed in his lap, watching the day turn toward evening, letting the last of the nervous energy settle into something closer to readiness.

---

The Ironyard's main floor was fuller than Cael had seen it in months by the time Vell called the bout to order.

Word had been moving through the district for two weeks — Brom's reputation traveling ahead of him the way real reputations did, mouth to mouth rather than posted on any board — and "the unranked practitioner fighting above his circuit rating" had by now become exactly the kind of story that drew a crowd on its own merits, independent of who was actually fighting. People who never usually bothered with anything below a Bronze-tier exhibition had shown up tonight. Cael counted at least a dozen faces he didn't recognize from his usual circuit crowd, standing three deep along the warehouse's far wall, and understood, filing the count alongside everything else the room offered, that some of them had likely come from outside Ardenmere entirely.

The lamps had been trimmed brighter than usual, throwing hard shadows across the packed dirt and giving the whole main floor a close, expectant quality that Cael felt settle into his chest before he'd even finished his own warm-up. The warm-up itself he kept deliberately ordinary — the same sequence he ran before every bout, joints first, then legs, then three unassisted practice bursts to confirm the step was resident — because a changed ritual on a big night was its own kind of tell, and he had no intention of showing this crowd anything before the ledger required it. Lira stood near the edge, arms crossed, watching the crowd fill in with the same tactical attention she brought to an opponent's opening stance — reading the room the way she read a fight, because a room this size changed the fight itself, whether either combatant wanted it to or not.

"Big crowd," she said, when he found her.

"Bigger than I expected."

"Bigger than he expected too, I think." She nodded toward the far side of the floor, where Brom stood alone, unbothered by the attention in the way of someone who'd simply decided, at some point, that other people's expectations weren't his to manage. "He doesn't perform for a crowd. That's either going to make this harder for you or easier. I genuinely don't know which."

"I don't either."

She studied him a moment longer. "You ready?"

"As ready as two weeks of watching makes anyone."

"That's not a real answer."

"I know." He looked past her, at Brom, at the crowd settling into its places. "It's the only one I've got."

Vell kept the ledger personally tonight, the way she did for anything she'd decided genuinely mattered — not delegating the recording to one of the junior keepers who sometimes handled smaller bouts, standing instead at the circle's edge with the volume open against her forearm and her expression giving away exactly nothing about which outcome, if either, she was hoping for. The pre-fight formalities were brief: both names stated for the record, circuit ratings confirmed — Assessed-Copper against Iron-equivalent, a gap wide enough on paper that a portion of the crowd had clearly come expecting a short night — and the no-kill rule restated and acknowledged by both fighters in turn.

Somewhere near the back, Cael caught sight of the old yard-owner, arms crossed over the low wall at the far corner he'd held for as long as anyone could remember, watching with the same patience he brought to everything. He didn't wave. He never did. But he'd found his way here for this one, out of a year of fights he'd mostly left Cael to attend without him, and Cael filed that fact away the way he filed everything that mattered too much for an immediate reaction.

Then the floor cleared, and it was just the two of them.

---

Vell did not raise her voice for the formalities. She never needed to. The main floor had its own acoustics — low ceiling, hard stone, forty years of bouts worn into both — and she had long ago measured exactly how little effort the room required of her.

"Cael. Assessed, Copper-equivalent." Her pen was moving before she finished the name. "Brom. Iron-equivalent. Both parties confirm the no-kill rule."

"Confirmed," Brom said.

"Confirmed," Cael said.

"Then it's a bout." She wrote one more line and looked up, and the room's noise changed shape — not quieter, exactly. Attentive. The largest crowd Cael had ever fought in front of, three deep at the rail and standing on benches behind that, settling into the specific hush of people who intended to remember what they saw.

He set the crowd aside the way he set aside anything that wasn't usable, and looked at the plan instead.

The plan was two lines. He had written them in the Power Log the night before and read them again that morning, because a rule that only existed in his head was a rule he'd renegotiate the first time it cost him something.

*Wind: read-fed bursts only. Never reactive.*

*Pressure: locked. Do not open it. Not once.*

The second line had a reason attached, and the reason was standing across the circle from him with his weight distributed so evenly that Cael's eye kept sliding off it, looking for the lean that wasn't there. Pressure-adjacent was impact amplification. Against most opponents, more force meant more result. Against a surface that hardened at the instant of contact and handed the force back, more force meant a larger donation. Every amplified strike he threw at Brom would come home with interest, at an angle of Brom's choosing. So the reserve stayed shut. Whatever happened tonight, it stayed shut.

Which left the hypothesis. Two weeks of watching Brom work the training posts in the side alcoves, session after session, and Cael had seen — or believed he'd seen — a seam. A softness in the Iron Skin between its two modes: the defensive set that met an incoming strike, and the offensive set that turned his own blows into something that arrived twice. A transition. A window. If it existed, it would open under alternation — if Cael could force the Iron Skin to switch modes fast enough, often enough, there would be a fraction of a second when it was neither, and a fraction of a second was a working space he'd fitted himself into before.

Vell said, "First exchange."

Cael went in at three-quarter speed, high line, nothing committed — a question, not an attack. Brom let it come. Cael's forearm met his shoulder, and for the first sliver of the contact it was a shoulder, ordinary muscle over ordinary bone with ordinary give in it — and then it was not. The set arrived under his arm like a floor arriving under a dropped stone. And the force he'd put in came back out.

Not blocked. Not absorbed. Returned — through the same contact point, rotated off his line by an angle no part of his structure had been braced against, so that his own strike climbed back up through his wrist and forearm and turned him a quarter-step to the left, into space he hadn't chosen. He recovered before the follow-up arrived, but the follow-up had been on its way before he'd finished turning. That was the economy of it. Brom didn't need to generate offense. He rented Cael's.

Cael reset and ran the alternation pattern. Speed changes, angle changes, contact high then low then high — feints that had to be answered as strikes, strikes shaped like feints. He was not trying to land anything. He was trying to make the Iron Skin change its mind quickly, over and over, and catch the moment between one mind and the next.

There was no moment.

By the fourth contact he understood the shape of his error, and it was an old error, the kind he filed with special care because it came from good procedure honestly executed. Two weeks of observation, thirty feet away, watching Brom work the posts — and what he'd read as a lag in the Iron Skin, a softness before the set, had been the post. Wood flexing under load. The give had belonged to the equipment, not the man. Live, at the length of his own arm, with real intent behind the contact, the transition from flesh to iron was not fast. It was not anything. There was no interval in it to be fast across. The window he had built two weeks of planning on did not exist, and had never existed, and the fifth contact confirmed it, and the sixth put the confirmation through his elbow hard enough to numb the forearm to the wrist.

Twice in that first exchange, the other thing offered itself.

The first time: one of his low probes turned Brom farther than usual, and for half a beat the ribs were there, open, square to him, and the Pressure-adjacent reserve leaned toward his hand — the familiar readiness, the sense of an account waiting to be drawn — and an amplified strike into that opening would have been the hardest hit he'd ever landed on anyone. On anyone else. He declined it. He put a plain strike there instead, because the opening was real even if the amplification was forbidden, and the plain strike met hardened surface and came back through his own elbow, and he paid for the refusal with half a step and an arm that rang like struck metal. The cost of keeping the rule was taking the redirect with nothing to show for it. He logged the cost and kept the rule.

The second time was worse, because it was better: a clean line to the sternum as Brom advanced, the kind of line that ended exchanges, and every trained instinct he had converged on it, and the reserve was right there. He declined again. Declining meant checking a motion he'd already half-committed, and the check left him square and stalled, and Brom's palm met his retreating guard and redirected the check itself — used the force of Cael's own stopping — and walked him two steps backward off the center of the circle. The cost of the second refusal was the ground. He gave it. The rule held.

Then Brom took the exchange, and took it out of the recovery Cael was proudest of. A redirect off the forearm turned Cael's cross-step into a spiral, and his balance went past the point his footwork could answer — genuinely past it, the stone coming up — and he spent the Wind burst.

Reactive. Unread. Exactly the deployment his own plan prohibited, fired not off a read but off a fall. It caught him, the short familiar surge putting his feet back under him a half-metre from where physics wanted him, and it worked perfectly, and it was still a violation. He felt the reserve drop by its usual amount. He had time to hate the arithmetic — *one line of the plan broken inside the first exchange* — and no time for more than that, because Brom had followed the recovery the way he followed everything, without hurry and without any gap to exploit, and the shoulder-drive that ended the exchange used the last of Cael's redirected momentum to sit him down on the pale-worn boundary line.

"First exchange," Vell said. "Brom."

Her pen scratched. The crowd's noise came up like weather and Cael filed it under irrelevant and walked back to his mark, cataloguing. Hypothesis: dead. The window was not at the point of contact. Contact was where the Iron Skin was perfect. But a read preceded the set — something told the surface where to harden, and a thing that reads must read *before*. If he could not be slow enough to slip between modes, perhaps he could be fast enough to arrive before the reading finished. Speed, then. Speed was the second door.

"Second exchange."

He went through the second door at the pace he'd been withholding all year.

There was no showing it in the alcove bouts; he'd been careful about that, keeping his fastest entries out of the circuit's visible record, and so the first angled entry of the second exchange was faster than anything Brom could have watched him do — a low diagonal that covered the distance in the time most fighters spent deciding to. It should have arrived before the read.

The read was waiting for him.

Not barely. Comfortably. The hardened set met him a full beat early, established, unhurried, and the redirect off it was almost gentle, a correction rather than a punishment, as if Brom were declining to spend force on an answer he'd had ready for a while. Cael broke off, circled, tried the mirror of it — high diagonal, opposite side, a tempo change inside the entry. Met again. Early again.

The observation record said Brom's read engaged at a certain speed. The record was wrong, and it was wrong in a specific, directional way, and midway through the third entry Cael concluded the thing he would have written down if he'd had a hand free: *the read has been modified against fast approaches.* Not natural talent outrunning his data — a tuned response, a door somebody else had tried before him, tried hard enough that Brom had rebuilt the lock. Every fast fighter Brom had ever faced had walked this same line, and Brom had closed it behind the last of them and kept the improvement. The two weeks of observation weren't worthless. They were a map of the previous version.

The hit came while he was mid-adjustment.

He'd committed to a low fast entry — weight in transfer, both feet loaded, the one instant in any step when the body is a bridge and not a stance — and Brom's redirect arrived not at his arms but *down*, through the line of his commitment, his own speed and mass returned through his hips at an angle nothing below his waist had been built to hold. It was not the glancing tax of the first exchange. It was weight-bearing, structural, both legs at once — hip to knee to ankle, the entire load-bearing arithmetic paying a bill in the same instant.

He went down. He got up, because getting up was still available. Everything held. Nothing held well. His knees answered him the way a rope bridge answers — present, functional, and honest about its new terms.

He tested the terms immediately, because untested terms were how fighters got finished: a read-fed Wind burst, by the plan, legal, to reopen the distance while Brom advanced. The burst fired. The reserve dropped by exactly its usual cost. And his body delivered perhaps a third of the usual ground, in not quite the direction he'd asked for — because the burst was not magic, had never been magic, it was force expressed through legs, and the instrument was compromised. Full price, fractional product. He tried nothing further with it. He shut the Wind-adjacent down the way he'd close a ledger with the ink still wet — deliberately, carefully, without any plan to reopen it tonight — because a resource that spent whole and delivered broken wasn't a resource. It was a leak.

Which made the accounting simple. Pressure: locked by rule. Wind: closed by damage. From here to the end of the bout, he had eyes, a plan's ruins, and whatever his legs had left.

Brom ended the second exchange almost kindly, if kindness could be measured in efficiency: one more entry Cael couldn't answer at speed, one more redirect, the floor. "Second exchange," Vell said. "Brom." Her pen was slower this time, adding something beyond the result, and Cael, back on his feet and breathing through the new information from his knees, ran the arithmetic he trusted more than hope. Two exchanges left in his mobility. At best. After that the legs would make the decision for everyone.

"Third exchange."

He came out with nothing, and fought with it.

No speed — speed was gone. No amplification — locked. No bursts — closed. He fought small: short angles, economical contact, weight kept over his feet like something carried in a shallow bowl. And he stopped trying to break the read. That was the one decision left to make and he made it consciously: the read could not be beaten head-on, so he stopped addressing it. He narrowed himself down to the next half-second, and the half-second after that. Where Brom's lead shoulder was. Where the floor was. What the distance was doing. Not how to solve the Iron Skin — just what, exactly, needed to happen next.

And something happened that he did not do.

He would try to reconstruct it afterward, many times, and every reconstruction would fail at the same place: there was no beginning to it. No decision he could locate, no draw against any account, none of the leaning readiness that the Wind-adjacent sometimes gave before it fired — nothing offered, nothing spent, nothing felt. He was moving through the small quiet arithmetic of the next half-second, all of his attention on the *what* of it, and then Brom's guard was pointed at a version of the floor where Cael was supposed to be standing.

And wasn't.

Cael saw it from the outside, the way he saw everything — Brom's weight checked mid-shift; the guard stayed committed to a line Cael had already left; and the composure that had governed every prior second of the bout produced its first graceless half-beat. Not fooled. Fooled implied the read had been handed a wrong answer. This looked like a read handed no answer at all — the hardened attention sweeping a space and finding nothing in it that it knew how to be hard against. Cael was there. Standing on the same stone, breathing the same air, close enough to touch. And for a stretch of time shorter than a full second, to the thing inside Brom that read the world, he was not.

He put contact into the gap because contact was what the half-second called for — a plain strike, the only meaningful offense he'd land all night — and Brom caught it late, off raw instinct, a hand arriving by eyesight and training rather than by the read, and the redirect that came back was imperfect for the first time: real, still stronger than anything Cael could answer, but *built in the moment* rather than waiting for him. Then the moment shut. Whatever it was ended without ending — no seam there either — and Brom's read had him again, and the exchange went on.

It went on three times longer than either exchange before it.

Cael measured it because measuring was what he had left. The first exchange had cost him roughly a minute. The second, less. The third ran long enough that the crowd's noise changed shape twice — the rising note of people watching an upset assemble itself, then the long uncertain vowel of a room that had stopped being sure what it was watching. Twice Brom built a solution and abandoned it half-built. He wasn't relying on the read anymore; Cael could see the difference, the eyes doing work the Iron Skin usually did, the approach of an excellent fighter navigating by sight and feel like everyone else in the world had to. It was enough. Of course it was enough — instinct at Brom's level was most fighters' best day — and the exchange ended the way the arithmetic said it must: a grip Cael's legs couldn't refuse, a turn, the stone. Controlled. Almost careful. Nothing new broken.

"Third exchange," Vell said. "Brom." A pause that hadn't followed the first two. Her pen moved for longer than a result required.

Walking back to his mark, Cael did the inventory, because the inventory was the only instrument he had pointed at himself.

Wind-adjacent: closed, and exactly as full as when he'd closed it. Pressure-adjacent: locked, untouched, whole. Nothing had been drawn. Nothing had been spent. There had been no warning before — none of the arrival the Wind step used to announce, back when it was new, present before being asked for. And there was nothing after. No ache that belonged to it. No drain, no residue, no echo he could follow back toward a source. He reached for it anyway, standing on his mark with his breath sawing — reached inward for whatever had been there in that half-second, some handle, some address — and found what a hand finds in a dark room that turns out to be empty. It had not asked him before, and it did not answer him now.

*It arrived the way the step arrived,* he thought — over a year ago, unasked, already his before he knew it existed — and he could not confirm even that. It was a shape, not a fact. He had no idea what he had done. He had no idea whether it could ever be done again, by him or by anything, and no way to find out, and nothing to write down except that Brom's read had swept across him and catalogued nothing, and that he had been, for less than one second, absent from a system built to see everyone.

Across the circle, Brom took the reset slowly. Slower than he needed to. He rolled his shoulders once, which he had not done all bout, and he watched Cael cross to his mark the whole way — and the composure was intact, the patience was intact, but the attention living inside them had changed its grip. Cael had spent two weeks learning the difference between Brom watching and Brom *watching*. This was the second one.

At the wall by Vell's table, Lira stood with her arms folded and her weight forward, saying nothing, which from Lira was its own report.

"Fourth exchange," Vell said.

Brom did not investigate. That was the thing Cael would remember about the fourth exchange — that a fighter who had just seen something impossible declined, professionally, to go looking for it again, because looking was not his job. His job was the bout, and the bout had a door standing open, and the door was Cael's legs.

He came in committed, at an angle that gave Cael no way to yield out of it — an entry that had to be met, structurally, with braced legs or not at all. Cael met it because the alternative was the same result with worse posture. He braced. The brace was the payment. Brom's redirect took the force Cael's own body was channeling into the floor and returned it down through the channel — clean, deliberate, exact, through both compromised legs at the one angle their new terms could not carry — and the terms were called, and the bridge went out, and the stone came up, and this time there was no getting up in it. Cael knew before he landed. His legs knew before he did.

The floor of the Ironyard was cool through the back of his shirt. The lamps burned steady in their brackets, the practitioner-installed light Vell had never let anyone modernize, and somewhere beyond his feet the crowd was making a sound too large for the room.

"Bout to Brom," Vell said, level as ever. "Four exchanges." And then the scratch of the pen, longer than four words needed.

Cael lay still and let the systems report in. Breath: ragged, but organizing, finding its count again the way it always did, two in, three out. Legs: paid twice over — once by the second exchange, and once more by every burst he'd drawn against them afterward, the damage and the spending stacked on the same two columns. And underneath it all, whole and shut and strange, the Pressure-adjacent reserve — completely untouched, for the first time since the night it had arrived. He had never once finished a bout without drawing on it. The one line of the plan that had survived contact with Brom, and it sat in him now with an odd weight, like a coin found unspent in a coat he'd been sure was empty.

Brom had not left the floor.

Cael registered that at the edge of vision — the winner's silhouette unmoving out there past his feet, in no hurry to be anywhere — and set it with everything else he wasn't ready to process. He started with his breathing, because it was nearest.

---

The audience cleared faster than usual. Cael registered it distantly, still working through the arithmetic of what still worked and what didn't below his waist — something in the fight's shape had clearly signaled to the crowd that whatever came next wasn't theirs to watch, and even the loudest of them thinned toward the exits within a few minutes, leaving the main floor's usual after-bout murmur far quieter than normal.

Lira came to check on him first, kneeling briefly, running a practiced eye over the damage. "Nothing broken?"

"Nothing broken."

"Good." She didn't linger, reading something in the moment that told her this wasn't hers to sit through — a small, deliberate withdrawal that Cael noted and appreciated more than he'd have known how to say out loud. She retreated to the wall near Vell's table, close enough to watch, far enough to leave the actual space clear.

Brom crossed the floor in no hurry, despite having just won the biggest bout of his short Ardenmere tenure, and sat down next to him. Not standing over him in the winner's usual position, the posture Cael had watched a dozen victors take across a year of circuit bouts — the small, deliberate claiming of height that came with a win. Next to him, on the floor, at the same eye level, as though the outcome hadn't actually settled anything worth standing over.

"I want to know how you did that thing in the third exchange."

Cael, still catching his breath: "I'm not sure."

"Not sure like you don't know, or not sure like you know but can't explain it?"

"Both."

Brom processed this for a moment, turning it over with visible care. "Your pressure signature disappeared. Not blocked — blocked would still read as active resistance, something pushing back against the read. It disappeared. Like you weren't there."

"I didn't plan it."

"I know. That's why I want to understand it." He was quiet a moment, then extended a hand — a formality that belonged nowhere in two weeks of arguing over breakfast about pressure-read response times, which was exactly why he bothered with it. "I'm Brom."

Cael understood, a half-second late, that this wasn't information. It was a ritual, offered because the third exchange had apparently earned one, and he decided to play along rather than point out the obvious. "I know."

"You're the one everyone says isn't possible."

"That sounds right." He took the offered hand, the shake deliberately formal on both sides — two people who'd spent two weeks talking past each other in a sparring alcove, performing an introduction neither of them actually needed.

"Good." Something that might, on a less economical face, have been a full smile crossed his expression. "I like problems I can't solve."

Something in the handshake — ordinary, faintly ridiculous, entirely on purpose — settled the whole encounter into a different category than he'd expected walking into the bout. He'd come in braced for a fight against a man he already knew, and walked out of it having, apparently, just been properly introduced to a friend.

"The transition-window hypothesis," Cael said, once his breathing had evened out enough to manage a full sentence. "It was wrong. Or half wrong. There's no window in the ordinary sense — you don't hesitate between modes, you just run them concurrently faster than I accounted for."

"Half wrong is still more right than most people manage against me on the first try." Brom said this without particular modesty, the flat assessment of someone reporting a fact rather than offering reassurance. "Most people don't come in with a theory at all. They come in with a plan built off what worked against someone else, and I eat that plan in the first exchange because it was never actually built for me."

"You've fought a lot of people who tried that."

"Enough to recognize the pattern before the first strike lands." He was quiet for a moment, considering something. "You didn't do that. You watched me, for two weeks, and built something aimed at me alone. It was still wrong. But it was wrong about me, not wrong about fighters in general. That's a different kind of wrong. I respect it."

"What's the compound gaze telling you now?" Brom asked, after a moment, genuine curiosity in the question rather than idle conversation. "Watching me from the inside of a loss instead of the outside of an observation. Different information, I'd imagine."

Cael considered this seriously, as he considered most of Brom's questions. "You conserve more than you show. The pressure-read stayed active even in the moments where you looked like you'd stopped paying attention — resetting your stance, catching your breath between exchanges. I assumed those were rest. They weren't. You were still reading me the whole time."

"I don't get to stop reading. Not against someone whose signature does what yours does." He said this without complaint, a simple statement of the cost his own Path imposed on him. "It's exhausting, if you want to know. Most opponents, I can afford small gaps. I couldn't afford any against you."

"That's useful to know."

"I assumed you'd think so." Something almost like amusement crossed his face. "You're already planning the next one, aren't you? Before the bruises have even finished forming."

"Would you rather I didn't?"

"I'd think less of you if you didn't." He said this plainly, and Cael understood, hearing it, that this was as close to a compliment as Brom's economy of language generally allowed itself to get.

Vell approached as the last of the crowd finished thinning, ledger still open against her arm, and looked down at the two of them sitting together on the floor with an expression Cael couldn't quite read — something between amusement and the satisfaction of a keeper watching her circuit produce exactly the kind of result that made thirty years of this work worthwhile.

"Iron-equivalent Brom, win," she said, for the record, making the final notation. "Assessed-Copper Cael. Atypical movement, third exchange." She glanced at Cael. "That's becoming a habit in my ledger."

"I've noticed."

"Keep it up. It's good for business." She closed the book and left them to it, her footsteps steady across the emptying floor.

Lira crossed over once Vell had gone, offering Cael a hand up that he accepted gratefully, his legs registering their complaints the moment he tried to put weight on them properly. "You're going to be sore for days," she said, matter-of-fact, already assessing the exact way he was favoring his left side.

"Worth it."

"You lost."

"I know. Worth it anyway." He looked at Brom, who was watching the exchange with the same quiet, curious attention he seemed to bring to everything. "I think I found something more useful than a win."

Brom didn't disagree.

---

He wrote the entry that night, once Lira had finally stopped fussing over his legs and gone to sleep, the boarding house quiet around him in the way it went quiet only after a genuinely long day.

*Lost to Brom, four exchanges. Transition-window hypothesis half-confirmed — no true window in the way I expected, but a real gap in the third exchange all the same, produced by something I still don't have language for. Whatever I did, it wasn't a fragment. Wasn't Wind-adjacent, wasn't Pressure-adjacent. Something my own architecture generated on the spot, under pressure, before it had a name.*

*Question worth tracking: is this a fourth thing developing, or is it the same underlying mechanism producing a new expression under new conditions? I don't have enough data yet. I have exactly one instance, which is not a pattern, only a data point worth watching for a second one.*

*Fragment accounting from the bout, while it's fresh. Wind-adjacent: four bursts total, three read-fed and affordable, one reactive and expensive. Confirmed under the worst possible conditions that the fragment's cost structure is real — and learned its failure mode properly for the first time: it doesn't stop working when the legs go, it stops being worth using, which is more dangerous, because habit keeps reaching for it. Pressure-adjacent: zero deployments, by rule, against an opponent built to profit from every one. The rule held. Refusing it mid-fight has a cost of its own — attention, mostly — that I hadn't priced in. Price that in next time. Every ability has two ledgers: what it costs to use, and what it costs to carry without using. I only knew about the first one until tonight.*

He paused, the pen resting against the page, and added one more line before closing the log for the night — smaller, less clinical than the rest, the kind of note that belonged in the log precisely because it wasn't about tactics at all.

*Also: made a friend today. Lost the fight and came out ahead anyway. Good trade.*

He thought, before sleep finally took him, about the old man at the back of the crowd, watching a fight he could have skipped and hadn't. About Lira's brief, practical hands checking for broken bone before she'd let herself say anything else. About Brom sitting down next to him instead of standing over him, the small, deliberate choice that had turned a loss into the start of something else entirely.

He'd come to Ardenmere with a bag and a direction and nothing beyond that. A year later, he had a district that recognized him, a ledger with his name written into it in someone else's careful hand, and now, apparently, a friend who liked him precisely because he couldn't be solved. It was not the life he'd imagined lying awake in Denvash the night before his Kindling. He found, turning it over one last time before sleep finally won, that he didn't mind the difference at all.

He closed the notebook, set it beside the bed, and slept better than the aching in his legs had any right to let him.

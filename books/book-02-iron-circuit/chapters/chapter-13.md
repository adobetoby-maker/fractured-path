# Chapter 13 — Fragment Three

The first session set the terms, though neither of them said so out loud at the time.

They met in the side alcove an hour before the Ironyard's morning crowd usually arrived, the stone floor still cool from the night, and Brom opened without preamble by adding two working conditions to the terms they'd already agreed at the market, counted off on his fingers like a man reading a short but non-negotiable amendment.

"The market terms stand — the full read every session, you tell me what you're testing, we stop if either of us breaks. Two more, now that we're actually doing this." A finger. "You tell me when something happens. Not eventually. Immediately, even if you don't understand it yet, even if all you can say is *something.*" A second finger. "And whatever you learn about how I work, you don't repeat it to anyone who could use it against me in a real bout. I'm trusting you with my actual method, not a demonstration of it."

"Agreed," Cael said, and meant it, understanding without needing it spelled out further that Brom was handing him something far more valuable than sparring time — the genuine, unguarded shape of a Path most people who'd fought him had only ever seen through its results, never its process.

Brom used his Iron Skin fully from that very first exchange — not holding back the pressure-read, not moderating the amplification mode, exactly as promised. The close-range, sustained exposure at genuine intensity was a different condition entirely from watching Brom from the audience, or even fighting him in the formal bout, where Cael's attention had been divided between observation and the immediate, urgent business of not losing too badly. Here, with nothing at stake beyond the information itself, Cael could simply watch, fully, for the first time — not defending, not calculating an exit, just absorbing the shape of something as it happened to him at close range, over and over, until the shape started to feel less like a threat and more like a text he was slowly learning to read.

The compound gaze changed at this range, and he spent the first hour learning how. Across an alcove, he had timed Brom's latencies against his own pulse and read the breath signature by eye. At arm's length, the same data arrived through entirely different channels: latency was something his knuckles reported now, the interval between contact and redirect measured in how far his own arm traveled before the return arrived; the inhale-hold was audible, a small catch in the rhythm of Brom's breathing that he could hear a half-beat before his strike met stone instead of clay. He was, he realized partway through the morning, converting a visual method into a tactile one exchange by exchange, and paying for the conversion in bruises the visual method had never charged him. He'd predicted the trade correctly — costlier data, better data — and still underestimated the invoice — by the session's end, both forearms carried the deep, ringing ache of a man who'd spent an hour striking a surface that struck back, and his notes that evening were written around the stiffness in his fingers.

The session also let him run the experiment the formal bout had forbidden. Midway through, with Brom's agreement — *what I'm testing: what amplification actually costs me against you* — he deployed Pressure-adjacent into a strike at quarter intensity, on-rhythm, into Brom's hardened lead forearm. The result confirmed the liability ledger with almost comic precision: the amplified force went in, the redirect came back scaled to what he'd fed it, and Cael sat down hard on the alcove floor with his shoulder reporting an impact he had, in every meaningful sense, thrown at himself. Brom looked down at him with what passed, on his face, for open amusement.

"You knew that would happen."

"I predicted it would happen. Now I know." Cael got up, rolling the shoulder, filing the difference — which was, to him, the entire point of the morning. Prediction was a map. Knowledge was the ground. "Quarter intensity cost me a shoulder. Full intensity in the bout would have cost me the bout. The rule was right."

"Most people wouldn't pay a shoulder to confirm a rule they already believed."

"Most people's rules stay theories. Then they break exactly when it matters." He reset his stance. "Again. Unamplified this time."

The first session taught him mostly what he already suspected: the amplification triggered at contact, not before, exactly as his weeks of alcove observation had suggested. But confirming it from the outside and feeling it happen to his own body turned out to be very different categories of knowledge. A strike that looked, from the crowd, like a clean absorption revealed itself, up close, as something with texture — a resistance that gathered a fraction of a second before impact and then released all at once, redirected, the way a held breath releases when you finally let it go. He hadn't been able to see that from the audience. He could feel it now, in the ringing ache along his forearm where he'd caught the first real exchange badly.

The second session refined the timing further — he could feel, now, physically, the fractional difference between Brom absorbing a strike defensively and Brom redirecting one offensively, a difference his eyes alone had never quite been able to isolate from the audience's distance. He started testing it deliberately partway through, throwing strikes at slightly different speeds to see whether the timing window shifted, and found, by the session's end, that it didn't — the trigger point stayed fixed regardless of how fast or slow the approach, which told him something useful about how deeply automatic the technique had become for Brom, embedded past the point where conscious adjustment could easily reach it anymore.

Brom, for his part, seemed to be running his own quiet experiments. Twice during the second session he varied his own response deliberately, watching Cael's reaction rather than simply defending, the attention of someone testing a hypothesis of his own that he hadn't yet chosen to share out loud. Cael noticed him doing it and said nothing, understanding that whatever Brom was testing, he'd explain it in his own time, the way he explained most things — directly, once he'd actually finished thinking it through, and not a moment before.

He came away from both sessions bruised in new and interesting places, and wrote each one into the observation notebook with the same careful, grateful precision he brought to anything genuinely useful — not the Power Log yet, not until there was something confirmed to put in it, just the patient, incremental accumulation of data that had carried him through every genuine advance since Weaver's Row.

It was the third session, mid-exchange, that changed the shape of what he was actually doing there.

He'd stopped thinking of the sparring as observation by then, somewhere around the twentieth or thirtieth exchange of the second day — it had become simply a thing his body did now, morning after morning, the rhythm of testing an angle, taking the redirected force, resetting, testing again. He was mid-reset, weight still settling back onto both feet, when the internal quiet arrived: the familiar signal that had preceded every fragment notice since the very first one, more than a year ago, in his room at Torvin's by lamplight, in what felt like a different lifetime belonging to a much younger version of himself.

It came as it always came and differently all the same — the quiet first, a stillness that arrived from the inside out, as though someone had drawn a heavy curtain between him and the morning's noise. And then the settling. The other two fragments had each announced themselves into an empty room; this one arrived into an occupied house, and he felt it find its place among the residents — not in the legs where Wind-adjacent coiled, not under the ribs where Pressure-adjacent sat its stone-weight, but *outward*, distributing itself across the surface of him, a thin new layer of attention laid over his skin like a garment he hadn't put on. For one strange moment he could feel the alcove — not see it, feel it: the cool mass of the stone wall at his back, Brom's presence in front of him registering as a shaped weight in the air, faint and blurred, the room suddenly a thing with texture pressed up against every inch of him at once.

Then the sensation collapsed, the curtain lifted, and he was just a boy standing in a training alcove with his weight half-settled, mid-morning light on old stone.

He stopped moving entirely. Brom noticed immediately — the kind of stop that wasn't fatigue, that carried an entirely different quality than the ordinary pause of someone catching their breath, a stillness with a shape to it that Brom had apparently learned to recognize even without knowing yet what it meant.

"What happened?"

"Something." Cael sat down where he was, right there on the training floor, dust settling around him, and opened the Power Log before he attempted to explain anything out loud — the same instinct that had made him write down the first fragment notice before he'd let himself feel anything about it at all. Some things needed to be recorded before they were allowed to become real.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Iron-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
Note: surface-awareness component. Pressure read, limited range.
```

He read it three times before he trusted it. The *Note* field was new — no previous fragment notice, not the Wind-adjacent one at Torvin's, not the Pressure-adjacent one after Feryn, had ever arrived with a functional descriptor attached at all. They'd simply named a type and left him to work out the rest through months of careful testing. This one had come with a hint already built in, as though whatever mechanism generated these notices had grown more articulate, or more confident, or simply had more practice describing what it was doing by now.

He thought, briefly, about the two prior notices — how sparse they'd felt by comparison, a bare word and nothing else, forcing him to build the entire understanding himself from scratch through months of patient testing. This one had arrived already partway explained, as though something on the other end of the process had learned, over a year, that he could be trusted with more information sooner. He didn't know if that was true. He filed the possibility anyway, next to the growing pile of things he suspected but couldn't yet confirm.

Brom crouched down across from him, patient, waiting without pressing until Cael was ready to show him rather than simply tell him. Cael turned the log around and held it out. Brom read the entry once, then again, more slowly — his lips moving very slightly the way a careful reader's did when they wanted to be certain of exactly what a sentence claimed rather than what they assumed it claimed — before handing it back.

"You absorbed part of my Path."

"A fragment of it. Not the whole thing — I don't have the depth of your Iron Skin, just a surface-read component, and I don't think I'm getting the rest of it just by continuing to spar with you. Whatever the mechanism is, it doesn't seem to hand over everything at once."

"Can you use it?"

"Not yet. I have it. Using it will take longer." He thought about the Wind-adjacent fragment's own slow crawl from acquisition to usable, months of clumsy deliberate practice before it had started arriving reliably under pressure. "Months, probably, based on how the others went."

"But eventually."

"I think so."

Brom sat back on his heels, considering this with the same weighing attention he brought to genuinely significant information — the stillness Cael had come to associate with him actually thinking, as opposed to simply reacting. "How many of these do you have?"

Cael thought about the question longer than its size seemed to warrant. It touched the arrangement they'd made two days ago — the promise of information traded honestly in both directions. He thought about what it would mean to show someone outside Lira the actual, complete document — not a summary, not a careful selection, but the whole thing, the private record that mapped out the entire uncertain shape of what he was still becoming.

He'd trusted Brom with smaller things already, over two weeks of watching and one bout and a handful of honest conversations. He decided the trust could bear the weight of the whole document.

He handed over the Power Log. All three entries now — Wind-adjacent, Pressure-adjacent, and the still-warm ink of Iron-adjacent, barely five minutes old.

Brom read through them without hurrying, the same care he brought to everything, turning each page slowly enough that Cael could watch him actually absorbing the content rather than simply scanning it for the parts that mattered to him personally. He paused longest on the Pressure-adjacent entry, its open questions about contact-sourced fragments behaving differently from observation-sourced ones, and something crossed his face that looked, for a moment, like a question he was deciding whether to ask.

He didn't ask it yet. He handed the log back when he'd finished, careful with it the way he'd been careful with everything Cael had shown him so far.

"You're going to be something no one has a word for."

"I know."

"Good." Something settled into his expression that Cael would come to recognize, over the following months, as Brom's own version of genuine delight — not a smile exactly, more a kind of stillness that meant he'd found a problem worth keeping. "That means you get to choose the word."

"What if I choose badly?"

"Then you choose again later. Words aren't fixed the way Paths are supposed to be. You get to keep revising until it fits." He said this with the flat certainty of someone who'd clearly thought about the question longer than the casual delivery suggested. "My family thought Iron Skin at Copper meant a fixed, disappointing word. I decided the word wasn't fixed. Turns out I was right and they were wrong. I expect it'll go the same way for you, on a far larger scale."

They sat with that for a while, the alcove's morning light shifting slightly as the sun climbed, the Ironyard beyond the wall beginning its ordinary drift toward midday noise.

"Does it change anything?" Cael asked, eventually. "Knowing all of it now. The full shape."

Brom considered the question with real care, as he considered most things that weren't simply reflex. "It makes me more interested, not less. I told you that already." He paused. "I think it also makes me more careful. Not of you — for you. Three fragments in a year is fast. Fast usually means either something is very wrong or something is very unusual, and I've watched enough people mistake the second for the first to want to be sure before I decide which one this is."

"You don't think it's wrong."

"I don't know yet. I think it's early. I think you should keep the log the way you're keeping it — precise, honest, more interested in the true answer than the comfortable one — and I think that's probably the best protection either of us has against getting it wrong." He stood, offering Cael a hand up, the sparring session apparently concluded early for the day by unspoken agreement. "Same time tomorrow?"

"Same time tomorrow."

---

Tomorrow, as it turned out, was when the humbling started.

Brom arrived with an agenda he announced from the alcove's entrance, before he'd even set his things down: "New arrangement for today. We're not sparring. You're going to try to use it, and I'm going to watch you fail, and then we're going to figure out why you're failing. I've spent four years learning what that component is supposed to do. I'm the closest thing to an instructor the fragment is ever going to have. We'd be stupid not to use that."

Cael had no objection. He'd spent half the night lying awake with his attention turned outward toward the new resident, trying to find its edges, and had learned exactly one thing: it was there. Present the way the others were present, a thin distributed film of potential laid across his skin, waiting. What it was waiting *for*, he couldn't find. Wind-adjacent had come with a natural handle — the step wanted legs, and legs he understood. Pressure-adjacent had wanted rhythm and a strike to ride. This one wanted something he didn't have a name for, and every attempt to simply *will* it into function slid off it like a hand on wet glass.

The first hour produced nothing at all. Brom stood three paces away, pressure-read fully active, generating — at Cael's request — the strongest, steadiest signature he could produce, a target Brom described as "standing in front of a lit lamp and asking you to notice the light." Cael noticed nothing. He tried reaching for the fragment as he reached for the step — a demand, sharp, timed — and got silence. He tried the Pressure fragment's approach, settling into rhythm and inviting it to join, and got silence. He stood, sat, closed his eyes, held his breath, and got silence, and by the end of the hour the silence had acquired the texture of a locked door being knocked on by someone who owned the house.

"You're commanding it," Brom said, finally, from his post. "I can see you commanding it. Your whole body does the thing it does before a strike — organizes, aims, executes. That's backwards. The read isn't a strike. It's not even an action, properly. It's a *posture.* You don't send it out. You get quiet enough that things come in." He paused, visibly searching his own body for language that had never needed to be language before. "When my instructors first trained the sensitivity, they made me sit against the estate wall for a month doing nothing. I thought it was punishment. It was the entire lesson. You can't listen and speak at the same time, and everything you've built — the step, the amplification, the whole way you fight — is speech."

It was, Cael would write later, the single most useful piece of instruction anyone had ever given him about his own architecture, and it still took the rest of the morning to apply. Getting quiet, it turned out, was a skill he had in abundance everywhere except inside his own body. His attention was a trained instrument that pointed at things; asked to point at nothing, to simply lie open and receive, it twitched back toward aiming like a compass needle finding north. He failed forty times in ways too small to describe. On roughly the forty-first, sitting cross-legged on the alcove floor with his eyes open and unfocused, breath run down to its slowest organized rhythm, he stopped trying to find Brom's signature — genuinely stopped, the internal equivalent of setting a tool down — and the signature walked in on its own.

It was not subtle. That was the shock of it. Brom's pressure-read, three paces away, arrived against his new surface-sense like a hand pressed flat against his sternum — a weight, directional, unmistakably *alive*, textured in a way he had no vocabulary for yet but recognized instantly from the bout: this was what had been reading him. He was feeling the feeler. He held it for four full breaths, mapping as fast as the stillness allowed — direction clean, distance vague, intensity vivid — and then Brom shifted his weight, and Cael's attention twitched toward the movement, and the whole channel collapsed like a startled bird leaving a branch.

The headache arrived about a minute later, a tight band behind both eyes, entirely unlike the honest muscular costs of the other two fragments. Wind spent his legs. Pressure spent his core. This spent something he could only locate as *attention itself* — four breaths of successful reading had drawn down the same reserve that a full morning of compound-gaze observation drew, compressed into seconds.

"Well?" Brom asked.

"I had you. Four breaths. Then you moved and I lost it." Cael pressed the heels of his hands briefly against his eyes. "And it costs. More than it should, for four breaths."

"It costs me almost nothing. But I've been paying into it for four years — the price comes down with use. Mine did." Brom crossed the alcove and sat down across from him, close enough now that when Cael cautiously opened the surface-sense again, the signature nearly shouted. "Again. This time I'm going to move, slowly, and you're going to try to keep me."

He couldn't. That was the afternoon's second lesson, delivered over two further hours of patient failure: the read, in its current infant state, survived only in stillness. The moment Cael moved — shifted weight, turned his head, so much as organized his body toward movement — the listening posture broke, displaced by the same trained readiness that made him useful in a fight. Worse: it wouldn't share. When he tried, experimentally, to hold the surface-sense open while priming the Wind-adjacent step, the two did not so much conflict as flatly displace one another, the step's gathering readiness shouldering the listening aside the instant it formed, one resident claiming the whole hallway. Brom, watching him try, offered the only consolation on offer: "The read runs under everything I do, but it took two years before it would run under movement. You're on day one. Stop grading day one against year four."

By the session's end, Cael's total functional capability with his third fragment amounted to this: seated or standing still, in a quiet space, against a single strong signature at three paces or less, he could open the channel in about four breaths and hold it for five or six before either the target moved, his own attention twitched, or the draining band behind his eyes forced him to let go. Against the crowded, roaring conditions of an actual bout, it was as useless as a candle in daylight. He said so, out loud, because honesty about capability was the whole discipline.

"Useless in a fight," Brom agreed, standing, gathering his things. "For now. So was I, the first year. My family's investment in the Path assumed a decade. You don't get a decade, so you'll practice the way you practice everything, which is more than anyone I've met. But hear the actual lesson of today, because it isn't about effort." He looked down at Cael, still seated, and said it plainly. "Every other thing you own is a weapon, and you learned it by aiming. This one will never once be aimed. If you try to make it a weapon, you'll build a bad copy of your other tools and miss what it's actually for. It's for *knowing.* You, of all people, should not need convincing that knowing is worth owning on its own terms."

---

Cael walked back to the boarding house alone that afternoon, Lira off running an errand of her own, the band behind his eyes finally loosening, and found himself turning the whole two days over as he did anything that had shifted the shape of what he understood about himself.

He thought about Feryn, months ago now, asking the same essential question — *what tier are you* — and accepting *I don't know yet* as a complete answer, satisfied simply to have asked. He thought about Darrow, on the ground after the marquee bout, asking the identical question in different words. He thought, now, about Brom, who'd gone one step further than either of them: not just curious about the shape of the mystery, but actively invested in helping him solve it, offering his own Path as raw material for the solving without ever once suggesting that made him owed anything in return. There was no precedent in Cael's experience for what had happened in the alcove today — a practitioner spending an entire training day teaching someone else to use a stolen piece of his own inheritance, patiently, as though the theft were the most interesting gift he'd ever been given.

It was, Cael understood, a different category of relationship than anything he'd built before Ardenmere — not the unconditional, foundational loyalty of Hesk, which had existed before Cael had done anything at all to earn it, and not quite the fierce, hard-won partnership he and Lira had built out of a year of shared risk and shared recovery. Something adjacent to both, built on curiosity rather than obligation, on two people who'd each decided, independently, that the other was worth understanding properly rather than simply tolerating.

He wrote it into the observation notebook first, briefly, a tactical note about how Brom processed new information — carefully, without visible alarm, weighing a fact fully before reacting to it rather than reacting first and reconsidering later. It was, Cael thought, a genuinely useful thing to know about someone he expected to keep training beside for a long time.

Then he opened the Power Log, and gave the third fragment the structured entry the first day's evidence had earned — the same format as the others, filled in with a day's honest failures:

*Iron-adjacent — working ledger, day one.*

*Gain: surface pressure read. Confirmed functional against a single strong signature at three paces, stationary, quiet conditions. What it returns: presence, direction, aliveness, some texture. Not yet: distance with precision, identity, anything at range, anything in motion.*

*Cost: attention itself — the same reserve the compound gaze draws on, spent at many times the rate. A few breaths of reading produced an hour of headache. Secondary cost: exclusivity. It will not run alongside either other fragment yet. Opening it means setting everything else down.*

*Useful where: reading one known subject under controlled conditions. Eventually — if Brom's arc is any guide — under movement, in noise, beneath the other abilities. Not yet.*

*Fails where: everywhere else. Any movement of mine. Any startle of attention. Multiple signatures — untested, but I already know what a crowd will do to an open channel, because I know what a crowd does to unguarded listening of any kind. Test carefully. Small doses.*

*Honest summary: I own something I cannot use. The last two times I owned something I couldn't use, the gap between owning and using was months of exactly this kind of day. Begin.*

And beneath it, the entry that actually mattered:

*Three fragments now. Wind-adjacent. Pressure-adjacent. Iron-adjacent. Brom knows all three — the full log, not a summary. He didn't flinch at any of it. He asked better questions about it than I've asked myself. Then he spent a full day teaching me to use the piece of himself I'd taken, and seemed to enjoy it.*

*I keep expecting the people who matter to me to eventually reach the edge of their patience with how strange this is. Hesk hasn't. Lira hasn't. Now Brom hasn't either. I'm starting to wonder if that edge was something I invented to brace myself against a disappointment that keeps declining to actually arrive — a wound I built in advance, out of Denvash's reflexes, that Ardenmere has never once given me reason to reopen.*

*Three fragments. One friend who reads pressure signatures for a living and still can't categorize mine. One partner who's known the whole shape of it since before I told her properly. A grandfather three days' letter away who's never once asked me to be smaller than whatever I turn out to be.*

*I don't know what I am yet. I know, more clearly than I did a year ago, that I'm not carrying the finding-out alone.*

He closed the log, and sat for a while in the quiet of the boarding house's common room — and caught himself, once, in the stillness, letting the new surface-sense drift halfway open just to feel the room's shape press faintly against it, the walls and the warm mass of the stove and somewhere above him a boarder's footsteps registering as a soft rhythmic weight. He let it close. He had months of failures ahead of him before it would be anything more than this. He found he didn't mind. He'd been here twice before, at the bottom of exactly this climb, and both times the climb itself had turned out to be where most of the understanding lived.

Ardenmere had given him more than he'd had any right to expect from a district he'd arrived in with nothing but a bag, a notebook he couldn't yet open, and one direction to walk in. Tonight it had given him a third thing that wasn't a Path, a teacher it had never occurred to him to hope for, and a headache he'd earned honestly. He went to bed early, and slept, and did not dream of doors.

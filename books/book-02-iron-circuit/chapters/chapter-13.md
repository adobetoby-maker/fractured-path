# Chapter 13 — Fragment Three

The first session set the terms, though neither of them said so out loud at the time.

They met in the side alcove an hour before the Ironyard's morning crowd usually arrived, the stone floor still cool from the night, and Brom opened without preamble by adding three working conditions to the terms they'd already agreed at the market, counted off on his fingers like a man reading a short but non-negotiable contract.

"No holding back on my end. If I moderate the pressure-read or soften the amplification, you're not learning anything about the real thing — you're learning about a version of me I invented for your comfort, and that's worthless to both of us." A second finger. "You tell me when something happens. Not eventually. Immediately, even if you don't understand it yet, even if all you can say is *something.*" A third finger. "And whatever you learn about how I work, you don't repeat it to anyone who could use it against me in a real bout. I'm trusting you with my actual method, not a demonstration of it."

"Agreed," Cael said, and meant it, understanding without needing it spelled out further that Brom was handing him something considerably more valuable than sparring time — the genuine, unguarded shape of a Path most people who'd fought him had only ever seen through its results, never its process.

Brom used his Iron Skin fully from that very first exchange — not holding back the pressure-read, not moderating the amplification mode, exactly as promised. The close-range, sustained exposure at genuine intensity was a different condition entirely from watching Brom from the audience, or even fighting him in the formal bout, where Cael's attention had been divided between observation and the immediate, urgent business of not losing too badly. Here, with nothing at stake beyond the information itself, Cael could simply watch, fully, for the first time — not defending, not calculating an exit, just absorbing the shape of something as it happened to him at close range, over and over, until the shape started to feel less like a threat and more like a text he was slowly learning to read.

The first session taught him mostly what he already suspected: the amplification triggered at contact, not before, exactly as his weeks of alcove observation had suggested. But confirming it from the outside and feeling it happen to his own body turned out to be considerably different categories of knowledge. A strike that looked, from the crowd, like a clean absorption revealed itself, up close, as something with texture — a specific resistance that gathered a fraction of a second before impact and then released all at once, redirected, the way a held breath releases when you finally let it go. He hadn't been able to see that from the audience. He could feel it now, in the ringing ache along his forearm where he'd caught the first real exchange badly.

The second session refined the timing further — he could feel, now, physically, the fractional difference between Brom absorbing a strike defensively and Brom redirecting one offensively, a difference his eyes alone had never quite been able to isolate from the audience's distance. He started testing it deliberately partway through, throwing strikes at slightly different speeds to see whether the timing window shifted, and found, by the session's end, that it didn't — the trigger point stayed fixed regardless of how fast or slow the approach, which told him something useful about how deeply automatic the technique had become for Brom, embedded past the point where conscious adjustment could easily reach it anymore.

Brom, for his part, seemed to be running his own quiet experiments. Twice during the second session he varied his own response deliberately, watching Cael's reaction rather than simply defending, the specific attention of someone testing a hypothesis of his own that he hadn't yet chosen to share out loud. Cael noticed him doing it and said nothing, understanding that whatever Brom was testing, he'd explain it in his own time, the way he explained most things — directly, once he'd actually finished thinking it through, and not a moment before.

He came away from both sessions bruised in new and interesting places, and wrote each one into the observation notebook with the same careful, grateful precision he brought to anything genuinely useful — not the Power Log yet, not until there was something confirmed to put in it, just the patient, incremental accumulation of data that had carried him through every genuine advance since Weaver's Row.

It was the third session, mid-exchange, that changed the shape of what he was actually doing there.

He'd stopped thinking of the sparring as observation by then, somewhere around the twentieth or thirtieth exchange of the second day — it had become simply a thing his body did now, morning after morning, the specific rhythm of testing an angle, taking the redirected force, resetting, testing again. He was mid-reset, weight still settling back onto both feet, when the internal quiet arrived: the familiar signal that had preceded every fragment notice since the very first one, months and months ago, in a courtyard behind Torvin's that felt like a different lifetime belonging to a considerably younger version of himself.

He stopped moving entirely. Brom noticed immediately — the kind of stop that wasn't fatigue, that carried an entirely different quality than the ordinary pause of someone catching their breath, a stillness with a specific shape to it that Brom had apparently learned to recognize even without knowing yet what it meant.

"What happened?"

"Something." Cael sat down where he was, right there on the training floor, dust settling around him, and opened the Power Log before he attempted to explain anything out loud — the same instinct that had made him write down the first fragment notice before he'd let himself feel anything about it at all. Some things needed to be recorded before they were allowed to become real.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Iron-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
Note: surface-awareness component. Pressure read, limited range.
```

He read it three times before he trusted it. The *Note* field was new — no previous fragment notice, not the Wind-adjacent one in a courtyard months ago, not the Pressure-adjacent one after Feryn, had ever arrived with a functional descriptor attached at all. They'd simply named a type and left him to work out the rest through months of careful testing. This one had come with a hint already built in, as though whatever mechanism generated these notices had grown more articulate, or more confident, or simply had more practice describing what it was doing by now.

He thought, briefly, about the two prior notices — how sparse they'd felt by comparison, a bare word and nothing else, forcing him to build the entire understanding himself from scratch through months of patient testing. This one had arrived already partway explained, as though something on the other end of the process had learned, over a year, that he could be trusted with more information sooner. He didn't know if that was true. He filed the possibility anyway, next to the growing pile of things he suspected but couldn't yet confirm.

Brom crouched down across from him, patient, waiting without pressing until Cael was ready to show him rather than simply tell him. Cael turned the log around and held it out. Brom read the entry once, then again, more slowly — his lips moving very slightly the way a careful reader's did when they wanted to be certain of exactly what a sentence claimed rather than what they assumed it claimed — before handing it back.

"You absorbed part of my Path."

"A fragment of it. Not the whole thing — I don't have the depth of your Iron Skin, just a surface-read component, and I don't think I'm getting the rest of it just by continuing to spar with you. Whatever the mechanism is, it doesn't seem to hand over everything at once."

"Can you use it?"

"Not yet. I have it. Using it will take longer." He thought about the Wind-adjacent fragment's own slow crawl from acquisition to usable, months of clumsy deliberate practice before it had started arriving reliably under pressure. "Months, probably, based on how the others went."

"But eventually."

"I think so."

Brom sat back on his heels, considering this with the same weighing attention he brought to genuinely significant information — the specific stillness Cael had come to associate with him actually thinking, as opposed to simply reacting. "How many of these do you have?"

Cael thought about the question longer than its size seemed to warrant, turning it over the way he turned over anything that touched on the arrangement they'd made two days ago, the promise of information traded honestly in both directions. He thought about what it would mean to show someone outside Lira the actual, complete document — not a summary, not a careful selection, but the whole thing, the private record that mapped out the entire uncertain shape of what he was still becoming.

He'd trusted Brom with less than this already, over two weeks of watching and one bout and a handful of honest conversations. He decided the trust could bear the weight of a little more.

He handed over the Power Log. All three entries now — Wind-adjacent, Pressure-adjacent, and the still-warm ink of Iron-adjacent, barely five minutes old.

Brom read through them without hurrying, the same unhurried care he brought to everything, turning each page slowly enough that Cael could watch him actually absorbing the content rather than simply scanning it for the parts that mattered to him personally. He paused longest on the Pressure-adjacent entry, its open questions about contact-sourced fragments behaving differently from observation-sourced ones, and something crossed his face that looked, for a moment, like a question he was deciding whether to ask.

He didn't ask it yet. He handed the log back when he'd finished, careful with it the way he'd been careful with everything Cael had shown him so far.

"You're going to be something no one has a word for."

"I know."

"Good." Something settled into his expression that Cael would come to recognize, over the following months, as Brom's particular version of genuine delight — not a smile exactly, more a kind of stillness that meant he'd found a problem worth keeping. "That means you get to choose the word."

"What if I choose badly?"

"Then you choose again later. Words aren't fixed the way Paths are supposed to be. You get to keep revising until it fits." He said this with the flat certainty of someone who'd clearly thought about the question longer than the casual delivery suggested. "My family thought Iron Skin at Copper meant a fixed, disappointing word. I decided the word wasn't fixed. Turns out I was right and they were wrong. I expect it'll go the same way for you, on a considerably larger scale."

They sat with that for a while, the alcove's morning light shifting slightly as the sun climbed, the Ironyard beyond the wall beginning its ordinary drift toward midday noise.

"Does it change anything?" Cael asked, eventually. "Knowing all of it now. The full shape."

Brom considered the question with real care, the way he considered most things that weren't simply reflex. "It makes me more interested, not less. I told you that already." He paused. "I think it also makes me more careful. Not of you — for you. Three fragments in a year is fast. Fast usually means either something is very wrong or something is very unusual, and I've watched enough people mistake the second for the first to want to be sure before I decide which one this is."

"You don't think it's wrong."

"I don't know yet. I think it's early. I think you should keep the log the way you're keeping it — precise, honest, more interested in the true answer than the comfortable one — and I think that's probably the best protection either of us has against getting it wrong." He stood, offering Cael a hand up, the sparring session apparently concluded early for the day by unspoken agreement. "Same time tomorrow?"

"Same time tomorrow."

---

Cael walked back to the boarding house alone that afternoon, Lira off running an errand of her own, and found himself turning the whole exchange over the way he turned over anything that had shifted the shape of what he understood about himself.

He thought about Feryn, months ago now, asking the same essential question — *what tier are you* — and accepting *I don't know yet* as a complete answer, satisfied simply to have asked. He thought about Darrow, on the ground after the marquee bout, asking the identical question in different words. He thought, now, about Brom, who'd gone one step further than either of them: not just curious about the shape of the mystery, but actively invested in helping him solve it, offering his own Path as raw material for the solving without ever once suggesting that made him owed anything in return.

It was, Cael understood, a different category of relationship than anything he'd built before Ardenmere — not the unconditional, foundational loyalty of Hesk, which had existed before Cael had done anything at all to earn it, and not quite the fierce, hard-won partnership he and Lira had built out of a year of shared risk and shared recovery. Something adjacent to both, built on curiosity rather than obligation, on two people who'd each decided, independently, that the other was worth understanding properly rather than simply tolerating.

He wrote it into the observation notebook first, briefly, a tactical note about how Brom processed new information — carefully, without visible alarm, weighing a fact fully before reacting to it rather than reacting first and reconsidering later. It was, Cael thought, a genuinely useful thing to know about someone he expected to keep training beside for a long time.

Then he opened the Power Log, and wrote the entry that actually mattered.

*Three fragments now. Wind-adjacent. Pressure-adjacent. Iron-adjacent. Brom knows all three — the full log, not a summary. He didn't flinch at any of it. He asked better questions about it than I've asked myself.*

*I keep expecting the people who matter to me to eventually reach the edge of their patience with how strange this is. Hesk hasn't. Lira hasn't. Now Brom hasn't either. I'm starting to wonder if that edge was something I invented to brace myself against a disappointment that keeps declining to actually arrive — a wound I built in advance, out of Denvash's reflexes, that Ardenmere has never once given me reason to reopen.*

*Three fragments. One friend who reads pressure signatures for a living and still can't categorize mine. One partner who's known the whole shape of it since before I told her properly. A grandfather three days' letter away who's never once asked me to be smaller than whatever I turn out to be.*

*I don't know what I am yet. I know, more clearly than I did a year ago, that I'm not carrying the finding-out alone.*

He closed the log, and sat for a while in the quiet of the boarding house's common room, and thought, not for the first time, that Ardenmere had given him considerably more than he'd had any right to expect from a district he'd arrived in with nothing but a bag, a notebook he couldn't yet open, and one direction to walk in.

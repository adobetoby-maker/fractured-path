# Chapter 22 — The Scout

The crowd took the better part of an hour to fully clear, the Ironyard's usual post-bout current of arguing gamblers and re-hashed exchanges thicker than Cael had ever seen it. Reydan's name went around in a dozen different tones — disbelief, grudging respect, and here and there the flat irritation of a man who'd bet against him and lost — and under all of it ran the low, steady hum of a crowd that knew it had watched something worth talking about for weeks. Nobody left quickly. Nobody ever did, after a bout like that. They drifted in loose knots toward the gates, arguing the fourth exchange back into shape from six different angles, and Cael sat where it had put him and let them.

He hadn't moved much since the fight ended. He'd meant to — there was a version of the evening where he stood, found water, walked the ache out of his legs before it set — but every time he gathered the intention his body quietly vetoed it, and by the time the crowd had thinned to its last stubborn stragglers he was still sitting in the packed dirt at the circle's western edge, forearms on his knees, breathing in the unhurried way a person breathes once the actual danger has passed and the body has finally been given permission to notice how much it cost. It wasn't the exhaustion of a long day. It was the kind that only showed up once he'd spent everything he had and then found, somehow, a little more underneath that — and was now presenting the full bill for both, all at once, with interest.

Lira sat beside him, close enough that her shoulder was nearly against his, one hand resting near — not on — his arm, the particular kind of nearness she offered a body that had just taken real damage and didn't need anything added to it yet. She hadn't said much since the fight ended either. She'd said what mattered in the first minute, the fierce, wordless grip of her arms around him the moment the crowd's noise had confirmed what he already knew, and everything since then had been the quieter work of simply staying close while he came back into himself.

"You're doing the thing," she said eventually, not looking at him, watching the last of the crowd instead.

"What thing."

"The thing where you sit very still and catalogue every part of your body that hurts, in order, like you're taking an inventory for someone who's going to ask you to justify the damage later."

"I am taking an inventory."

"I know. That's the thing." But there was no real correction in it, just the familiar shape of an old observation, offered without heat. She'd stopped trying to talk him out of the habit months ago. It was, she'd told him once, simply how he came down from something — the same instinct that filled the notebook every night, turned sideways and pointed at his own ribs instead of the world outside them.

Brom stood a few feet off, arms crossed, watching the last stragglers filter out through the main doors with the same unhurried attention he brought to everything — a man who'd apparently decided that if the crowd wanted to take its time leaving, he could take his time watching it go. He hadn't said much since the bout ended either, though his silence carried a different texture than Lira's. Hers was presence. His was assessment, the kind he did automatically now, whether or not anyone had asked him to.

"You held the fourth exchange longer than you needed to," he said, apropos of nothing, still watching the doors rather than Cael.

"I noticed."

"I'm not criticizing it. I'm noting it. There's a difference between holding a position because it's still working and holding it because stopping feels like admitting something. You did the second one, for about two seconds longer than the first one justified." He finally looked over. "Worth knowing, for next time. Two seconds is a long time, against someone who's paying attention."

"Was Reydan paying attention?"

"Reydan," Brom said, "was paying more attention to you tonight than anyone else in this yard, including me. I'd factor that into whatever you're planning next."

Somewhere behind them, near the gate, a voice rose above the general noise long enough for Cael to catch his own name in it — one of the regulars, someone whose face he half-recognized and whose name he'd never learned, calling something congratulatory across the thinning crowd before being swallowed back into the general current of departure. It should have felt good. Some distant part of him registered that it probably did feel good, the way a fact could be true without yet being fully felt. Mostly what he registered was that his shoulders ached in a way that suggested he'd hold the fourth exchange's stance in his sleep tonight, replaying it whether he wanted to or not.

Cael let that sit, filing it the way he filed most things Brom said after a fight — not immediately useful, but the kind of observation that tended to matter later, once the adrenaline had cleared enough for him to actually use it. He flexed his right hand experimentally, found it stiffer than he'd like, and decided that whatever damage the fourth exchange had done, it hadn't done anything that a night's sleep and Lira's grudging insistence on actually resting wouldn't mostly fix.

"I keep thinking I should feel more like celebrating," he admitted, after a while. "This was the fight. The one that was supposed to matter."

"You will," Lira said. "Give it an hour. Right now you're just a body that got hit a lot and hasn't caught up with what the body accomplished yet."

"That's a very practical way of putting it."

"I'm a very practical person, when I'm not being the other kind."

He almost smiled at that — the first genuine loosening of the evening — and might have said something back, except that was the moment he first registered the woman.

She hadn't been near the circle's edge a moment before, or if she had, she'd been near it in the unremarkable way a dozen other stragglers were near it, present without registering as anything worth a second look. Nobody had noticed the woman approaching until she was nearly at the circle's edge — which was, Cael understood the moment he registered her properly, itself a kind of skill. She moved through a thinning crowd the way he imagined a genuinely careful observer moved through any room: present, unremarkable, gone from anyone's attention half a second after they'd looked away. He'd spent months learning to notice exactly that quality in other people, and seeing it now, fully formed and clearly practiced, sent a small, cold thread of attention up through the general fog of exhaustion.

She was perhaps forty, dressed plainly enough to have passed for a circuit regular at a glance, except that nothing about her carried a circuit regular's particular wear — no bruising, no calluses visible on the hands, the bearing of someone who'd spent a career observing rather than fighting. Her coat was travel-worn rather than fight-worn, her boots the kind that had covered distance rather than dirt-circle ground. She stopped a respectful distance away and waited, without impatience, for Cael to notice her fully before she spoke — the same patience, he thought, that had presumably let her stand at the edge of six different bouts without anyone in this yard clocking her as anything but background.

Lira's hand went still against his arm. Brom uncrossed his own, a small shift that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't spent months learning what it meant.

"Quenna," she said, introducing herself directly, no hesitation in it at all. "Senior teaching-practitioner, Greyvane Academy. Silver-tier. I specialize in irregular Path development assessment." She said this with the plain efficiency of someone who'd delivered the same introduction many times before and had long since stopped needing to soften it, the sentences arriving in the exact same order they'd probably arrived in a hundred times before this one. "I've attended this circuit six times over the past two months. Dace knew my pass was academy-issued. He didn't know which academy until tonight."

Cael sat up straighter despite the ache in doing so, cataloguing her the way he cataloged anything new and potentially significant — even now, even exhausted, the habit didn't switch itself off. "Six times," he said. "Watching me specifically?"

"Watching the circuit generally, at first. You specifically, for the last four of the six." She said this without any performance of secrecy, as though the surveillance itself weren't the interesting part, merely the necessary preamble to whatever came next. "I don't waste institutional travel budget on curiosity alone. By the second visit I'd narrowed my attention considerably."

She had, Cael noticed, a way of standing that gave nothing away and cost her nothing to maintain — not the deliberate blankness of someone hiding discomfort, but the easy neutrality of someone who had simply had this conversation, or one close enough to it, often enough that it no longer required effort. Lira was watching her with the particular focus she reserved for anyone claiming authority she hadn't yet verified. Brom had gone entirely quiet, which Cael had learned by now meant he was listening harder, not less.

Her voice, now that he had a proper stretch of it to work with, carried none of the performed warmth Cael associated with people trying to sell him something, and none of the flat institutional coldness he'd braced for either. It sat somewhere between the two — plain, unhurried, the voice of a woman reporting facts she'd already confirmed rather than facts she was hoping he'd simply believe. She hadn't once glanced toward Lira or Brom while she spoke, though he suspected that was less a slight than a calculation: whatever she'd come to say, she'd come to say it to him directly, and would circle back to the others only once the ground with him had been properly laid.

She turned to Cael fully, and delivered the offer without further preamble, the way Brom might have delivered it — no warming up, no softening, simply the information laid out plainly for him to evaluate on its own merits.

"Greyvane has a provision called the demonstration-provision track. It was written, originally, for unkindled prospects under observation — young practitioners whose ability has shown itself before an Arbiter has formally classified it, admitted on the strength of demonstrated capability rather than a paper designation that doesn't exist yet." She paused, letting that land before continuing. "You're demonstrably beyond unkindled. But the provision's actual language — I've read it more times than most people who wrote it, I suspect — applies to any practitioner without a standard Arbiter-issued classification. Which you qualify for, whatever the specific reason happens to be. We've been looking for the right candidate to test that reading of the provision for some time. I believe you're it."

There was a small, precise satisfaction underneath the last sentence — not pride, exactly, more the quiet pleasure of a professional who'd spent a long time refining a theory and had finally found something to test it against. Cael filed that too. Whatever else Quenna was, she wasn't merely reciting policy. She had a stake in this beyond her institution's.

"The Compact monitors me," Cael said. It felt important to say plainly, before anything else, rather than let her discover it later and wonder why he'd withheld it.

"We're aware. We have institutional legal staff who exist specifically to handle exactly this category of complication — it isn't the first time Greyvane has admitted a student the Compact took an interest in, and I doubt it will be the last."

She said it the way she'd said everything else — without weight, without reassurance performed for his benefit, simply a fact laid alongside the other facts. Cael found that, oddly, more convincing than sympathy would have been. Sympathy could be manufactured for an audience of one. This sounded like something she'd said to enough people, in enough versions of this exact conversation, that it had worn down into procedure.

"My Path classification is—"

"Our assessment staff will evaluate you monthly, by demonstration, not by declaration." She held up a hand, not unkindly, cutting off the explanation before he could offer it. "That's the provision's actual requirement, in full. We're not asking what your Path is. We're not asking you to name it, defend it, or produce paperwork that doesn't exist. We're asking whether you can demonstrate it consistently, month after month, under observation, to a standard our assessment staff considers meaningful." She glanced toward the circle, still marked in the packed dirt from the night's bout. "Tonight qualified. Comfortably."

Cael let the words settle before he answered, running them the way he ran everything that mattered — turning them over for the catch he half expected to find and not finding one. "You're not asking me to explain what I am."

"I'm asking you to keep doing what you did tonight, on a schedule, in front of people qualified to notice it. That's the entire requirement. Everything else is administrative texture around that one fact." She said it without impatience, though he suspected she'd had to say some version of that sentence to a great many nervous, suspicious prospects before him, each of them waiting for the hidden clause that would eventually turn out to be the real cost.

"And if the assessment staff decides, some month, that whatever I demonstrate doesn't meet their standard?"

"Then we'd have a conversation about what changed, and why, before anyone made a decision about your standing. I don't administer this provision to catch people failing it. I administer it because the ordinary institutional pathway fails people like you before they ever get the chance to prove anything, and I happen to think that's a waste worth correcting." For the first time since she'd approached, something in her voice sharpened slightly — not anger, but the edge of a conviction she'd evidently held for a long time and argued for more than once, probably against colleagues who didn't share it. "I've watched circuits like this one long enough to know what they produce when nobody with actual resources bothers to look. You're not the first talent I've found in one. You're the first I've been this certain about."

"How many others?" Lira asked, before Cael could.

"Three, over eleven years. One washed out within a season — not a failure of ability, a failure of temperament. He wasn't built for the structure of an institutional life, and I should have seen that sooner than I did. Two are still at Greyvane, doing well. One of them teaches now." Quenna met Lira's look directly, unbothered by the question, the answer arriving with the same unhurried economy as everything else she'd said tonight. "I don't offer this lightly, and I don't offer it to flatter anyone. It costs an institution real resources to run an irregular candidate through assessment every month instead of clearing them once at intake and never looking again. I wouldn't be standing in this yard if I didn't think the cost was worth it."

Cael turned that over the way he turned over anything that arrived sounding too convenient — testing it for the seam where the pitch would show through the practicality. He couldn't find one. That, more than anything she'd said outright, was what settled the last of his doubt about whether this was a genuine offer or an elaborate one; a recruiter selling a fantasy would have made the numbers rounder, the washout story softer, the whole arrangement sound easier than three tracks and a monthly assessment schedule actually was. Quenna hadn't smoothed a single edge off it. She'd simply laid the edges out and let him decide whether he wanted to hold them.

---

Lira spoke before Cael could respond. "What about expelled practitioners?"

Quenna looked at her with the same unhurried, thoroughly prepared attention she'd brought to the entire conversation so far — nothing improvised in it, the look of a woman who had done her reading well before tonight and simply hadn't mentioned it until asked. "Wind Path, Copper-tier, expelled from Fenmark Academy."

"Yes."

"We have a re-certification candidate provision. Different track, same academic access — requires monthly reassessment, demonstrated continued advancement."

"I can do that."

"I assumed you could."

There was something almost warm buried in that last exchange, underneath the flat professional cadence Quenna had maintained through everything else — not quite approval, but the recognition of someone who had clearly reviewed a file before ever crossing the yard tonight and had already formed her own opinion of it, independent of whatever Fenmark's official record said. Lira didn't relax exactly, but something in her posture eased by a degree, the particular tension of a person bracing for judgment that hadn't arrived.

Brom, who had said nothing throughout the entire exchange, shifted his weight slightly, and Quenna's eyes moved to him for the space of a breath — a brief, assessing look, the same kind Cael had watched her give the circle itself earlier, cataloguing something and filing it away without comment. She didn't address him directly, and whatever this offer meant, or didn't mean, for a Copper-tier practitioner with no irregularity attached to his name at all, she left it unspoken tonight. If Brom noticed the omission, he gave no outward sign of it.

Cael asked for a day to think it over. Quenna gave him three, and left as unceremoniously as she'd arrived — no lingering, no attempt to press the advantage of a pitch that had clearly landed, just a small nod to each of them in turn before she turned and walked back the way she'd come. He watched her go the same way he'd watched her arrive, tracking her progress until she'd folded back into what remained of the crowd and vanished from sight entirely, the same trick performed in reverse. Within a dozen steps she was simply another figure leaving the Ironyard, indistinguishable from anyone else drifting toward the gates, and he understood, watching it happen, that this too was a skill, deliberately maintained rather than accidental — visible exactly when she chose to be, and invisible the rest of the time. It left him faintly unsettled, in a way he couldn't entirely name, to have watched both halves of that skill performed so completely in the space of a single conversation.

---

Once she was gone, the yard felt larger than it had all evening, the way a room does once the person who'd been holding everyone's attention finally leaves it. The last stragglers were gone too now, the torches along the walls burning low, and the three of them sat in the kind of quiet that follows something too large to process all at once — each of them turning it over in whatever private way they turned things over.

"She wasn't surprised by any of your questions," Brom said finally.

"No," Cael agreed. He hadn't been surprised by any of his own answers either, now that he thought about it — every response had arrived exactly where a reasonable question would expect it to, no hesitation, no visible calculation behind it, the answers of someone who had already mapped this entire conversation long before tonight, probably in more than one version, for more than one possible candidate sitting where Cael was sitting now.

"She's done this before."

"Or she prepared very carefully for us." Cael turned the possibility over as he said it, weighing it against the alternative Brom had offered. Both were probably true, in some proportion he had no way of measuring yet. A woman who'd watched him across six visits and narrowed her attention by the second one hadn't wandered into tonight without a plan for how the conversation would go if he asked exactly the questions he had, in exactly the order he'd asked them.

"Both, probably." A silence settled over the three of them, weighted with everything the offer actually meant.

Cael sat with that weight for a long moment, letting it settle into whatever shape it would eventually take rather than forcing it into something manageable before he'd properly felt it. An academy. A real one, with a provision written into its charter for people close enough to his situation that the language could be stretched to cover him — which was its own strange kind of comfort, and its own kind of uncertainty, both at once. He thought about the Ironyard's ceiling, the one he'd half-known was coming for weeks now, even before Quenna had arrived to put a name and a timeline to it — the growing, steady sense that the circuit had given him nearly everything it had left to give, and that whatever came after would require something the circuit itself simply couldn't provide. He thought about Hesk's letters, and what it would mean to write the next one. He thought, with a small, guilty pull under everything else, about Vell's ledger, and the baker two streets from Torvin's, and how strange it would feel to leave a place that had only just finished learning his name.

Lira was working through something of her own, he could tell — the particular stillness she wore when a decision was arriving faster than she wanted to admit out loud. She'd asked about expelled practitioners in the flat, careful tone of someone who already knew the answer mattered enormously and had trained herself not to let that show before she'd heard it. Whatever Quenna had told her, it had clearly been the answer worth having. He didn't ask her about it yet, not tonight. There would be time for that over the three days Quenna had given them — more time than either of them had actually asked for, he suspected, though he doubted she'd offered the extra days by accident. A single day pressed a decision into shape too quickly to trust. Three let it settle honestly, on its own.

Brom hadn't said much beyond his two observations, but Cael had learned enough about him by now to know that his silence carried its own weight — Brom's version of turning something over, done in stillness rather than words, the same discipline he brought to a fight or a lesson or anything else that deserved his full attention before he offered an opinion on it. Whatever Quenna's offer meant, or didn't mean, for a Copper-tier practitioner with no irregularity attached to his record at all, Brom hadn't raised it tonight, and Cael filed that fact away too, uncertain yet what to make of it beyond a vague, unformed sense that the conversation wasn't actually finished — only paused, the way Brom paused most things he intended to come back to later, on his own terms.

"So," Lira said, eventually.

"So," Cael agreed.

Neither of them moved to fill the silence further. The Ironyard had gone quiet around them, the last of the torches guttering low, the packed dirt of the circle still holding the marks of a fight that had, an hour ago, felt like the largest thing that could plausibly happen to him in Ardenmere. It didn't feel small now, exactly. It felt like the first half of something — the way the certification office in Denvash had once felt like the first half of something, before he'd understood how much further the second half would actually carry him.

He thought, unexpectedly, of Feryn — the memory arriving sideways, the way memories tended to when a body was too tired to keep them properly filed. Feryn hadn't offered him an academy. Feryn had offered him a fight, and left behind something Cael hadn't fully understood the value of until months later, once the fragment had settled into something he could actually use. He wondered, sitting in the packed dirt with the offer of an entire institution now sitting in his lap instead, whether Quenna's assessment staff would ever produce anything as unexpectedly generous as that single afternoon had, or whether an institution, however well-intentioned, simply worked differently than a person choosing to hand something over because they'd decided he was worth the risk of it.

Lira shifted beside him, drawing her knees up and wrapping her arms loosely around them, watching the last of the low torchlight the way she watched most things when she was thinking hard and didn't want it to show on her face. He knew that posture. It meant she'd already half-decided something and was giving herself the evening to catch up with her own conclusion before she said it out loud to either of them. He didn't push. There would be three days for that, and pushing tonight, on a night this heavy already, felt like exactly the wrong instinct to indulge.

Brom, for his part, had gone back to watching the doors, though there was nothing left out there to watch — the last of the Ironyard's regulars long since gone, the gates standing open on an empty street. It was, Cael suspected, simply where Brom's eyes went when the rest of him needed somewhere to rest while he worked through something too large to say plainly. He wondered what a real academy's faculty made of a man like Brom on paper — Copper-tier, no particular renown, years older than the standard enrollment age for anyone without an unusual reason for arriving late — and found, turning the question over, that he wasn't actually worried about the answer. Whatever Brom decided to do about Greyvane, he'd decide it the same way he decided everything: carefully, on his own schedule, and without asking anyone's permission to take his time about it.

He sat with that thought a while longer, the ache in his hands and ribs a quiet, steady counterpoint to everything else, and let the evening finally, fully arrive — the fight already receding into memory even as the shape of what came after it was only just beginning to take one of its own.

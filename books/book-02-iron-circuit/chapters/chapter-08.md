# Chapter 8 — Brom

He was sixteen, and he had walked out of Velmere before his family could finish deciding what to do about him.

The estate sat in the hill country two provinces east of Ardenmere, old enough that nobody in the household bothered mentioning how old it was anymore, the way you stop mentioning a fact everyone already carries. Three generations of Velmere Kindlings had resolved to Gold-tier or close enough to it that the difference didn't register at dinner. His grandmother had been Gold. His mother had been Gold, technically — closer to Silver by the raw numbers, but Gold on the classification that actually mattered to the family's standing, and nobody in the house had ever heard her use the word "technically" about it. His older sister, two years into her own guild placement, was already being discussed in the hushed, proud tone reserved for someone the family expected great things from and had never once had reason to doubt.

The Kindling had produced Iron Skin Path, Copper-tier — a genuinely solid result by most reasonable measures, the kind of classification that opened real doors in most families' calculations. His family had expected Gold. Not hoped for it, in the vague way most families hoped for good outcomes — expected it, with the certainty of people who'd built three generations of assumptions around a bloodline they believed owed them a particular kind of result, and had simply never had cause to question whether the debt was actually owed.

Copper-tier Iron Skin, against that expectation, wasn't a disappointment so much as an event requiring management — the kind of result that produced quiet, extended conversations about training intensification, about which instructors might be engaged to correct whatever oversight had produced this shortfall, about how the family would frame the situation to people who mattered. Nobody said anything cruel. Brom had turned that fact over more than once in the two years since, testing whether it made the memory easier to carry or harder, and had settled, mostly, on harder — cruelty would at least have been something to push against. What he'd gotten instead was the quiet, reasonable, endlessly patient assumption that something about him needed correcting, delivered by people who loved him and had simply never once considered that the correction might not exist.

He'd left before any of the conversations concluded. He'd taken what he could carry, walked out the gate his family's estate had never needed to lock — a detail that had stayed with him longer than almost anything else about that morning, the unremarkable ease of leaving a place built on the assumption that nobody who belonged there would ever want to — and spent the following two years doing circuit work in two other cities before arriving in Ardenmere. He'd come here deliberately rather than by drift. Its underground circuit was known, in the loose network practitioners used to trade information about which venues actually rewarded unusual talent rather than merely tolerating it, for accepting the most unusual competitors anywhere on the continent. He'd wanted, more than anything else in those first raw weeks away from home, to be somewhere that measured him by what he could do rather than by what a bloodline had promised on his behalf.

The network itself had been an education his family would never have permitted. It ran on no institution at all — traveling fighters carrying word between cities, Ledger-keepers exchanging occasional letters, a vocabulary of reputation that stayed accurate because inaccuracy cost the carrier their own credibility the next time they vouched for anything. Venues had standing in that network the way fighters did. The river-city circuit where he'd started ranked low — *purses honest, records loose* was the network's verdict, and both halves proved true. Ardenmere ranked high, and the reason attached to its name was always the same: *the keeper's ledger is worth more than a registry entry.* He'd walked four hundred miles, in stages, on the strength of that one clause.

The two years between Velmere and Ardenmere had taught him things his family's estate never could have, whatever else they cost him. The first circuit, in a river city he'd chosen mostly because it was far enough from home that nobody there would recognize the name Velmere, had been rougher than he'd expected — smaller, less organized than the Ironyard, run by a Ledger-keeper far less careful than Vell would turn out to be, and he'd learned there, the hard way, what it actually felt like to be hit by someone who genuinely wanted to hurt him rather than simply win a formal exchange under controlled conditions. He'd lost more than he'd won in that first month. He'd kept fighting anyway, because losing honestly, he'd discovered, taught him more in a week than winning at his family's carefully arranged sparring sessions had ever taught him in a year.

It was in that first city, too, that the technique had actually been born — out of necessity rather than insight, which he'd come to believe was where most real technique came from. His family's instructors had taught Iron Skin as the manuals taught it: a defensive art, hardness raised across the whole surface, a wall you lived behind. The wall worked, and the wall lost fights — an opponent who couldn't hurt him simply outlasted him, because holding hardness everywhere drained him in a slow, even tax that emptied him by the sixth exchange while costing his opponent nothing. The redirect had arrived one desperate night as an economy measure, not an attack: hardening only the contact point, only at the instant of impact, spending in one sharp pulse what the wall spent in rent. The discovery underneath the discovery had come a week later — that a surface hardened at precisely the right instant didn't just refuse force, it returned it, angled, through the point of contact, his own momentum folded into the opponent's and handed back at a geometry no body braced for. His family's facility had never taught this because his family's facility had never needed it. You did not learn to spend precisely until spending loosely started costing you fights, and nobody at Velmere had been permitted to make him lose.

Ardenmere had confirmed the network's verdict within his first three evenings. He'd assessed the Ironyard the way he assessed everything — structurally, load first, ornament never — and found it sounder than either circuit he'd fought in. The river city's keeper had recorded results a day late, from memory, in a hand that wandered; the second city's keeper had been honest but lazy, rating fighters generously to keep the schedule full. Vell recorded in the room, during the bout, and her ratings ran conservative — he'd cross-checked a handful of her Iron-equivalents against fighters he'd known in both cities, and her Iron-equivalent would have been called Bronze-material in the river city's inflated ledger. A conservative record was worth traveling for. It meant that anything this circuit said about a fighter understated the truth, and the network knew it, which was precisely why word of an unclassifiable unranked boy beating Copper-tier formals in Ardenmere had reached a boarding house two cities away and been believed.

The venue itself he'd read the same way. Three hundred standing, low ceilings that punished any technique needing vertical room — a fact he'd noted with professional satisfaction, since Iron Skin needed none — a stone floor dense enough to take a full redirect without transmitting it, and lighting someone had arranged with actual thought, no shadowed angles where a bout could half-disappear from its witnesses. Whoever ran the logistics here ran them the way Brom's grandmother had once described running a guild treasury: so well that nobody ever noticed the job existed. He'd identified Dace within one evening and spent part of the second watching him instead of the fights, which had told him more about the circuit's real hierarchy than the chalk board had.

He'd been watching Cael for three weeks, since the night he'd first sat down beside him at a bout and told him his notebook was wrong.

---

What he observed, across those three weeks of careful, patient attention, accumulated slowly, the way real conclusions usually did rather than arriving all at once.

The first week told him only that the boy was good — good in the hard-won way of someone who'd built a method out of necessity rather than inherited it from an instructor, the kind of good that always interested Brom more than raw talent did, because raw talent told you nothing about a person and hard-won competence told you almost everything. He watched two bouts. He filed the impression and didn't act on it yet.

He recognized the method itself before he understood its results, which was its own point of interest: the boy prepared. The bout Brom watched him win that first week had been won days earlier, somewhere in the audience, in the pages of the notebook that never left his hands — Brom had asked around, casually, and learned that the unranked one watched every prospective opponent for hours before agreeing to terms, and that the circuit had stopped finding this strange. Brom found it strange in the best sense. He did his own preparation through the pressure-read, passively, a capability doing his watching for him. The boy did it with nothing but eyes and ink, manually, hour over hour, and arrived somewhere Brom's instructors would have insisted required a Path to reach.

The second week told him something stranger. Cael's pattern recognition was extraordinary in a way experience level alone didn't explain. A practitioner who'd been fighting seriously for roughly a year, even an unusually gifted one, shouldn't have been reading opponents this cleanly, this fast, with this little visible effort. His technique was heterodox in a way Brom's considerable formal training didn't have a ready category for — it didn't match any Path framework he recognized, not because it was primitive, but because it seemed to be operating on principles the standard frameworks simply hadn't been built to describe in the first place.

The third week told him the part that actually mattered. Brom's Iron Skin Path included a passive pressure-read capability, a background sense for the shape of a practitioner's declaration architecture that he'd used, reflexively, on every fighter he'd watched in the Ironyard since arriving — an old habit from home, where reading a sparring partner's signature before a bout had been considered basic professional courtesy.

The read, for Brom, was less like seeing than like standing in shallow water and feeling what moved nearby. Every practitioner displaced something — a pressure against the sense, shaped by their Path and sharpened by their tier, as individual as a gait. A Blade practitioner read as a thin, forward-leaning insistence; a Force practitioner as a slow swell that grew with their build phase; the unkindled as a flat, recognizable blankness, water with nothing in it. Two years of circuits had given him a library of such signatures broad enough that he'd stopped expecting additions to it.

Cael's signature, when he applied the read, came back wrong.

Not absent. He'd read absent before — unkindled prospects, people with no formal Path at all, produced that flat, recognizable blankness. This wasn't that. It was wrong, the way a word in a language you speak fluently can still occasionally not be a word at all, close enough to something familiar to trip your ear and then simply refuse to resolve into anything you already know. There were moments — he'd confirmed them across four separate bouts, because a Velmere education did at least teach a man not to trust a single sample — when the boy's pressure *moved,* briefly, into shapes the library almost recognized: a flicker of something Wind-adjacent during an evasion, there and gone; once, held longer, a density that read nearly like Pressure Path and then didn't. As though the signature weren't one thing at all. As though it were several things taking turns, none of them wearing the shape correctly, all of them orbiting a center his read couldn't find. Like a Path operating outside the categories the pressure-read had been designed to sort things into at all.

This was, Brom decided, extremely interesting. More interesting, if he was honest with himself, than anything he'd encountered in two years of deliberately searching two different cities for exactly this kind of interesting.

He found Dace after watching Cael's latest bout — a clean, methodical win against a Copper-tier formal opponent that had confirmed, in Brom's own private assessment, everything he'd suspected about the shape of what he was watching. He'd made his decision walking across the Ironyard floor, the settled certainty of someone who rarely needed to deliberate once he'd actually gathered enough information to act.

He'd made a study, in his two years of circuit work before Ardenmere, of exactly this kind of decision — the moment where enough watching finally converted into enough certainty to move. His old instructors, back at Velmere, had always framed patience as a virtue you cultivated for its own sake, a discipline practiced regardless of whether the situation actually called for more waiting. Brom had come to see it differently, out here, on his own. Patience wasn't a virtue. It was a tool, useful exactly until the moment it stopped producing new information, and no longer than that. Three weeks of watching Cael had produced new information right up until tonight. Tonight, watching him close out a bout that should have taken longer than it did, Brom had understood, with the flat finality of a conclusion rather than a hunch, that the watching phase was over.

"I want to fight the unranked one."

Dace looked up from his scheduling board, taking his time about it. "He's Assessed now. Copper-equivalent."

"I know."

"He's been fighting Iron-equivalent opponents and winning some of them."

"I know."

Dace studied Brom for a long moment, the reading attention he brought to everyone who asked him for something, weighing not just the request but the person making it. "You're Iron-equivalent yourself."

"Yes."

"Interesting." He made a mark on the board, already three names deep into the actual work of finding a slot that wouldn't collide with anything else the district had planned that week. "Two weeks. I'll clear the schedule for it. Side alcoves are too small for what this is going to draw — I'll put it on the main floor, and expect a crowd. People remember 'unranked practitioner fighting above his rating,' and you're about to become the second half of that sentence."

Brom hadn't considered the crowd. He filed the detail without much reaction and left to find Cael at his own pace, the way he did most things once a decision was actually made.

---

He found him the following morning, at the tail end of a training session, Lira already walking off toward the market and Cael still catching his breath by the alcove wall. Brom crossed the floor without breaking stride, no hesitation in his approach at all, the economy of someone who found small talk an inefficient use of everyone's time and had never seen a reason to pretend otherwise.

"Brom. Iron Skin Path. We fight two weeks from today."

Cael, who had clocked him instantly as the stranger from the bout three weeks prior, felt something settle into place — a data point finally connecting to the rest of its context. "I know who you are."

Something flickered across Brom's expression — mild, genuine surprise, quickly absorbed. "Do you."

"You've been watching for three weeks."

"You noticed."

"Three weeks ago."

A pause, longer this time, Brom visibly recalibrating something in his own assessment. "What do you know about Iron Skin?"

"Not enough."

Brom's expression settled into something that might, on a less economical face, have been the beginning of a smile. "Good," he said. "Two weeks."

He turned to leave, then stopped, glancing back with the directness he'd bring to most of his future interactions with Cael — no unnecessary preamble, no softening of an observation he'd apparently decided was worth stating plainly.

"For what it's worth," he said. "The sentence I said to you three weeks ago. You reformatted your notebook after it. I saw the difference in how you watched the bout last week." A short pause. "You listened well. Most people don't, the first time someone tells them their whole method needs revising."

He left before Cael could formulate a response, the same economical exit Cael remembered from three weeks before — three steps, and gone, the crowd folding around the space where he'd been standing as though he'd never occupied it at all.

Cael stood there a moment, turning the exchange over, and found, underneath the immediate tactical questions a scheduled fight against an unknown Iron Skin opponent naturally raised, something closer to anticipation than concern. He tried to name the feeling more precisely, the way he tried to name most things, and settled, eventually, on something close to relief — the plain relief of a mystery finally agreeing to become a person, someone he could actually study rather than simply wonder about.

Then, because anticipation was not a plan, he sat down on the alcove bench with the notebook and did what he did at the start of every prepared fight: he opened a page, wrote the name at the top, and inventoried what the page could honestly hold.

It held very little, which was itself the first finding. *Brom. Iron Skin Path, self-declared. Iron-equivalent per Dace's scheduling logic — Dace doesn't clear the main floor for mismatches.* Below that, the registry-level knowledge, which he could have written from memory because there was so little of it: Iron Skin existed; it was classified in the defensive specialist branch; it provided surface hardness enhancement; its actual training documentation was proprietary to its guild cohort, which meant the public record described the Path the way a locked door described a house. Everything else on the page came from his own two encounters, and he wrote those out in full because they were the only primary data he owned. *Sat inside my gaze's blind spot for a quarter hour without triggering any instinct I have — either luck, or he reads attention itself well enough to position around it. Diagnosed my method's flaw from watching me write, not from watching me fight — which means he watches the way I watch, one layer under the obvious one. Noticed the notebook reformat from across a floor, three weeks after one glance at the original. Memory to match the observation.* He looked at the short list and understood, with the clean, bracing clarity that a good problem always brought him, that he was two weeks from fighting the first opponent he'd ever faced who might be running a version of his own method back at him.

The two-week problem had a second dimension, and he made himself write that one down too, because leaving it unwritten wouldn't make it smaller. His own inventory, as it stood tonight: the Wind-adjacent fragment, fluent now within its terms — the seam guarded by footwork, the landing beat priced and repurposed, three bursts an exchange before the hip and the air gave out. The Pressure-adjacent fragment, dependable and slow — a full breath to gather, a visible sink in his stance, one limb's worth of dispersal, the upper limit still an unopened box. Against most opponents, the inventory plus the watching was enough; the year's record said so. Against a defensive specialist whose entire Path was built to receive force — against a man whose surface could apparently be *hard where and when he chose* — he could not currently name what either fragment bought him. Evasion won nothing from an opponent who didn't need to hit you to outlast you. Dispersal won nothing from an opponent who dealt no force to disperse until he chose to. Two weeks, he wrote, to learn what the locked door was actually protecting. The written record first — due diligence, even knowing how little of it there will be — then the man himself. He knows I'll be watching. He's chosen to be watchable. Add that to the file too — an opponent who wants to be studied is telling you something about what he thinks study will find.

Lira caught the change in him at dinner that evening.

"You look like you solved something."

"I did. The stranger from the bout — the one who told me I was writing the wrong things. He's back. We're fighting in two weeks."

She set down her cup. "That's either wonderful news or a genuinely terrible idea, depending entirely on how good he actually is."

"I don't know yet. That's the interesting part."

"Of course it is." But she was smiling when she said it, the particular fondness she reserved for exactly this kind of answer — an answer that treated an unknown, dangerous opponent as a gift rather than a threat, because to Cael it had always been both at once, and she'd long since stopped expecting him to weigh the two halves any differently than he did.

"What's he actually like?"

Cael considered the question seriously, turning back through the two brief exchanges he'd now had with Brom — the sentence at the bout, and today's flat, economical introduction. "Direct. Doesn't waste words. He noticed I'd changed my notebook format after one comment, and he noticed it precisely enough to mention it again three weeks later without checking his own memory first." He paused, working out the last piece of it. "He's spent time being underestimated by people who should have known better. I don't think he minds it happening again. I think he's decided it's more useful to let people be wrong about him and then simply show them otherwise, rather than spend the effort correcting them in advance."

"You got all of that from two conversations."

"I got all of that from two conversations and three weeks of him watching me without my noticing. That's not nothing to work with."

Lira shook her head, something warm underneath the exasperation. "You're going to like him."

"I think I already do."

After dinner, with the light not yet fully gone, he went back to the Ironyard's side alcove alone and spent an hour on the one piece of preparation that couldn't wait for observation to begin: establishing his own baseline. He'd learned this from the winter testing — before any fight that mattered, measure yourself first, because a plan built on last month's body was a plan built on a stranger.

The Wind-adjacent fragment answered the way it answered now, which was to say: like his. The hip-drop, the held half-breath, the floor's borrowed half-inch — a year ago the sequence had arrived clumsy and over-deliberate, a borrowed coat he was still learning the sleeves of; six months ago it had arrived reliably but singly, each burst an isolated event with its full recovery owed between. Tonight he ran the progression he'd been building since the seam work: single bursts to each viable angle of the left-breaking fan, clean; then the harder thing, the two-burst chain — first burst, landing beat taken in full, second burst launched off the beat's far edge with no ordinary footwork between. The chain had failed the first eleven times he'd ever attempted it, weeks ago, his lungs refusing the second held breath before the first had been repaid. It failed once tonight, on the third repetition, the old familiar oxygen wall arriving on schedule — and then succeeded four times running, because he'd learned the trick was in the breath before the first burst, not between them: over-fill, spend half on the first, hold the remainder ready. His body could carry two bursts' worth of air if he loaded it in advance. It could not carry three. He tested three anyway, once, to keep the boundary honest, and spent thirty seconds afterward bent over his knees while his vision resolved from grey back to lamplight, the hip's line of strain burning from crest to knee on both the first and second landing sites.

*Baseline, two weeks out,* he wrote, sitting on the alcove floor because standing felt optional. *Chain of two: repeatable, at the cost of the pre-load breath (visible — an opponent who knows to watch my chest will see the fight's biggest tell). Chain of three: not available, confirmed again, price of confirmation as expected. Pressure fragment: unchanged, slow, honest. This is what I bring. Whatever the locked door is hiding, it will be answered with this and with whatever two weeks of watching adds — nothing else is arriving in time.*

He wrote that evening, in the observation notebook, the entry brief and, for once, not entirely about tactics:

*Brom. Iron Skin Path, Copper-tier. The stranger from three weeks ago. Fight in two weeks. I think I've been looking forward to a fight for the first time since Weaver's Row, and not entirely because of what I'll learn from losing it.*

He read the last clause back and let it stand, because the honesty in it was the kind he'd built both notebooks to protect. He expected to lose. The inventory said so; the locked door said so; the calm arithmetic he ran on every scheduled fight said so, and he'd never once improved a fight's odds by lying to the page about them. What the arithmetic couldn't price — what he noticed in himself with the detached interest he'd once reserved for other people's anomalies — was that the expected loss changed nothing about the anticipation. Somewhere in a year of Ardenmere, losing to the right opponent had stopped being a cost and become a purchase. Feryn had taught him that. He suspected, closing the notebook and turning down the lamp, that Brom was about to teach him what the next price bracket looked like.

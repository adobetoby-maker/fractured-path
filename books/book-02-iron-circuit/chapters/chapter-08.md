# Chapter 8 — Brom

He was sixteen, and he had walked out of Velmere before his family could finish deciding what to do about him.

The estate sat in the hill country two provinces east of Ardenmere, old enough that nobody in the household bothered mentioning how old it was anymore, the way you stop mentioning a fact everyone already carries. Three generations of Velmere Kindlings had resolved to Gold-tier or close enough to it that the difference didn't register at dinner. His grandmother had been Gold. His mother had been Gold, technically — closer to Silver by the raw numbers, but Gold on the classification that actually mattered to the family's standing, and nobody in the house had ever heard her use the word "technically" about it. His older sister, two years into her own guild placement, was already being discussed in the specific hushed, proud tone reserved for someone the family expected great things from and had never once had reason to doubt.

The Kindling had produced Iron Skin Path, Copper-tier — a genuinely solid result by most reasonable measures, the kind of classification that opened real doors in most families' calculations. His family had expected Gold. Not hoped for it, in the vague way most families hoped for good outcomes — expected it, with the certainty of people who'd built three generations of assumptions around a bloodline they believed owed them a particular kind of result, and had simply never had cause to question whether the debt was actually owed.

Copper-tier Iron Skin, against that expectation, wasn't a disappointment so much as an event requiring management — the kind of result that produced quiet, extended conversations about training intensification, about which instructors might be engaged to correct whatever oversight had produced this shortfall, about how the family would frame the situation to people who mattered. Nobody said anything cruel. Brom had turned that fact over more than once in the two months since, testing whether it made the memory easier to carry or harder, and had settled, mostly, on harder — cruelty would at least have been something to push against. What he'd gotten instead was the quiet, reasonable, endlessly patient assumption that something about him needed correcting, delivered by people who loved him and had simply never once considered that the correction might not exist.

He'd left before any of the conversations concluded. He'd taken what he could carry, walked out the gate his family's estate had never needed to lock — a detail that had stayed with him longer than almost anything else about that morning, the specific, unremarkable ease of leaving a place built on the assumption that nobody who belonged there would ever want to — and spent the following two months doing circuit work in two other cities before arriving in Ardenmere. He'd come here deliberately rather than by drift. Its underground circuit was known, in the loose network practitioners used to trade information about which venues actually rewarded unusual talent rather than merely tolerating it, for accepting the most unusual competitors anywhere on the continent. He'd wanted, more than anything else in those first raw weeks away from home, to be somewhere that measured him by what he could do rather than by what a bloodline had promised on his behalf.

The two months between Velmere and Ardenmere had taught him things his family's estate never could have, whatever else they cost him. The first circuit, in a river city he'd chosen mostly because it was far enough from home that nobody there would recognize the name Velmere, had been rougher than he'd expected — smaller, less organized than the Ironyard, run by a Ledger-keeper considerably less careful than Vell would turn out to be, and he'd learned there, the hard way, what it actually felt like to be hit by someone who genuinely wanted to hurt him rather than simply win a formal exchange under controlled conditions. He'd lost more than he'd won in that first month. He'd kept fighting anyway, because losing honestly, he'd discovered, taught him more in a week than winning at his family's carefully arranged sparring sessions had ever taught him in a year.

The second city had gone better, and by the time he'd arrived in Ardenmere, he'd stopped thinking of himself as someone in flight from a disappointing Kindling result and started thinking of himself as someone building a method — the force-redirect technique refined further with every circuit he passed through, tested against opponents his family's estate would never have allowed near him, each new city adding another layer to a version of Iron Skin Path that belonged entirely to him rather than to any lineage.

He'd been watching Cael for three weeks before he ever spoke to him.

---

What he observed, across those three weeks of careful, patient attention, accumulated slowly, the way real conclusions usually did rather than arriving all at once.

The first week told him only that the boy was good — good in the specific, hard-won way of someone who'd built a method out of necessity rather than inherited it from an instructor, the kind of good that always interested Brom more than raw talent did, because raw talent told you nothing about a person and hard-won competence told you almost everything. He watched two bouts. He filed the impression and didn't act on it yet.

The second week told him something stranger. Cael's pattern recognition was extraordinary in a way experience level alone didn't explain. A practitioner who'd been fighting seriously for roughly a year, even an unusually gifted one, shouldn't have been reading opponents this cleanly, this fast, with this little visible effort. His technique was heterodox in a way Brom's considerable formal training didn't have a ready category for — it didn't match any Path framework he recognized, not because it was primitive, but because it seemed to be operating on principles the standard frameworks simply hadn't been built to describe in the first place.

The third week told him the part that actually mattered. Brom's Iron Skin Path included a passive pressure-read capability, a background sense for the shape of a practitioner's declaration architecture that he'd used, reflexively, on every fighter he'd watched in the Ironyard since arriving — an old habit from home, where reading a sparring partner's signature before a bout had been considered basic professional courtesy. Cael's signature, when he applied the read, came back wrong.

Not absent. He'd read absent before — unkindled prospects, people with no formal Path at all, produced a specific, recognizable blankness. This wasn't that. It was wrong, the way a word in a language you speak fluently can still occasionally not be a word at all, close enough to something familiar to trip your ear and then simply refuse to resolve into anything you already know. Like a Path operating outside the categories the pressure-read had been designed to sort things into at all.

This was, Brom decided, extremely interesting. More interesting, if he was honest with himself, than anything he'd encountered in two months of deliberately searching two different cities for exactly this kind of interesting.

He found Dace after watching Cael's latest bout — a clean, methodical win against a Copper-tier formal opponent that had confirmed, in Brom's own private assessment, everything he'd suspected about the shape of what he was watching. He'd made his decision walking across the Ironyard floor, the settled certainty of someone who rarely needed to deliberate once he'd actually gathered enough information to act.

He'd made a study, in his two months of circuit work before Ardenmere, of exactly this kind of decision — the moment where enough watching finally converted into enough certainty to move. His old instructors, back at Velmere, had always framed patience as a virtue you cultivated for its own sake, a discipline practiced regardless of whether the situation actually called for more waiting. Brom had come to see it differently, out here, on his own. Patience wasn't a virtue. It was a tool, useful exactly until the moment it stopped producing new information, and no longer than that. Three weeks of watching Cael had produced new information right up until tonight. Tonight, watching him close out a bout that should have taken longer than it did, Brom had understood, with the specific finality of a conclusion rather than a hunch, that the watching phase was over.

"I want to fight the unranked one."

Dace looked up from his scheduling board, unhurried. "He's Assessed now. Copper-equivalent."

"I know."

"He's been fighting Iron-equivalent opponents and winning some of them."

"I know."

Dace studied Brom for a long moment, the reading attention he brought to everyone who asked him for something, weighing not just the request but the person making it. "You're Iron-equivalent yourself."

"Yes."

"Interesting." He made a mark on the board, unhurried, already three names deep into the actual work of finding a slot that wouldn't collide with anything else the district had planned that week. "Two weeks. I'll clear the schedule for it. Main alcove's too small for what this is going to draw — I'll put it on the side floor, but expect a crowd anyway. People remember 'unranked practitioner fighting above his rating,' and you're about to become the second half of that sentence."

Brom hadn't considered the crowd. He filed the detail without much reaction and left to find Cael, in no particular hurry, the way he did most things once a decision was actually made.

---

He found him the following morning, at the tail end of a training session, Lira already walking off toward the market and Cael still catching his breath by the alcove wall. Brom crossed the floor without breaking stride, no hesitation in his approach at all, the economy of someone who found small talk an inefficient use of everyone's time and had never seen a reason to pretend otherwise.

"Brom. Iron Skin Path. We fight two weeks from today."

Cael, who had clocked him instantly as the stranger from the bout three weeks prior, felt something settle into place — a data point finally connecting to the rest of its context. "I know who you are."

Something flickered across Brom's expression — mild, genuine surprise, quickly absorbed. "Do you."

"You've been watching for three weeks."

"You noticed."

"Three weeks ago."

A pause, longer this time, Brom visibly recalibrating something in his own assessment. "What do you know about Iron Skin?"

"Not enough."

Brom's expression settled into something that might, on a less economical face, have been the beginning of a smile. "Good," he said. "Two weeks."

He turned to leave, then stopped, glancing back with the specific directness he'd bring to most of his future interactions with Cael — no unnecessary preamble, no softening of an observation he'd apparently decided was worth stating plainly.

"For what it's worth," he said. "The sentence I said to you three weeks ago. You reformatted your notebook after it. I saw the difference in how you watched the bout last week." A short pause. "You listened well. Most people don't, the first time someone tells them their whole method needs revising."

He left before Cael could formulate a response, the same economical exit Cael remembered from three weeks before — three steps, and gone, the crowd folding around the space where he'd been standing as though he'd never occupied it at all.

Cael stood there a moment, turning the exchange over, and found, underneath the immediate tactical questions a scheduled fight against an unknown Iron Skin opponent naturally raised, something closer to anticipation than concern. He tried to name the feeling more precisely, the way he tried to name most things, and settled, eventually, on something close to relief — the specific relief of a mystery finally agreeing to become a person, someone he could actually study rather than simply wonder about.

Lira noticed the change in him at dinner that evening, the way she noticed most things.

"You look like you solved something."

"I did. The stranger from the bout — the one who told me I was writing the wrong things. He's back. We're fighting in two weeks."

She set down her cup. "That's either wonderful news or a genuinely terrible idea, depending entirely on how good he actually is."

"I don't know yet. That's the interesting part."

"Of course it is." But she was smiling when she said it, the specific fondness she reserved for exactly this kind of answer — an answer that treated an unknown, dangerous opponent as a gift rather than a threat, because to Cael it had always been both at once, and she'd long since stopped expecting him to weigh the two halves any differently than he did.

"What's he actually like?"

Cael considered the question seriously, turning back through the two brief exchanges he'd now had with Brom — the sentence at the bout, and today's flat, economical introduction. "Direct. Doesn't waste words. He noticed I'd changed my notebook format after one comment, and he noticed it precisely enough to mention it again three weeks later without checking his own memory first." He paused, working out the last piece of it. "He's spent time being underestimated by people who should have known better. I don't think he minds it happening again. I think he's decided it's more useful to let people be wrong about him and then simply show them otherwise, rather than spend the effort correcting them in advance."

"You got all of that from two conversations."

"I got all of that from two conversations and three weeks of him watching me without my noticing. That's not nothing to work with."

Lira shook her head, something warm underneath the exasperation. "You're going to like him."

"I think I already do."

He wrote that evening, in the observation notebook, the entry brief and, for once, not entirely about tactics:

*Brom. Iron Skin Path, Copper-tier. The stranger from three weeks ago. Fight in two weeks. I think I've been looking forward to a fight for the first time since Weaver's Row, and not entirely because of what I'll learn from losing it.*

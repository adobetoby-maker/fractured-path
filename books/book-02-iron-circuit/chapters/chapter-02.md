# Chapter 2 — The Ironyard

"How does the circuit actually know who's who?"

Cael asked it plainly, one slow midday when the Ironyard's main floor sat empty between bouts and Vell had nothing more pressing than the ledger already open in front of her. He'd been meaning to ask for months — the question had been sitting in the back of his notebook, filed under things he understood the practical shape of but not the actual mechanism.

Vell looked up from the ledger, considered him for a moment with the same weighing attention she'd given him the first day he'd walked up to her registration table, and then closed the book and stood.

"Come here, then."

She led him to the archive shelf at the back of her small office — not the oldest section, which she kept separate and rarely opened, but the current forty years, bound volume after bound volume, her own handwriting running through decades of them with the mechanical consistency he'd noticed the first time he'd watched her write.

"This is how," she said. "Every practitioner who has ever fought in this circuit. Name, classification if they've got one worth listing, circuit rating, full bout record." She pulled one down at random, opened it to a page in the middle. "No Arbiter verification. No Compact check. The record is what happened, and I decide what happened by being here to see it."

"How do you calibrate the ratings?"

"Against known benchmarks. A Copper-equivalent rating means this practitioner has beaten someone who's beaten someone who holds a formal Copper-tier classification. You can trace the chain, if you want to. I've had guild inspectors try, back when guild inspectors still bothered." She said this without particular bitterness, the flat statement of someone reporting a fact rather than nursing a grievance. "The chain holds. It always holds. That's the whole discipline of it."

"And the no-kill rule?"

"Foundation of everything else." She set the volume back on the shelf with the specific care of someone who'd handled these particular objects enough times to have stopped needing to think about the handling. "If someone dies in my circuit, the Compact has cause to involve themselves, and the circuit has to move — venue, name, sometimes city. In thirty years I've had three practitioners come close enough that I had to stop the bout myself. All three left under their own power." She didn't sound proud of this, or dismissive of it. It was simply the record, stated the way she stated everything.

Cael filed the number. Three, in thirty years, against a fight count that had to run into the thousands. He didn't ask how she'd stopped them. He suspected the answer was less about her Path and more about thirty years of knowing exactly when a fight had stopped being a fight.

---

Dace found them partway through the tour, drifting past with the specific unhurried purpose of a man mid-schedule, already three conversations deep into the day and headed toward a fourth.

"Teaching him the archive?" he asked Vell, not stopping fully.

"He asked how the circuit knows who's who. Seemed worth a proper answer."

Dace looked at Cael for a moment — not unfriendly, just assessing, the way Cael imagined Dace assessed everything, constantly, as a matter of professional habit rather than suspicion. "Good question. Most people just accept the ledger works and never ask why."

"I like knowing why things work."

"I've noticed." Dace almost smiled. "You want to see the actual logistics? Scheduling's the part nobody asks about, and it's the part that actually keeps this place from falling apart."

Cael did want to see it, and said so, and spent the next half hour watching Dace work through the day's matching problem — practitioners who could fight each other, practitioners who couldn't for reasons of health or unpaid debt or simple bad blood, audience capacity against venue size, timing that kept the Ironyard's rhythm from ever feeling either dead or dangerously overcrowded. Dace had no Path. He was not a practitioner in any sense the registry would recognize. He had, in Cael's rapidly forming estimation, better pattern recognition than almost anyone he'd met since Weaver's Row.

"You don't fight," Cael said, at one point, not quite a question.

"Never Kindled anything worth mentioning. Copper Rank 1, Path so minor it's barely worth the classification." Dace said this without any visible discomfort, the specific ease of a man who'd made peace with his own shape a long time ago. "Turns out running this place doesn't require a Path. It requires knowing which two people in a room shouldn't be put in a circle together, and I turned out to be good at that. Good enough that it stopped mattering what my registry said."

Cael thought about this for the rest of the afternoon — the specific, quiet dignity of a man who'd built genuine authority out of a skill the Compact's tier system had no category for at all.

---

The Ironyard's social ecosystem, once Cael started paying deliberate attention to it rather than simply moving through it, revealed itself to be considerably more structured than the loose, informal texture it presented on the surface.

Status here wasn't tier. It wasn't even circuit rating, exactly, though rating mattered. It was reliability — whether you showed up when you said you would, fought honestly, accepted a Ledger-keeper's ruling without making it someone else's problem. A Bronze-tier washout with a spotless thirty-fight record commanded more genuine respect in this room than a formally ranked visitor who'd once tried to argue a loss. Cael had watched this dynamic play out a dozen times over the past year without fully naming it until today.

The district, beyond the Ironyard's walls, watched the circuit with a specific mix of pride and practical interest. Winners earned. Earning meant spending, in the market, at the boarding houses, at the small shops that had learned to extend credit to people whose income arrived in irregular, sometimes-large bursts. The circuit wasn't separate from the Unranked District's economy. It was one of the district's load-bearing walls, whether the Compact's maps acknowledged the district's existence in any detail at all.

At the tour's end, back at the archive shelf, Vell gestured to an empty space at the end of the current volume's row — where the next book would go, once this one filled.

"The Compact's Registry is a lie about who people are," she said, matter-of-fact, no drama in the delivery at all. "My ledger is the truth. These fights happened. These results are real. Nobody gets to say otherwise, not a guild inspector, not a Compact official, not anyone with a fancier document than mine."

Cael absorbed this without visible reaction, the way he absorbed most things that mattered too much to react to immediately.

He wrote it down that evening, alone, in the observation notebook rather than the fragment log, because it wasn't about his own architecture — it was about the world's, the specific shape of an authority built entirely on the discipline of being present and honest, requiring no Arbiter, no registry, no permission from anyone who'd never bothered to actually watch.

*Vell's ledger. The truth that doesn't need the Compact's cooperation to be true.*

He starred it, the way he'd starred Hesk's line in his very first notebook, a lifetime and a year ago.

# Chapter 1 — Rated

The bout had ended twenty minutes ago, and Cael was still writing.

He sat in the alcove at the Ironyard's east wall, the one with the bench worn smooth by a year of practitioners doing exactly what he was doing now — sitting down right after a fight while the details were still sharp enough to catalogue accurately, before the body's own adrenaline had a chance to sand the specifics down into something vaguer and more flattering. The alcove had no formal name. Everyone who used it regularly just called it the east bench, the way you'd name a chair in your own kitchen without ever deciding to. A single lamp hung from an iron hook someone had driven into the beam above it years before Cael arrived, and the light it threw was warm and a little uneven, catching the grain of the bench where a thousand elbows had worn the wood pale.

Beyond the alcove's low wall, the Ironyard's main floor was cycling toward its evening rhythm — the low murmur of a crowd thinning after the last scheduled bout, someone sweeping loose grit off the stone floor with the unhurried patience of a job done the same way every night for years, Dace's voice somewhere near the entrance, negotiating tomorrow's schedule with a fighter who clearly wanted a better slot than his record currently earned him. Cael had stopped registering most of it as noise months ago. It had become, instead, something closer to weather — a condition his attention simply lived inside, the way it had once lived inside Denvash's mornings, and then Ardenmere's first strange weeks, and now this.

He worked through the entry with the same unhurried care he brought to everything that mattered.

*Opponent: Copper-tier formal, Blade Path, six years' guild training. Watched him fight four times before agreeing to terms. Third exchange: committed strike telegraphs through a shoulder-drop his own instructors probably corrected out of him twice and never fully removed. Used it. Won in the fifth exchange.*

He set the pen down for a moment and let himself replay the actual fight rather than just the compressed version already living on the page — a habit he'd kept from the very beginning, checking his own written account against the fuller memory before the memory had time to simplify itself into something less useful.

The opponent — Corren, a name he'd learned only because Vell's ledger required it, not because either of them had bothered with introductions — had come in confident, the settled ease of a guild-trained Blade practitioner who'd fought plenty of circuit opponents and rarely found one worth adjusting his opening approach for. Cael had let him keep that confidence through the first two exchanges, giving up ground he didn't need, letting Corren's read of him settle into exactly the shape Corren expected it to take. It was a technique he'd built slowly, over a year, out of watching how badly overconfidence punished itself once it finally met something it hadn't accounted for.

The shoulder-drop had shown up first in the second of the four bouts he'd watched — a small, almost invisible tell, the kind of thing that would have meant nothing without three other data points to confirm it wasn't simply an accident of that particular fight. By the fourth watched bout, he'd been certain enough to build the whole plan around it: wait for the third exchange, let Corren's guard settle into its most confident shape, and answer the telegraph a half-beat before the strike actually committed rather than after.

It had worked cleanly. Not dramatically — nothing about it would have looked, from the crowd's perspective, like anything more than a solid, competent circuit win. But Cael understood, in the quiet satisfaction that followed every fight built this way, that the cleanliness itself was the actual achievement. A messy win taught you less than an ugly loss. A clean win built on six hours of prior watching taught you that the method worked, again, one more time, in a fight that could have gone very differently against a less patient opponent.

He was fifteen now. He had been in Ardenmere for just over a year, which felt like both an enormous stretch of time and no time at all, depending on which part of himself he asked. The observation notebook — the original one, the one that had started with Renn's telegraphed strikes and Dessa's transition windows — had twenty-three entries in it now, each one a little more precise than the last. The fragment log, which he'd finally separated out into its own slim volume six months ago because keeping tactical opponent data and his own private architecture in the same pages had started to feel like a filing error, had two entries. Wind-adjacent. Pressure-adjacent.

Vell's ledger, three tables over, would already show the result: *Assessed-Copper (Hesk-ward, C.), win, atypical movement pattern in third exchange, opponent Copper-tier formal.* Atypical movement pattern. It was, by now, close to a permanent fixture of his entries in that ledger, the way *win by exchange-four incapacitation* had once been. Vell never asked him to explain the atypical part. He suspected she'd stopped needing an explanation months ago.

She'd caught him on the way to the alcove, actually, ledger still open, and glanced at the entry she'd just finished writing before glancing back at him.

"Corren's going to want a rematch," she said. "He won't get one for a while. His pride needs longer to recover than his body does."

"I wasn't trying to hurt his pride."

"I know. That's usually what does it." She'd closed the ledger, unhurried. "You've beaten eleven Copper-tier formals this year. Every one of them thought guild training meant something a circuit fighter couldn't answer. You keep proving them wrong without ever once looking like you're trying to make a point of it. That's a specific kind of insult, whether you mean it that way or not."

"Should I apologize?"

"Absolutely not. Just don't be surprised when some of them stop being gracious about it." She'd said this without any particular warning in her tone, just the flat, practical observation of someone who'd watched the circuit's social currents shift for forty years and had long since stopped being surprised by any particular pattern in them. "Go write your notes. I've got three more results to log before I can go home."

He'd gone. The exchange sat with him now, a small addition to the evening's accounting alongside the fight itself — one more data point in the slow, ongoing project of understanding exactly what his growing reputation was actually costing him, and what it was buying him in return.

He closed the observation notebook and opened the fragment log instead, which he did rarely enough — twice a week, sometimes less — that the act itself still felt significant every time, a different kind of writing than anything else he did.

It was a slim volume, deliberately so. He'd chosen the smallest binding the stationer carried when he'd finally separated it out from the observation notebook six months ago, on the theory that a book too large would tempt him to fill it with speculation rather than confirmed fact. Two entries lived in it now, each one taking up most of a page, the surrounding white space a kind of honesty in itself — evidence of how little he actually knew, laid out plainly rather than padded with guesses dressed up as data.

He'd added a third column to each entry this month, after six months of methodically testing what the fragments could actually do rather than simply confirming, over and over, that they existed. *Functional deployment range.* It had taken him most of the winter to work out a testing method he trusted — controlled sparring sessions with Lira, logged separately from their ordinary training, each one designed to isolate a single variable rather than let the fragments blur together the way they tended to under real pressure.

The method itself had gone through three failed versions before he'd settled on one that produced clean data. The first attempt — simply trying to summon a fragment deliberately, outside any fight, and timing how long it took to arrive — had told him almost nothing useful, because the fragments behaved differently under deliberate summons than they did under genuine pressure, and he'd needed weeks to understand that the difference itself was the actual finding rather than an obstacle to it. The second attempt, sparring at reduced intensity to make the fragments easier to isolate, had produced results he no longer trusted at all; a fragment tested gently didn't behave the same as a fragment tested under the kind of pressure that actually mattered in a real bout, and he'd wasted most of a month building conclusions on data that turned out to be measuring the wrong thing entirely.

The third version had worked, mostly because Lira had been the one to suggest it. Full-intensity sparring, exactly as hard as either of them could manage, with the single difference that she called a stop the instant a fragment visibly activated, giving him thirty full seconds to record what had just happened before the memory of it started smoothing itself into something less precise. It was, he'd come to understand, a method that only worked because she trusted him enough to actually fight him at full intensity in the first place, and cared enough to stop exactly when he needed her to. Neither part of that arrangement was small.

*Wind-adjacent: evasion framework, short burst. Consistent. Does not fail under pressure.* He read the line back, satisfied with it the way he was satisfied with anything he'd tested thoroughly enough to state without hedging. This one he trusted completely now. He could deploy it in a fight without the small, private fear that had accompanied every early attempt — the fear that it might simply not answer this time, the way his Arbiter never had.

The Pressure-adjacent entry sat beneath it, visibly less complete. He had a working deployment range — he knew, broadly, what it did and roughly when it tended to activate — but its upper limits under sustained combat pressure remained uncharacterized, a gap in the page that bothered him more than he usually let himself admit. He'd been careful about testing that particular boundary. Pushing a fragment past its known limit, in the wrong setting, was the kind of experiment that could go badly enough to matter, and he hadn't yet found a controlled circumstance safe enough to justify the risk. Some questions, he'd learned, over a year of careful work, were worth leaving open a little longer than his own curiosity wanted to allow.

He was midway through annotating the Wind-adjacent entry's new column when Lira appeared in the alcove doorway, damp from her own morning session, and stopped.

She didn't say anything. She looked at the small, precise handwriting, the two entries, the column he was filling in — and something in her expression settled into a familiar shape: patient curiosity, held rather than spoken.

She already had guesses. She'd had guesses for months. She was waiting until he was ready to confirm them properly, the way she waited for most things that mattered — not passively, but with the discipline of someone who understood that some doors opened faster if you didn't lean on them.

"Good fight?" she asked instead.

"Efficient," Cael said, closing the log. "He had a tell his own instructors probably tried to train out of him. Didn't take."

"You watched him four times before you agreed to it."

"Four times is usually enough now."

Lira leaned against the doorframe, studying him with the attention she still brought to him after a year of this — not surprise anymore, just ongoing interest, the way you stay interested in a puzzle that keeps producing new pieces long after you've stopped expecting it to run out. Her hair was damp at the temples, tied back in the quick, practical knot she used for training rather than the neater one she saved for circuit days, and she still had a training weapon slung across her back that she hadn't bothered to put away before coming to find him. "You used to need six."

"I've gotten faster."

"You've gotten faster at everything." She said this without particular emphasis, but there was something underneath it, a genuine note of something between pride and unease that Cael had learned to recognize over the past year — the quality of someone watching a person she cared about accelerate past a pace either of them had originally planned for, and not being entirely certain, yet, whether to be glad about it or simply watchful.

She crossed the alcove and sat on the far end of the bench, close enough to see the log page without leaning over him for it, an old habit between them by now — she didn't read what he hadn't offered, and he'd learned to offer more than he used to, slowly, over the year.

"How's the leg?" she asked, nodding at the slight favoring he was doing without quite noticing he was doing it.

"Fine by tomorrow. He caught me once, clean, second exchange. My own fault — I was watching his hands instead of his weight."

"You'll fix it."

"I already wrote it down to fix it."

They'd started that morning the way most mornings started now — before the market's first vendors had finished setting up, the Ironyard's side alcoves still cool and mostly empty, the two of them running through drills that had, over a year, worn as smooth and familiar as the bench Cael wrote on now.

Lira had pushed him hard, harder than usual, working a combination she'd apparently spent the previous evening designing specifically to expose whatever gap she'd noticed in his footwork the week before. She never told him what she was testing until after — she'd decided, sometime in the last few months, that a warning changed how he moved, made him defend against the theory instead of the actual attack, and she wanted the actual attack.

"Again," she'd said, for the fourth time, after he'd failed to close the gap the same way twice.

"You could just tell me what you're looking for."

"I could. It'd be faster. It'd also teach you less." She'd reset her stance, unbothered by his mild frustration, the patience of someone who'd learned, over a year of training him, that his frustration burned itself out fast and productively if she simply let it run its course. "Again."

He'd found it on the sixth attempt — not the gap itself, which he still hadn't consciously identified, but the shape of his own hesitation right before it opened, a half-beat of over-caution that had nothing to do with his feet and everything to do with a decision he was making too slowly. He'd closed it on the seventh. She'd nodded, satisfied, and moved on to something else without further comment, the way she always moved on once a lesson had actually landed rather than merely been delivered.

That had been hours ago. His legs still remembered it, faintly, underneath the fresher ache from the evening's actual bout — the specific layered fatigue of a full circuit day, morning training stacked under an afternoon fight stacked under whatever the evening still had planned for either of them.

Between the two, there'd been the market, the way there almost always was — the middle-hour errand that had become as fixed a fixture of the day as either training session bracketing it. He'd stopped at the dried-fruit stall first, out of a habit that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with the particular comfort of a routine kept exactly the same for a year running. The vendor, an older woman who'd sold to him nearly every week since he'd first wandered into the district with a bag over his shoulder and no idea what any of it cost, had asked, as she always did, how the leg was healing from whatever he'd walked away favoring that week.

"You always ask that."

"You're always favoring something." She'd weighed out his usual portion without needing to be told the amount, the specific economy of a transaction repeated often enough to no longer require words at all. "One day you'll surprise me and walk in here without a limp."

"I'll try."

"Don't try too hard. A boy who never limps isn't learning anything." She'd said it lightly, but there was something underneath it he recognized — an echo, faint but real, of the same principle Lira built her training around, the same one Hesk had written into a notebook years ago in a house Cael sometimes had to work to picture clearly anymore. Watch. Fail usefully. Adjust. Everyone in his life, it seemed, had independently arrived at some version of the same lesson.

---

Life in Ardenmere, a year on, had organized itself entirely around the circuit, in a way that felt less like a single choice made once and more like a hundred small choices compounding daily until the shape simply was what it was.

He and Lira lived in two adjoining rooms at the back of a practitioners' boarding house two streets from the Ironyard — a step up from Torvin's, paid for with circuit winnings rather than Hesk's dwindling savings pouch, which Cael had stopped touching almost entirely once his own earnings became reliable enough to make the pouch feel more like an heirloom than a necessity. His room held a bed, a desk he'd built himself out of salvaged crating with more enthusiasm than skill, and a shelf that had grown, month by month, into a small and specific library: the observation notebook's predecessors, retired one at a time as they filled; Hesk's leather-bound notebook, still read most weeks even though he'd long since memorized every line in it; and now the fragment log, kept apart from the others on its own end of the shelf, the way you'd keep something valuable slightly out of easy reach.

Mornings were training, in the Ironyard's side alcoves before the main floor properly woke up — the hour when the light through the high windows was still thin and grey, and the only sounds were their own breathing and the occasional distant clatter of someone opening up a stall two streets over. Middle hours were the market, where he'd learned, over a year of repeat business, which vendors extended informal credit to circuit practitioners between fights and which ones simply liked circuit practitioners' company regardless of what business it brought them — the old fruit seller who always asked how Lira's shoulder was healing, the baker's apprentice who'd started saving the good loaves without being asked, the specific quiet economy of a district that had decided, gradually and without ceremony, that the two of them belonged to it. Afternoons, more often than not, were the Ironyard itself — watching whoever was fighting on the main floor, adding entries to the observation notebook, building the kind of information advantage that had become, without him quite deciding it should, his entire method of being good at anything at all.

The Ironyard itself had become as familiar to him as Denvash's Outer District once had been, back before any of this had a name — a converted warehouse at the eastern edge of the Unranked District, three fighting spaces inside it, low ceilings and practitioner-installed lighting rigged from scavenged fixtures, and a stone floor worn smooth by years of feet doing exactly what his were doing most days now. It smelled like impact dust and old iron fittings and, faintly, the specific mineral tang of the lamp oil Dace bought in bulk from a supplier two districts over because it burned longer than anything sold locally. Cael had stopped noticing any of it consciously months ago, the way you stop noticing your own house — except on evenings like this one, when a good fight or a strange result nudged his attention sideways long enough to catch the details fresh again, and he remembered, briefly, that this had once been a place he'd never heard of, on a night he could no longer clearly picture without effort.

They walked back to the boarding house together once the evening crowd had thinned enough to make conversation possible without shouting over it, the observation notebook and the fragment log both tucked into the same worn bag, the way they'd traveled together for a year even after he'd finally separated their contents onto different pages. The street outside the Ironyard was busy in its own particular evening way — vendors closing stalls, a pair of younger circuit hopefuls arguing good-naturedly about a bout they'd both watched from the cheap standing section, someone's dog trotting past with the specific unbothered confidence of an animal that had decided, long ago, this entire district belonged to it.

"You're going to have to tell me eventually," Lira said, not quite out of nowhere — she had a way of returning to a subject after enough silence that it felt like a fresh thought rather than a resumed one, a habit Cael had noticed months ago and never once managed to predict the timing of.

"Tell you what?"

"Whatever's in that second book that you only open when you think I'm not paying attention." She said this lightly, but her eyes, when he glanced over, were serious underneath the lightness. "I've had guesses for months, Cael. I'm not going to make you say it before you're ready. I'm just telling you I notice."

He thought about the two entries. Wind-adjacent, sourced from months of watching her move, still — a year later — the foundation everything else in the log had been built on top of. He thought about how she'd already lived with that fact for months without demanding to see it named on a page.

"I know you notice," he said. "I'll show you when I understand it well enough to show you properly."

"That's what you said three months ago."

"I understand it better now than I did three months ago."

Lira studied him for a moment, then let it go, the way she always eventually let things go once she'd registered that pushing wouldn't move the timeline, only the mood. "Fine. But I'm keeping count."

"I know you are."

"Three months, Cael. I mean it. If it's still 'not ready' by then, I get to start guessing out loud, and you don't get to correct me."

"That seems like an unreasonable threat."

"It's not a threat. It's an incentive." She bumped his shoulder with hers, easy and familiar, the gesture doing more to close the distance than either of their sentences had. "I like you. I'm not going anywhere. I'd just like to actually understand the thing that's clearly shaping the rest of your life, sometime before it's finished shaping it."

"You already understand most of it. You've understood most of it since before I told you anything."

"Understanding the shape of a thing and understanding the thing aren't the same." She said this simply, without pressing it further, the way she said most of the observations she'd decided were true enough to state once and then let sit. "I know you're carrying something that started with me, months ago, in a sparring session neither of us has ever properly talked about. I know it's grown since then. I don't know what it actually is, underneath the two careful words in that little book. That's the part I'm asking for. Not permission. Just — eventually, the actual thing."

Cael thought about the two entries, the careful column he'd finally finished filling in that evening, the six months it had taken him to trust a testing method enough to write anything down with confidence at all. He thought about how much of the work — the sparring sessions, the thirty seconds she gave him after every activation, the patient willingness to be studied as much as she studied him — had been hers as much as his, even though only his name went in the log.

"Soon," he said, and meant it more than the word usually carried. "Not because of the three months. Because I think I'm getting close enough to actually explain it properly, and I'd rather you hear it that way than rushed."

"That's a better answer than the ones you usually give me."

"I've been practicing better answers. You've been teaching me, mostly without meaning to."

They walked the rest of the way without talking much, the comfortable quiet of two people who'd spent a year building a life together out of circuit winnings and morning drills and a shared, unspoken agreement that whatever was actually happening to Cael, they were going to face it together whenever it finally arrived. The boarding house came into view at the street's end, lamplight already lit in the front window, and Cael found himself, not for the first time, doing the small private arithmetic of how far he'd come from a fourteen-year-old standing in front of a notice board he hadn't understood yet at all.

Two entries in the fragment log. A year in Ardenmere. A life that had, somewhere along the way, stopped feeling borrowed and started feeling like his own.

It would have to be enough, for tonight. He suspected, without quite being able to say why, that it wasn't going to stay this simple for very much longer.

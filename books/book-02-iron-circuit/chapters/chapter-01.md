# Chapter 1 — Rated

The bout had ended twenty minutes ago, and Cael was still writing.

He sat in the alcove at the Ironyard's east wall, the one with the bench worn smooth by a year of practitioners doing exactly what he was doing now — sitting down right after a fight while the details were still sharp enough to catalogue accurately — and worked through the entry with the same unhurried care he brought to everything that mattered.

*Opponent: Copper-tier formal, Blade Path, six years' guild training. Watched him fight four times before agreeing to terms. Third exchange: committed strike telegraphs through a shoulder-drop his own instructors probably corrected out of him twice and never fully removed. Used it. Won in the fifth exchange.*

He was fifteen now. He had been in Ardenmere for just over a year, which felt like both an enormous stretch of time and no time at all, depending on which part of himself he asked. The observation notebook — the original one, the one that had started with Renn's telegraphed strikes and Dessa's transition windows — had twenty-three entries in it now, each one a little more precise than the last. The fragment log, which he'd finally separated out into its own slim volume six months ago because keeping tactical opponent data and his own private architecture in the same pages had started to feel like a filing error, had two entries. Wind-adjacent. Pressure-adjacent.

Vell's ledger, three tables over, would already show the result: *Assessed-Copper (Hesk-ward, C.), win, atypical movement pattern in third exchange, opponent Copper-tier formal.* Atypical movement pattern. It was, by now, close to a permanent fixture of his entries in that ledger, the way *win by exchange-four incapacitation* had once been. Vell never asked him to explain the atypical part. He suspected she'd stopped needing an explanation months ago.

He closed the observation notebook and opened the fragment log instead, which he did rarely enough that the act itself still felt significant.

Two entries. He'd added a third column to each this month — *functional deployment range* — after six months of methodically testing what each fragment could actually do rather than simply confirming that it existed.

*Wind-adjacent: evasion framework, short burst. Consistent. Does not fail under pressure.*

The Pressure-adjacent entry was less complete. He had a working deployment range — he knew, broadly, what it did and when it activated — but its upper limits under sustained combat pressure were still uncharacterized. He'd been careful about testing that particular gap. Some experiments needed a controlled setting he hadn't found yet.

He was midway through annotating the Wind-adjacent entry's new column when Lira appeared in the alcove doorway, damp from her own morning session, and stopped.

She didn't say anything. She looked at the small, precise handwriting, the two entries, the column he was filling in — and something in her expression settled into a familiar shape: patient curiosity, held rather than spoken.

She already had guesses. She'd had guesses for months. She was waiting until he was ready to confirm them properly, the way she waited for most things that mattered — not passively, but with the specific discipline of someone who understood that some doors opened faster if you didn't lean on them.

"Good fight?" she asked instead.

"Efficient," Cael said, closing the log. "He had a tell his own instructors probably tried to train out of him. Didn't take."

"You watched him four times before you agreed to it."

"Four times is usually enough now."

Lira leaned against the doorframe, studying him with the specific attention she still brought to him after a year of this — not surprise anymore, just ongoing interest, the way you stay interested in a puzzle that keeps producing new pieces. "You used to need six."

"I've gotten faster."

"You've gotten faster at everything." She said this without particular emphasis, but there was something underneath it, a genuine note of something between pride and unease that Cael had learned to recognize over the past year — the specific quality of someone watching a person she cared about accelerate past a pace either of them had originally planned for.

---

Life in Ardenmere, a year on, had organized itself entirely around the circuit, in a way that felt less like a a choice made once and more like a hundred small choices compounding daily until the shape simply was what it was.

He and Lira lived in two adjoining rooms at the back of a practitioners' boarding house two streets from the Ironyard — a step up from Torvin's, paid for with circuit winnings rather than Hesk's dwindling savings pouch, which Cael had stopped touching almost entirely once his own earnings became reliable. Mornings were training, in the Ironyard's side alcoves before the main floor woke up. Middle hours were the market, where he'd learned which vendors extended informal credit to circuit practitioners and which ones simply liked circuit practitioners' company. Afternoons, more often than not, were the Ironyard itself — watching whoever was fighting on the main floor, adding entries to the observation notebook, building the kind of information advantage that had become, without him quite deciding it should, his entire method of being good at anything.

The Ironyard itself had become as familiar to him as Denvash's Outer District once had been — a converted warehouse at the eastern edge of the Unranked District, three fighting spaces inside it, low ceilings and practitioner-installed lighting and a stone floor worn smooth by years of feet doing exactly what his were doing most days now. It smelled like impact dust and old iron fittings, a smell he'd stopped noticing consciously months ago the way you stop noticing your own house.

He walked back to the boarding house that afternoon with Lira, the observation notebook and the fragment log both tucked into the same bag, the way they'd traveled together for a year even after he'd separated their contents onto different pages.

"You're going to have to tell me eventually," Lira said, not quite out of nowhere — she had a way of returning to a subject after enough silence that it felt like a fresh thought rather than a resumed one.

"Tell you what?"

"Whatever's in that second book that you only open when you think I'm not paying attention." She said this lightly, but her eyes, when he glanced over, were serious underneath the lightness. "I've had guesses for months, Cael. I'm not going to make you say it before you're ready. I'm just telling you I notice."

He thought about the two entries. Wind-adjacent, sourced from months of watching her move, still — a year later — the foundation everything else in the log had been built on top of. He thought about how she'd already lived with that fact for months without demanding to see it named on a page.

"I know you notice," he said. "I'll show you when I understand it well enough to show you properly."

"That's what you said three months ago."

"I understand it better now than I did three months ago."

Lira studied him for a moment, then let it go, the way she always eventually let things go once she'd registered that pushing wouldn't move the timeline, only the mood. "Fine. But I'm keeping count."

"I know you are."

They walked the rest of the way without talking, the specific comfortable quiet of two people who'd spent a year building a life together out of circuit winnings and morning drills and a shared, unspoken agreement that whatever was actually happening to Cael, they were going to face it together whenever it finally arrived.

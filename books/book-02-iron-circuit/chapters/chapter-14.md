# Chapter 14 — The Circuit's Seasons

Autumn settled over the Unranked District the way it always did — later than in Denvash, Cael had noticed, the heat holding on a few extra weeks before finally giving way — and with it came the rhythm the Ironyard fell into once the main circuit season was underway in earnest. More bouts. More money changing hands. More of the small, grinding disputes that Vell handled as she handled everything: patiently, precisely, and without ever once looking like the patience cost her anything.

He'd noticed, somewhere in the last month, that he'd started thinking in seasons rather than weeks. His first months in Ardenmere had been counted almost obsessively — days since Denvash, days since the district gate, days until the six-week reprieve ran out — the anxious arithmetic of someone who didn't yet trust that tomorrow would look like today. He didn't count that way anymore. He thought instead in the district's own rhythm: the season the market sold dried apricots cheap, the season the Ironyard's evening crowds thinned because the cold kept casual spectators home, the season Vell's dispute load doubled because circuit betting always spiked once the main season's stakes climbed high enough to matter. It was, he understood, a small thing to notice about himself. It felt, nonetheless, like evidence of something larger — a year's worth of accumulated trust in a place finally settling into something that no longer needed conscious effort to maintain.

He kept the season's numbers, of course, because keeping numbers was what his attention did when left unattended. The main season roughly doubled the Ironyard's bout schedule — from four or five recorded matches a week to nine or ten — and the purses moved with the crowds: a mid-week bout between Assessed fighters might split a purse worth two weeks' boarding-house rent, while a main-floor match between rated names on a season evening could clear ten times that before the informal book's money was counted at all. The vendors adjusted a half-step behind the schedule, the bread stall baking a third batch on bout evenings, the man who sold hot skewers outside the Ironyard's east door appearing precisely one hour before any match Dace had chalked as likely to fill the floor — Dace's own predictions, Cael had noted with some appreciation, being reliably better than the betting odds that supposedly aggregated everyone else's.

The season brought its recurring cast of grievances too, most of which Cael had watched Vell resolve a dozen times over by now, each one following roughly the same shape: a fighter unhappy with a result, a claim of unfairness that rarely survived contact with the actual record, and Vell's flat, patient refusal to let the claim outrun the evidence. He'd started, without quite meaning to, keeping a running tally of dispute types in a corner of the observation notebook — not for any tactical purpose — just the reflexive habit that made him count everything eventually. Betting grievances led the tally by a wide margin — fourteen this season against three of anything else — followed by training-facility rivalries that spilled over into the circuit uninvited, followed by the rare, genuine rules dispute that actually required Vell's full attention rather than her patience alone.

This dispute, when it came, was smaller than most of what Vell had to adjudicate this season, but it arrived with the kind of implicit threat that made it worth handling carefully rather than waving off.

The practitioner who cornered Vell that morning was the same one from the coaching-interference dispute months earlier — older by a season, apparently no wiser for the wait, though this time his grievance had nothing to do with Orvet or the west post. He'd been in the audience for a bout three days earlier — a mid-afternoon match of Cael's own, a clean win Cael had already filed as one entry among dozens. The practitioner had bet against him and lost, apparently, and had spent the intervening days deciding that his loss required an explanation other than simply having bet on the wrong outcome — the same instinct that had sent him to this table once before, dressed up this time in a different complaint.

He cornered Vell at her table on a crowded morning, loud enough that the benches nearest the scheduling board quieted to listen — and Cael watched Vell's expression shift, just slightly, into the flat resignation of someone recognizing a repeat customer rather than meeting a stranger.

Cael had been running a quiet experiment of his own that morning, and the crowd's sudden reorientation ended it in a manner worth recording. He'd come in early, claimed his usual bench, and — following the small-doses protocol he'd set himself after the alcove sessions — cautiously opened the Iron-adjacent surface-sense in a room holding perhaps thirty people. The result had confirmed his prediction with unpleasant thoroughness: not thirty distinct signatures, but a single undifferentiated wash of presence, weight without direction, like trying to hear one conversation by pressing his ear against a wall shared with a tavern. He'd managed to hold the channel open for a slow count of twenty, extracting exactly nothing usable, before the band behind his eyes announced its arrival — and then the dispute's raised voice snapped his attention across the room, the channel collapsed as it did at any startle, and he was left with the headache and one honest data point: *crowds, confirmed: total flood. The fragment cannot yet count past one.* He rubbed his eyes once, closed the experiment, and gave the dispute the ordinary kind of attention instead.

"That result was manipulated," the practitioner said. "The unranked one — everyone knows he's doing something the Arbiter can't track. How is anyone supposed to bet honestly against that?"

Vell didn't look up from the ledger right away. When she did, her expression carried the same flat patience Cael had watched her deploy against a dozen similar complaints over the past year.

"The bout was fought under standard terms. Both parties agreed to them beforehand. You didn't."

"I'm not the one who fought it. I'm telling you the odds were false because nobody can properly assess what he's actually capable of."

"That's not a rules violation. That's a complaint that circuit betting carries risk, which it always has, for every fighter whose full capability isn't published somewhere convenient." She set the pen down — the same small gesture Cael had learned, months ago, marked the moment she stopped performing patience and simply delivered her ruling. "The bout stands."

The practitioner's jaw tightened. He glanced around at the gathering audience, apparently recalculating whether a bigger crowd made his position stronger or simply more exposed. "My training facility is guild-affiliated, same as I told you last time. Except this time I've actually written the complaint down, and I have connections who'd be very interested to hear that an unclassified fighter is competing in an unsanctioned circuit without any oversight at all."

The threat landed exactly the way it was meant to — a ripple through the nearest benches, the attentive stillness of people recognizing that the conversation had just changed shape.

Cael, seated in his usual spot with the observation notebook open, didn't look up immediately either. He'd learned, watching Vell work for a year now, that the useful information in a moment like this rarely lived in what was being said. It lived in what happened next.

"Do that," Vell said, "and you'll find out how fast word travels between keepers — same as I told you the last time you stood at this table. I'd think carefully about whether that's a reputation you want attached to your name twice in one season, given how much of your own income depends on circuits exactly like this one continuing to let you fight in them."

The practitioner opened his mouth to argue further, and that was when Cael spoke, quietly, from where he sat.

"I have the exchange sequence written down. All of it. If you want to argue the outcome, I can walk you through exactly what happened, in order, rather than what you remember happening after three days and a lost bet."

The whole exchange stopped. Vell looked at him — surprised, though she covered it quickly, the way she covered most surprise. The practitioner looked at him too, far less composed.

"You keep notes on your own bouts?"

"I keep notes on every bout. Mine most of all. It's not personal to you." Cael turned the notebook around so it faced the table, the entries dense and precise in his small, careful hand — timing, angle, the exact moment his opponent's defense had genuinely failed rather than been manipulated by anything beyond a better-prepared fighter reading a weaker one correctly. "Third exchange, here. He committed to a stance that telegraphed for a full half-second before the strike. That's not manipulation. That's a formal-trained fighter meeting someone who's spent a year learning to read exactly that kind of tell."

Vell took the notebook, studied it for a long moment, and then set it beside her own ledger, cross-referencing the two records with the same careful attention she brought to everything. The match was exact — his compound-gaze account of the bout's key exchanges lined up precisely against her own contemporaneous notation, each one confirming the other in a way that left no reasonable room for the complaint to survive.

She turned a page, checking a second exchange, then a third, her finger tracing the entries with the thoroughness she gave to anything worth verifying properly rather than rushing past. The room had gone quiet around them, forty-odd people watching a fifty-year-old woman and a fifteen-year-old boy settle a dispute with nothing but two sets of handwritten notes and the discipline it had taken to keep them.

"You had the shoulder-drop written down before the bout was ever fought," she said, not quite a question, more an observation offered out loud for the benefit of everyone listening. "Two entries. Dated."

"I'd already seen him do it against two other opponents. I was waiting to see if he'd do it a third time."

"And if he hadn't?"

"Then I'd have crossed out the pattern and started a new one. That's most of what the notebook actually is. Patterns that survive being tested more than once."

Vell nodded slowly, closing both books with the finality of a decision already made rather than one still forming. "The claim doesn't hold," she said, addressing the practitioner directly now rather than the crowd around him. "The bout stands. This matter is closed."

The practitioner looked between the two of them, understanding — for the second time in one season, from the same table — that his complaint had just evaporated in front of forty witnesses, and left without further argument, the deflated posture of a man discovering that testing the same door twice doesn't make it open any wider.

---

Afterward, Dace found him near the archive shelf, something like genuine appreciation showing through his usually unreadable expression. He had a way of approaching that Cael had come to recognize over the year — never quite direct, always arriving from an angle that let the other person notice him before he actually spoke, as if he'd learned, running a venue full of practitioners who didn't love being startled, that a sudden presence was its own kind of provocation.

"I've never had someone's notes actually be useful before," he said, leaning against the shelf's end with the loose posture of a man between the day's dozen small emergencies rather than in the middle of one.

"I take useful notes."

"You take obsessive notes. There's a difference, and most people who take obsessive notes never manage to make them useful to anyone but themselves." He nodded toward the notebook, still open in Cael's hands. "That's not a criticism. I've watched five years of practitioners come through here who couldn't tell you what happened in their own last bout without arguing about it for twenty minutes. You just settled a dispute in under two."

"Vell settled the dispute. I just gave her the evidence."

"Vell settles disputes with her judgment, which is good enough on its own most days. Today she settled one with your judgment backing hers, which is a different and, frankly, more useful thing to have in this room." Dace considered him for a moment, something calculating behind the casual tone — not unkindly, just the habit of a man who was always, on some level, running the logistics of everyone around him. "The Compact would love to have a record like that. Detailed. Timestamped. Cross-referenced against outcomes." A beat, deliberate, his eyes flicking briefly toward the door as though the thought itself needed checking for eavesdroppers. "Good thing we don't share."

Cael understood the weight underneath the offhand delivery. This wasn't idle commentary. Dace was telling him, in the only register he seemed to use for things that actually mattered to him, that the circuit's independence from the Compact wasn't a convenience he tolerated — it was something he protected, the same way Vell protected her ledger's integrity, for reasons that ran deeper than simple self-interest or professional habit.

"You've thought about what it would mean," Cael said. "If a record like mine ended up somewhere it shouldn't."

"I think about it more than I'd like to admit." Dace's easy posture didn't change, but something in his voice sharpened, just slightly. "Every fighter who comes through that door is here because the formal system either rejected them or couldn't see them clearly. A detailed record — timestamped, precise, exactly the kind of thing you're building — would be a gift to exactly the people this circuit exists to keep out."

He glanced toward the scheduling board, where the day's remaining bouts waited in chalk, and something in his expression softened slightly, the sharpness of a moment ago receding back into his usual calm. "I had a fighter here, years back, before your time. Talented. Genuinely talented, the kind that only comes through once every few seasons. Guild scout found out he trained here, offered him a legitimate placement — real money, real standing, everything he'd never get from an unranked classification. He took it." A pause, longer than the story strictly needed. "Six months later the guild had a full record of every unsanctioned bout he'd ever fought, and used it to make sure he understood exactly how replaceable he was if he ever thought about negotiating his own terms. He came back here eventually. Different person by then. Careful in a way he hadn't needed to be careful before." Dace shrugged, the gesture settling the memory back into wherever he usually kept it. "I don't share records anymore. Not with anyone. Not even the good ones."

Cael filed the story the way he filed most things Dace offered him — not as tactics, but as evidence of the actual shape of the thing he'd chosen to build his life around.

Dace pushed off the shelf, already turning to go, the conversation apparently concluded as far as he was concerned. "So. Keep taking your notes. Just remember whose hands they're for."

"I already knew that."

"I know you did. I just like saying it out loud occasionally. Keeps everyone honest, including me."

---

That evening, the boarding house held all three of them at once for the first time — not by careful arrangement, just the ordinary accumulation of an evening where nobody had anywhere else particularly urgent to be. Lira had cooked, which she did rarely and announced loudly whenever she did, as though daring anyone to comment on the smoke that inevitably followed. Brom had arrived with a jar of something pickled that he refused to explain the origin of beyond "a trade," and the three of them had ended up crowded around the small table in Cael and Lira's shared front room, plates balanced on knees, the evening settling into something neither planned nor especially structured.

The room itself had accumulated, over the year, the clutter of a life actually being lived rather than merely passed through — Cael's two notebooks stacked on the shelf by the window, Lira's spare training weapons leaned in the corner where she always meant to find them a better home and never did, a single chipped cup that had somehow become permanently designated as Cael's despite nobody ever formally deciding it should be. Brom looked around at all of it while he ate, the quiet attention of someone taking inventory of a life he didn't currently have and was, perhaps, beginning to want.

"You two have made an actual home out of this," he said, at one point, not quite a question.

"Two rooms and a chipped cup," Lira said. "Don't get sentimental about it."

"I'm not being sentimental. I'm making an observation." He gestured with his own cup at the shelf, the weapons, the general lived-in disorder of it. "I've been in three cities in two years. None of them looked like this. I think that says something, though I haven't decided yet what."

"It says you haven't stayed anywhere long enough to accumulate clutter," Cael offered.

"Maybe. Or it says I hadn't found anywhere worth accumulating it in." He said this plainly, without any particular weight, but something in the room shifted slightly at the admission — the first time, Cael realized, that Brom had said anything that sounded less like a settled fact and more like an open question he was still working through.

Lira, who noticed these things faster than either of them, reached over without comment and refilled his cup, a small, wordless gesture that said more than anything either of them might have offered in words. Brom accepted it with a short nod, and the moment passed as most of the evening's moments did — noted, absorbed, not dwelt on longer than it needed to be.

Sometime after the plates were cleared, in a lull while Lira argued the pickled jar's contents into categories of edible and inedible, Cael ran the day's second experiment — because the conditions, he'd realized, were the exact inverse of the morning's: a quiet room, a seated body, a single strong signature four feet away. He let the surface-sense drift open, in no rush about it, and Brom's presence arrived against it almost gently — and taught him something within three breaths that two days of alcove drilling hadn't. The signature at rest wasn't at rest. Beneath the loose posture and the cup and the visible ease, Brom's pressure-read idled on, turned low the way a banked fire is low, a thin background attention that apparently never fully went out. *I don't get to stop reading,* he'd said, after the bout, and Cael had filed it as a statement about difficult opponents. It wasn't. It was a statement about his life.

The channel held for perhaps eight breaths this time — his longest yet — before Brom's eyes came up and found his, mildly, across the table.

"You're doing it right now."

"Yes."

"I can feel it. Faintly. Like someone standing slightly too close." Brom took an unbothered sip from his cup. "Your range is terrible."

"I know. A few feet, seated, in a quiet room. That's the entire current capability." The band behind his eyes was arriving on schedule; he let the channel close and pressed two fingers briefly against the bridge of his nose. "It cost me a headache to learn that you never stop reading. Even here."

Something crossed Brom's face that was not quite discomfort — the look of a man whose oldest habit had just been observed from the outside for the first time. "No," he said, finally. "Even here. It stopped being a choice sometime in my second year of training. My instructors called it progress." He turned his cup once on the table. "I've never been in a room with someone who could notice. I find I don't mind it as much as I'd have predicted."

Lira looked up from the jar, glancing between them. "Are you two doing something invisible at my dinner table?"

"Yes," they said, at the same time, and she threw a pickled something at each of them, and the evening absorbed the moment and moved on.

Lira had been cautious of Brom since the fight — the wary, watchful caution of someone who took a loss personally on a friend's behalf even when the friend himself clearly hadn't taken it that way at all. She hadn't said much to him beyond what politeness required, in the days since, and Cael had noticed the careful distance she kept even while she trained a few dozen feet away from wherever Brom happened to be sparring.

That distance dissolved over the course of the evening, gradually and then all at once, the way most of Lira's positions tended to shift once she'd gathered enough evidence to actually change her mind. Brom's brand of unadorned honesty — no performance in it, no softening, just a plain reading of whatever was actually true — turned out to be exactly the kind of quality she recognized as valuable, having spent a year building her own circuit reputation on a rougher version of the same thing.

"You don't talk like someone who grew up with money," she said, partway through the meal, watching him pick through the pickled jar's contents with visible suspicion despite having brought it himself.

"I talk like someone who left money behind because it wasn't telling me the truth about myself." He said this without any weight on it, as he said most things about his family now — processed, settled, no longer requiring careful handling. "Money's very good at making you believe things that aren't true, if you let it. I stopped letting it."

"That's either very wise or very convenient to say once you've already left."

"Probably both." He didn't seem bothered by the observation. "Most useful things are."

Lira laughed at that — a real laugh, surprised out of her — and something in the room's temperature shifted, warmer, the last of her guardedness finally settling.

Later, once the conversation had drifted somewhere looser, Brom set his cup down and looked at her directly, the way he looked at most things he'd decided were worth saying properly.

"The Wind technique you're using in the circuit isn't what the standard manuals describe."

Lira went still, braced instinctively for the kind of correction she'd have gotten at her old academy — the dismissive tone of an instructor informing her that improvisation wasn't discipline, that deviation from the manual was itself the failure, regardless of results.

"I improved it," she said, flatly, daring him to argue.

"I know. You should be competing at Iron-equivalent."

She stared at him for a long moment, checking, the way she checked most surprising things, for the performance underneath the statement. There wasn't one. He meant it exactly as plainly as he'd said it — a fact, offered because he believed it was true and saw no reason to dress it up or soften it into something more comfortable to hear.

"You've watched, what, two of my bouts?"

"Three. And I read pressure signatures for a living, more or less. I don't need a fourth to know what I'm looking at." He shrugged, entirely unbothered by her skepticism. "You can disagree if you want. I won't be less right about it."

She turned to Cael, something bright and slightly disbelieving in her expression. "I like him."

"I thought you might."

"You could have told me sooner that he was going to be like this."

"You wouldn't have believed me until you heard it from him directly. Some things you have to arrive at yourself."

She threw a piece of bread at him for that, and Brom, watching the exchange with quiet satisfaction, as though he'd just confirmed something he'd suspected for weeks, said nothing at all — just refilled his own cup and let the evening continue around him, comfortable in a way Cael suspected he hadn't been comfortable in someone else's company for a long time.

The three of them stayed up later than any of them had planned. Lira's laughter carried through the thin walls loud enough that Cael half-expected a complaint from the room next door, though none ever came; Brom's dry, economical observations landed better each time he made one, until even he seemed mildly surprised by how easily the room laughed at things he hadn't intended as jokes. Cael mostly watched, as he watched most things worth watching closely, and found himself, somewhere past midnight, no longer taking any notes at all, internal or otherwise — just living inside the evening, the way you live inside weather rather than studying it from a window.

He thought, later, lying awake after Brom had finally gone and Lira's breathing had settled into sleep in the next room, about how strange it was that a year could hold this much change and still feel, most days, like simple continuity — one morning following the last, one season easing into the next, until you looked up and realized the whole shape of your life had quietly become something you never could have planned for at the start of it.

He wrote a short entry that night, not in the Power Log, not in the observation notebook, but on a loose page he kept folded into the back of the fragment log for exactly this kind of thought — the kind too personal for tactics and too small for anything else.

*A year ago I had a bag, a direction, and a grandfather's notebook I hadn't finished reading. Tonight I had a home with two people in it who chose to be there. I don't know what the Compact's file says about me right now, and for one evening, I found I didn't much care.*

He folded the page back into the log, closed it, and slept better than he had in weeks.

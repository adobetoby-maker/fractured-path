# Chapter 12 — What Brom Knows

He'd woken sore in a way that had nothing to do with the previous day's fight and everything to do with the specific exhaustion of a body that had spent four exchanges compensating for compromised legs — not the sharp, immediate ache of a fresh injury, but the deeper, duller kind that settled in overnight and made itself known the moment he tried to swing his feet off the cot. He'd taken the morning slowly because of it, later than his usual pace to the market, favoring one side without quite thinking about it.

Brom found him the day after the fight, at the market, and fell into step beside him with the unceremonious ease of someone who'd already decided they were past the point of needing an invitation.

Cael was buying bread from the stall he'd favored for the better part of a year, the vendor waving off the exact coin the way she'd taken to doing whenever his morning came early enough that she hadn't yet counted her till. He noticed Brom's approach the way he noticed most things now, out of habit rather than alarm, and didn't break stride, though he did register, with a flicker of something that wasn't quite surprise, that Brom moved through a crowded market with the same unhurried economy he used in the circle — no wasted motion, no unnecessary weight in his footsteps, a body that had clearly spent a great deal of time learning exactly how much space it needed to occupy.

"I've decided you should have equivalent information about me," Brom said, without preamble, matching Cael's pace like it was the most natural thing in the world, "if I'm going to understand what you're doing."

"That's not usually how people introduce a conversation."

"I don't waste time on the versions of things that take longer to say the same amount." He said it without any edge to it, a simple statement about how he preferred to move through the world. "You'll get used to it."

Cael suspected he would. He gestured toward a low wall at the market's edge, out of the main thoroughfare's traffic, and they sat.

Brom explained without waiting to be asked, laying out his own story with the flat, processed clarity of someone who'd already spent considerable time working through it alone and had come out the other side without residual bitterness clinging to any of it.

His family held land at Velmere — not vast, but old, the kind of holding that had produced three generations of practitioners whose Kindlings had, without exception, resolved to Gold-tier or close enough to it that the family had stopped bothering to imagine a different outcome. His grandmother had been Gold. His mother had been Gold, barely, a hair's breadth from Silver by the numbers but Gold on the classification that mattered to the family's standing. His older sister, two years into her own guild placement, was already being discussed as a candidate for regional advancement.

"And then there's me," Brom said, without any particular weight on the sentence, the way you'd report a measurement rather than a wound. "Iron Skin Path. Copper-tier. My family expected Gold. Iron Skin at Copper is the kind of result that ends conversations at dinner — not because anyone says anything cruel, you understand. Nobody in my family says anything cruel. They just go quiet, and reschedule, and start talking about training intensification in a tone that makes it very clear what they actually think happened."

"What happened?"

"Nothing happened. I Kindled. The Arbiter told me what I was. My family had already decided what I was going to be, years before that, and the two things didn't match." He picked up a stone from the ground beside the wall, turned it once in his fingers, set it back down — the closest thing to a nervous gesture Cael had yet seen from him, gone as quickly as it appeared. "I left before they could finish deciding what to do about the embarrassment. Training intensification. New instructors. A quiet campaign to have the classification reviewed, as though a second Arbiter reading might produce a different, more convenient result. I didn't want to be in the house while they worked through all of that. So I wasn't."

"How long did you wait, before you left?"

"Three weeks. Long enough to be sure the plan was actually the plan, and not just a bad week they'd talk themselves out of." Brom's voice stayed level, but something in his pace slowed, the specific care of someone choosing to walk through a memory carefully rather than rushing past it. "My father called in an instructor from two estates over — a man who specialized in what he called 'corrective assessment,' which is a polite phrase for finding reasons an inconvenient Kindling result might not be entirely accurate. He spent a week watching me train, taking his own notes, the way I imagine you take yours. At the end of it he told my father, in front of me, that my technique showed 'irregular development inconsistent with proper Copper-tier instruction' — which was true, in its way. I'd never had proper Copper-tier instruction. I'd been teaching myself since I was twelve, whenever the family instructors weren't watching closely enough to correct me back onto the approved path."

"Your family didn't know."

"My family assumed. That's different from knowing." He said this without particular bitterness, simply naming the distinction the way he named most things. "They assumed I was following the standard progression, the same one my mother and grandmother had followed, because that was the only progression they'd ever needed to imagine for anyone in the family. It never occurred to them to actually watch closely enough to notice I'd quietly built something else underneath it."

"What changed, at the end of the three weeks?"

"The corrective instructor recommended a second Kindling assessment — an unofficial one, informal, done by a private evaluator rather than a registered Arbiter, on the theory that if it produced a more favorable read, they could use it to petition for an official re-classification." Brom's jaw tightened, very slightly, the only real sign of anything like anger Cael had seen from him yet. "I understood, hearing that, exactly what the plan actually was. Not correcting an error. Manufacturing a better one, and hoping nobody looked too closely at the seams. I left that night."

He fell quiet for a moment, and Cael, recognizing the specific shape of a pause that wasn't finished yet, didn't fill it.

"I don't think they were bad people," Brom said, eventually. "I think they loved an idea of me more than they'd ever actually looked at the real one, and when the idea and the reality stopped matching, they tried to fix the reality instead of the idea. I don't know if that's forgivable. I know it's not something I wanted to stay and keep negotiating."

"Where did you go?"

"Two cities before this one. Circuit work in both — smaller circuits, less organized than Vell's, but enough to survive on and enough to actually learn something." He said this plainly, no self-pity in it at all. "I found that I cared more about being good at the thing than about what anyone thought the thing was worth. My family's taxonomy — Gold matters, Copper is a disappointment, the tier is the whole conversation — never told me what Iron Skin could actually *do*. I found that out myself. Alone, mostly. In cities that had never heard of Velmere and couldn't have cared less what my family expected of me even if they had."

"That's why the technique you use doesn't match any formal framework."

"Probably. Nobody taught me the force-redirect. I built it because I was fighting people who could hit harder than me, and absorbing the hit without doing something useful with it seemed like a waste of a perfectly good impact." He glanced at Cael, something almost amused in his expression. "My old instructors would call it unorthodox. I call it the only version of Iron Skin that's actually mine."

Cael recognized the structure of this immediately, even under entirely different circumstances — different classification, different family, different city, the same underlying logic running underneath all of it. A taxonomy that claimed to describe a person, failing to describe them at all, and a person who'd gone and found out the truth for themselves rather than accept the taxonomy's verdict as final.

"My grandfather used to say something like that," Cael said, after a moment. "That the system draws a map, and you're not required to fit inside the drawing."

"I'd like your grandfather."

"He'd like you too. He values people who go find things out themselves instead of waiting to be told."

---

Brom let the silence sit for a moment after Cael's answer about the map and the drawing, then turned to face him fully on the low wall, the easy conversational rhythm of the last several minutes sharpening into something more deliberate.

"My turn to ask the question I've actually been building toward," he said. "What's your classification?"

Cael had known this was coming since the moment Brom sat down at the fight the day before and said *I'm Brom* like it was still worth saying, the two of them already well past the point of needing introductions. He'd had a day to decide how he wanted to answer it, and had landed, in the end, on the same principle that had carried him through every version of this conversation since Weaver's Row: say it plainly, first, before anyone else got the chance to say it around him.

"[SHATTERED]."

Brom's expression didn't change. Not the flicker of alarm Cael had learned to expect and mostly stopped bracing for, not even the brief, considering pause most people needed to process the word before responding to it. He simply nodded, the way you'd nod at a fact that confirmed something you'd already worked out from other evidence.

"I know," he said. "I've known since the second exchange, more or less. The pressure-read doesn't malfunction. It reads everyone I've ever pointed it at, cleanly, into some category or another, even the strange ones. It didn't do that with you. It just — failed to sort you into anything at all. I've read Gold-tier practitioners with strange, rare Paths. I've read people training abilities they hadn't formally declared. Nothing has ever come back simply *unreadable* before you did."

"That's why you sat down next to me at that first bout. Before we'd even spoken."

"Partly. Mostly I sat down because I wanted to see what you'd write about a fight you weren't fighting, and it turned out to be more interesting than the fight itself." He said this with no particular emphasis, a plain report of fact rather than flattery. "But yes. I'd already read you wrong — I mean that in both senses. Wrong as in *incorrect,* and wrong as in *unable to produce a reading.* I wanted to understand which one it actually was before I decided how much attention you deserved."

"And now?"

"Now I know it's the second kind. Which is considerably more interesting than the first." He asked his questions the way he did most things — directly, without cushioning, the specific economy of someone who'd decided long ago that softening a real question rarely made the answer any easier to receive. "What does the Arbiter show, when you check it?"

"Nothing. It went dark at Kindling. Hasn't spoken since, not in any way the registry would recognize."

"And what you're actually doing — in the circle, in the third exchange against me. What is that?"

"Something." Cael turned the word over before continuing, choosing the next part carefully, because it was the part he least liked admitting out loud even now, a year into carrying it. "I don't have a name for it yet. Pieces of something, maybe — I don't fully understand the mechanism myself. I've been building a private record of what I do know for almost a year now."

"Does having a name matter?"

Cael considered this seriously, the way he considered most questions Brom asked, which so rarely came with an obvious answer already attached. "For what I'm trying to build toward, eventually — yes. I think a name will matter a great deal, someday. For what I'm actually doing right now, in any given exchange, no. The fragments work whether or not I can name the shape they're part of."

"Then build toward the name," Brom said, "and operate without it in the meantime. That's not a contradiction. That's just two different timelines for the same project."

"That's what I've been doing. I didn't have it phrased that cleanly until you just said it."

Brom nodded, apparently satisfied, and then said the thing that mattered more than anything else in the entire conversation, delivered with the same flat directness as everything else he'd said that afternoon: "Show me the log sometime."

Cael looked at him for a long moment. It was, in context, a considerably larger request than its four words suggested — the Power Log was private in a way that had nothing to do with shame and everything to do with honesty. Lira had earned her way into its pages slowly, over months, the way she'd earned her way into most of what he carried; Hesk knew it existed only through letters. Nobody else had ever read a line of it. It was the single most technical, unguarded document he kept, more precise than anything he'd ever said aloud, and he had never once shown it to anyone who hadn't already earned the access some other way first.

He weighed Brom against the benchmark, quietly, the way he weighed most decisions that actually mattered — not the fight the day before, which had told him plenty about Brom's skill and composure, but this conversation, the unhurried honesty of it, the specific way Brom had received *unreadable* and *fragments* and *I don't fully understand the mechanism* without once flinching or performing sympathy he didn't feel.

"Maybe," he said, finally, which was, given everything Brom had already learned to read correctly in him over the past two days, close enough to a yes that neither of them needed to pretend otherwise.

---

The practical question came next, the way it usually did with Brom once the larger, harder conversation had actually been finished rather than merely paused.

"So," he said. "If I'm going to help you build toward a name for whatever this is, I need better data than one fight and one afternoon of talking gives either of us. I want to spar with you. Regularly. Not for the circuit's benefit — not a formal, scheduled bout with Vell keeping the ledger and a crowd deciding what it means. Just the two of us, on whatever timing actually suits us both."

"You want close-range observation."

"I want to know what your architecture reads as under controlled, repeated conditions, instead of the single high-pressure data point a formal bout gives me. One data point tells you almost nothing reliable. Ten tell you the shape of something." He said this with the same plain conviction he brought to everything, as though the logic were self-evident enough not to require further defense. "And you want the same thing from me, whether or not you've said it yet. Sustained, close-range exposure to Iron Skin under real pressure — not the single fight, where half your attention was busy not losing too badly instead of purely watching."

Cael considered this, and found he had no honest objection to make. "I do want that."

"There are terms, though. I want to be clear about them before we start, so neither of us is surprised later." Brom held up a hand, counting on his fingers the way a man ticking off a checklist rather than negotiating a friendship. "First: I use the pressure-read fully, every session, no holding back to make it easier on either of us. Half-measures produce half-useful data."

"Agreed."

"Second: you tell me what you're testing, before we start, each time. Not the full theory necessarily — I understand some of this is still forming — but at least the shape of the question. I don't want to be an unwitting variable in an experiment I don't know I'm part of."

Cael turned this over. It was, he recognized, exactly the kind of condition he'd have proposed himself, if their positions were reversed. "That's fair. I'd want the same from you."

"Then you have it. Third — and this is the one that actually matters more than the other two." Brom's tone shifted, very slightly, something more careful entering it. "If either of us gets hurt badly enough that the sparring itself becomes the problem instead of the solution, we stop. Immediately. No finishing the session to prove a point, no pushing through because the data's almost there. I've seen training partnerships wreck themselves because neither person wanted to be the one who said *enough* first. I'd rather say it early and be wrong than say it late and be right too late to matter."

"Agreed. All three."

"Good." Something eased in his posture, the specific settling of a man who'd said the careful part and could now relax into the rest of the conversation. "Then we're agreed. Neither of us is doing the other a favor. We're both getting something we actually need." Brom said it without any attempt to dress the arrangement up as friendship for its own sake, which struck Cael, oddly, as more honest — and somehow warmer — than if he had.

Both of them understood, without needing to spell it out further, exactly what the other stood to gain. Neither of them minded the transaction being named plainly instead of dressed in softer language. If anything, Cael thought, walking back toward the boarding house afterward, the plainness of it was what made it feel trustworthy in the first place — Brom wasn't offering friendship as a cover for curiosity, or curiosity as a cover for friendship. He was simply offering both, honestly, at the same time, and letting Cael decide what to call the combination.

"Tomorrow," Brom said, as they parted at the market's edge, already turning to go. "Morning. Before the crowds start."

"I'll be there."

He was — early, in fact, waiting at the side alcove before Brom himself arrived, notebook already open on the bench beside him, the observation section reformatted weeks ago and the Power Log tucked underneath it, not yet shown to anyone but ready, for the first time, to be shown to someone who might actually understand what it meant.

---

He found Lira before dinner, back at the boarding house, and told her the whole of it — not because he owed her a report, but because keeping something this size from her for even an evening had started to feel, in the year since he'd learned better, like its own small betrayal.

She listened without interrupting, sitting on the edge of her own cot through the open door between their two rooms, working a knot out of her boot laces with the specific patient attention she gave anything that let her hands stay busy while her mind did the harder work of actually absorbing what she was hearing.

"You told him everything," she said, once he'd finished. Not quite a question.

"Everything. The classification. The fragments. The fact that I still don't fully understand the mechanism."

"How did it feel?"

Cael considered the question honestly, the way he tried to consider most things she asked him. "Easier than I expected. I think I'd built up telling people as something that had to cost me, every time, because it cost me so much the first time I said it out loud — to you, at the notice board, a year ago." He paused. "It didn't cost anything today. He just absorbed it and asked practical questions, the way he asks most things."

"That's because you're not the same person you were a year ago at that notice board." She said this simply, without any particular drama, the flat observation of someone who'd watched the change happen up close, day by day, closely enough that the scale of it had mostly escaped her own notice until she said it out loud just now. "You used to brace for the reaction before you gave anyone the information. You don't do that anymore. Not with me. Not, apparently, with him."

"When did that change?"

"I don't know exactly. Sometime this year. I noticed it before you did, probably." She finally worked the knot loose, set the boot aside, and looked at him properly. "I like him. For what that's worth. Not just because he's interesting, or because he's clearly the strongest thing to walk into this circuit in longer than either of us has been fighting in it. Because he heard what you are and decided the correct response was curiosity instead of caution. That's rarer than either of us probably wants to admit."

"Brom would probably say the two aren't actually different reactions. Just different names for the same instinct."

"Brom," Lira said, with the specific fondness that had crept into her voice for him faster than either of them had quite expected, "says a lot of things that sound like they should be obvious and somehow aren't, until he's the one saying them."

They ate dinner together after that, the ordinary rhythm of an evening that had, underneath its ordinariness, quietly marked something neither of them needed to name directly: a second person now fully inside the circle of who knew, who'd chosen to stay anyway, adding one more thread to whatever it was the two of them had been slowly, carefully building together — soon, though neither of them fully realized it yet, to become three — in a district that had given all of them considerably more than any of them had arrived expecting.

---

He wrote a short entry that evening, in the private section of the log, before the tactical work of the coming weeks properly began.

*Brom knows now. All of it — the classification, the fragments, the fact that I don't fully understand my own mechanism yet. He didn't treat any part of it as a problem to be managed or a secret to be handled carefully. He treated it as a fact, interesting on its own terms, worth understanding rather than worth fearing.*

*I keep waiting for the people who matter to me to eventually reach some limit — a point past which the strangeness of what I am becomes too much to keep absorbing without flinching. Lira never found that limit. Hesk never found it. Now Brom, on the second day of actually knowing me, hasn't found it either.*

*I'm starting to think the limit was never real. I think it was something I built for myself, back in Denvash, out of the reactions I got before I ever left — the gate guard, the neighbors who wouldn't meet my eyes. I carried that expectation with me a long way past the point where it stopped being accurate. Maybe it's time to finally put it down.*

He closed the notebook, and lay back on his cot, and thought — not for the first time, but with a clarity the day's conversation had sharpened considerably — that Ardenmere had given him more than he'd ever had reasonable grounds to expect from a district he'd arrived in with nothing but a bag, a set of aching legs from two days on the road, and Hesk's careful handwriting for company.

Tomorrow, he would learn what Iron Skin actually felt like from the inside of a sustained exchange, without the distraction of an audience or the pressure of a formal result attached to it. He found, turning the thought over one last time before sleep took the choice away from him, that he was looking forward to it in a way that had very little to do with tactics at all.

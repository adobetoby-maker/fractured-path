# Chapter 23 — The Offer

Cael's internal conflict wasn't about whether to go. He'd known, in some quiet way, since arriving in Ardenmere that the circuit had a ceiling — it had given him everything it had to give, and whatever waited beyond that ceiling required the structure and opponents that only a legitimate institution could actually provide. Quenna's offer wasn't a temptation he needed to be argued into. The moment she'd said the word *Greyvane,* some part of him that had been quietly totaling a ledger for months had simply closed the account and reported the answer: yes.

His conflict was about what the going would cost, and he spent the first hour after she'd left simply walking the district, not toward anywhere in particular, letting the question sit with him the way he let a difficult opponent's tell sit with him before he trusted himself to act on it.

Ardenmere had become real, in a way he hadn't fully let himself notice until the leaving was suddenly on the table. He walked past the bakery two streets from the boarding house, the one whose owner had started setting aside the good loaves without being asked, months ago now, once she'd learned his schedule well enough to have an opinion about it. He walked past the reading room where he'd spent a frustrating, useful afternoon failing to find anything real about Iron Skin Path, and past the low wall outside the Ironyard where he and Lira had once sat working out the shape of a hypothesis that had, eventually, cost him two legs' worth of mobility and taught him more than the loss itself. He walked past Vell's office, dark at this hour, and thought about the archive shelf, and the space where the next volume would go once the current one filled, and understood, with a specificity that surprised him, that some part of the record in that room was going to end whether he wanted it to or not. Not erased. Just closed. A chapter of a ledger with his name in it, complete, the way Vell closed any account once the relevant activity had stopped.

The Ironyard was a place that knew his name without ever requiring his registry document. Vell's ledger held a record of him that was entirely, uncontestably his own, built fight by fight over a year of exactly the kind of patient, honest work his grandfather had once told him mattered more than any classification a stranger could hand him. He had never, in his life before Ardenmere, had a record like that. Denvash had never offered him one. The Compact certainly hadn't. He had built this one himself, out of nothing but attention and persistence, in a warehouse that smelled like impact dust and old iron fittings, alongside two people who had decided, independently and for their own reasons, that he was worth the trouble of knowing.

Leaving that didn't feel like abandoning a place. It felt closer to abandoning a version of himself — the one who had arrived here with a bag and a direction and nothing else, who had built, slowly and without any plan beyond survival, an actual life. He understood, walking, that Greyvane would ask him to build another one, from a similarly bare beginning, and that the fact he'd done it once before was the only real evidence he had that he could do it again.

He thought, too, about the people the leaving didn't touch — Vell, who would keep the ledger regardless of who walked through her door next season; Dace, who would find someone else's schedule to manage; the old man at the yard's far corner, who had never once asked Cael to stay and would likely never ask him not to leave, because staying and leaving had never been the terms he measured anyone by. Ardenmere would continue being exactly what it was, with or without him in it. That, more than anything else, was what finally let him stop turning the question over. A place that needed him to justify its own existence would have been a harder place to leave. A place that would simply keep being itself, whether he stayed or not, was one he could walk away from without feeling like he was taking something from it that it couldn't afford to lose.

---

The three-person conversation happened that evening, in the boarding house's common room, unhurried, each of them arriving at their own answer in their own time and in their own particular way.

"I'm going," Lira said, immediately, no equivocation at all, before Cael had even finished laying out the full shape of the offer. "I'm not staying here to have an unofficial ceiling because I can't access a legitimate Arbiter evaluation. I've known that gap was going to catch up with me eventually. I'd rather choose the moment than have it choose me."

"I know."

"You knew before I said it."

"I knew before I woke up this morning." She nodded, unsurprised, the two of them having reached the same place by different routes, the way they usually did by now — Cael arriving through careful, deliberate consideration, Lira arriving through the same fierce, immediate clarity she brought to most decisions that actually mattered to her.

"What about the re-certification track," Cael asked, because it mattered to him whether the offer actually served her, not just whether it existed. "Monthly reassessment. That's not nothing, especially with what you're used to here — the circuit doesn't ask you to prove yourself over and over. Vell's ledger just keeps accumulating."

"I know what it's asking." Lira considered this seriously, turning her cup once between her hands. "I think I want to be asked to prove it again. I think a year of clean circuit wins without anyone official ever having to acknowledge them has made me hungrier for the acknowledgment than I like admitting, and Greyvane's the only place currently offering it." She looked at him. "You get that. You've been building toward being seen properly since before I met you."

He did get it, more completely than he'd have been able to articulate before she'd said it that plainly.

Brom took longer to answer, which surprised none of them — he approached every genuinely large decision the same methodical way he approached an opponent's Path, gathering information before committing to anything. The offer wasn't explicitly for him. The demonstration-provision and re-certification tracks were specific to Cael and Lira's particular circumstances, and he spent the better part of an hour working through what that actually meant for him, asking practical questions with the same unhurried thoroughness he'd once brought to watching Cael's earliest bouts from the audience: what kind of circuit-adjacent training Greyvane offered, whether Iron Skin Path had any real representation on the faculty at all, whether an unaffiliated Copper-tier practitioner with a clean record and no formal complaints could simply apply for standard enrollment like anyone else.

"You don't need a special provision," Cael pointed out, midway through the questioning. "You have an actual classification. A real one."

"I know that. I want to know if a real classification from a family estate two provinces away is going to matter to them, or if what matters is what I can currently do." Brom said this without any particular heat, just the flat precision of someone determined to understand the actual terms of a thing before agreeing to them. "I left home because a piece of paper told my family what I was worth before I'd had the chance to find out myself. I'm not walking into another institution on the strength of the same kind of paper. If I go, I go on what I can demonstrate."

Cael relayed the full set of questions to Quenna the following morning, at the small inn near the market where she'd taken a room for the duration of her stay. She listened to each one without impatience, and confirmed, without hesitation, that a standard Copper-tier practitioner with demonstrated performance — and she'd clearly already looked into Brom's specifically, given how little she needed explained to her — could apply under the regular enrollment provision, no special designation required, evaluated on the same terms as any other Greyvane applicant.

He carried the answer back to the boarding house that afternoon and delivered it plainly, the way Brom preferred things delivered.

Brom was quiet for a moment, processing it with the same careful weight he gave everything. Then, finally: "Then I'm coming as a practitioner." He looked at Cael directly, something settling in his expression that read, unmistakably, as a decision rather than a hope. "You're not done being interesting, and I haven't solved the problem yet."

"The problem being me."

"The problem being you." A pause, and then, quieter, something closer to the honesty he rarely offered without being asked for it first: "And because I've had a home once, and lost the version of it that was supposed to matter, and found a stranger one instead, out here, that turned out to matter more. I'm not in a hurry to let that go just because the location's about to change."

Lira reached over and put a hand briefly on his shoulder, the specific unshowy gesture she reserved for moments that meant more than either of them wanted to make a production of.

---

Cael wrote to Hesk that same evening, keeping the letter short: the academy, the offer, his own inclination toward accepting it. He didn't expect an answer before he left — Denvash was three or four weeks distant by the fastest post, longer than he had left in Ardenmere to wait for one. But there was already a letter from Hesk waiting for him at the boarding house the next morning, one that had been in transit since the week he'd first mentioned, almost in passing, that someone from Greyvane had started asking careful questions around the circuit. Hesk had answered a question Cael hadn't officially asked yet, as though he'd been prepared for exactly this letter before it ever arrived.

*Go. You need people who have seen you clearly and haven't left. Find more of them.*

Cael read it twice, and then a third time, the specific economy of Hesk's handwriting doing what it always did — saying less than most people would have needed to say the same thing, and somehow landing with more weight for the restraint. He thought about the workshop, the smell of solder and the particular oil Hesk used on instrument housings, the rooftop where they'd once sat together the night before everything changed. He thought about how easily Hesk could have written something longer, more anxious, more full of the specific fears a grandfather might reasonably carry about a fifteen-year-old accepting an offer from an institution two provinces distant. He hadn't. He'd trusted Cael to already know the fears existed and to have weighed them properly before ever writing to ask.

*Find more of them,* he'd written. Not *be careful.* Not *are you certain.* An instruction, not a caution — the same shape of trust Hesk had been extending since a marked circle in a certification office had first tried to tell them both who Cael was supposed to be.

He wrote back that same evening, shorter than usual, because some replies didn't need length to carry their full weight: *I found two more already. I think that's most of what a person actually needs. I'll write when I arrive.*

He sealed it, and the following morning walked to the small inn near the market and told Quenna, plainly, that he accepted. She produced the paperwork with the same unhurried efficiency she'd brought to everything since introducing herself, and by midday the demonstration-provision track carried his name on it, formal in a way nothing about his classification had ever managed to be formal before.

---

That evening, back in the boarding house's common room, he sat with the Power Log open in his lap, not writing anything yet — just holding the weight of the decision, the way he'd learned to hold most weight that mattered, before he trusted himself to put it into words.

Lira was across the room, already sorting through what she'd actually need to pack, and Brom sat near the window doing nothing in particular, simply present the way he'd become, over the months, a fixture of most evenings that mattered. Cael looked at the two of them for a while before he finally picked up the pen.

*Accepted. Greyvane, within the week. Three fragments, one anomaly, two people who know all of it and are coming with me anyway.*

*I don't know what Greyvane will actually be. I know it's going to ask for more of me than Ardenmere ever did, formally — assessments, demonstrations, a faculty that will look at me the way Havel and Coss and every Compact official before them has looked at me, except this time with something closer to curiosity than suspicion. I don't know yet whether that's better or simply different.*

*What I know is this: I arrived here with a bag and a direction and nothing else, and I'm leaving with a record that's entirely my own, a friendship that survived a loss neither of us treated as the point, and a partnership built the hard way, out of a year of choosing each other every single morning rather than assuming the choice, once made, would simply hold itself together.*

*Whatever Greyvane turns out to be, I'm not walking into it alone. That's the whole of what I actually needed to know before saying yes.*

He closed the log, and set it beside Hesk's leather-bound notebook, the two of them sitting together the way they always eventually did, and let the evening settle into the particular, unhurried quiet of a decision finally made and no longer requiring anything further from him except the simple, practical work of preparing to leave.

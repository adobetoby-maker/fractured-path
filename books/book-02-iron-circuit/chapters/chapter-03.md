# Chapter 3 — Vell's Rules

The dispute started the way most disputes at the Ironyard started — quietly, with someone unwilling to accept a result they didn't like, testing whether unwillingness alone might be enough to change it.

Cael had noticed him earlier that morning, before the trouble made itself obvious: a mid-tier practitioner, guild-adjacent by the cut of his training clothes, pacing the edge of the main floor with the restless energy of someone rehearsing an argument he hadn't yet decided how to deliver. He'd lost his bout the day before, cleanly by every account Cael had heard, and had spent the intervening hours apparently deciding that clean wasn't the same as fair.

Cael was in the audience, notebook open on his knee, when the man finally crossed to Vell's table with the over-formal posture of someone who'd rehearsed a grievance until it sounded, to his own ear at least, righteous.

"That result was compromised," he said, loud enough that the nearby benches quieted to listen. "I had coaching interference from the audience. Someone was calling adjustments to my opponent from the side."

Vell didn't look up from the ledger immediately. When she did, her expression carried the flat, unhurried patience of someone who had heard a version of this complaint many times before and had long since stopped finding it novel.

"Who was coaching."

"I don't know his name. Standing near the west post. Calling out stance corrections."

"That's Orvet. He does that for half the fighters who train out of his gym, whether they've paid him or not — it's a documented habit, on record in this ledger going back four years." She turned a page, unhurried, and tapped a line as if to prove the point though she didn't turn the book to show him. "It's not prohibited. Your complaint isn't that the rules were broken. Your complaint is that you lost."

"That's not remediable, in case you were unclear on the distinction."

Someone near the back muttered something Cael didn't catch, and a low ripple of quiet laughter moved through that corner of the benches before dying out under Vell's flat, unimpressed glance in its direction.

"I'm not unclear on anything. I've been doing this thirty years." She set the pen down, which Cael had learned, over months of watching her adjudicate, was itself a kind of signal — the moment she stopped performing patience and simply delivered the ruling plainly. "You lost to a better-prepared opponent, in front of forty witnesses, under terms you agreed to before the bout started. That's the record. That's what happened."

The practitioner's jaw tightened. "My training facility is guild-affiliated. I could make this a formal Compact inquiry."

A ripple moved through the nearby benches — not loud, just the specific attentive stillness of a crowd recognizing that the stakes of the conversation had just changed shape. Cael stopped writing and watched instead, because Vell's reaction to an actual threat was more interesting than anything he could have caught with a pen.

She looked at the man for a long moment. Not alarmed. Not even mildly rattled, as far as Cael could tell — just the stillness of someone who had absorbed this exact threat more times than she could probably count and had never once seen it produce a different outcome than the one she was about to describe.

"Do that," she said, "and you won't fight in any circuit from here to the coast. The keepers talk. We're a small enough community that a formal complaint against one of us tends to travel considerably faster than the complaint's actual merits." A pause, timed with the precision of long practice. "I'd think carefully about whether that's the reputation you want attached to your name before you walk out that door. Once it's attached, it doesn't come off easily. I've watched it happen to better fighters than you."

"You're threatening me."

"I'm informing you. There's a difference, even though I understand why it might not feel like one from where you're standing." She picked the pen back up, already turning her attention to the next entry in the ledger, the conversation apparently concluded as far as she was concerned. "The bout stands. If you want to fight again in this circuit, come back when you've decided losing honestly is something you can live with. Most people manage it eventually."

The practitioner opened his mouth, closed it again, and left without further argument — the deflated posture of someone who'd walked in with certainty and was walking out having recalculated, in real time, the actual cost of that certainty.

The benches loosened back into their ordinary noise within a few seconds, the whole confrontation absorbed into the day's texture as though it had barely happened at all. A pair of regulars near the west post picked their earlier conversation back up mid-sentence, unbothered, the way people do when a scene they've watched play out a dozen times before finally resolves in the direction they'd already assumed it would.

One of them — a broad-shouldered woman Cael recognized as a Bronze-equivalent fighter who trained most mornings in the alcove next to his own — caught his eye and gave a small, knowing shake of her head, as if to say *this again,* before turning back to her companion. It struck Cael, watching the whole room reabsorb the moment so completely, that the dispute hadn't actually been about Orvet's coaching at all, not really. It had been a test — the kind every new arrival to the circuit eventually ran, consciously or not, to find out exactly where the ledger's authority actually ended. Most people ran the test once, got their answer, and never bothered again. A few kept testing anyway, the way some fighters kept throwing the same losing combination because admitting it didn't work felt worse than losing again.

He thought about which category the departed practitioner would turn out to belong to. He suspected, without much confidence either way, that it could go either direction.

---

Cael watched the entire exchange with the close, cataloguing attention he brought to anything involving how authority actually functioned, as opposed to how it was formally supposed to function.

He waited until the crowd had thinned back to its usual rhythm before writing the entry, wanting the shape of it clear in his memory rather than half-formed and rushed.

*Circuit infrastructure — enforcement without formal authority. Vell has no legal standing the Compact would recognize. What she has instead: a network of Ledger-keepers who talk to each other, and a reputation economy that makes crossing that network genuinely expensive for anyone who depends on circuits to earn. The threat of a Compact inquiry didn't work because the actual cost of losing circuit access everywhere outweighs whatever a formal inquiry might theoretically produce. This only works because the people it protects take it seriously enough to enforce it themselves.*

He thought about it a while longer, turning the mechanism over the way he turned over an opponent's tell. It struck him as a smaller, informal cousin of the same principle Vell's whole ledger operated on — legitimacy that didn't ask the Compact's permission to exist, built instead on consistency, witnesses, and the accumulated weight of a record nobody outside the room had any power to rewrite.

He found Vell afterward, back at her table, the ledger closed now that the morning's business had quieted.

"That happens often?"

"Often enough. Guild-trained fighters sometimes come here expecting the same deference their credentials buy them elsewhere. Most of them adjust, once they understand the deference doesn't transfer." She glanced up at him, something dry in her expression. "You're taking notes on my adjudication style now?"

"I take notes on most things."

"I've noticed." She said this without particular alarm — the accepting tone of someone who'd decided, months ago, that Cael's habits were simply part of the fixture of having him around, no more remarkable than the way Dace always double-checked a schedule twice before posting it.

She folded her hands over the closed ledger, and her tone shifted, almost offhand, into something that carried more weight than the delivery let on.

"There have been more compliance sweeps through this district over the past six months than I remember in the previous ten years combined. Something out there has the Compact paying closer attention than usual."

Cael's expression didn't change. He already had a fairly clear idea of what — or who — had earned that attention, though he kept the thought to himself, filed alongside a growing list of things he understood the shape of without yet understanding the full mechanism underneath them.

"Interesting," he said, which was true, and also incomplete, in the same careful way most of his answers to questions about the Compact had become incomplete over the past year in Ardenmere.

Vell studied him for a moment longer than the answer strictly warranted, as if she suspected there was more behind it than he'd offered. She didn't press. She rarely did, when Cael's answers came back deliberately thin — she'd learned, apparently, that pressing rarely produced more than the thinness itself.

"Well," she said, finally. "Whatever's drawing their attention, it's not slowing down. Keep your registration current. Keep your address accurate. Don't give them an easy excuse to make your life more complicated than it already is."

"I always do."

"I know. That's why I'm not more worried about you than I am."

---

Lira had heard about the morning's dispute by the time he found her in the alcove for their afternoon session — circuit gossip moved fast, faster than the ledger itself sometimes, and a confrontation loud enough to quiet the benches was exactly the kind of story that outran the person who'd started it.

"Vell threatened him with the whole coastline," she said, grinning, resetting her stance opposite him. "I heard three different versions on the way over. In one of them she actually named the towns."

"She named the coast generally. Not the towns."

"You were there. You'd know." She threw the first combination without further warning, testing whether his attention had actually followed him back from the morning or was still somewhere in Vell's office. It hadn't; he read the opening cleanly and adjusted. She nodded, satisfied, and kept working. "What did you actually learn from it? You've got that look."

"That the whole thing works because everyone with something to lose has agreed it should work. No formal authority behind any of it. Just consequence, distributed evenly enough that nobody wants to be the one who tests it and finds out the consequence is real."

"That's basically how everything out here works, if you think about it long enough." She circled him, unhurried, waiting for an opening rather than forcing one. "The district. The market credit system. Us, honestly — you and me. None of it's official. All of it holds anyway, because everyone involved has decided it's worth more than whatever the alternative would be."

Cael turned that over mid-exchange, the observation landing with more weight than the casual delivery suggested it should. He said nothing about it yet, filing it instead the way he filed most things that arrived sideways, during training, when his attention was split enough that the real shape of an idea sometimes slipped past his usual caution before he'd decided how to guard it.

They trained through the rest of the afternoon without returning to the subject, though it sat with him longer than most of Lira's offhand observations tended to.

Somewhere in the middle of a combination he'd run a hundred times before, he found himself thinking about Hesk's workshop back in Denvash — the load calculations his grandfather double-checked before signing off on anything, the specific patience of a man who trusted a structure only once he'd tested every joint himself rather than taking someone else's word for its soundness. Vell's ledger operated on the same principle, he realized, just applied to people instead of instrument housings. Trust built joint by joint, tested under load, never assumed. He filed the comparison away without writing it down, the kind of thought that felt truer for staying private a little longer.

---

Later that same afternoon, she took him back into the archive on an unrelated errand — locating an old ledger volume for a different dispute entirely, one that had nothing to do with the morning's confrontation. Cael followed, notebook still tucked under one arm out of habit rather than any expectation he'd need it.

The archive smelled the way it always did: old paper, dust, the faint mineral tang of ink that had been mixed decades ago by hands long since retired or dead. Vell moved through the shelves with the ease of someone who could probably navigate them blind, pulling volumes by feel as much as by sight.

She mentioned it almost in passing, running a finger along a row of spines that grew progressively older and more weathered toward the shelf's far end.

"My records aren't all mine, you understand," she said. "I inherited some from the keeper before me. Some of those go back further than his own tenure did." She stopped at a section noticeably rougher than the rest — bindings cracked at the spine, ink faded to a brown that had once, unmistakably, been black. Her hand rested there, not quite pulling anything down. "The oldest ones use different words for things."

"Different how?"

She considered the question longer than its size seemed to warrant, her finger still resting against the cracked spine as though she were deciding, in real time, how much of an answer the moment actually deserved.

"I'd have to show you properly sometime," she said, at last. "It's not a quick explanation, and these pages don't take kindly to being handled without a reason worth the wear." She withdrew her hand and moved on down the shelf, toward the volume she'd actually come for, the conversation closing the way conversations with Vell sometimes closed — not dismissively, exactly, just with the unhurried pacing of someone who decided things would be shown when they were ready to be shown, and not a moment before.

Cael noted the phrase anyway, the way he noted everything that felt like it might matter later even when he couldn't yet say why.

*Different words for things. Ask again. Not yet.*

He watched her pull the volume she actually needed, watched her check its spine against some private catalogue she kept entirely in memory, and lingered a moment longer near the oldest section than the errand strictly required, studying the cracked bindings from a respectful distance without touching them.

"How old is the oldest one?" he asked.

Vell glanced back, the volume she'd retrieved already tucked under one arm. "Older than the Ironyard's current building. Older than the last building it replaced, if the stories about my predecessor's predecessor are even half accurate — and I've learned to trust that they're mostly accurate, because the people who told them to me had no reason to exaggerate a dead man's record-keeping habits." She considered the shelf for a moment, something almost fond crossing her weathered face. "Circuits like this one have existed in some form for longer than anyone bothers writing down properly. The venues move. The names change. The habit of keeping an honest record, independent of whoever happens to be running the official registry that particular decade — that part seems to survive every move."

"Does the Compact know these exist?"

"The Compact knows unsanctioned circuits exist, in the abstract. Whether any specific official has ever seen inside this particular archive is a different question, and one I've never had reason to test." She said this with the flat, unbothered practicality she brought to most subjects that would have made someone less settled visibly nervous. "I don't advertise it. I don't hide it either, particularly — anyone with legitimate reason to be back here eventually sees the shelf. I've just never had a Compact official with legitimate reason to be back here, and I intend to keep it that way as long as it's within my power to arrange."

"That seems like it could be dangerous to keep, if it ever did draw the wrong attention."

"Everything worth keeping is dangerous to keep, eventually. That's not a reason to stop keeping it. It's a reason to be careful about who you show it to." She looked at him for a moment, something evaluating in her gaze that reminded him, not for the first time, of the exact look she'd given him the day he'd first walked up to her registration table as an unrated fourteen-year-old with nothing to his name but a bag and a direction. "I showed you the tour today because I trust your judgment about what to do with things you don't fully understand yet. That's not a small thing, coming from me. I don't extend it casually."

Cael absorbed this the way he absorbed most things that mattered too much for an immediate response — quietly, filing the weight of it alongside the words themselves. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. I haven't shown you the oldest section properly. Today was the tour. The actual reading is a different, longer conversation, for whenever both of us have the time it deserves." She turned to leave the archive, the volume she'd actually come for settling more comfortably under her arm. "Soon, maybe. Not today."

---

They walked back out into the Ironyard's ordinary afternoon noise together, the archive's particular hush giving way to the familiar clatter of practitioners drilling in the side alcoves, a dispute-free bout running its unremarkable course on the main floor, the whole warehouse settling back into the rhythm Cael had come to think of, without quite noticing the transition, as simply the sound of a place he belonged.

Vell peeled off toward her table, ledger business waiting the way it always was. Cael lingered a moment at the archive's outer door, looking back at the shelf he could no longer quite see from this angle, turning over the shape of what he'd just been shown and what he hadn't.

*Different words for things.* He didn't yet know what those words were. He suspected, without evidence beyond the specific care Vell had taken in showing him even this much, that whatever they turned out to be would matter more than most things he'd learned in his first year in Ardenmere combined.

He wrote a final entry into the observation notebook before closing it for the day, brief and speculative in a way most of his entries deliberately weren't:

*Vell keeps records older than the current registry system itself. She trusts my judgment enough to show me the shelf exists, though not yet enough to show me what's actually written on the oldest pages. I don't know what that means yet. I know it's the kind of thread worth being patient about — the kind that unravels properly only if you don't yank it before it's ready.*

He closed the notebook and went to find Lira for the afternoon's training, the small, unresolved thread sitting quietly in the back of his attention the way most unresolved threads did in him now, waiting for the day he'd finally have reason to pull it.

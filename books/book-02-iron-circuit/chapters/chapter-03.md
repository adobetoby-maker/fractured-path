# Chapter 3 — Vell's Rules

The dispute started the way most disputes at the Ironyard started — quietly, with someone unwilling to accept a result they didn't like, testing whether unwillingness alone might be enough to change it.

Cael had noticed him earlier that morning, before the trouble made itself obvious: a mid-tier practitioner, guild-adjacent by the cut of his training clothes, pacing the edge of the main floor with the restless energy of someone rehearsing an argument he hadn't yet decided how to deliver. He'd lost his bout the day before, cleanly by every account Cael had heard, and had spent the intervening hours apparently deciding that clean wasn't the same as fair.

The rehearsal itself was legible, if you knew what rehearsal looked like — and Cael, who spent his working life reading the half-second before a committed strike, had found that arguments telegraphed the same way strikes did, only slower and with more vanity. The man's pacing had a fixed turn-point at either end, seven strides and pivot, seven strides and pivot, the closed loop of thought running on rails rather than actually thinking. His lips moved, fractionally, at the same point in each circuit — the good line, the one he was polishing. And twice, in the quarter hour Cael watched him, his hand went to the breast of his jacket and touched something there, flat and rectangular under the cloth: credentials, or a letter from his facility, some document he was carrying the way a nervous fighter carried a favorite guard, for reassurance rather than use. Cael logged the whole read in three lines and added a prediction, because predictions were how he kept his reads honest: *he'll wait until the floor is busy enough to give him witnesses, and he'll open with the strongest word he's rehearsed.* Then he found a seat with sightlines to Vell's table and waited to be marked right or wrong.

He was in the audience, notebook open on his knee, when the man finally crossed to Vell's table with the over-formal posture of someone who'd rehearsed a grievance until it sounded, to his own ear at least, righteous. The benches nearby were about half full — busy enough for witnesses. Cael put a small check beside the prediction.

"That result was compromised," he said, loud enough that the nearby benches quieted to listen. "I had coaching interference from the audience. Someone was calling adjustments to my opponent from the side."

*Compromised.* The strongest rehearsed word, delivered first. Second check.

Vell didn't look up from the ledger immediately. When she did, her expression carried the flat, patient stillness of someone who had heard a version of this complaint many times before and had long since stopped finding it novel.

"Who was coaching."

"I don't know his name. Standing near the west post. Calling out stance corrections."

"That's Orvet. He does that for half the fighters who train out of his gym, whether they've paid him or not — it's a documented habit, on record in this ledger going back four years." She turned a page, in no hurry at all, and tapped a line as if to prove the point though she didn't turn the book to show him. "It's not prohibited. Your complaint isn't that the rules were broken. Your complaint is that you lost."

Cael, watching, catalogued the adjudication itself as he would a technique, because that was what it was — a technique, refined over thirty years, executed in front of witnesses with the same economy a good fighter brought to a committed strike. Step one: the claim restated as a question of fact — *who was coaching* — stripping the grievance of its adjectives before it could build any height. Step two: the record consulted, physically, in view of the room — the page actually turned, the line actually tapped, the ledger's authority performed rather than merely invoked. He noted that she hadn't needed to look; she'd known it was Orvet from the words *west post* alone, and had checked the book anyway, because the checking was the point. The room needed to see that even she asked the ledger before answering. Step three, which she hadn't needed today but which he'd seen her use in a smaller dispute a month ago: the witnesses themselves, called by name, asked one question each, their answers spoken where everyone could hear. The whole procedure ran on the same discipline as his own three columns — claim, evidence, ruling, nothing promoted past what the record supported.

"That's not remediable, in case you were unclear on the distinction."

Someone near the back muttered something Cael didn't catch, and a low ripple of quiet laughter moved through that corner of the benches before dying out under Vell's flat, unimpressed glance in its direction.

"I'm not unclear on anything. I've been doing this thirty years." She set the pen down, which Cael had learned, over months of watching her adjudicate, was itself a kind of signal — the moment she stopped performing patience and simply delivered the ruling plainly. "You lost to a better-prepared opponent, in front of forty witnesses, under terms you agreed to before the bout started. That's the record. That's what happened."

The practitioner's jaw tightened. "My training facility is guild-affiliated. I could make this a formal Compact inquiry."

A ripple moved through the nearby benches — not loud, just the attentive stillness of a crowd recognizing that the stakes of the conversation had just changed shape. Cael stopped writing and watched instead, because Vell's reaction to an actual threat was more interesting than anything he could have caught with a pen.

She looked at the man for a long moment. Not alarmed. Not even mildly rattled, as far as Cael could tell — just the stillness of someone who had absorbed this exact threat more times than she could probably count and had never once seen it produce a different outcome than the one she was about to describe.

"Do that," she said, "and you won't fight in any circuit from here to the coast. The keepers talk. We're a small enough community that a formal complaint against one of us tends to travel a good deal faster than the complaint's actual merits." A pause, timed with the precision of long practice. "I'd think carefully about whether that's the reputation you want attached to your name before you walk out that door. Once it's attached, it doesn't come off easily. I've watched it happen to better fighters than you."

"You're threatening me."

"I'm informing you. There's a difference, even though I understand why it might not feel like one from where you're standing." She picked the pen back up, already turning her attention to the next entry in the ledger, the conversation apparently concluded as far as she was concerned. "The bout stands. If you want to fight again in this circuit, come back when you've decided losing honestly is something you can live with. Most people manage it eventually."

The practitioner opened his mouth, closed it again, and left without further argument — the deflated posture of someone who'd walked in with certainty and was walking out having recalculated, in real time, the actual cost of that certainty.

Cael ran the man's arithmetic for him, privately, because the arithmetic was the interesting part. A guild-affiliated training facility meant fees paid, a formal track maintained, a career that officially happened elsewhere — and yet the man was here, fighting for circuit purses, which meant the official track wasn't paying him enough in either coin or opponents. That was the pattern with most of the guild-adjacent fighters who passed through: the formal world gave them standing and starved them of practice, so they came to the one place that would feed them fights, and then some of them made the mistake of thinking the standing had come along in their luggage. A Compact inquiry might, at best, cost Vell an uncomfortable month. It would cost the complainant every circuit from here to the coast — his actual income, his actual development, the whole unofficial half of his life that the official half quietly depended on. Vell hadn't threatened him. She'd simply shown him his own balance sheet.

The benches loosened back into their ordinary noise within a few seconds, the whole confrontation absorbed into the day's texture as though it had barely happened at all. A pair of regulars near the west post picked their earlier conversation back up mid-sentence, unbothered, the way people do when a scene they've watched play out a dozen times before finally resolves in the direction they'd already assumed it would.

One of them — a broad-shouldered woman Cael recognized as a Bronze-equivalent fighter who trained most mornings in the alcove next to his own — caught his eye and gave a small, knowing shake of her head, as if to say *this again,* before turning back to her companion. It struck Cael, watching the whole room reabsorb the moment so completely, that the dispute hadn't actually been about Orvet's coaching at all, not really. It had been a test — the kind every new arrival to the circuit eventually ran, consciously or not, to find out exactly where the ledger's authority actually ended. Most people ran the test once, got their answer, and never bothered again. A few kept testing anyway, the way some fighters kept throwing the same losing combination because admitting it didn't work felt worse than losing again.

He thought about which category the departed practitioner would turn out to belong to. He suspected, without much confidence either way, that it could go either direction.

---

Cael watched the entire exchange with the close attention he brought to anything involving how authority actually functioned, as opposed to how it was formally supposed to function.

He waited until the crowd had thinned back to its usual rhythm before writing the entry, wanting the shape of it clear in his memory rather than half-formed and rushed.

*Circuit infrastructure — enforcement without formal authority. Vell has no legal standing the Compact would recognize. What she has instead: a network of Ledger-keepers who talk to each other, and a reputation economy that makes crossing that network genuinely expensive for anyone who depends on circuits to earn. The threat of a Compact inquiry didn't work because the actual cost of losing circuit access everywhere outweighs whatever a formal inquiry might theoretically produce. This only works because the people it protects take it seriously enough to enforce it themselves.*

*Adjudication procedure, as observed: (1) strip the claim to a question of fact; (2) consult the record physically, in front of witnesses, even when the answer is already known — the consulting is the authority; (3) if needed, witnesses by name, one question each, answered aloud; (4) ruling delivered plainly, pen down, no appeal offered because no appeal exists. Total elapsed time today: under four minutes. The speed is not carelessness. The speed is thirty years of having already done the slow version enough times to have earned the fast one.*

*Prediction record: called the timing (waited for witnesses) and the opening (strongest rehearsed word first). Missed the document he kept touching under his jacket — never produced. Either reassurance only, or a card he decided not to play. Filed: people carry their unplayed cards somewhere on their body, and their hands visit them under stress. Worth watching for in fighters too.*

He thought about it a while longer, working the mechanism the way he'd work an opponent's tell. It struck him as a smaller, informal cousin of the same principle Vell's whole ledger operated on — legitimacy that didn't ask the Compact's permission to exist, built instead on consistency, witnesses, and the accumulated weight of a record nobody outside the room had any power to rewrite.

He found Vell afterward, back at her table, the ledger closed now that the morning's business had quieted.

"That happens often?"

"Often enough. Guild-trained fighters sometimes come here expecting the same deference their credentials buy them elsewhere. Most of them adjust, once they understand the deference doesn't transfer." She glanced up at him, something dry in her expression. "You're taking notes on my adjudication style now?"

"I take notes on most things."

"I've noticed." She said this without alarm — the accepting tone of someone who'd decided, months ago, that Cael's habits were simply part of the fixture of having him around, no more remarkable than the way Dace always double-checked a schedule twice before posting it.

She folded her hands over the closed ledger, and her tone shifted, almost offhand, into something that carried more weight than the delivery let on.

"There have been more compliance sweeps through this district over the past six months than I remember in the previous ten years combined. Something out there has the Compact paying closer attention than usual."

Cael's expression didn't change. He already had a fairly clear idea of what — or who — had earned that attention, though he kept the thought to himself, filed alongside a growing list of things he understood the shape of without yet understanding the full mechanism underneath them.

He'd seen two of the sweeps himself, from the middle distance he preferred for watching officials work. They had a choreography as readable as any fighter's: always two officers, never one, entering the district in the slack mid-morning hours when the market was staffed but not crowded; always the same route inward, main street to the notice boards to the boarding-house row, because the route was written in a procedure somewhere and procedure didn't know the district had four better streets; always the credential cases carried in the left hand, leaving the writing hand free. The district's response had its own choreography, older and better rehearsed. Vendors didn't close — closing looked like guilt — but transactions slowed to exact-change purchases, nothing that required a ledger-book to come out under an official eye. Children evaporated from the sweep's path a street ahead of it, reappearing a street behind. And the Ironyard, both times, had gone quiet in a precisely calibrated fashion: training continued in the side alcoves, audible and innocent, while the main floor's schedule board — Cael had checked, both times — spent the sweep's duration wiped clean, rebuilt from Dace's memory within an hour of the officers clearing the district gate. Nothing hidden, exactly. Just nothing left lying where a procedure could trip over it.

"Interesting," he said, which was true, and also incomplete, in the same careful way most of his answers to questions about the Compact had become incomplete over the past year in Ardenmere.

Vell studied him for a moment longer than the answer strictly warranted, as if she suspected there was more behind it than he'd offered. She didn't press. She rarely did, when Cael's answers came back deliberately thin — she'd learned, apparently, that pressing rarely produced more than the thinness itself.

"Well," she said, finally. "Whatever's drawing their attention, it's not slowing down. Keep your registration current. Keep your address accurate. Don't give them an easy excuse to make your life more complicated than it already is."

"I always do."

"I know. That's why I'm not more worried about you than I am."

---

Lira had heard about the morning's dispute by the time he found her in the alcove for their afternoon session — circuit gossip moved fast, faster than the ledger itself sometimes, and a confrontation loud enough to quiet the benches was exactly the kind of story that outran the person who'd started it.

"Vell threatened him with the whole coastline," she said, grinning, resetting her stance opposite him. "I heard three different versions on the way over. In one of them she actually named the towns."

"She named the coast generally. Not the towns."

"You were there. You'd know." She threw the first combination without further warning, testing whether his attention had actually followed him back from the morning or was still somewhere in Vell's office. It hadn't; he read the opening cleanly and adjusted. She nodded, satisfied, and kept working. "What did you actually learn from it? You've got that look."

"That the whole thing works because everyone with something to lose has agreed it should work. No formal authority behind any of it. Just consequence, distributed evenly enough that nobody wants to be the one who tests it and finds out the consequence is real."

"That's basically how everything out here works, if you think about it long enough." She circled him, patient, waiting for an opening rather than forcing one. "The district. The market credit system. Us, honestly — you and me. None of it's official. All of it holds anyway, because everyone involved has decided it's worth more than whatever the alternative would be."

He let the point stand. It was correct, and arguing with correct things was a way of spending breath he had other plans for. Instead he stepped back out of her range and let his hands drop, and she stopped circling at once, because she read a change of agenda off his shoulders faster than anyone he had ever watched read anything.

"I want to run the landing," he said. "Properly this time."

She straightened out of her stance. "Same drill as the winter testing?"

"Same drill. Different terms." He rolled his shoulders, feeling where the session already sat in them. "Full intensity. Attack the landing. Every time. Don't pull off it because I look bad. I'm going to look bad. That's the data."

"You've been walking around this for a month."

"I know. I'm done walking."

She gave him the look she used when she was measuring the distance between the thing he wanted and the thing that was good for him, deciding whether the gap was worth a fight. The alcove was quiet around them — mid-afternoon, the main floor's noise reduced by two walls to a low tide of impact and voices, dust hanging in the light from the practitioner-lamps. Nobody watching. That was the other reason for today.

"How many?" she said.

"Ten. That's what I can afford."

The problem had a shape, and he had known the shape since winter without once testing its edges at full pressure. The Wind-adjacent burst did exactly what his log said it did: a short, committed displacement, faster than any footwork he owned, consistent, reliable under load. And then it billed him. The instant a deployment ended, his body locked — half a breath, no more, no less — and for that half-breath he could not move, could not turn, could not shift an ounce of weight from one foot to the other. What he could do, the whole time, was perceive. Sight, hearing, balance-sense, prediction — all of it ran clean through the lock, cleaner than usual if anything, the way a held breath sharpens a room. Perfect information, zero capacity. He had written that phrase in the log in winter and then spent a month not finding out what it was worth.

Trial one, she was moving before the burst finished spending itself, and her strike arrived in the middle of the lock like a scheduled delivery. He watched it the whole way in. He saw her weight gather, saw the line, knew the exact point below his collarbone it would find, and stood there — a fencepost with excellent eyesight — while it found it.

Trials two through four settled the question of angle. He varied the burst's exit line each time; she read the endpoint regardless and came in low, then crossing, then from behind his leading shoulder. Four deployments. Four landings. Four clean hits, each one watched in full from inside the lock, each one unanswerable. The impacts stung and faded. The pattern didn't.

Claim: the lock has a seam. Evidence: four trials, four angles, full commitment. Ruling: no seam. That was the baseline, and the baseline was the easy part.

Trial five he tried the first real idea. Spend the landing early — cut the displacement short, take the lock sooner, be through it and free before her strike finished traveling. The truncated burst dropped him short of the line she had committed to, and her strike sheared through the air where a full deployment would have put him. No contact. Also no victory. He hadn't beaten her strike; the geometry had, once, by surprising them both. And the price was already visible: he had unlocked a full step nearer to her than the drill had ever put him.

Trial six, she recalibrated for the shorter arrival and hit him harder than any of the first four, because there was less distance for anything to decay across.

Trial seven he cut the burst deeper. The lock came sooner, ended sooner, and it did not matter, because her strike had less ground to cover than the time he'd bought. It took him across the hip, and unlike the others, that one stayed.

Trial eight he cut deeper still, arrived so short and so awkward that her committed line missed him entirely — and he stumbled coming out of the unlock, off-balance, worthless, while the trade curve finished drawing itself in his head. Every sliver of recovery he bought cost him distance, and distance was the thing keeping her strikes survivable.

Trial nine he over-spent. The burst died with his weight already going sideways, and the lock held him in a falling shape the way it held him in every shape: totally. She pulled the strike. Not kindness — hitting a man who was already going down proved nothing, and she was here for proof; it was his rule she was honoring, not his ribs. He went down onto one knee on the worn stone and stayed there, breath gone somewhere it would take a while to come back from, hip announcing itself with every heartbeat.

Nine bursts inside an hour. Call it three nights of ordinary use, spent in one afternoon, and his body was invoicing him in real time — the particular hollowed-out weight behind the sternum that the log described, politely, as *depletion*.

Lira crouched in front of him, forearms on her knees, not touching him. "One left. We can bank it."

"No."

"Then tell me what it's for. Because the last five were you paying to make it worse."

She was right, and being right, she had handed him the finding. Five trials of trying to shorten the beat had established that the beat did not shorten — it only relocated, and every relocation was bought with distance he needed more. He had been asking the lock the wrong question all month. *How do I get out of you* had one answer, tested nine ways now: you don't. The question he hadn't asked, because it sounded like surrender: *what are you worth from the inside?* Half a breath in which nothing — not fear, not footwork, not the option of flinching — could interrupt his attention. He'd been treating the stillness as the wound. He had never once treated it as the instrument.

"Last one," he said, getting up. "Don't change anything."

"Wasn't going to."

Trial ten. Full displacement, no cleverness, the burst spent to its natural end. He landed. The lock took him.

And he gave it everything. No part of the half-breath went to wanting out. Her strike was already committed to the fixed point where he stood, and for the first time he read it whole: the load gathering through her back foot, the hips turning over it, the shoulder rolling into line, the hand — her right, traveling for his left shoulder, arriving at an angle he could price exactly. Affordable. He paid it forward in his head, accepted the cost before it arrived, and spent every remaining grain of the stillness building the answer: her arm would be extended, her weight committed past her lead foot, her recovery a known quantity because he had watched her recover from full commitment four hundred times.

The counter was moving before his body was. That was the strangest part, the thing he'd want the log to get exactly right — the decision complete, the line mapped, everything loaded and waiting, so that when the lock released, it released *into* motion instead of ahead of it. Her strike took his shoulder, full intensity, an honest hit that turned him half a step. His hand arrived on her overextended arm in almost the same instant, caught the recovery she hadn't started yet, and torqued. They ended in a graceless knot — her strike spent, his counter tangled in her balance, both of them fighting for the same square foot of floor and neither getting it clean.

A Ledger-keeper, had one been in the alcove, which one was not, would have scored it an exchange. Not a hit for her. Not a hit for him. An exchange — bought with a shoulder he could already feel stiffening.

They came apart. Lira stepped back, flexing the arm he'd caught, and looked at him with an expression that had several things in it, not all of them exasperation. "That was different."

"Yes."

"Tell me what you did."

"Nothing." He worked the shoulder, cataloguing the hit's exact weight. "That's the finding. I stopped doing things. The lock isn't a flaw. Flaws train out — five trials say this doesn't. It's a fact, like the length of my arms. So it stops going in the log as a defect and goes in as a price." He could see the entry already, the shape it would take tonight in his own hand. "And a price implies a purchase. Half a breath where nothing can pull my attention — not reflex, not fear, nothing, because there's nothing to decide. One committed strike, read completely, start to finish. If I can afford the hit, I come out of the lock with the whole answer already moving."

"And if you can't afford the hit?"

"Then nothing I read matters, because I'm not standing up to use it." He said it flatly, because it deserved flatness. "Against one strike I can price in advance — a trade. Against anything heavy enough to end the exchange outright — a donation. So, rule, from today: I don't spend the burst close to anyone whose one hit I can't price before I pay it. Ever. That's the whole discipline."

Lira considered him for a long moment. "Ten bursts and seven hits to learn to stand still."

"Six hits," he said, "and an exchange."

"I'll let the stillness argue with your hip tomorrow." She said it lightly, but her eyes had already done the arithmetic on what ten bursts meant, and tomorrow was in there too — he knew from winter what the day after this kind of spending felt like, and the collecting wouldn't be finished by morning. She jerked her chin toward the center of the alcove. "On your feet properly. Combinations, ordinary speed. You've spent enough of the expensive thing today — the cheap thing still needs work."

He got up, set his feet, and went back to the cheap thing.

Somewhere in the middle of a combination he'd run a hundred times before, he found himself thinking about Hesk's workshop back in Denvash — the load calculations his grandfather double-checked before signing off on anything, the patience of a man who trusted a structure only once he'd tested every joint himself rather than taking someone else's word for its soundness. Vell's ledger operated on the same principle, he realized, just applied to people instead of instrument housings. Trust built joint by joint, tested under load, never assumed. He filed the comparison away without writing it down, the kind of thought that felt truer for staying private a little longer.

---

Later that same afternoon, she took him back into the archive on an unrelated errand — locating an old ledger volume for a different dispute entirely, one that had nothing to do with the morning's confrontation. Cael followed, notebook still tucked under one arm out of habit rather than any expectation he'd need it.

The archive smelled as it always did: old paper, dust, the faint mineral tang of ink that had been mixed decades ago by hands long since retired or dead. Vell moved through the shelves with the ease of someone who could probably navigate them blind, pulling volumes by feel as much as by sight.

She mentioned it almost in passing, running a finger along a row of spines that grew progressively older and more weathered toward the shelf's far end.

"My records aren't all mine, you understand," she said. "I inherited some from the keeper before me. Some of those go back further than his own tenure did." She stopped at a section noticeably rougher than the rest — bindings cracked at the spine, ink faded to a brown that had once, unmistakably, been black. Her hand rested there, not quite pulling anything down. "The oldest ones use different words for things."

"Different how?"

The question sat between them longer than its size seemed to warrant, her finger still resting against the cracked spine as though she were deciding, in real time, how much of an answer the moment actually deserved.

"I'd have to show you properly sometime," she said, at last. "It's not a quick explanation, and these pages don't take kindly to being handled without a reason worth the wear." She withdrew her hand and moved on down the shelf, toward the volume she'd actually come for, the conversation closing the way conversations with Vell sometimes closed — not dismissively, exactly, just with the measured pacing of someone who decided things would be shown when they were ready to be shown, and not a moment before.

Cael noted the phrase anyway, the way he noted everything that felt like it might matter later even when he couldn't yet say why.

*Different words for things. Ask again. Not yet.*

He watched her pull the volume she actually needed, watched her check its spine against some private catalogue she kept entirely in memory, and lingered a moment longer near the oldest section than the errand strictly required, studying the cracked bindings from a respectful distance without touching them. Even the physical objects told a story, read carefully. The old volumes weren't uniform like Vell's thirty-eight — they varied in height and thickness, bound in whatever leather their decade had afforded, and two of the oldest had been rebound at some point, newer spines grafted onto older bodies, which meant someone between then and now had judged their contents worth the cost of a binder when a binder cost real money. Records survived by being carried, and carrying was a choice, made fresh by each keeper in turn. Somebody had chosen these, every time, for longer than the Compact's current registry had existed.

"How old is the oldest one?" he asked.

Vell glanced back, the volume she'd retrieved already tucked under one arm. "Older than the Ironyard's current building. Older than the last building it replaced, if the stories about my predecessor's predecessor are even half accurate — and I've learned to trust that they're mostly accurate, because the people who told them to me had no reason to exaggerate a dead man's record-keeping habits." She considered the shelf for a moment, something almost fond crossing her weathered face. "Circuits like this one have existed in some form for longer than anyone bothers writing down properly. The venues move. The names change. The habit of keeping an honest record, independent of whoever happens to be running the official registry that particular decade — that part seems to survive every move."

"Does the Compact know these exist?"

"The Compact knows unsanctioned circuits exist, in the abstract. Whether any specific official has ever seen inside this particular archive is a different question, and one I've never had reason to test." She said this with the flat, unbothered practicality she brought to most subjects that would have made someone less settled visibly nervous. "I don't advertise it. I don't hide it either, particularly — anyone with legitimate reason to be back here eventually sees the shelf. I've just never had a Compact official with legitimate reason to be back here, and I intend to keep it that way as long as it's within my power to arrange."

"That seems like it could be dangerous to keep, if it ever did draw the wrong attention."

"Everything worth keeping is dangerous to keep, eventually. That's not a reason to stop keeping it. It's a reason to be careful about who you show it to." She looked at him for a moment, something evaluating in her gaze that reminded him, not for the first time, of the exact look she'd given him the day he'd first walked up to her registration table as an unrated fourteen-year-old with nothing to his name but a bag and a direction. "I showed you the tour today because I trust your judgment about what to do with things you don't fully understand yet. That's not a small thing, coming from me. I don't extend it casually."

Cael took it in quietly, the weight of it filed alongside the words themselves. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. I haven't shown you the oldest section properly. Today was the tour. The actual reading is a different, longer conversation, for whenever both of us have the time it deserves." She turned to leave the archive, the volume she'd actually come for settling more comfortably under her arm. "Soon, maybe. Not today."

---

They walked back out into the Ironyard's ordinary afternoon noise together, the archive's hush giving way to the familiar clatter of practitioners drilling in the side alcoves, a dispute-free bout running its unremarkable course on the main floor, the whole warehouse settling back into the rhythm Cael had come to think of, without quite noticing the transition, as simply the sound of a place he belonged.

Vell peeled off toward her table, ledger business waiting as it always was. Cael lingered a moment at the archive's outer door, looking back at the shelf he could no longer quite see from this angle, turning over the shape of what he'd just been shown and what he hadn't.

*Different words for things.* He didn't yet know what those words were. He suspected, without evidence beyond the care Vell had taken in showing him even this much, that whatever they turned out to be would matter more than most things he'd learned in his first year in Ardenmere combined.

He wrote a final entry into the observation notebook before closing it for the day, brief and speculative in a way most of his entries deliberately weren't:

*Vell keeps records older than the current registry system itself. She trusts my judgment enough to show me the shelf exists, though not yet enough to show me what's actually written on the oldest pages. I don't know what that means yet. I know it's the kind of thread worth being patient about — the kind that unravels properly only if you don't yank it before it's ready.*

*Also today: the landing beat is a fact, not a habit. Ten trials say so. Reclassified as a trade instead of a flaw. My body paid nine bursts for that sentence and will be collecting on the debt through tomorrow. Fair price.*

He closed the notebook and went to find Lira for the evening meal, the small, unresolved thread sitting quietly in the back of his attention the way most unresolved threads did in him now, waiting for the day he'd finally have reason to pull it.

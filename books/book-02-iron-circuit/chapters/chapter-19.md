# Chapter 19 — Two Weeks

Brom found the first practitioner within a day — a Bronze-tier washout named Ansel who trained two streets from the boarding house and who went visibly quiet the moment Reydan's name came up, the guarded reluctance of someone revisiting a loss he'd rather have left alone.

"Three years ago," Ansel said, when Brom finally coaxed the story out of him over a meal neither of them finished. "I was better then than I am now, if that tells you anything about how it went. I thought I understood burst compression. I'd fought two other Pressure practitioners that season, both sustained-wave, both the kind you can see building if you're paying attention." He shook his head slowly. "Reydan doesn't build. He just fires. I lasted two exchanges. I'm not ashamed of that anymore, but I was for a long time."

"What actually beat you?"

"Everything. Nothing specific. He read me completely inside of ninety seconds and there wasn't a single thing I did in that fight he hadn't already accounted for before I did it." Ansel's expression, telling it, still carried the weight of an old defeat that had never quite finished teaching its lesson. "Don't try to out-think him early. He's already three moves past whatever you're planning. Survive first. Think second."

The second account arrived four days later — not fresh correspondence, but an existing report a contact of Dace's had already filed away years before and simply had to dig back out, a written account, several pages, dense with the exacting detail of someone who'd studied Reydan professionally rather than simply survived him once. Brom read it twice before handing it to Cael, and Cael read it three times, extracting what he could into the observation notebook in his own compressed shorthand.

Both accounts aligned on the essentials, different as their sources were: Reydan's burst compression had an extremely short wind-up, barely perceptible even to a trained eye. He adapted between exchanges faster than most opponents could track, discarding a failed approach the instant it failed rather than trying it twice out of stubbornness. And he never fought the same way twice against the same person — every account of a rematch, and there were a few, described a completely different fighter each time, as though he kept a private catalogue of every opponent's counters and simply declined to offer the same counter a second chance to work.

He was dangerous, the correspondence account concluded, not simply because of raw power, but because of intelligence applied ruthlessly, exchange after exchange, until an opponent's strategy — however good it had started out — simply ran out of new ideas before he did.

"That's not a comfortable combination," Lira said, reading through Brom's compiled notes at the boarding house table that evening, the lamp between them throwing long shadows across two weeks' worth of gathered intelligence.

"No," Cael agreed. "It's not."

"Ansel's advice is good, though. Survive first, think second. That's basically been your whole method since Weaver's Row, just phrased more bluntly than you'd phrase it yourself."

"I'd have gotten there eventually."

"You'd have gotten there in about four more paragraphs of careful reasoning. He said it in one sentence because he actually lived it." She said this without unkindness, the respect she reserved for people who'd earned a piece of wisdom the expensive way rather than the careful way. "Use it anyway. Good advice doesn't care how it arrived."

Brom, reading the correspondence account for a third time that evening, added his own observation without looking up. "There's something else in here worth noting. The correspondent watched Reydan lose exactly once, two years ago, to an opponent nobody expected to trouble him. Not a stronger fighter. A patient one — someone who simply refused to be rushed, who let Reydan's own aggression run out of new material before committing to anything decisive." He tapped the page. "He beat that opponent in the rematch three months later. But it happened once. It's not nothing."

"How'd the opponent do it?"

"The account doesn't say, exactly. Just that they 'declined to be interesting' for most of the fight, and Reydan apparently found that more disorienting than any counter-attack would have been." Brom set the pages down, considering. "He's used to opponents trying to solve him. Someone who simply waits him out is a different kind of problem."

"That's not really my method, though. I solve people. That's the whole log."

"I know. I'm not saying abandon it. I'm saying there might be a version of patience inside your method that you haven't fully used yet — watching without needing to act on what you see immediately, the way you did against Dessa a year ago, before you had fragments to spend." Brom looked at him directly. "You've gotten faster since then. Faster isn't always better against someone whose whole method depends on opponents needing to act quickly."

Cael turned this over for a long while after Brom had gone to bed that night, turning the observation over the way he turned over anything that arrived sideways rather than head-on. He wasn't sure, in the end, whether it changed anything concrete about the plan he'd already built. But he wrote it into the log anyway, in the tactical section rather than the private one, because it felt like exactly the kind of thing worth having available even if he didn't yet know how he'd use it.

---

Lira's contribution to the two weeks was exhausting by design.

She drilled him in evasion architecture calibrated for burst attacks — full-speed Wind Path evasion, the best available counter to fast compression that she knew of, and she ran the drills at an intensity Cael hadn't seen from her since the earliest, most desperate weeks after she'd decided to stop winning cleanly. Every session opened the same way: her stance dropping into the fast, low readiness that meant she wasn't going to give him a warning beat before the first attack came, because Reydan certainly wouldn't either. She threw combinations at him faster than he could consciously track, again and again, until the exact angle of evasion he needed stopped living in his head as a plan and started living in his legs as something closer to reflex — the kind of instinct that didn't need to pass through conscious decision before his body acted on it.

"Again," she said, after he'd caught a hit he should have avoided, no sympathy in the word at all. "You're thinking about it. You don't have time to think about it. Reydan's whole method is built around opponents who need one more half-second to decide, and you don't get that half-second against him. You get this." She demonstrated the correct reaction herself, fast enough that Cael only caught the shape of it after the fact. "Again."

She did not spare him. He came away from every session with new bruises layered over the old ones — a bad week for his forearms, a worse one for his ribs — and neither of them mentioned it as anything other than the necessary cost of preparation adequate to the actual danger. Once, catching him favoring one side after a particularly hard session, she softened just slightly, just for a moment, pressing a hand briefly against his shoulder. "I know it's a lot," she said. "I'm not doing this because I don't care what it costs you. I'm doing it because I'd rather you hurt now, with me, than hurt worse later, with him, when I'm not there to catch you." Then the moment passed, and she reset her stance, and the drilling continued.

The drilling had a texture that his log entries, however careful, kept failing to capture. By the fourth day his calves burned climbing the boarding-house stairs, an ordinary muscle exhaustion he knew how to spend. By the sixth, the fatigue had moved somewhere less familiar — into the small stabilizing muscles along his ankles and hips that the burst-calibrated footwork loaded in ways ordinary evasion never had, so that he woke stiff in joints he'd never previously had opinions about. The pattern itself changed residence somewhere in that stretch. He could feel the exact evening it happened: a drill he'd been failing all afternoon, the drop-and-roll against Lira's fastest angle, and then, without any conscious adjustment, his body performed it while his mind was still assembling the instruction. He stood there afterward, breathing hard, mildly offended at his own legs for not consulting him. Lira read his expression and laughed, once — the short, satisfied laugh she almost never let out during working hours. "There it is," she said. "Now we do it four hundred more times, so it's still there when you're exhausted and frightened." They did it four hundred more times.

Brom's contribution ran differently — he used his own Iron Skin pressure-read to give Cael information about how burst-compression signatures felt from the outside, the closest approximation available without an actual burst-compression practitioner to spar against directly. It wasn't a perfect substitute, and both of them knew it wasn't; Iron Skin's own signature was slower, heavier, fundamentally built around a different principle than Pressure's rapid contraction-and-release. But Brom did what he could, simulating the containment-then-release rhythm as closely as his own Path allowed, session after session, refining the approximation each time based on what the written correspondence account had described.

"It compresses inward before it fires outward," Brom told him, demonstrating as best his own Path allowed — a visible tightening across his forearm, held for a beat, then released. "The inward compression is a tell, if you can read it in time. The problem is the time. From what Ansel and the written account both describe, you're talking about a fraction of a second between the tell starting and the burst actually firing. Barely enough to register, let alone react to."

The second half of Brom's afternoons went to something with a higher price on it. If the read failed — and both of them assumed that against a real burst it sometimes would — Cael needed an answer for the hit he couldn't avoid, and the answer they settled on was the Pressure-adjacent fragment turned to a use he'd never put it to in a real bout: redirection. Not blocking, which against compression was merely a slower way of taking the hit. Redirect — plant the weight, take the force through hip and shoulder at a deliberate angle, let it pass through the frame instead of stopping inside it, and spend the stolen momentum on closing distance.

They drilled it perhaps a dozen times across the two weeks, and a dozen was all Cael's body would sell them. The redirect worked — that was never the question. The question was the price, which was unlike anything the Wind fragment had ever charged him. Each full-force attempt sent the impact through muscle and bone in a single hard shock, a toll paid in the shoulder and the ribs and somewhere behind the sternum, and by the third repetition in any session his form degraded enough that a fourth would have taught his body the technique wrong. Three a session. That was the budget. He wrote the arithmetic into the log the first evening, because it was exactly the kind of fact that mattered most when it would be least convenient to remember: *Pressure-adjacent redirect: converts an unavoidable hit into position. Cost: real, physical, cumulative — assume three clean uses in any fight, fewer if already damaged. This is not an evasion. It is a purchase. Know what I'm buying before I pay.*

"The good news," Brom observed, watching him work his shoulder loose after a session's third and final repetition, "is that anyone you use this on is having a worse day than you are."

"The bad news?"

"You only get about three people's worth of worse days. Choose them carefully."

The preparation had a shape to it beyond just training, too — a texture Cael only fully noticed in retrospect, once the two weeks were nearly spent. Vell asked after his readiness twice, briefly, in the understated way she asked after anything she genuinely cared about without wanting to make a production of the caring. Dace mentioned, once, almost offhandedly, that word of the upcoming match had already reached two circuits in neighboring districts, which meant the Ironyard's own reputation was now tangled up with however the fight actually went, whether either of them had asked for that or not. Even the boarding-house keeper had started leaving an extra portion at dinner on the nights Cael came back from training too depleted to properly ask for one.

None of it was said directly. All of it added up to the same unmistakable fact: the whole small, real world he'd built in Ardenmere over the past year had quietly decided this fight mattered, and was doing what it could, in whatever small ways were available to it, to make sure he arrived at it as ready as fourteen days could make him.

The fragment itself came up on the third afternoon, between simulated bursts, when Brom paused mid-demonstration and looked at him with the expression of a man who'd been waiting to be told something.

"I have a partial pressure-read of my own now. From the fragment."

"I know. Is it functional?"

"It's developing."

"Then develop it faster. You have two weeks, and from everything I'm reading about him, two weeks might not be enough regardless of how hard we work." He said this without discouragement, simply stating the honest shape of the problem the way he stated most things. "We work with what we have. That's the only version of this that's ever actually available to anyone."

So they developed it. The pressure-read became the third front of the preparation, and the one where the two weeks produced the most measurable movement. Against Keth, the fragment had returned clean signal roughly half the time — a coin toss he'd been careful never to lean his weight on. Brom, once he understood the failure rate, treated it as a training problem rather than a fixed fact, and built him a progression: read at rest, read in motion, read while defending, read while exhausted, each stage run until the static cleared. The fragment answered use the way the Wind fragment had answered use a year earlier, which was itself worth an entry — the borrowed architecture didn't simply sit in him at whatever depth it arrived; it deepened along whatever seam he worked. By the eleventh day the failure rate at close range had fallen to perhaps one pulse in five, and the cost per pulse had thinned alongside it, the temple-ache arriving later in a session and leaving sooner. It would never be Brom's read. It didn't need to be. It needed to catch one containment signature, once, at the moment it mattered.

What the two weeks couldn't manufacture was the thing his method had always run on: a file. He reread the Reydan pages of the observation notebook on the twelfth evening and confronted, honestly, how little they weighed — two secondhand accounts, one written report about fights already discarded, a handful of impressions from watching the man watch other people. Against Keth he'd walked in owning four months of architecture. Against Reydan he would walk in owning a tell, a tendency, and a warning. Which meant the compound gaze would have to do in minutes, under fire, what it had always been given the luxury of doing across weeks from a bench: build the file during the fight itself, exchange by exchange, while the subject of the file was actively trying to end him. He'd never run the method at that speed with stakes attached. There was no way to rehearse it. He wrote the plan's honest shape into the log without decorating it: *Enter with less than I've ever entered with. Observe faster than I've ever observed. Every exchange is reconnaissance until it suddenly isn't. The method still works. It just has to work at his tempo now, not mine.*

---

By the ninth day, the preparation had settled into its own grinding rhythm — mornings with Lira, afternoons with Brom, evenings spent reviewing the correspondence account line by line until Cael could recite whole passages of it from memory. He was tired in the accumulated way that came from nine straight days of a body being asked to do more than it had ever been asked to do before, and he'd stopped, somewhere around day six, expecting any single session to feel like a breakthrough. Progress had become incremental. Small, hard-won inches.

Day nine's session was meant to be ordinary — another round of Brom simulating the containment-then-release rhythm as closely as Iron Skin allowed, another round of Cael trying to read it half a beat earlier than he'd managed the day before. They'd run maybe a dozen exchanges already, nothing remarkable in any of them, the familiar grind of two people testing the same small margin over and over in search of some fractional improvement.

Then Brom fired the thirteenth simulated burst, and something happened that neither of them had planned, and that neither of them would fully understand even afterward.

Cael caught it.

Not with evasion — he was too close, too committed to the exchange's rhythm by then for a clean evasion to have worked. Not with absorption, not with anything the Pressure fragment had ever done before, not with anything he recognized as belonging to his three confirmed entries at all. It arrived instead as something that felt, in the half-second it was happening, like reading and responding in the exact same motion — no gap between perceiving Brom's pressure signature and answering it, no translation step where information became decision became action. The response simply was, the moment the signature was, and it used what it read to dissipate the compression before it had finished committing to its own shape, the way you might catch a falling cup before your conscious mind had finished registering that it had slipped.

It worked. The simulated burst simply wasn't there anymore by the time it should have landed — not blocked, not evaded, dissolved somehow in the space between Brom's intention and its arrival.

What stayed with Cael afterward, longer than the fact of it, was how it had felt: not like effort at all. Every fragment he owned had a cost he could name by now — the Wind step's faint subtraction, the read's slow ache, the redirect's toll through the shoulder. This had cost nothing he could find, and that frightened him more than any price would have. Things that charged nothing up front, Hesk had taught him young, were generally things that billed later, in a currency you hadn't agreed to.

Then, before either of them had said a word, Cael tried to do it again.

Nothing came.

He reset his stance, called for another exchange, and tried to recreate the exact conditions as closely as he could remember them — the same distance, the same rhythm, the same half-committed readiness he thought he'd been holding the moment before it happened. Brom fired another simulated burst. Cael reached for whatever he'd reached for the first time.

Nothing.

He tried a second time. A third. Each attempt felt, from the inside, like reaching for a word that had been on the tip of his tongue a moment ago and had since slipped somewhere he couldn't follow it. The door that had opened once, cleanly, for less than a second, had simply closed itself again, and nothing he did in the following several minutes came anywhere close to reopening it.

They stopped. Both of them, breathing hard, staring at each other across the training floor.

"What was that?" Brom asked.

"I don't know. What did you read?"

Brom considered the question with real care, the same weighing stillness he brought to anything he wasn't willing to answer carelessly. "I don't know either. Not fully." He sat down on the training floor rather than continuing to stand over the question, an unspoken signal that this was going to take longer than a quick exchange. "It felt like the pressure-read, but more. Faster. Like I was reading and responding in the same motion, without the gap between them that's usually there — the gap that's usually there for everyone, not just for you. I don't get to skip that gap. I've never gotten to skip it, not with any opponent, not in any Path I've ever trained against." He paused, working through it further, choosing his next words with visible care. "You haven't integrated a Tide Path."

"No." Cael said this with complete confidence — the Power Log's three entries, Wind, Pressure, Iron, and now nothing new beyond an unreproducible anomaly, were the entire honest catalogue of what he had. He knew the shape of each one intimately. Nothing in that catalogue matched what had just happened.

"What I read in that exchange was Tide-adjacent." Brom said it plainly, without embellishment, the way he delivered most information that mattered. "I don't have a category for Tide Path signatures the way I do for the others — I've never fought a Tide practitioner closely enough to build a real read on one. But I've heard the general shape described, secondhand, by someone who has. Fluid. Responsive rather than forceful. Reading and answering as close to simultaneously as a Path can manage." He looked at Cael steadily. "That's what I read. For less than a second. Then it was gone, and you were just you again."

"Could you be wrong?"

"Yes. I could be entirely wrong. I'm working from secondhand description of a Path I've never personally studied, applied to a signature I've never encountered from anyone but you, generated by architecture I already told you I don't have a category for." He said this without any defensiveness, simply laying out the honest limits of his own certainty. "I'm not telling you what it was. I'm telling you what it read like to me, in the moment, with the tools I actually have. Those aren't the same claim."

Cael turned this over, appreciating, not for the first time, Brom's discipline about the difference between an observation and a conclusion — a discipline that matched, closely enough to feel like recognition, the same distinction his own log had been built around from its very first entry.

They tried a few more times before giving up for the day — not out of any real hope of reproducing it, but because stopping immediately after a genuine unknown felt, to both of them, like conceding something they weren't ready to concede. Nothing came. The training floor gave back nothing but the ordinary, familiar shape of Iron Skin's simulated bursts, no trace of whatever had briefly opened and closed nine days into their preparation.

---

Cael wrote it into the Power Log that evening, careful and precise, the entry occupying more space than any single fragment had before it — more careful, if anything, because this one refused to resolve into a clean confirmation the way the others eventually had.

*Session 9, exchange 13: unidentified response. Not a known fragment — doesn't match Wind, Pressure, or Iron in shape or timing. Tide-adjacent signature per Brom's read, though he's honest that his own basis for the read is secondhand and limited. Could not reproduce despite multiple immediate attempts under matching conditions.*

*Hypothesis one: new integration in progress, not yet complete enough to summon deliberately. If true, this would be the first time a fragment has announced itself before formally confirming — every previous one arrived as a discrete notice, named and dated. This would be different: a preview, not an acquisition.*

*Hypothesis two: an existing fragment expressing a function I hadn't previously discovered, surfacing only under a narrow combination of pressure and proximity that hasn't recurred since. If true, the question isn't what's new — it's what I've been missing in fragments I thought I already understood completely.*

*Hypothesis three, the one I like least and am recording anyway because the log doesn't get to only hold the comfortable possibilities: that this isn't about a fragment at all. That it's something closer to the architecture itself — whatever the Fractured Path actually is, underneath the individual borrowed pieces — showing a glimpse of its own shape rather than showing me someone else's ability filtered through mine.*

*Evidence insufficient to choose between the three. Filed as anomaly. Monitor.*

He read the entry back twice before closing the book, and found, underneath the familiar discomfort of an unanswered question, something that had become, over the past year, a strange and durable comfort: the anomalies kept arriving, and he kept surviving them, and each one taught him something even when it refused to fully explain itself. He didn't know what session nine's thirteenth exchange actually was. He suspected, closing the log for the night, that he wouldn't know for a long time yet — possibly not until long after Reydan, long after whatever came next had already happened and receded into the same kind of settled history that Weaver's Row had become.

He'd learned, by now, to be patient with that particular kind of not-knowing. It was, he was beginning to understand, simply the ordinary condition of being whatever he actually was.

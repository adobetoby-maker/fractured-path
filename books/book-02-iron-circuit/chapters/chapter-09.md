# Chapter 9 — Preparation

Iron Skin Path documentation, Cael discovered within his first day of trying to research it properly, was frustratingly proprietary.

He started where he started most things: the district's public reading rooms, the ones tucked behind the market that most people forgot existed until they needed something the vendors couldn't sell them. There were two of them, technically — a front room with six tables and a stove that the attendant fed twice a day whether the season required it or not, and a back room, colder, where the reference volumes lived under the theory that cold air was kinder to bindings than warm readers were. The attendant charged nothing to sit and a half-copper to take a volume off the reserve shelf, an economy Cael had worked out months ago: the half-copper wasn't for the book, it was for the attendant's memory of your face, which determined whether you'd be allowed near the reserve shelf a second time.

He pulled every general reference volume that so much as mentioned surface hardness enhancement, and found, across three separate books and an afternoon of increasingly irritated cross-referencing, almost nothing he didn't already know. Iron Skin Path existed. It provided surface hardness enhancement. It fell within the defensive specialist branch of formal classification, filed alongside a dozen other Paths that shared the same broad shape without sharing anything about how any of them actually worked in a fighter's hands. One volume devoted four pages to the Path's guild history — which cohorts trained it, which families were associated with it, a genealogy of institutional ownership — and exactly one sentence to its function. Cael copied the sentence anyway, dated it, and noted beside it: *four pages on who owns the knowledge, one sentence on the knowledge. That ratio is the whole registry system in miniature.*

That was the entire extent of what the public archives had to offer. He tried a second reading room, further from the market, on the theory that a less-trafficked collection might have older or more detailed material. It didn't. He tried asking two circuit veterans who'd mentioned, in passing over the preceding months, having fought Iron Skin opponents before. The first, a heavyset Stone Path fighter named Corrin who'd lost to an Iron Skin practitioner in another city years ago, gave him something almost useful: "It's not the hit that gets you. It's that your own hit comes back wrong. You throw your best strike and your wrist reports something your eyes didn't see coming — like punching water that decided halfway through to be a wall." The second veteran mostly wanted to complain about the loss. Both gave him fragments of secondhand impression, a sense of what it felt like to be on the receiving end of the redirect, but nothing systematic, nothing he could build a hypothesis on with any confidence.

The practitioner-level knowledge that would have actually helped him — the kind that explained not just what the Path did but how one body learned to do it — lived somewhere he couldn't reach at all: inside the guild cohorts that trained Iron Skin practitioners directly, institutions with no reason whatsoever to share their methods with an unclassified circuit fighter doing research out of a public reading room two districts from anywhere they'd ever bothered to send a representative.

He gave up on the archives by the second evening. The watching had started regardless — mornings, from the first day, the reading rooms filling only the afternoons — and now it had his whole attention: the thing he always did when the written record ran dry, watching in person, for as long and as carefully as the subject would tolerate.

---

Brom, to his mild surprise, didn't seem to mind being watched at all.

He trained in the Ironyard's side alcoves at the same hour most mornings, visible to anyone present, with the unconcerned openness of someone who'd already worked out the trade for himself and decided it favored him: whatever Cael learned from watching, Brom stood to learn something equally useful about Cael's own reactions in return, simply by being observed observing.

Cael attended every session for the full two weeks leading up to the fight. He arrived early enough on the first morning to claim a spot against the alcove's back wall, notebook open on his knee, and settled into the patient attention he'd once brought to Renn's telegraphed strikes and Dessa's transition windows.

By the end of the first hour, he understood he had a methodological problem.

The compound gaze, as he'd built it, was a system for watching people who initiated. Every subject he'd studied seriously — Lira's Wind sequences, Feryn's Pressure builds, Keth's declaration chains from the audience, a year's worth of circuit fighters filed by their tells — had given him the same fundamental material to work with: the interval between intention and action. A striker decided, and the decision leaked. Weight shifted onto the driving foot a quarter-beat early. Breath caught at the top of the inhale instead of releasing through the strike. A shoulder dropped, a jaw set, a declaration's priming phase produced its small physical signature before the committed phase produced its large one. His whole notation system was organized around that leak. Three columns: *tell, interval, action.* Watch long enough and the columns filled themselves.

Brom initiated almost nothing. He stood, and things happened to him, and he answered them. The columns stayed empty.

Cael spent most of the first session writing observations that, reread that evening, told him nothing — *stance neutral, breathing even, no visible priming* repeated in a dozen small variations, the notational equivalent of staring at a closed door and recording that it remained closed. It was the first time in a year that the method itself had failed him rather than merely demanding patience, and he sat with the failure longer than was comfortable before he found the correction.

The correction, when it arrived, was almost embarrassingly simple: a defensive subject didn't leak intention, because a defensive subject's intentions were reactions. What a defensive subject leaked was *latency* — the interval on the other side of the event. Not the gap between deciding and acting, but the gap between receiving and answering. If Brom's Iron Skin read incoming force and answered it, then somewhere between the arrival of a strike and the arrival of its redirect, there was a measurable interval, and intervals could be timed, and timed intervals produced patterns, and patterns were the only raw material his method had ever actually needed.

He rebuilt the notation that night. Three new columns: *stimulus, latency, response.* He had no clock, so he timed the way he'd taught himself to time everything in the circuit, against his own resting pulse — a beat and a half between a training partner's strike landing on Brom's forearm and the partner staggering wide off the redirect. A beat flat when the strike came faster. Half a beat, sometimes less, when the strike arrived at an angle Brom had seen twice already that session. The latency shortened with familiarity. That single fact filled half a page of notes on its own, because it meant the read wasn't a fixed reflex — it was learning, inside each session, at a rate Cael could roughly graph.

The second thing he learned to track was breath. Brom's breathing, watched across a full session, ran level in a manner that Cael initially took for simple conditioning until he noticed the exceptions: a fractional deepening of the inhale, held perhaps a half-beat longer than the rhythm called for, that preceded the hardening by just enough to be visible if you already knew to watch for it. Not a tell in the striker's sense — Brom wasn't deciding to harden, exactly. It read more like the breath of a man settling his weight before catching something heavy. Cael logged eleven instances across two sessions before he trusted it: *inhale-hold precedes contact-hardening, roughly one half-beat. Not conscious. Consistent.*

The third was weight. Brom stood with more load on his heels than any striker Cael had ever charted — sixty percent, maybe more, an allocation that would have been a defect in a fighter who needed to launch and was instead, Cael slowly understood, the platform the whole technique stood on. Force redirected through a hardened surface had to go somewhere, and it went down, through the heels, into the floor. The stone in Brom's preferred alcove corner was visibly smoother than the stone around it. Some of that was decades of other fighters. Some of it, Cael suspected, watching Brom reset onto his heels for the two-hundredth time, was two months of one fighter doing exactly this.

The first few sessions taught him almost nothing beyond confirming what the registry summary had already told him. It was the fourth session before he noticed the detail that actually mattered — the thing that separated Brom from whatever standard training had produced the registry's flat, unhelpful description.

Iron Skin, at its formal base, was a defensive Path. Brom didn't use it defensively.

By hardening chosen contact points at the precise moment of strike impact — not before, not after, but exactly as his fist or forearm met resistance — he made his own hits carry compounded force. The hardened surface redirected his own momentum back through the point of contact at an angle a body braced for a simple strike rarely anticipated. Cael watched him do it against a padded post for the better part of an hour on the sixth session, logging the timing window strike by strike, and understood, slowly, that he was watching something closer to invention than technique.

It wasn't standard. He was certain of that now, certain enough to write it down as fact rather than hypothesis: this was something Brom had developed on his own, independent of whatever formal instruction his family's estate had paid for, in the two years of solitary circuit work between Velmere and Ardenmere. It went a long way toward explaining, in Cael's estimation, why a family expecting textbook Gold-tier progression had found Copper-tier Iron Skin so difficult to properly evaluate in the first place. Brom hadn't been under-trained. He'd been differently trained — by himself, in a direction none of his instructors had ever thought to build an evaluation for.

He wrote the observation into the notebook that evening, restructured now in the architecture-first format Brom's own comment had prompted weeks before — there was a certain circularity, he'd noted, in using the man's advice to study the man himself:

*Iron Skin — defensive base, offensive application. Amplification triggers at the moment of contact, not before. This is not standard technique. This is a fighter who built his own method rather than inheriting one, and it shows in how confidently he deploys something no manual would have taught him to deploy this way.*

The sessions that followed added detail rather than new categories, each one refining the shape of something he'd already begun to understand rather than overturning it. On the seventh session he noticed that the hardening didn't apply evenly — Brom favored his lead forearm and the outer edge of his lead foot, the two points he clearly trusted most to find contact cleanly, and left everything else at something closer to baseline resilience. Cael charted it as a coverage map, the same format he used for evasion seams: two zones of full commitment, a broad band of partial coverage across the chest and shoulders, and — interesting enough to underline — almost nothing allocated below the knee. Either the Path couldn't cheaply reach that far from wherever Brom's attention centered, or Brom had simply never fought anyone who made him pay for the gap. Both possibilities went in the notebook. On the ninth, he timed the recovery window between one hardened strike and the next: not instantaneous, a small but real gap, brief enough that no opponent slower than Cael himself would likely ever find it, but a gap nonetheless.

He tested a rough version of the transition-window idea on the tenth session, carefully, without telling Brom what he was actually probing for. He varied his own approach speed twice in quick succession against the padded post Brom was working, close enough that Brom noticed the odd rhythm and glanced over, one eyebrow raised.

"You're not sparring."

"Watching your reset."

"You could just ask what my reset looks like."

"I could. I'd rather see it than be told about it." Cael said this without apology, and Brom, after a moment's consideration, seemed to find the answer acceptable enough to simply shrug and continue, unbothered by being studied for a purpose he hadn't been given the details of yet.

The early test told him little on its own — a single fighter working a stationary target didn't produce the same conditions a live, adversarial exchange would, and Cael was careful, even this early, not to let a clean-room result convince him of more than it had actually earned. But it confirmed the reset gap existed at all, which was itself useful: a real phenomenon, not simply a hopeful pattern he'd talked himself into seeing.

*Confirmed against a static target,* he wrote that night. *Needs confirmation under live exchange pressure before I trust it in an actual bout. Static conditions are a poor substitute for a fighter who's actually trying to hit me back. I know this. I'm noting it anyway, because it's exactly the kind of caution I'm most likely to forget once the fight itself starts and the plan feels more certain than the evidence actually supports.*

The live confirmation came sooner than he expected. On the eleventh session, watching Brom spar a visiting Bronze-tier fighter for the first and only time that fortnight, he saw the technique finally pushed hard enough to show its actual limits — three consecutive hardened strikes in rapid succession left Brom's guard measurably slower to reset on the fourth, a fatigue signature that ten days of solo training against a padded post had never once revealed. The static-target gap was real. Under real pressure, it widened. Cael timed the widened version at two full pulse-beats and noted, beside the number, the question the number raised: *what does the hardening cost him? Nothing is free. If three fast activations slow the fourth, the Path draws on something finite, and finite things can be spent down. Find out what the account looks like when it's empty.*

He filed each observation as its own line in the notebook, dated, cross-referenced against the growing central hypothesis, until by the twelfth session the entry had grown from a single paragraph into something closer to a working manual — the exact document, he understood with some private satisfaction, that the guild cohorts training actual Iron Skin practitioners almost certainly kept for themselves and had no reason to publish anywhere he could reach.

---

The other half of the preparation was harder, because the other half was about himself, and the news there was not good.

He laid it out on the boarding house table one evening the way he laid out any problem that resisted being held entirely in his head: the Power Log open to both entries, a loose page beside it for the working, Lira's spare whetstone weighting the corner against the draft from the window.

Two fragments. Against Brom, both of them ran into trouble.

Wind-adjacent was the older of the two, the more thoroughly tested, the one he trusted furthest under pressure. Its function had been stable for months: an evasion framework, short-burst repositioning, a step that arrived faster than his legs alone could produce and put him somewhere an opponent's committed strike no longer reached. He knew its price the way he knew the price of bread. Each burst drew hard on his legs — a deep, immediate spending from the calves and thighs, nothing borrowed, everything paid at the moment of use — and pulled a sharp draw of breath with it, so that three bursts inside a single exchange left him breathing like a man who'd sprinted a street, and a fourth came out measurably shorter than the first. Beyond that lay the tell Lira had found and he was still training out: a small drop of the left shoulder, a half-beat before the step, that anyone who'd fought enough Wind practitioners would eventually learn to read. He'd shrunk it over a month of drills from obvious to subtle. Subtle was not gone.

Against most opponents, the ledger balanced easily: the cost of the burst was almost always smaller than the cost of being hit. Against Brom, the arithmetic collapsed, because Brom did not chase. An evasion framework earned its keep by making committed attacks miss, and Brom committed to almost nothing. Cael could spend his legs dancing around a man who was content to stand on his heels and wait, and at the end of it Brom would have paid nothing and Cael would have paid for every step. Wind-adjacent, against this opponent, wasn't a weapon. It was, at best, insurance — worth carrying, ruinous to rely on.

Pressure-adjacent was worse, and it took him an evening of honest accounting to admit how much worse. The fragment kept two ledgers of its own — the receiving half, the slow dispersal he'd charted through the winter testing, which won nothing from an opponent who dealt no force until he chose to; and the delivery half, the one that mattered in most fights. That half's gain was force: an amplification laid into a strike at the moment of delivery, weight arriving from somewhere his mass alone couldn't have supplied. Its costs he had characterized over six patient months. It drew from somewhere deeper than the legs — the core, the breath's foundation, a spending he felt afterward as a hollowness under the ribs rather than an ache in any muscle. It demanded rhythm: deployed on-beat, integrated into a strike's natural timing, it flowed; deployed off-beat, forced into a strike that hadn't earned it, it misfired, and a misfire meant the amplification discharging partly back up his own arm — he'd sprained his wrist that way twice in the early months, and still felt the second sprain in cold weather. And it recovered slowly. Two amplified strikes in an exchange was sustainable. Three was a gamble. He had never landed four without the fourth being noticeably weaker than an unassisted hit, which defeated the entire point of the thing.

All of which was manageable, had been managed, in a year of bouts against fighters who received force the ordinary way. Brom did not receive force the ordinary way. Brom's entire Path was a machine for accepting delivered force and returning it with interest, and Pressure-adjacent was a machine for delivering more force. Every amplified strike Cael landed on a hardened surface would hand Brom a larger sum to redirect. He would be paying out of his own core to increase the size of the payment coming back at him.

He wrote the conclusion in the log because writing it made it harder to talk himself out of later:

*Against Brom: Wind-adjacent is insurance, not offense — he doesn't commit, so there's nothing to make miss. Pressure-adjacent is actively counterproductive at any hardened contact point — amplification feeds the redirect. The two abilities I've spent a year building are, respectively, nearly useless and worse than useless against this opponent. What remains: timing, positioning, the latency map, and the transition-window hypothesis. This fight will be won or lost with the notebook, not the fragments. That is either a problem or the most useful test conditions I've ever been handed. Probably both.*

He trained with Lira through the same two weeks, mornings, and the training had the flavor of the conclusion. She'd stopped drilling him on the seam in his evasion coverage — closed now to her satisfaction, or close enough that exploiting it cost her more than it won her — and shifted to something crueler and more useful: making him fight without leaning on either fragment at all. She'd stand in front of him, absorb rather than evade, refuse to chase, a rough imitation of Brom's shape built out of her own tactical understanding, and make him find answers with nothing but feet and hands and the reading of her.

"You keep reaching for the step," she said, the third morning, after catching him in the shoulder mid-burst for the fourth time. "I can see you decide not to use it. The deciding costs you a half-beat. Against me that's a bruise. Against him it's the exchange."

"I know."

"Then stop deciding. Leave it home. You can't half-carry a thing into a fight." She reset, breathing hard herself — the sessions were not gentle in either direction. "You told me once that information from a loss isn't a loss. Fine. Here's some information from your last four: you've gotten so used to having the fragments that you've stopped fully trusting the fighter underneath them. He was good enough to survive this district for months before the first one ever arrived. Go find him again."

He wrote that down too, that evening, in the log's margin, where the things went that were about him rather than about opponents.

---

Lira watched one of Brom's sessions from over Cael's shoulder, roughly a week in, arms crossed, studying the technique with her own considerable tactical eye rather than simply taking Cael's account of it secondhand.

"He's going to absorb everything you throw at him and return it," she said, after a long, silent stretch of watching.

"I know."

"So what are you going to do?"

Cael considered the question with real care before answering. "I'm going to find out if he has a transition window. A moment between defensive and offensive activation where his read is incomplete — where the amplification hasn't fully engaged yet, or where the shift from absorbing to redirecting leaves some small gap I can actually use."

"What if he doesn't have one?"

"Then I'll know that too. Still useful." He said this plainly, without performance, the same principle that had carried him through a year of losses that had taught him more than most of his wins ever had. Information extracted from a loss was not, by his own accounting, a loss at all — it was simply a different kind of currency, one he'd learned to collect as reliably as coin.

Lira studied him for a moment, something considering in her expression. "You're not actually planning to win this."

"I'm planning to learn everything I can. Winning would be a pleasant surprise on top of that, not the actual goal." He said it honestly, and she seemed to accept the honesty, nodding once before returning her attention to Brom's next combination.

That evening, he wrote the hypothesis into the Power Log, methodical and precise, the entry occupying most of a page by the time he'd finished:

*Iron Skin Path — external pressure read, internal force redirect. Activation appears continuous, with a selective amplification mode triggered exactly at contact. Hypothesis: rapid alternation between defensive and offensive activation should create a transition window, if one exists — a moment where the read hasn't fully committed to either mode and can be caught between them. If the window exists, it will most likely appear in the third exchange, the point where a fighter is most settled into an established pattern and least braced for genuine disruption to it. Two exchanges of testing, one exchange of exploitation, is the working plan. Testing Tuesday.*

The two weeks passed in the compressed intensity Cael had come to recognize as the shape his preparation took whenever an opponent genuinely mattered — mornings with Lira learning to fight without his own best tools, afternoons watching Brom, evenings converting the day's observations into structured hypotheses the Power Log could actually use.

Around him, the district ran its own quiet arithmetic on the fight. He heard the odds twice without seeking them — once at the bread stall, where two regulars debated whether an Assessed-Copper rating meant anything at all when the fighter attached to it kept beating Iron-equivalent names, and once inside the Ironyard itself, where the informal book that Dace officially knew nothing about had settled, according to the man running it, at figures unflattering enough that Cael declined to hear the exact numbers. The circuit's economics interested him more than the odds did. A bout like this one filled the main floor, and a full main floor meant the vendors outside sold out early, and the boarding houses nearest the Ironyard filled their spare cots with out-of-district watchers, and some fraction of every coin that changed hands traced back, eventually, to two fighters agreeing to let a room full of strangers watch them solve each other. Vell recorded the results. The district lived, partly, off the recording.

He fought two smaller circuit bouts in the interim, both wins, both handled with the efficiency of someone whose real attention was elsewhere — quick, clean, the kind of result that barely registered against the much larger question occupying most of his actual thinking. The first gave him one thing worth keeping: a moment in the second exchange where he deployed the Wind-adjacent step off a read rather than a reaction, moving before his opponent's strike had fully formed instead of after, and felt the difference in cost — the burst spent from balance rather than from urgency drew noticeably less from his legs. He logged it as the first solid evidence that the fragment's price wasn't fixed; it varied with how early the decision was made. Cheaper when informed. Everything, apparently, was cheaper when informed.

He didn't mention the Brom fight to anyone beyond Lira and, eventually, Vell, who took the news with her usual flat, measuring calm, closing her ledger halfway through his explanation as though the rest of the day's business could wait.

"Brom's good," she said, when he told her. "Genuinely good. Not just Iron-equivalent on paper — good in the way that doesn't always show up in a rating. I've watched him fight twice since he arrived. Both times I came away thinking the number attached to his name undersold him."

"I know. I've been watching him for two weeks."

"You're not worried."

Cael weighed this honestly, as he weighed anything that deserved an accurate answer rather than a reassuring one. "I'm interested. I don't think those are the same feeling, but they're close enough that I understand why you'd ask."

"They're not the same feeling. Interested people ask good questions afterward. Worried people just want it to be over." She studied him for a moment, something almost like approval in her weathered expression — the look she reserved, he'd learned over the year, for the rare occasions someone answered her honestly instead of with whatever they thought she wanted to hear. "Good luck, then. Or good information. Whichever you actually end up needing more."

"Is there a version where I get both?"

"There's always a version where you get both. There's just no guarantee it's this one." She reopened the ledger, the conversation apparently concluded on her end, though she added, almost as an afterthought, without looking up: "Whatever happens Tuesday, the ledger will record it accurately. That's the only promise I can actually make you."

He thanked her, and went back to watching, and preparing, and testing the hypothesis against everything he'd charted one more time. By the second week's end, he had filled four pages of careful notes on a technique no manual anywhere had ever bothered to record — latency tables, the breath signature, the heel-load platform, the coverage map with its unexplained gap below the knee, the fatigue account that three fast activations could visibly draw down. He had also filled one page, harder-won, on himself: two fragments whose honest ledgers said *insurance* and *liability* for the fight ahead, and a fighter underneath them who would have to be enough.

He understood, closing the notebook as the second week ran down, that whatever happened in the circle, the two weeks themselves had already been worth having.

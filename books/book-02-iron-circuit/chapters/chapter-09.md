# Chapter 9 — Preparation

Iron Skin Path documentation, Cael discovered within his first day of trying to research it properly, was frustratingly proprietary.

He started where he started most things: the district's public reading rooms, the ones tucked behind the market that most people forgot existed until they needed something the vendors couldn't sell them. He pulled every general reference volume that so much as mentioned surface hardness enhancement, and found, across three separate books and an afternoon of increasingly irritated cross-referencing, almost nothing he didn't already know. Iron Skin Path existed. It provided surface hardness enhancement. It fell within the defensive specialist branch of formal classification, filed alongside a dozen other Paths that shared the same broad shape without sharing anything about how any of them actually worked in a fighter's hands.

That was the entire extent of what the public archives had to offer. He tried a second reading room, further from the market, on the theory that a less-trafficked collection might have older or more detailed material. It didn't. He tried asking two circuit veterans who'd mentioned, in passing over the preceding months, having fought Iron Skin opponents before — both gave him useful fragments of secondhand impression, a sense of what it felt like to be hit by the redirect, but nothing systematic, nothing he could build a hypothesis on with any confidence.

The practitioner-level knowledge that would have actually helped him — the kind that explained not just what the Path did but how a specific body learned to do it — lived somewhere he couldn't reach at all: inside the guild cohorts that trained Iron Skin practitioners directly, institutions with no reason whatsoever to share their methods with an unclassified circuit fighter doing research out of a public reading room two districts from anywhere they'd ever bothered to send a representative.

He gave up on the archives by the second evening and did what he always eventually did when the written record ran dry: he went to go watch, in person, for as long and as carefully as the subject would tolerate.

---

Brom, to his mild surprise, didn't seem to mind being watched at all.

He trained in the Ironyard's side alcoves at the same hour most mornings, visible to anyone present, with the unconcerned openness of someone who'd already worked out the trade for himself and decided it favored him: whatever Cael learned from watching, Brom stood to learn something equally useful about Cael's own reactions in return, simply by being observed observing.

Cael attended every session for the full two weeks leading up to the fight. He arrived early enough on the first morning to claim a spot against the alcove's back wall, notebook open on his knee, and settled into the same patient, unhurried attention he'd once brought to Renn's telegraphed strikes and Dessa's transition windows — except Brom, he understood within the first ten minutes, was going to require considerably more patience than either of them had.

The first few sessions taught him almost nothing beyond confirming what the registry summary had already told him: surface hardness, activated and released in a rhythm Brom controlled with obvious, practiced ease. It was the fourth session before Cael noticed the detail that actually mattered — the thing that separated Brom from whatever standard training had produced the registry's flat, unhelpful description.

Iron Skin, at its formal base, was a defensive Path. Brom didn't use it defensively.

By hardening specific contact points at the precise moment of strike impact — not before, not after, but exactly as his fist or forearm met resistance — he made his own hits carry compounded force. The hardened surface redirected his own momentum back through the point of contact at an angle a body braced for a simple strike rarely anticipated. Cael watched him do it against a padded post for the better part of an hour on the sixth session, cataloguing the exact timing window, and understood, slowly, that he was watching something closer to invention than technique.

It wasn't standard. He was certain of that now, certain enough to write it down as fact rather than hypothesis: this was something Brom had developed on his own, independent of whatever formal instruction his family's estate had paid for, in the two years of solitary circuit work between Velmere and Ardenmere. It went a long way toward explaining, in Cael's estimation, why a family expecting textbook Gold-tier progression had found Copper-tier Iron Skin so difficult to properly evaluate in the first place. Brom hadn't been under-trained. He'd been differently trained — by himself, in a direction none of his instructors had ever thought to build an evaluation for.

He wrote the observation into the notebook that evening, restructured now in the architecture-first format Brom's own comment had prompted weeks before — there was a certain circularity, he'd noted, in using the man's advice to study the man himself:

*Iron Skin — defensive base, offensive application. Amplification triggers at the moment of contact, not before. This is not standard technique. This is a fighter who built his own method rather than inheriting one, and it shows in how confidently he deploys something no manual would have taught him to deploy this way.*

The sessions that followed added detail rather than new categories, each one refining the shape of something he'd already begun to understand rather than overturning it. On the seventh session he noticed that the hardening didn't apply evenly — Brom favored his lead forearm and the outer edge of his lead foot, the two points he clearly trusted most to find contact cleanly, and left everything else at something closer to baseline resilience. On the ninth, he timed the recovery window between one hardened strike and the next: not instantaneous, a small but real gap, brief enough that no opponent slower than Cael himself would likely ever find it, but a gap nonetheless.

He tested a rough version of the transition-window idea on the tenth session, carefully, without telling Brom what he was actually probing for. He varied his own approach speed twice in quick succession against the padded post Brom was working, close enough that Brom noticed the odd rhythm and glanced over, one eyebrow raised.

"You're not sparring."

"Watching your reset."

"You could just ask what my reset looks like."

"I could. I'd rather see it than be told about it." Cael said this without apology, and Brom, after a moment's consideration, seemed to find the answer acceptable enough to simply shrug and continue, unbothered by being studied for a purpose he hadn't been given the details of yet.

The early test told him little on its own — a single fighter working a stationary target didn't produce the same conditions a live, adversarial exchange would, and Cael was careful, even this early, not to let a clean-room result convince him of more than it had actually earned. But it confirmed the reset gap existed at all, which was itself useful: a real phenomenon, not simply a hopeful pattern he'd talked himself into seeing.

*Confirmed against a static target,* he wrote that night. *Needs confirmation under live exchange pressure before I trust it in an actual bout. Static conditions are a poor substitute for a fighter who's actually trying to hit me back. I know this. I'm noting it anyway, because it's exactly the kind of caution I'm most likely to forget once the fight itself starts and the plan feels more certain than the evidence actually supports.*

The live confirmation came sooner than he expected. On the eleventh session, watching Brom spar a visiting Bronze-tier fighter for the first and only time that fortnight, he saw the technique finally pushed hard enough to show its actual limits — three consecutive hardened strikes in rapid succession left Brom's guard measurably slower to reset on the fourth, a fatigue signature that ten days of solo training against a padded post had never once revealed. The static-target gap was real. Under real pressure, it widened.

He filed each observation as its own line in the notebook, dated, cross-referenced against the growing central hypothesis, until by the twelfth session the entry had grown from a single paragraph into something closer to a working manual — the exact document, he understood with some private satisfaction, that the guild cohorts training actual Iron Skin practitioners almost certainly kept for themselves and had no reason to publish anywhere he could reach.

---

Lira watched one session from over Cael's shoulder, roughly a week in, arms crossed, studying Brom's technique with her own considerable tactical eye rather than simply taking Cael's account of it secondhand.

"He's going to absorb everything you throw at him and return it," she said, after a long, silent stretch of watching.

"I know."

"So what are you going to do?"

Cael considered the question with real care, turning over four days' worth of accumulated observation before answering. "I'm going to find out if he has a transition window. A moment between defensive and offensive activation where his read is incomplete — where the amplification hasn't fully engaged yet, or where the shift from absorbing to redirecting leaves some small gap I can actually use."

"What if he doesn't have one?"

"Then I'll know that too. Still useful." He said this plainly, without performance, the same principle that had carried him through a year of losses that had taught him more than most of his wins ever had. Information extracted from a loss was not, by his own accounting, a loss at all — it was simply a different kind of currency, one he'd learned to collect as reliably as coin.

Lira studied him for a moment, something considering in her expression. "You're not actually planning to win this."

"I'm planning to learn everything I can. Winning would be a pleasant surprise on top of that, not the actual goal." He said it honestly, and she seemed to accept the honesty, nodding once before returning her attention to Brom's next combination.

That evening, he wrote the hypothesis into the Power Log, methodical and precise, the entry occupying most of a page by the time he'd finished:

*Iron Skin Path — external pressure read, internal force redirect. Activation appears continuous, with a selective amplification mode triggered exactly at contact. Hypothesis: rapid alternation between defensive and offensive activation should create a transition window, if one exists — a moment where the read hasn't fully committed to either mode and can be caught between them. If the window exists, it will most likely appear in the third exchange, the point where a fighter is most settled into an established pattern and least braced for genuine disruption to it. Two exchanges of testing, one exchange of exploitation, is the working plan. Testing Tuesday.*

The two weeks passed in the compressed intensity Cael had come to recognize as the shape his preparation took whenever an opponent genuinely mattered — mornings with Lira refining his own technique against the seam she'd identified weeks before, afternoons watching Brom, evenings converting the day's observations into structured hypotheses the Power Log could actually use.

He fought two smaller circuit bouts in the interim, both wins, both handled with the efficiency of someone whose real attention was elsewhere — quick, clean, the kind of result that barely registered against the much larger question occupying most of his actual thinking. He didn't mention the Brom fight to anyone beyond Lira and, eventually, Vell, who took the news with her usual flat, measuring calm, closing her ledger halfway through his explanation as though the rest of the day's business could wait.

"Brom's good," she said, when he told her. "Genuinely good. Not just Iron-equivalent on paper — good in the way that doesn't always show up in a rating. I've watched him fight twice since he arrived. Both times I came away thinking the number attached to his name undersold him."

"I know. I've been watching him for two weeks."

"You're not worried."

Cael considered this honestly, turning the question over the way he turned over most things that deserved an accurate answer rather than a reassuring one. "I'm interested. I don't think those are the same feeling, but they're close enough that I understand why you'd ask."

"They're not the same feeling. Interested people ask good questions afterward. Worried people just want it to be over." She studied him for a moment, something almost like approval in her weathered expression — the specific look she reserved, he'd learned over the year, for the rare occasions someone answered her honestly instead of with whatever they thought she wanted to hear. "Good luck, then. Or good information. Whichever you actually end up needing more."

"Is there a version where I get both?"

"There's always a version where you get both. There's just no guarantee it's this one." She reopened the ledger, the conversation apparently concluded on her end, though she added, almost as an afterthought, without looking up: "Whatever happens Tuesday, the ledger will record it accurately. That's the only promise I can actually make you."

He thanked her, and went back to watching, and preparing, and turning the transition-window hypothesis over one more time before the day finally arrived to test it properly. By the second week's end, he had filled four pages of careful notes on a technique no manual anywhere had ever bothered to record, and he understood, closing the notebook on the final evening before the fight, that whatever happened in the circle tomorrow, the two weeks themselves had already been worth having.

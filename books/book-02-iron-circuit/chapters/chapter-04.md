# Chapter 4 — Two Notebooks

He wrote the preamble on a Sunday morning, before training, when the boarding house was quiet enough that the words came in the order he actually wanted them.

Sunday mornings had a texture in Ardenmere that Cael had come to value without ever quite deciding to — the market two streets over didn't open until later, the Ironyard's side alcoves stayed empty until closer to midday, and the boarding house itself settled into the kind of hush that let a person hear their own thinking clearly for once. The building's sounds simplified down to their skeleton: the landlady's kettle at first light, one floor down and two rooms over; the practitioner in the corner room running through her breathing exercises, a slow four-count he could set his own pulse by; Lira's door staying shut a full hour longer than any other day of the week, the single luxury she permitted herself. He'd taken to using the hour for exactly this sort of work: the unglamorous, structural labor of organizing what he knew so that it would still be useful to him in six months, rather than just true today and forgotten by winter.

The observation notebook sat open on the table beside the slim volume he'd kept separate for the fragments since not long after Fragment 2 confirmed itself — and even so, the line between the two had been blurring for months. An opponent's tell, three lines down in the observation notebook. Beneath it, with no separation at all, a private note about the Wind-adjacent step's clumsiness under deliberate summons — the kind of line that belonged in the fragment volume, not buried here where it cluttered a page that was supposed to be about someone else's footwork entirely. Beneath that, a tactical read on a Copper-tier Blade practitioner's footwork, itself half-obscured by a stray question about deployment range he'd scrawled in the margin without thinking. He'd been sloppy about the boundary between the two books almost since he'd started keeping them apart, private notes drifting into the observation notebook whenever he was mid-thought and reached for whichever page happened to be open. It had taken him embarrassingly long to notice that the drift was making both categories worse — the tactical entries cluttered with private uncertainty that didn't belong there, the fragment entries themselves half-buried in opponent data whenever he'd made the opposite mistake.

*The observation notebook records other people's abilities — what I see them do, the physical shape of their declaration architecture, the tells that precede each phase, the gaps I identify. The fragment log records what I myself have — what fragments are confirmed, what each does under different conditions, what questions remain unanswered.*

*They were supposed to be separate categories of information from the start. Letting them blur together, even at the margins, had made both sloppier than they needed to be.*

There was a deeper reason for the separation too, one he made himself write down because leaving it unwritten had let him avoid it for months: the two books required different kinds of honesty. The observation notebook could afford confidence — a misjudged tell cost him one fight, at most, and the loss itself corrected the record. The fragment log could not. It was the only document in existence describing what he actually was, and an overclaim in it wouldn't be corrected by any opponent; it would simply sit there, wearing the costume of a fact, waiting for the day he bet something real on it. The observation notebook was allowed to be a working tool. The fragment log had to be closer to what Vell's ledger was — a record that would rather stay incomplete than say more than it had witnessed.

He'd been calling the second book the fragment log in his own head since he'd bought it, for lack of anything better, but the name had started to feel too small for what the book was actually tracking — not just which fragments he had, but what they implied about what he was slowly becoming. *The Power Log,* he decided, turning the phrase over once and finding no real reason to reconsider it. Not elegant. Accurate, which mattered more today than elegant did.

He hadn't needed to buy anything for today's work — the slim volume was still, months later, the smallest-bound book the stationer near the market carried, and it had pages enough left for what he actually needed to do. He knew that stationer's stock as well as he knew Vell's shelf by now: three grades of paper, of which only the middle one took ink without feathering; bindings from a workshop in the Ranked core, sold here at a district markup the stationer never apologized for; ink at four coppers the bottle, which meant the year's writing — both books, all of it — had cost him less than a single bout's purse. He'd done that arithmetic once, idly, and been quietly satisfied by it. Everything he'd learned this year, everything the notebooks held, had been purchased for four bottles of ink and the willingness to actually look. The looking was the expensive part. The looking cost him something no vendor priced. What it needed wasn't replacing. It needed reorganizing: the deployment-range column he'd finished for Fragment 1 sat two pages away from Fragment 2's conditions, separated by an unrelated aside about Lira's technique that had wandered in months ago and never left. He wanted the whole book restructured — one consistent format, front to back, so that in six months he could open to any entry and know exactly what he'd known when he wrote it, without having to reconstruct the shape of his own thinking from scattered marginalia.

He designed the format before he copied anything, because a format designed mid-copy would inherit the mess it was meant to fix. Six fields per entry, in fixed order. *What it is,* as best he could determine. *Source* — where it came from, under what conditions. *Conditions* — when it answered and when it didn't. *Deployment range* — what it had actually demonstrated, tested, no speculation. *Costs* — everything it charged him, itemized; the year's fights had taught him what an unpriced capability was actually worth. And *Open questions,* last, given the most space on the page, because the honest ratio of known to unknown was still tilted heavily toward the second and the format should show that at a glance.

He opened the Power Log to a fresh page near the back and began recopying the two existing entries into the new structure — the parts he'd been reconstructing from memory and marginalia each time he needed them, finally fixed in place properly.

*Fragment 1 — Wind-adjacent.*
*Source: sustained observation, Lira, roughly three months of morning training preceding first confirmed acquisition.*
*Conditions: arrives most reliably under combat pressure. Deliberate summons outside combat context works, but with reduced fidelity — clumsier, slower to arrive.*
*Deployment range: evasion framework, short burst. Consistent. Does not fail under pressure. This is the most tested and most reliable of the two.*
*Costs: one held half-breath per burst — no air for the duration, oxygen debt if bursts stack. Landing beat at burst's end — locked, irreducible (ten trials), reclassified as a trade: the beat buys an uninterrupted read of anything committed at the landing point. Hip strain, leading side, cumulative. Ceiling: three bursts per exchange before reliability degrades.*
*Duration: undetermined.*
*Open questions: upper limit of sustained use, if any. Whether repeated deployment in a single bout degrades reliability. Whether the burst ceiling is fixed or trainable. Whether the coverage angle has gaps I haven't found — L. believes it does.*

*Fragment 2 — Pressure-adjacent.*
*Source: single instance of direct contact, Feryn, during a bout the year before — the entry read simply "the Bronze-tier fight, fourth exchange, his hand on my shoulder longer than the movement required."*
*Conditions: less understood than Fragment 1. Working deployment range established, but upper limits under sustained combat pressure remain uncharacterized.*
*Deployment range: force-receipt and dispersal, one limb or one side. Turns a staggering hit into an acceptable one. Four combat deployments this year, all deliberate, none reactive.*
*Costs: needs most of a full breath to gather — cannot be used as a reaction, only as a plan. One deployment drains roughly what three evasion bursts do. The braced limb runs leaden for two exchanges after. Deployment is visible: weight sinks, stance widens. It announces itself.*
*Duration: undetermined.*
*Open questions: the upper limit — how much force the dispersal can actually spread before it fails and lets everything through. Untested, deliberately. Whether contact-sourced fragments behave differently from observation-sourced ones in some structural way, or whether the difference is simply that I've had less time with this one.*

He sat with the two entries for a while after finishing them, a quiet satisfaction settling in at having made something disorganized into something legible. It was a small thing, in the scheme of what the entries actually described — two fragments, borrowed pieces of two other people's abilities, still more mystery than method even after a year of careful attention. But the format itself felt like progress in a way the fragments' contents hadn't yet earned. He could look at this page in six months and know exactly what he'd known today, exactly how confident he'd been in each claim, exactly what remained unanswered. The old notebook could never have given him that. It had been too busy being everything at once.

Reading the two entries side by side in the new format also showed him something the scattered version had hidden: the fragments had opposite temperaments. The Wind fragment was fast, cheap per use, reactive, and got *more* reliable under pressure; the Pressure fragment was slow, expensive, deliberate, and had never once fired without his explicit intention. One behaved like a reflex he'd been lent. The other behaved like a tool he'd been handed. Whether that difference traced to their sources — months of watching Lira versus one second of Feryn's contact — or to something about the abilities themselves, he had no way to test with a sample of two. He added it to the open questions anyway. A sample of two was still a pattern worth writing down as a pattern-shaped guess, clearly labeled as one.

He read back through the observation notebook's final page before closing it for the morning — a habit he'd developed without quite noticing, a kind of before-and-after check to make sure the reorganization had actually improved anything rather than just rearranged the same clutter into a slightly different shape. The tactical entries read cleaner already, stripped of the private uncertainty that had been muddying them. He made a note to recopy the older entries properly, sorting a year's accumulated observations into their correct new homes, and set the project aside for another Sunday. Some jobs were worth doing in pieces rather than all at once.

---

Lira found him an hour later than planned, notebook still open, and didn't ask what had kept him — she'd learned, over the year, exactly which silences meant *give me a minute* and which meant *I'm avoiding something,* and this one was clearly the first kind.

"You're doing the thing again," she said, settling into her stance across from him in the side alcove.

"What thing?"

"Being quietly pleased with yourself about paperwork." She said this with genuine affection rather than mockery, and Cael found he didn't have a real objection to the characterization.

"I finally got the log properly organized."

"Good. Now come lose to me for a while, it's been too long since I properly humbled you."

She said it lightly, but Cael had learned, over a year of mornings exactly like this one, that Lira's version of affection rarely announced itself as affection directly. It arrived instead as a challenge, an insult delivered with a grin, an offer to spar dressed up as an accusation that he'd gotten soft — and somewhere underneath all of it, unstated but entirely reliable, the simple fact that she'd shown up again this morning, the way she'd shown up every morning for a year, because some part of her had decided a long time ago that this was worth doing every single day rather than most of them.

He set the Power Log aside, carefully, on the small shelf where it had lived, separate from everything else, since not long after Fragment 2 confirmed itself, and followed her out into the alcove proper.

They trained through the morning with the rhythm they'd built over a year of mornings — drills, sparring, the constant correction that came from two people who'd stopped needing to soften feedback for each other months ago. Lira was working a particular angle today, deliberate about it in a way Cael recognized from experience: she'd found something, and she was building toward telling him about it rather than simply announcing it outright, the way she preferred to make a point land through demonstration rather than declaration whenever the lesson allowed for it.

She caught him twice in the first twenty minutes with the same angle of approach, both times landing a touch that would have counted in a real bout. He adjusted after the first, or thought he had. The second landed anyway, cleaner than the first.

"Again," he said, resetting.

"You're not going to find it by resetting. You're going to find it by asking why the adjustment you made after the first one didn't actually close anything."

He thought about that instead of immediately trying a third time, running back through the exchange in his head the way he'd run back through an opponent's tell after a fight — not the motion itself, but the decision underneath the motion, the moment where his read had gone wrong rather than the moment the strike had landed.

"Your evasion framework has a seam," she told him, mid-session, after catching him cleanly with an angle he hadn't fully accounted for. "A gap in the coverage — right about here." She demonstrated the angle slowly. "I can exploit it with my own Wind technique specifically, because I know your framework's shape better than anyone else does. But anyone who fights enough Wind practitioners is going to find this eventually."

"You're not showing me this to win more easily."

"I'm showing you this because I want you to close it before someone who isn't me finds it in an actual bout." She reset her stance, unbothered by having just made herself temporarily less useful as a sparring advantage. "Find it. Close it."

Finding it took him most of the next half hour, and the finding was its own education, because the seam turned out to live somewhere he'd never thought to look: not in the fragment, but in himself. The Wind-adjacent burst began, every time, with the hip-drop — and the hip-drop began, every time, on his left. He'd never chosen that. His body had simply imported the movement whole from wherever it had imported it, left-initiated, and a burst that always initiated left broke, always, toward the same family of angles: forward and left, back and left, a fan of escape routes that covered perhaps two-thirds of the circle around him. The remaining third — his right-rear quarter — wasn't undefended, exactly. His ordinary footwork covered it, at ordinary speed. But the fragment didn't go there, had never gone there, and an opponent who herded him until the only escape lay through the right-rear quarter would be fighting a boy with no fragment at all. That was what Lira had been doing, twice, in twenty minutes: not beating the burst, but steering it into the one direction it refused to fire.

"You knew," he said, working it out aloud. "You've known for a while."

"I've suspected for about two weeks. I confirmed it this morning." She spun her training weapon once, an idle habit she only indulged when she was pleased with herself. "You always break left off the drop. Always. Even when right is closer and safer. I kept offering you right, and you kept paying extra to go left, and eventually the pattern stopped being deniable."

Closing it was not a matter of deciding to. He spent the next stretch of the session trying to initiate the drop from his right hip, and the attempts were humbling in an instructive way — the fragment simply declined. The left-hip drop summoned that familiar loosening, the floor's borrowed half-inch, the hollow-boned launch; the right-hip drop summoned nothing but an ordinary, mortal sidestep executed by an ordinary, mortal boy who'd just telegraphed his intention by dropping his hip for no reason. It was like discovering that a word he used daily could only be spoken in one direction. The fragment wasn't his, not fully — the entry in the Power Log said *integration: partial* and here, in his hips, was what partial meant, rendered physical: the movement had come from watching Lira, and Lira, he realized with something between amusement and vertigo, initiated her own Wind evasions off her left hip. He had inherited her asymmetry along with her technique. He was carrying her fingerprint in his skeleton.

"I can't mirror it," he reported, after the ninth or tenth failed attempt, breathing harder than the failures individually justified — even failed summons, it turned out, collected a partial toll, a shadow of the hip-strain and the held breath, the fragment charging him for the attempt whether or not it delivered. "The fragment only fires off the left. Because yours does."

Lira stopped mid-reset. "Mine does what?"

"You initiate off your left hip. Every Wind evasion you've ever thrown in front of me. I'd bet the notebook on it."

She stood very still for a moment, running through her own body from the inside, and then said, in the tone of someone discovering a stranger in her own house, "Huh. I do." A pause. "My first instructor was left-dominant. I never — huh." She shook it off, filed it, came back to the practical problem with the discipline that made her worth training with. "Fine. You can't mirror it. So don't. If the burst only breaks left, then the fix isn't a right-breaking burst. The fix is never letting anyone herd you right-rear in the first place. Footwork solves what the fragment won't."

That was the work of the rest of the morning: not changing the fragment, but rebuilding the frame around it. Lira drilled him on angle-denial — reading the herding pattern three steps early, spending ordinary footwork cheaply and continuously to keep his left-forward fan open, treating the right-rear quarter as ground to be defended by position rather than by escape. It was unglamorous, exhausting, entirely fragmentless work, and by the end of it the seam had closed the only way it could close: not sealed, but guarded. She tried the herding angle twice more, adjusting each time, and both attempts died three steps early, before the trap's geometry ever finished assembling.

"There," she said, stepping back, faintly out of breath and visibly pleased. "That's closed. Properly closed, not just closed against me specifically."

"You're sure?"

"I know my own technique better than anyone. If I can't find it anymore, a stranger who's fought me twice isn't going to find it either." She wiped sweat from her forehead with the back of her wrist, the satisfied exhaustion of a morning well spent. "That's the whole reason I showed you in the first place instead of just quietly enjoying the advantage."

"I know. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Buy me lunch." She was already moving toward the alcove's water barrel, the conversation's serious portion apparently concluded as far as she was concerned. "I'm starving, and closing your seams is thirsty work."

He was midway through agreeing, already calculating which market stall would be fastest at this hour, when something shifted.

Cael stopped mid-motion, the way he'd learned to stop when the internal signal arrived — not pain, not fatigue, something else entirely, a familiar internal quiet that preceded information rather than injury.

Lira stopped too, immediately, reading the stillness for what it was. She'd seen this before, though not often enough to have stopped finding it remarkable.

The fragment notice arrived, brief, and unlike anything he'd received before — not an acquisition. A revision.

```
FRAGMENT UPDATE
[Wind-adjacent] — Duration revised: sustained.
Integration: partial.
```

He stood very still, processing the shape of what had just happened. Not new. Different. The entry he'd written that very morning — *duration: undetermined* — had just updated itself, unprompted, mid-session, as though something underneath the fragment had continued developing on its own schedule regardless of whether he was actively testing it, regardless of whether he was even thinking about it at all.

That was the part that unsettled him more than the update's actual content. He'd assumed, without ever quite stating the assumption to himself, that the fragments were static things he was slowly coming to understand better — fixed objects, and his own perception the only variable improving over time. This suggested something else. That the fragments themselves might be doing something, on their own schedule, whether or not he was paying attention. That his own understanding might not be the only thing moving.

"What happened?" Lira asked, quietly, giving him the space to answer or not.

"I need a moment," he said.

She gave him one, without asking why — trusting, by now, that the explanation would come when he was ready to give it. She didn't resume the drill either. She simply waited, arms loose at her sides, watching him work through whatever had just landed with the same patient attention she brought to reading an opponent mid-exchange.

"It updated," he said, eventually, once the internal quiet had passed and words felt manageable again. "Not a new one. The same one, but different. It said sustained now, where it used to say undetermined."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know yet." He turned the thought over once more before continuing. "I wrote 'undetermined' in the log this morning. Before training. It changed itself sometime in the last hour, without me doing anything specific to cause it."

Lira considered this with the seriousness she brought to any genuinely new information. "So it's not you getting better at reading it. It's the thing itself doing something."

"Maybe. I don't know that either. That's the part I need to sit with."

"Then sit with it. I'll go again whenever you're ready." She didn't press further, didn't ask the dozen follow-up questions the moment probably deserved. She simply reset into her stance and waited, giving him exactly the space the situation required without making him ask for more of it than he already had.

He didn't go again, not right away. He sat on the alcove's low bench and worked the timing question first, because timing was evidence and evidence didn't keep. The update had arrived within an hour of a morning spent doing two things: recopying the fragment's record into a stricter format, and drilling the fragment harder and more deliberately than he had in weeks — the failed mirror attempts, the angle-denial work, the closest, most sustained attention he'd paid to the Wind fragment's actual boundaries since the winter testing. Coincidence was possible. Coincidence was always possible. But if he'd designed an experiment to provoke a revision — *press the fragment against its own limits and document the pressing* — it would have looked exactly like this morning, and he hadn't designed it, which made the result more interesting rather than less. Still: one instance, which his own rule refused to promote past accident. He'd need a second revision, someday, with its own preceding conditions, before the pattern earned a hypothesis. He wrote the conditions down anyway, in full, so that the future comparison would have something honest to compare against.

---

He wrote the entry that evening, in the Power Log, in handwriting slightly less steady than his usual careful hand.

*The fragments are not static. They develop. Or I'm developing my understanding of them — I genuinely can't tell which yet, and the distinction matters more than it might seem to. If the fragments themselves are changing independently of my attention, that's one kind of phenomenon. If my perception of them is simply getting more accurate over time, that's a different one entirely, and it changes what I should actually be tracking.*

*Need more examples of revision versus new acquisition before I can tell the difference. Filed as an open question. Not the first one in this book, and I suspect not the last.*

*Conditions preceding today's revision, recorded for future comparison: full recopy of the fragment's record into the new format that morning; hardest deliberate boundary-testing in weeks that midday — nine failed mirror attempts, sustained angle-denial work. If a second revision ever arrives, check what preceded it. If pressing a fragment against its limits is what provokes development, that is the single most useful fact this book will ever contain, and I am not entitled to believe it on one example.*

*Also today, from the seam work: the fragment is left-handed because Lira is left-handed. I am carrying her instructor's habits, two people removed. Whatever the fragments are, they are not abstractions. They are copies of real people, taken on real days, asymmetries included. Worth remembering that everything I ever borrow will come with fingerprints.*

He read the entry back once, checking it for the particular kind of overreach that came from writing too soon after something unsettling — the temptation to sound more certain than the evidence actually supported. It held up. He hadn't claimed more than he knew. That, at least, was still within his control, whatever the fragments themselves turned out to be doing on their own schedule.

He closed the log and set it beside the observation notebook, the two of them sitting together now in their proper, separated places on the small shelf beside his cot — one recording the world, one recording whatever it was he was quietly becoming inside it. A year ago there had been a single notebook and eleven descriptions of Kindling copied from strangers he'd never see again. Now there were two books, a fragment log with an entry that had just revised itself without his permission, and a friend two rooms over who'd learned exactly how long to wait before asking what was wrong.

He thought, lying awake later than he'd meant to, that he still didn't have a name for what he was. He had, at least, gotten much better at keeping honest records of not knowing.

*End of Chapter 4 — approximately 4,500 words*

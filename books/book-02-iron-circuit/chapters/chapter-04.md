# Chapter 4 — Two Notebooks

He wrote the preamble on a Sunday morning, before training, when the boarding house was quiet enough that the words came in the order he actually wanted them.

Sunday mornings had a particular texture in Ardenmere that Cael had come to value without ever quite deciding to — the market two streets over didn't open until later, the Ironyard's side alcoves stayed empty until closer to midday, and the boarding house itself settled into the kind of hush that let a person hear their own thinking clearly for once. He'd taken to using the hour for exactly this sort of work: the unglamorous, structural labor of organizing what he knew so that it would still be useful to him in six months, rather than just true today and forgotten by winter.

The observation notebook sat open on the table beside the slim volume he'd kept separate for the fragments since not long after Fragment 2 confirmed itself — and even so, the line between the two had been blurring for months. An opponent's tell, three lines down in the observation notebook. Beneath it, with no separation at all, a private note about the Wind-adjacent step's clumsiness under deliberate summons — the kind of line that belonged in the fragment volume, not buried here where it cluttered a page that was supposed to be about someone else's footwork entirely. Beneath that, a tactical read on a Copper-tier Blade practitioner's footwork, itself half-obscured by a stray question about deployment range he'd scrawled in the margin without thinking. He'd been sloppy about the boundary between the two books almost since he'd started keeping them apart, private notes drifting into the observation notebook whenever he was mid-thought and reached for whichever page happened to be open. It had taken him embarrassingly long to notice that the drift was making both categories worse — the tactical entries cluttered with private uncertainty that didn't belong there, the fragment entries themselves half-buried in opponent data whenever he'd made the opposite mistake.

*The observation notebook records other people's abilities — what I see them do, the physical shape of their declaration architecture, the tells that precede each phase, the gaps I identify. The fragment log records what I myself have — what fragments are confirmed, what each does under different conditions, what questions remain unanswered.*

*They were supposed to be separate categories of information from the start. Letting them blur together, even at the margins, had made both sloppier than they needed to be.*

He'd been calling the second book the fragment log in his own head since he'd bought it, for lack of anything better, but the name had started to feel too small for what the book was actually tracking — not just which fragments he had, but what they implied about what he was slowly becoming. *The Power Log,* he decided, turning the phrase over once and finding no real reason to reconsider it. Not elegant. Accurate, which mattered more today than elegant did.

He hadn't needed to buy anything for today's work — the slim volume was still, months later, the smallest-bound book the stationer near the market carried, and it had pages enough left for what he actually needed to do. What it needed wasn't replacing. It needed reorganizing: the deployment-range column he'd finished for Fragment 1 sat two pages away from Fragment 2's conditions, separated by an unrelated aside about Lira's technique that had wandered in months ago and never left. He wanted the whole book restructured — one consistent format, front to back, so that in six months he could open to any entry and know exactly what he'd known when he wrote it, without having to reconstruct the shape of his own thinking from scattered marginalia.

He opened the Power Log to a fresh page near the back and began recopying the two existing entries into the new, consistent format he'd just settled on — the parts he'd been reconstructing from memory and marginalia each time he needed them, finally fixed in place properly.

*Fragment 1 — Wind-adjacent.*
*Source: sustained observation, Lira, roughly three months of morning training preceding first confirmed acquisition.*
*Conditions: arrives most reliably under combat pressure. Deliberate summons outside combat context works, but with reduced fidelity — clumsier, slower to arrive.*
*Deployment range: evasion framework, short burst. Consistent. Does not fail under pressure. This is the most tested and most reliable of the two.*
*Duration: undetermined.*
*Open questions: upper limit of sustained use, if any. Whether repeated deployment in a single bout degrades reliability.*

*Fragment 2 — Pressure-adjacent.*
*Source: single instance of direct contact, Feryn, during a bout the year before — the entry read simply "the Bronze-tier fight, fourth exchange, his hand on my shoulder longer than the movement required."*
*Conditions: less understood than Fragment 1. Working deployment range established, but upper limits under sustained combat pressure remain uncharacterized.*
*Open questions: whether contact-sourced fragments behave differently from observation-sourced fragments in some structural way, or whether the difference is simply that I've had less time with this one.*

He sat with the two entries for a while after finishing them, a quiet satisfaction settling in at having made something disorganized into something legible. It was a small thing, in the scheme of what the entries actually described — two fragments, borrowed pieces of two other people's abilities, still more mystery than method even after a year of careful attention. But the format itself felt like progress in a way the fragments' contents hadn't yet earned. He could look at this page in six months and know exactly what he'd known today, exactly how confident he'd been in each claim, exactly what remained unanswered. The old notebook could never have given him that. It had been too busy being everything at once.

He read back through the observation notebook's final page before closing it for the morning — a habit he'd developed without quite noticing, a kind of before-and-after check to make sure the reorganization had actually improved anything rather than just rearranged the same clutter into a slightly different shape. The tactical entries read cleaner already, stripped of the private uncertainty that had been muddying them. He made a note to recopy the older entries properly, sorting a year's accumulated observations into their correct new homes, and set the project aside for another Sunday. Some jobs were worth doing in pieces rather than all at once.

---

Lira found him an hour later than planned, notebook still open, and didn't ask what had kept him — she'd learned, over the year, exactly which silences meant *give me a minute* and which meant *I'm avoiding something,* and this one was clearly the first kind.

"You're doing the thing again," she said, settling into her stance across from him in the side alcove.

"What thing?"

"Being quietly pleased with yourself about paperwork." She said this with genuine affection rather than mockery, and Cael found he didn't have a real objection to the characterization.

"I finally got the log properly organized."

"Good. Now come lose to me for a while, it's been too long since I properly humbled you."

She said it lightly, but Cael had learned, over a year of mornings exactly like this one, that Lira's version of affection rarely announced itself as affection directly. It arrived instead as a challenge, an insult delivered with a grin, an offer to spar dressed up as an accusation that he'd gotten soft — and somewhere underneath all of it, unstated but entirely reliable, the simple fact that she'd shown up again this morning, the way she'd shown up every morning for a year, because some part of her had decided a long time ago that this was worth doing every single day rather than most of them.

He set the Power Log aside, carefully, on the small shelf where it had lived, separate from everything else, since not long after Fragment 2 confirmed itself, and followed her out into the alcove proper.

They trained through the morning with the rhythm they'd built over a year of mornings — drills, sparring, the constant correction that came from two people who'd stopped needing to soften feedback for each other months ago. Lira was working a particular angle today, deliberate about it in a way Cael recognized from experience: she'd found something, and she was building toward telling him about it rather than simply announcing it outright, the way she preferred to make a point land through demonstration rather than declaration whenever the lesson allowed for it.

She caught him twice in the first twenty minutes with the same angle of approach, both times landing a touch that would have counted in a real bout. He adjusted after the first, or thought he had. The second landed anyway, cleaner than the first.

"Again," he said, resetting.

"You're not going to find it by resetting. You're going to find it by asking why the adjustment you made after the first one didn't actually close anything."

He thought about that instead of immediately trying a third time, running back through the exchange in his head the way he'd run back through an opponent's tell after a fight — not the motion itself, but the decision underneath the motion, the moment where his read had gone wrong rather than the moment the strike had landed.

"Your evasion framework has a seam," she told him, mid-session, after catching him cleanly with an angle he hadn't fully accounted for. "A gap in the coverage — right about here." She demonstrated the angle slowly. "I can exploit it with my own Wind technique specifically, because I know your framework's shape better than anyone else does. But anyone who fights enough Wind practitioners is going to find this eventually."

"You're not showing me this to win more easily."

"I'm showing you this because I want you to close it before someone who isn't me finds it in an actual bout." She reset her stance, unbothered by having just made herself temporarily less useful as a sparring advantage. "Find it. Close it."

They worked the gap for the better part of an hour, Cael running variations, Lira exploiting each one until the coverage held clean against her best attempts — the satisfying grind of two people who trusted each other enough to be genuinely useful rather than merely kind. It was, he'd come to understand over the year, a different kind of training than anything the observation notebook could give him. Watching an opponent taught him what other people's bodies did. Sparring Lira, day after day, taught him what his own body could be talked into doing differently, given enough patient, repeated correction from someone who actually wanted him to improve rather than simply wanted to win.

By the fortieth or fiftieth repetition — neither of them was counting anymore, the drill having long since dissolved into pure rhythm — the seam had genuinely closed. She tried the angle twice more, adjusting each time, and both attempts skated off a defense that had, sometime in the last hour, actually learned something rather than merely performed correction.

"There," she said, stepping back, faintly out of breath and visibly pleased. "That's closed. Properly closed, not just closed against me specifically."

"You're sure?"

"I know my own technique better than anyone. If I can't find it anymore, a stranger who's fought me twice isn't going to find it either." She wiped sweat from her forehead with the back of her wrist, the specific satisfied exhaustion of a morning well spent. "That's the whole reason I showed you in the first place instead of just quietly enjoying the advantage."

"I know. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Buy me lunch." She was already moving toward the alcove's water barrel, the conversation's serious portion apparently concluded as far as she was concerned. "I'm starving, and closing your seams is thirsty work."

He was midway through agreeing, already calculating which market stall would be fastest at this hour, when something shifted.

Cael stopped mid-motion, the way he'd learned to stop when the internal signal arrived — not pain, not fatigue, something else entirely, a familiar internal quiet that preceded information rather than injury.

Lira stopped too, immediately, reading the stillness for what it was. She'd seen this before, though not often enough to have stopped finding it remarkable.

The fragment notice arrived, brief, and unlike anything he'd received before — not an acquisition. A revision.

```
FRAGMENT UPDATE
[Wind-adjacent] — Duration revised: sustained.
Integration: partial.
```

He stood very still, processing the shape of what had just happened. Not new. Different. The entry he'd written that very morning — *duration: undetermined* — had just updated itself, unprompted, mid-session, as though something underneath the fragment had continued developing on its own schedule regardless of whether he was actively testing it, regardless of whether he was even thinking about it at all.

That was the part that unsettled him more than the update's actual content. He'd assumed, without ever quite stating the assumption to himself, that the fragments were static things he was slowly coming to understand better — fixed objects, and his own perception the only variable improving over time. This suggested something else. That the fragments themselves might be doing something, on their own schedule, whether or not he was paying attention. That his own understanding might not be the only thing moving.

"What happened?" Lira asked, quietly, giving him the space to answer or not.

"I need a moment," he said.

She gave him one, without asking why, the way she gave him most things he needed without demanding the explanation up front — trusting, by now, that the explanation would come when he was ready to give it properly. She didn't resume the drill either. She simply waited, arms loose at her sides, watching him work through whatever had just landed with the same patient attention she brought to reading an opponent mid-exchange.

"It updated," he said, eventually, once the internal quiet had passed and words felt manageable again. "Not a new one. The same one, but different. It said sustained now, where it used to say undetermined."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know yet." He turned the thought over once more before continuing. "I wrote 'undetermined' in the log this morning. Before training. It changed itself sometime in the last hour, without me doing anything specific to cause it."

Lira considered this with the seriousness she brought to any genuinely new information. "So it's not you getting better at reading it. It's the thing itself doing something."

"Maybe. I don't know that either. That's the part I need to sit with."

"Then sit with it. I'll go again whenever you're ready." She didn't press further, didn't ask the dozen follow-up questions the moment probably deserved. She simply reset into her stance and waited, giving him exactly the space the situation required without making him ask for more of it than he already had.

---

He wrote the entry that evening, in the Power Log, in handwriting slightly less steady than his usual careful hand.

*The fragments are not static. They develop. Or I'm developing my understanding of them — I genuinely can't tell which yet, and the distinction matters more than it might seem to. If the fragments themselves are changing independently of my attention, that's one kind of phenomenon. If my perception of them is simply getting more accurate over time, that's a different one entirely, and it changes what I should actually be tracking.*

*Need more examples of revision versus new acquisition before I can tell the difference. Filed as an open question. Not the first one in this book, and I suspect not the last.*

He read the entry back once, checking it for the particular kind of overreach that came from writing too soon after something unsettling — the temptation to sound more certain than the evidence actually supported. It held up. He hadn't claimed more than he knew. That, at least, was still within his control, whatever the fragments themselves turned out to be doing on their own schedule.

He closed the log and set it beside the observation notebook, the two of them sitting together now in their proper, separated places on the small shelf beside his cot — one recording the world, one recording whatever it was he was quietly becoming inside it. A year ago there had been a single notebook and eleven descriptions of Kindling copied from strangers he'd never see again. Now there were two books, a fragment log with an entry that had just revised itself without his permission, and a friend two rooms over who'd learned exactly how long to wait before asking what was wrong.

He thought, lying awake later than he'd meant to, that he still didn't have a name for what he was. He had, at least, gotten considerably better at keeping honest records of not knowing.

# Chapter 20 — The Main Floor

The Ironyard the night before the Reydan fight was fuller than Cael had ever seen it, and it wasn't even the night of the bout itself.

Word had traveled well past the district. Dace had spent the past week managing an actual waiting list for standing space — a small, dog-eared ledger of his own, kept separate from Vell's official one, where he'd been penciling in names and crossing them out again as the count against the Ironyard's practical capacity kept shifting. Cael had watched him work through it twice, the same fluent, half-muttered arithmetic he brought to ordinary scheduling, except louder now, more visibly strained. "Four hundred comfortable," Dace had said, more to the ledger than to Cael. "We're going to have five hundred people who think they deserve to be here tomorrow, and forty of them are going to be wrong about that, and I get to be the one who tells them."

Main floor. Full lighting — every practitioner-installed lamp in the warehouse lit at once, which Cael had only seen happen twice before in a year of evenings here. Vell keeping the ledger personally rather than delegating any part of the preparation to one of the junior keepers who sometimes handled the smaller side-alcove bouts. Nobody had needed to say, out loud, exactly how much weight this particular fight was carrying. It showed in every visible decision the Ironyard's people were making around it, the way a held breath shows in a room without anyone announcing they're holding it.

Cael arrived early, the way he arrived early for most things that mattered, and found a place near the east post — not his usual alcove seat, which would have put him too far back to read the crowd building around him, but a spot at the main floor's edge where he could watch the preliminary bouts and the audience gathering for tomorrow in more or less the same field of view.

He watched the preliminary bouts with half his attention. The rest tracked something more specific: Reydan, seated on the floor's opposite side, doing the exact same thing.

Reydan hadn't come to fight tonight. He'd come, Cael understood within the first few minutes of noticing him, for the same reason Cael himself had come — not to watch the bouts exactly, but to watch the room the bouts were happening inside of, the specific texture of a crowd on the eve of something it had decided mattered. He sat with the stillness of a fighter conserving effort even in his posture, forearms resting loosely on his knees, attention moving between the ring and the gathering audience with an unhurried, professional patience that told Cael more about him than anything that had traveled secondhand since his arrival had managed to.

They didn't speak. They watched each other watch the fighters in the ring — a contained, professional attention that felt less like surveillance and more like two people conducting the same careful study from different angles, each aware of the other's presence without either needing to acknowledge it directly. It went on for the better part of an hour, three preliminary bouts' worth of shared, silent observation, until the last of the evening's scheduled matches finished and the crowd began its slow, reluctant drain toward the doors.

Cael found himself, somewhere in the second bout, cataloguing Reydan the way he cataloged everyone — not tactically, not yet, there'd be time for that tomorrow, but simply as a fact of the room. The way he sat. The way his attention moved. The quality of patience in someone who had already decided, apparently, that tomorrow would answer whatever questions tonight's watching raised, and saw no reason to rush the answer by asking them out loud early.

He thought, watching him, about Feryn — the first Bronze-tier professional he'd ever fought, back when Ardenmere itself had still felt new to him. Feryn had watched him the same way, in the end. Curious rather than hostile. Interested in the problem rather than invested in the win. He wondered if that was simply what genuine skill did to a person eventually — burned off whatever needed the crowd's noise to feel like it mattered, and left behind something closer to pure appetite for a real question. He hoped, distantly, that it would do the same to him, someday, whenever someday arrived.

Lira found him during the gap between the second and third preliminary bouts, settling onto the bench beside him without asking, the two of them watching the crowd's texture shift as more people filtered in through the doors.

"That's a lot of people for a fight that isn't even tomorrow's main event yet," she said.

"They're not here for tonight's bouts. They're here to be able to say they saw the buildup."

"That's a very Cael way of describing enthusiasm." She nudged him with her shoulder, the old, easy gesture that had become, over a year, one of the fixed points of how they talked to each other. "You've been watching him for an hour."

"He's been watching me too."

"I noticed. Neither of you have said a word." She studied Reydan for a moment with her own considerable eye, the flat, professional assessment of a fighter sizing up another fighter regardless of whose bout it actually was. "He doesn't look nervous."

"He isn't. I don't think he gets nervous the way most people do anymore. I think he's past that, the same way some of the older circuit veterans are past it — not because the danger's gone, but because he's made his peace with what losing would actually cost him, and it isn't as much as it would have cost him five years ago."

"That's either very wise or very tired."

"Possibly both." He glanced at her. "Same as most things worth being, eventually."

She smiled at that, briefly, and didn't push the conversation further, letting it settle back into the comfortable, watchful quiet the two of them had gotten so practiced at sharing.

---

A new figure appeared quietly toward the back of the crowd partway through the evening — well-dressed relative to the circuit's usual audience, attentive, taking her own notes in a small book of her own, the kind of unhurried economy of motion that Cael had learned, over the past year, to associate with people who'd trained themselves out of wasted gesture a long time ago. Not a regular. He was fairly sure of that within a few minutes of noticing her — nobody in the Ironyard's ordinary rotation carried themselves quite like that, dressed quite like that, watched the room quite like that.

He clocked her fully within ten minutes, the instinct for an unfamiliar pattern that a year of circuit life had trained into him without his ever deciding to cultivate it. She wasn't watching the bouts the way the rest of the crowd watched them — not with the loose, invested attention of someone rooting for an outcome, but with something closer to Cael's own compound gaze, cataloguing structure rather than simply enjoying spectacle. She wrote in her small book only occasionally, and always, as far as he could tell from a distance, after something specific had happened rather than continuously.

He asked Dace about her, quietly, between bouts, catching him at the edge of the scheduling board during a lull.

"Not circuit-regular," Dace said, not even needing to look toward her to know who Cael meant. "Came in with a credentialed pass — the kind academy scouts use for venue observation. First time I've seen one of those used here in longer than I can remember."

"Which academy?"

"Pass doesn't say. Just the credential itself, which is enough to get her through the door without anyone asking further questions — that's rather the point of a credential like that." Dace glanced toward her once, briefly, then back to his board. "She's been here before, if that's useful to you. Not often. Not never."

Cael filed all of it without visible reaction, the way he filed most information that mattered too much for an immediate response. He didn't mention it to Lira or Brom yet, not that evening. He wanted to understand the shape of it fully — why an academy would send a scout to an unsanctioned circuit at all, and why now, on the eve of a fight that had already drawn more attention than anything the Ironyard had hosted in a year — before deciding what, if anything, the information actually required him to do.

He found himself watching her almost as much as he watched Reydan for the rest of the evening, careful to keep the attention indirect — a glance timed to a crowd reaction, a turn of the head that could pass for simply following the room's noise. She never once looked back at him directly, which struck him, the longer he considered it, as its own kind of information. Either she hadn't noticed him at all, which seemed unlikely given how carefully she seemed to be cataloguing everyone else in the building, or she'd noticed him immediately and had decided, deliberately, not to let him see that she had.

He wrote a short entry into the observation notebook before he went to find Brom and Lira, the private section rather than the tactical one, because it felt closer to a question than a fact: *Academy scout. Watching, not participating. Doesn't want to be watched back, or doesn't care to be seen wanting that. Tomorrow is either going to be a fight, or it's going to be a fight that becomes something else entirely by the time it's over. I don't know which yet.*

---

Brom found him near the doorway to the preparation space, just before Cael went in to be alone with the night's last hour, his usual economical directness carrying something heavier than its ordinary weight tonight.

"You can win this."

"I know."

"I mean you can actually win. Not survive. Not extract useful information and call the loss a lesson, the way you've learned to do with almost everything else this past year. Win."

Cael looked at him for a moment before answering, understanding that the distinction mattered to Brom in a way it hadn't fully occurred to him to examine on his own. "I know," he said again, differently this time — with the weight of someone who had run the calculation enough times to actually trust the result, rather than simply hoping it would hold. He believed it, and the believing felt new, distinct from the careful, information-hungry confidence that had carried him through every real fight since Weaver's Row. That confidence had always left room for a loss that taught him something. This didn't.

"Lira thinks you're ready," Brom said. "I think you're ready. I don't think either of those things matter as much as whether you think it, though, so I'm asking you directly instead of just telling you what we've already decided." A pause, uncharacteristically long for him. "Do you think you're ready?"

"Yes."

"Good." Something in Brom's shoulders eased, very slightly — not relief exactly, more like the settling of a question he'd genuinely needed answered before he could let the night close. "Then go be alone with it for a while. I'll walk Lira back. We'll see you in the morning."

He left without further ceremony, the same economical exit Cael had come to expect from him over the past year, and Cael went into the preparation space alone, the door settling shut behind him with a soft, final sound that felt, tonight, larger than the sound itself should have carried.

---

He sat with the Power Log open in his lap for a long while before writing anything at all, the lamp beside him burning low, the Ironyard's last few departing voices fading into the district's ordinary night noise beyond the walls.

Three integrated fragments. One anomaly that might, eventually, become a fourth, if the anomaly ever consented to be summoned on purpose rather than simply happening to him under pressure he couldn't fully control. He turned the pages back through the log's short history, and further back in memory than the log itself reached — Renn's telegraphed strikes, a courtyard behind Torvin's boarding house, Dessa's transition windows, Feryn's contact-sourced fragment, Brom's careful, patient teaching, the Tide-adjacent anomaly he still couldn't name. A year of careful, accumulated evidence, building toward a night like this one without either of them ever quite planning for it to arrive this specific way.

He wrote a single entry for the coming bout, the handwriting steadier than he expected it to be: *Reydan. Iron-tier Rank 8. Pressure-burst variant. Known tells: containment signature, per Brom's read. Known weaknesses: adapts between exchanges, which means he commits to a read and then has to abandon it when it fails. If I can force him to abandon three consecutive reads, his fourth response will be pattern-broken. That's when I move.*

He read it back once, satisfied with its accuracy, and closed the log.

He didn't go find Lira and Brom right away. He sat a while longer instead, alone with the last of the evening's quiet, letting the weight of tomorrow settle into something he could actually carry rather than simply brace against — the specific, hard-won steadiness of someone who had learned, the slow way, that the waiting before a fight that mattered was survivable if you let yourself feel the whole size of it first, rather than rushing past the feeling toward the fight itself.

When he finally went to find them, the district had gone mostly quiet around him, the Ironyard's noise replaced by the ordinary late sounds of a place settling into its own night — voices from open windows, someone's laundry line creaking somewhere overhead, the specific restless hush he'd stopped finding unfamiliar the better part of a year ago.

Tomorrow would answer what tonight could only prepare him for. He'd learned, by now, that this was usually enough.

# Chapter 5 — The Scouting Economy

Every market Cael had ever catalogued — Denvash's fair-day stalls, Ardenmere's circuit floors, the registry's own queues — had a second market standing behind it, quieter and better capitalized, and the tournament's arrived at the second meet wearing three different coats.

He charted it from the gallery on the bracket days, between bouts, because it was the most interesting apparatus in the hall and nobody else was watching it. The first coat was official: academy scouts, credentialed, listed in the program like everything else, seated in the gallery's best rows with their institutions' colors on their folios. Auremont had two at the mill town — Auremont, reigning champion, sent scouts to a hill-country regional the way a Gold-tier read a Copper bracket's results, which was to say completely, on principle, because information cost less than surprise. The second coat was commercial: the handicappers, who worked for the betting houses and sat where the light was good and wrote continuously, pricing everything with a heartbeat, and whose chalked boards in the square were — Cael verified this across two meets — more accurate about form than half the academies' own coaching files. The third coat was the one that concerned the delegation, and it did not sit anywhere. It bought.

"Scouting houses," Karis said, when he brought her the chart — and she took the chart itself first, the way she took every document, holding it to the light as though provenance were a watermark, and read his columns for a silent minute before she answered the question underneath them, which was her oldest courtesy: treating his observations as evidence before treating them as questions. She had known before he finished drawing it; she had the notebook's tournament section open to a page already headed *the information market*. "Commercial compilers. They buy observation — from stewards, from gallery regulars, from anyone whose seat has a view — and they compile dossiers, and they sell to anyone with the fee. Academies buy them to prepare. Betting houses buy them to price. Families buy them to feel involved." She tapped the page. "The tournament pretends they don't exist because the tournament can't license them, and you can't regulate what you refuse to see. Note who that logic protects."

"The compilers."

"The buyers," said Karis. "The compilers are visible the moment you look. It's the clients who get to be nobody."

He tested the visibility claim the next morning, because claims were for testing. It took him forty minutes at the gallery rail to find the mill town's compiler-observer: a woman of middle years in unremarkable wool, third row, hall side, who watched the bouts the way nobody watched bouts — evenly. A partisan's eyes followed their fighter. A bettor's eyes followed the odds-board between exchanges. A coach's eyes followed technique and went glazed during ceremony. Hers moved on a schedule, every fighter, every corner, the stewards' table, the panel, at an even rate, like a lighthouse. She wrote in a shorthand her hand had stopped thinking about years ago, and she left before every final — before, because finals were public record and her clients paid for what wasn't.

He watched her for three bouts. During the third, without hurry, she turned her even lighthouse gaze up to the rail, held on him for precisely the interval her schedule allotted a subject of interest, wrote, and moved on.

*She logged me logging her,* the notebook got. *No alarm, no pretense — a professional entering a fact. Somewhere in the next edition of my dossier there will be a line: subject conducts counter-observation, competent. And she's right, and the line will be accurate, and the people who buy it will know one more true thing about the mask. This economy is the first apparatus I've met that watches back at my own speed. The Compact observes me the way weather observes a coastline. These people observe me the way I observe me. Noted without comfort.*

---

The odds question produced the season's best comedy, and Lira collected it the way she collected everything worth keeping: personally, at the source.

The mill town's chalk board priced every bracket bout by midday of the first meet-day — tidy columns, tidy fractions, the handicappers' craft compressed into arithmetic anyone could read. The exhibition category had a column too. It was blank. It stayed blank through the first day, and on the second morning it acquired, instead of odds, a single chalked annotation that drew a small permanent crowd: *no book on the demonstration bouts.*

Lira went and asked why, because the delegation contained exactly one person who could walk up to a betting house's board-man and get a straight answer inside two minutes, and it was her, and she knew it.

"No book," the board-man told her, entirely candid — board-men, she reported, were candid the way bakers were floury, "because a book needs two believable outcomes. Nobody in this town will put money against your lad, so there's no price. And before you ask — we tried a different market at the wool town. Not whether he wins. *How much he wins by.* Margin betting. Closed it inside a day." He wiped his chalky hands with feeling. "Every bet came in on the same margin. Tight win, three exchanges, nothing showy. The whole town bet the same number like they'd been handed it."

"They had," Lira said. "They'd all read the same dossier."

"Aye. And here's the thing I'll tell you for free, because it's the strangest thing I've seen in twenty years of making books." He looked over at the exhibition floor, where the boy in question was rewrapping a wrist, unremarkable, in-band. "They were *right*. Every one of them. The dossier called his margin like a ferry timetable. I can't price a man whose performances arrive on schedule. There's no uncertainty in it, and uncertainty is the whole product." He picked his chalk back up. "You want my professional opinion of your unclassifiable friend? He's the most classified fighter on this circuit. Somebody should tell the registry they're measuring the wrong thing."

She delivered the whole exchange at the evening table verbatim, with the board-man's hand gestures, and the table laughed, and Cael laughed too, and wrote it down after with the flat care he kept for things that were funny and also findings: *a betting house has independently confirmed Seln's variance problem, priced the doctrine at zero uncertainty, and concluded I'm the most predictable fighter on the circuit. The mask is now so well-fitted that professional gamblers refuse to gamble on it. Rooke's variance program stops being a courtesy next meet. It's cover.*

---

Brom got his dossier on the second bracket morning, and he got it as a courtesy, which was the interesting part — because courtesy, in an economy where information was the whole currency, was never only courtesy, and the delegation spent a profitable evening pricing what else it was.

The Copper final's other side was a southern academy's entry, and the southern academy's coach — a broad, courteous man who had been losing regionals to better-funded institutions for a decade and had developed the survivor's ethic about it — crossed the warm-up floor beforehand and handed Brom four folded pages with the air of a man returning a dropped glove.

"My institution bought this on you last month," he said. "We're not ashamed; everyone buys them. But my boy's about to fight you off the back of it, and I've read it, and page three is wrong in a way that'll get him hurt if he trusts it. I'd rather lose honest." He nodded once and went back to his corner, and Brom stood holding the four pages, and brought them to the delegation table at the break unopened, and opened them there, because Brom did not read documents about himself alone — a discipline he had never announced and never once broken since the Velmere letters.

The dossier was better than anyone wanted it to be. Four pages, close-written, professionally organized: bout history back to the Ardenmere circuit, correct; the Iron Skin architecture described in functional terms, correct; his patience priced, his pricing habit priced, the compound trap he'd run on strikers described with an example. And page three: the force-redirect seam, the recommitment gap under the left-side reset — Book 3's flaw, documented in loving tactical detail, with a recommended exploitation sequence.

Repaired fourteen months ago.

"Out of date," said Ephram, reading over his shoulder. "That's good, isn't it?"

"It's better than good," Brom said slowly. "It's a map of what they'll try." He turned the page around so the table could see it. "Every fighter who bought this comes at the left-side reset expecting the old seam. I've spent a year making that the strongest door in the house. They're going to queue at it." He folded the pages with the care he gave to useful things. "The kind man just handed me the whole bracket's plan."

The final confirmed it, and confirmed it with a completeness that made it the meet's quiet masterclass for anyone who knew what they were watching. The southern entry, warned off page three by his own coach, fought honestly: he came in behind a probing high line, worked Brom's right side — the side the dossier called strong, because attacking documented strength was what you did when you'd been told the documented weakness was a lie — and built his exchanges with the deliberate courage of a young fighter operating without a map. He lost in four, to the pricing game, the way everyone lost to the pricing game, and Brom set him down gently and told him at the ropes, audibly, "You fought me. Not the paper. That's rarer than you think this season," which the boy would be turning over for a year.

But the instructive bout was the semifinal before it, which Brom had won while the dossier was still folded in his kit. His semifinal opponent had bought page three at full price. Cael charted it from the rail exchange by exchange: the fighter's whole architecture aimed at the left-side reset, feints invested to draw the recommitment gap, position spent to be standing at the seam when it opened. It never opened. And the fighter had nothing else — that was the funnel doing its work — so by the third exchange he was a man knocking at a bricked-up door with his budget gone, and Brom priced the knocking, and the flags ended it. Two other Copper fighters in the gallery tracked the bout with the dossier's exact expectations on their faces, waiting for the gap that never came, recalibrating nothing, and Cael wrote the chapter's first finding into the observation notebook while the flags were still up: *stale information doesn't just fail its buyers. It herds them. Every out-of-date page is a funnel, and Brom is standing at the narrow end charging admission.*

Karis borrowed the dossier that evening and audited it against her own knowledge, and returned it with the annotation that mattered: "Compiled from gallery observation and meet records only. Nothing internal, nothing from inside Halcenvane's walls. Whoever built this has never seen our training floor." She let that reassurance sit exactly one sentence before she completed it. "Which tells us the walls hold, and tells us nothing about whether anyone's been buying at a better shop."

---

The better shop, as it happened, was already being audited.

Seln's meet ran on a different clock from everyone's. The assessment clerk's duties — floor schedules, credential files, the travel ledger — occupied the hours the delegation could see, and were performed with a competence nobody thought about anymore, which was their design. The other hours had no witnesses, which was theirs.

He worked the mill town's second market the way he worked every room, from the service side. Stewards drank at the long inn by the weighbridge; gallery regulars sold their seats' sightlines for a mug and the pleasure of being asked; the handicappers' runners knew every buyer's runner by coat. Nobody in that economy looked twice at a mild administrative man settling a delegation's accounts, because delegations' account-settlers were the economy's own furniture.

Three evenings bought him what he had come for: the Cael dossier, current edition, acquired through two removes and a fee that would appear in no ledger anywhere, and a name for its compiler — a scouting house, continental, respectable in the way of businesses whose product was other people. How the three evenings were spent, no record anywhere would ever hold, which was the point of how they were spent.

He read it in his room by one candle, professionally, twice.

It was good work. Bout history complete back through the Greyvane transcripts. The exhibition ratings charted, meet by meet — with, he noted, a compiler's marginal comment on the band's tidiness that used the word *managed*, which meant the market had found in five weeks what the Compact's statisticians had not found in three years, which was a fact about incentives he filed without surprise. The public suite described accurately. The assay architecture summarized from the paid transcript. And threaded through the compilation, in its sourcing and its structure, a pattern he had seen before: the tasking style, the order of assembly, the specific appetite — records first, capabilities second, associations third — of the brokered inquiry that had come at Halcenvane's records office fourteen months ago and been turned away at the wall.

He laid the two patterns side by side in his memory, which was where he kept the things that mattered, and let them resolve.

The records-office broker had never been the Compact. The Compact did not buy what it could requisition. The broker had been the scouting house's procurement — the industry tooling up, fourteen months early, for the product it was selling this season on every meet ground on the circuit. The commissioning client of that first inquiry was unrecoverable now, two removes back through a market built of removes, and it no longer mattered: the client had been the market itself, assembling its stock in advance of demand, the way any competent wholesaler did.

He burned nothing, kept nothing, wrote nothing. He returned the dossier through the removes it had come by, because a copy that stayed bought was a transaction somebody could find. Then he sat for a while with the candle, in the configuration of a man doing accounts, closing a fourteen-month-old file in the only ledger he trusted.

---

He told the inner circle at the next evening's table, in the register he told them everything: obliquely, administratively, in sentences that would have survived any audit on the continent.

"The office has completed its reconciliation of last year's records-office inquiry," he said, to the teapot, approximately. "The tasking pattern matches a commercial scouting house's procurement cycle. The product is the dossier currently circulating. Conclusion for the file: private interest, confirmed — the market, not the Compact. The original client is unrecoverable and, in the office's assessment, no longer material." He poured. "The residue is structural. The dossier is a commercial product in general circulation. Every opponent of consequence has read a version of you before they meet you. That condition is now permanent."

The table absorbed it. Lira said what three of them were thinking: "Fourteen months we've carried that thread. A broker probes the records office, we build half a season's caution on it — and it was a *catalogue company* stocking shelves?"

"Most threats are commerce wearing a cloak," Seln said. "The cloak is usually better than the commerce."

"How certain," said Karis, because Karis audited even the auditors, "is the pattern match?"

"The office would defend it at any standard you name." He said it in the tone he kept for entering totals — the arithmetic finished, only the reading left. "Tasking order, sourcing appetite, assembly structure. Procurement styles are handwriting. A house that has bought information the same way for twenty years cannot buy it a new way for one job, because the people it buys through only know the old way." A beat, and then, because the question deserved its whole answer: "And there is a confirming absence. If the records-office client had been institutional — Compact, registry, any office with standing — the inquiry would have recurred after it failed. Institutions that want a thing send a second letter. Markets that want a thing wait for the price to drop. Nothing recurred. The price dropped. Here's the catalogue."

Karis wrote for a moment. "Accepted," she said, which from her was a seal, and struck a line through a heading in the notebook that had stood open since the spring — and Cael watched her do it and understood he was watching the delegation's ledgers reconcile in real time, a liability fourteen months old reclassified before his eyes into paid history.

"One residual," Karis added, pen still down. "The dossier means the finals field studies you before Norhold. All of them. Auremont's had scouts at both meets — the reigning champion academy is compiling its own file, and theirs won't be bought from a bookshop."

"No," said Seln. "Theirs will be better."

"Good," said Cael.

The table looked at him. Seln looked at him one second longer than usual, which from Seln was a raised eyebrow, a request for the working, and Cael showed it.

"They'll prepare for the version in the file. I've read the version in the file — " Seln had tabled a summary of its contents, sourced, he said, from a courtesy copy the way Brom's had been, which everyone at the table heard and nobody examined — "and the version in the file is the record, and the record is the mask. A continent of opponents studying my mask is a continent of opponents training against something I built for exactly that purpose." He turned his cup once. "I'd rather fight people who prepared for the wrong me than people who prepared for nothing. Preparation makes you commit. Commitment is readable."

"Spoken like page three," said Brom, and the table's laugh was the good kind, and the thread that had been a wire across fourteen months of dark went into the ledger as what it had always been: texture.

But Cael caught Seln's look before it folded away, and gave the log its honest margin that night: *he looked at me too long because he heard what I actually said. Everyone else heard tactics. He heard a person describing, with satisfaction, the condition of being permanently unknown — and he is the one other person at that table who lives there. Two professionals of the same strange trade, recognizing each other across a teapot. Neither of us said so. Saying so is not what our trade does.*

---

The delegation's own information discipline got its audit that same week, because Karis did not let a season's finding go unapplied for longer than a wagon ride.

She convened it at the residence table the evening after Seln's reconciliation — not the inner circle only; the whole traveling company, Ephram included, Rooke included, because the subject was walls and walls protected everyone inside them or no one. She ran it like the researcher she was: here is what the market knows about each of us, itemized; here is where each item came from — meet records, gallery observation, paid transcripts, all external; here is what the market does not have — training-floor interiors, the variance program, session schedules, anything spoken inside Halcenvane's walls or these residences.

"The walls hold," she said. "Here is how they keep holding. Nothing tactical discussed in taprooms, in warm-up yards, or within thirty feet of a gallery rail — the compilers buy sightlines *and* earshots. Coaching corrections happen in closed sessions or not at all." She looked at Ephram. "Your bout habits are being charted by a woman in grey wool who sits third row hall-side. Nothing you can do about the bouts. But you rehearsed your semifinal entry sequence in the open yard at dawn, twice, and I watched a steward watch you do it, and your opponent declined the exact entry you rehearsed. Sold inside a day."

Ephram, to his considerable credit, took it the way the cohort took coaching. "Closed floors from now on. What else?"

"Letters go through Bracken's pouch, not the town post. And — " the smallest pause of the evening — "assume every kindness from a rival delegation is also a transaction. The southern coach who gave Brom the dossier did a decent thing. He also, in one gesture, told our whole table his academy buys from the same shop, established a courtesy debt against a future season, and watched all six of our faces while we read page three." She closed the notebook. "Decent and instrumental at once. Most of this economy is. Budget for both."

Rooke, from the end of the table, gave the audit his highest available review: "Cheaper than a loss." And Cael, writing the session into the log, appended the only line the evening still needed: *she built us a counter-intelligence doctrine in eleven minutes and nobody in the room found it strange, which is the finding. Two years ago we were four people keeping one secret. We are becoming an institution with an information policy. Institutions survive seasons. Note which direction the becoming runs.*

Auremont's two scouts, for the record, had watched every one of Cael's exhibition bouts at both meets, and he had watched them watch.

They were better than the grey-wool woman — or differently aimed: where she metered the whole hall, they attended, and their attention had structure. The senior one charted position and never wrote during exchanges, only after, in long retrospective passages, which meant he was keeping whole sequences in his head and trusting his recall, which meant he had been a fighter. The junior wrote continuously and only, as far as Cael could reconstruct from the rhythm of her pen, during Cael's recoveries — not the technique, the *resets* — and he spent an evening failing to be sure what that was for before conceding the failure to the log: *Auremont's junior scout is charting something in my resets that I haven't identified, which makes her the first observer in three years to work a seam of the mask I haven't already surveyed. The reigning champions did not get to be the reigning champions by buying from bookshops. Karis's residual stands. Somewhere in Auremont's files there is a version of me assembled by professionals, and I will meet the fighters who trained on it at Norhold, and I don't know what it says.*

*The season's first genuinely open question about the mask, entered as such. Note the sensation. It's been a while.*

---

The quarterly filing went north at the season's midpoint, as the arrangement had required since a hearing room fixed its terms two years ago, in the assessment office's standard form, over the standard channel, in Seln's standard hand.

*Subject's competitive activity conducted within enrollment basis. Demonstration exhibitions per charter provisions; performance ratings within expected parameters. No undisclosed capability observed. Season continues per schedule. Routine.*

True. Complete in form. Empty — the office's finest work of the season, filed on time, in triplicate, saying nothing at all.

# Chapter 24 — Placement

The closing ceremony ran on the last morning, under the same hard spring light the finals had owned, and the Concourse filled one final time for the tournament's oldest liturgy: the standings read whole, the standards raised, the banner passed.

The liturgy ran in the charter's order, which was the reverse of the standings, because the tournament's oldest instruction held that a placement is heard properly only after everything beneath it has been heard first. The presiding official read from fourteenth. Each delegation came to the floor's mark as its number was called, and stood while its academy's standard went up on the mast assigned to it, and the Concourse applauded every one of them for the same measured count, which the charter also specified and the stewards also enforced. Cael counted the count. It was eight. Eight for fourteenth, eight for ninth, eight for third — the fairest instrument on the continent extending its fairness as far down as the clapping.

Fifty minutes to reach the third mark, and the fifty minutes were the ceremony's whole argument. Every academy heard by name. Every placement read as a fact rather than a verdict. No delegation on the floor permitted to be smaller than its number, and none permitted to be larger.

Rhagen took the fourth mark two places ahead of them and took it well: the institute in black-and-glass, standing at attention under a standard that would hang all cycle in a hall where every one of them would know exactly what it had cost to miss third by an exchange. Their coach's face, at thirty feet, was doing what Rooke's had done after the semifinal, which was arithmetic.

Then Halcenvane. Their standard went up on the Concourse's honor mast — silver-stamped, the tournament's own arithmetic rendered in cloth — and the delegation stood on the main floor for it in their plain colors while the presiding official read the placement into the record: *third of fourteen, Halcenvane Academy*, four syllables and a number retiring eighteen years.

The delegation performed the standing the way each of them performed everything, and Cael, in the line's last place, catalogued his people at the year's summit because the ledger would want it exactly. Withrow watched the standard rise with her hands folded and her face doing nothing at all — the spending already done, at a table, in a toast the log held — and beside her Rooke watched it the way coaches watch flags, counting the cohorts it would recruit. Brom stood at parade weight with the Copper plaque's ribbon at his collar, evidence at rest. Karis had the notebook out and did not open it once, which was its own entry. Ephram, when the standard reached the mast's head, said something to himself that Cael read off his lips because the instrument never rested — *counted* — the whole year in one word borrowed from a chancellor. And Lira looked up at the silver-stamped cloth with her jaw set and her eyes bright and said, aloud, to the delegation line, in the voice she had once used to demand a ledger prove her: "Eighteen years they didn't come. Three years we did. Somebody write the Fenmark winter tables and tell them what the scrapyard placed."

"Noted," said Karis, opening the notebook after all, and did.

Then the banner.

The Silver Standard came down from the champion's gate with the tournament's full ceremony — the color-guard, the old forms, the whole Concourse standing — and passed to Auremont for another cycle, the reigning academy receiving back the object it had defended.

The transfer itself was the charter's oldest surviving procedure, and it took nine minutes, all of them mechanical and none of them hurried, and Cael watched every step of it with the attention he gave instruments. The gate's staff was drawn from its socket by two color-guards of the outgoing academy and carried level to the floor's center mark — never raised, never dipped, the charter being specific about both. There the banner was struck from the staff by the tournament's own hand, the presiding official working the sleeve's four ties in the order the text names, which is bottom, top, and then the two middles together, and no other order has been lawful since the text was written. The cloth was folded once lengthwise and once across and laid in the transfer case, which was pine and three centuries newer than what went into it. Two registrars countersigned the transfer at a table set on the floor, in ink, while the entire assembly stood and watched them write — the single most Valdrisian thing Cael had ever seen a crowd get to its feet for: a full Concourse standing in silence for a countersignature.

Then the case was opened again at Auremont's mark, and the banner re-sleeved on Auremont's staff by Auremont's hands, and raised, and the procession bore it once around the main floor's perimeter per the charter's oldest instruction, so that the whole assembly could see the mark's own flag at eye level, silver on grey, three hundred years of the fairest instrument on the continent walking past on a staff.

The last image the year gave him of the object was the one the rail gave, and he took it standing at the delegation's block with the left side still wrapped and the frameworks still resettling: the banner carried past him at twenty feet — the fairest instrument ever built, in the keeping of the one delegation on the continent whose first fighter had felt, from inside a single exchange, exactly what it could not hold — and as the procession drew level, Daeva broke it.

Four seconds. She stepped out of the color-guard's formation — a breach of ceremony that would cost her a program citation she would never read — crossed to the rail, and stopped in front of him with the banner's shadow moving on behind her.

"Rematch," she said.

An appointment, not a challenge — the word delivered in the flat professional register of a colleague confirming terms, with, underneath it, arriving the way spring arrives, the thing the woodcut artists had finally learned to draw. The near tiers caught the breach and made a sound; the broadsides would run four separate accounts of the four seconds by morning, each wronger than the last; and she was back in the procession before the color-guard's sergeant had finished deciding whether anything had happened, and the Standard went on around the floor and up to the champion's gate, and the cycle closed.

---

The ledger of the year ran that evening, in its established forms, at the guesting-house table and the window after, because years that ended wanted closing out properly and he had the volume for it now.

He did it properly, because the volume deserved procedure and because procedure was where he kept the things he had no other way of holding. The volume had been a birthday gift in midwinter and had not been opened past its fourth leaf all season; he opened it now to the first clean recto, ruled the margin with the straight edge of the meet program for want of a rule, dated the head of the page, and set the pen down for a moment before using it, which was a thing he did once a year and had never written about.

Then the inventory, in acquisition order rather than importance order, because importance was a judgment and the log did not keep judgments in the inventory column.

The Power Log inventory, in Hesk's book, in the form the log had kept since a boarding-house window in Ardenmere:

*Seven confirmed fragments. Wind-adjacent — Lira. Pressure-adjacent — Feryn. Iron-adjacent — Brom. Compression-adjacent — Reydan. Ember-adjacent — Karis. Shadow-adjacent — held sealed through the most public season of my life; deployment still none, anywhere, ever; the debt still unpaid and still unspoken. Storm-adjacent — Daeva. First fragment from a Gold-equivalent source. First notice to carry a stability flag, and the flag has earned its keep: integration cost logged honestly across the post-match week — the frameworks resettling slower than after any acquisition before it, the Wind economy still returning to rate, the whole architecture re-seating around the new mass like a house settling on a heavier stone. Gold architecture is heavy. The ledger notes it the way Hesk would: a true instrument records its own strain.*

*One anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent. Turned over once at the year's close, per custom. Still open. Still real. Patience.*

*One log line for an old promise, because the accounts of this match will travel everywhere, including to an Iron-tier Rank 8 with a standing question: Reydan will read the accounts. It still isn't the answer. Not yet.*

He sanded the page when it was done — Bracken's habit, borrowed off a records-hall counter two years ago and never given back — and let it stand a minute before he turned the leaf, because ink laid over wet ink is a lie about when a thing was written.

The letters went north with the last of Bracken's pouch, and the writing of them took the evening the ceremony left, at the guesting-house window, in the season's last quiet. Bracken's collection ran at the eighth bell and not a minute past it, which meant the evening had a shape, and Cael worked to the shape, three sheets ruled and stacked before he wrote a word on any of them.

To Hesk, the tournament told straight, the way the man preferred: the whole account from roster to referral, the clause and the qualifying arithmetic, the brackets and the trial, the challenge with its one-word reason, the four exchanges rendered at the log's own precision — and the entry, copied exact, because his grandfather was owed the eleven words in their original type. He wrote the match honestly, the stumble included, the stoppage included, because Hesk had taught him that an instrument-maker reads the strain gauge first and the polish never. Ephram put his head round the door somewhere in the fourth page, saw the volume and the spread sheets and the face above them, and withdrew without saying anything, which from Ephram was the most eloquent act of his year.

It ran six pages, the longest letter of his life, and he ended it with the only sentence the whole season had been for: *I don't know what I am. The difference this year is that now the continent doesn't know either, in writing, and the not-knowing is finally standing where I can see it.* The reply came back before the delegation had even cleared its floors — fast as ever, the page half-written before the letter finished traveling — and Cael read it four times and filed it behind the volume's front cover, where such things lived:

*Third on the continent, and the judges wrote "no number goes here." Your grandmother would have had that framed. Seventeen, unmeasured, and true. Come home when the road allows. — H.*

To Vell, because her ledger still held his first honest record and she would want the tournament told straight too: the season, the ratings, the match, the entry — *unscorable under standard* copied exact, because the ledger-keeper of Ardenmere would understand better than any living person what it had cost an instrument's keeper to write it, and would enter it, he had no doubt, cross-referenced, beside a circuit rating from a smaller honest book. He told her about Umber — the seal verified in public, the blank chosen over the lie — because the two of them belonged to the same scattered guild of pattern-holders and she would want to know the guild had held at continental scale, and he closed with the line she had earned across four years of stamped receipts: *Your ledger rated me before any of theirs could. It's still the one I check the others against.*

Brom's advancement review stood scheduled and pending at the season's close — the registry's summons waiting at the district seat on the road home, the continental Copper title's mandatory machinery unresolved on the page by design — and Brom's whole comment on it, packing the plaque into his kit beside a one-line letter, was: "Summer's problem."

And Lira stood at the rail on the last evening, watching Auremont's quarter of the city where the banner now hung.

She had been there an hour when he found her. The harbor was going dark below and the lamps were coming up in the pattern a city makes when half of it has already gone home, and her wrists were untaped for the first time in three weeks, which was its own small posting.

"Say the number," she said, without turning.

"Which number?"

"Mine. Off Zerin. You counted it. You count everything."

"Four flags and an exchange," Cael said. "She came into the tournament having conceded nothing. She left your bout having conceded that."

"Four flags and an exchange." She turned it over the way she turned over a floor. "I lost. I want that on the record, because I've watched people lose and then spend a year describing it as a different event, and I'm not going to be one of them. She was better. She's been better since she was twelve and she'll be better next year." Her hands stayed flat on the rail. "But she had to spend for it. Nobody else made her spend. And every fighter who made me look slow this season is going up a bracket, and I would rather lose to them there than win the Iron bracket twice and call it a career."

She said the last of it plainly, the way she said the things that had cost the most, and then she gave the harbor the appointment her arc had made in a torchlit court after her semifinal: "Silver bracket. Next cycle." Nobody at the rail said the other sentence, the one a man in inspection greys had already said in a borrowed office, about whether the official channels would last that long. The rail had its own discipline. The building continued.

The road home from Norhold ran seventeen days in reverse, and the delegation's two wagons took it loaded with a season — the standard, the plaque, the files, the kit — and the six of them, because it was six now.

The sixth's arrangement had been conducted, like everything about him, as administration: the assessment office's clerk traveling with the delegation he served, a line in the travel manifest, unremarkable on its face.

It had gone through in four days and three signatures, none of which were remarkable either. The assessment office authorized the clerk's travel on the grounds that the delegation's floor allocations for the coming term were the clerk's own work and could be drafted as well on a road as at a counter. The registrar noted the addition to the manifest. The Compact's regional office was informed, as the Compact's regional office was informed of everything, in a return of two lines that came back acknowledged by a stamp and nothing else. Cael watched the entire procedure assemble across a week from a distance of about eight feet and said nothing to anybody about it, the man conducting it included, because the procedure was the point: a thing done wholly inside the rules, in daylight, on paper, that nobody could object to and anybody could read, and which quietly added a chair to a table. What it was instead declared itself at the first waystation's table, on the first evening of the road, and the table let it declare itself without help. The delegation came in from the wagons to the long board by the fire, and sorted itself into the configuration three years had built — Lira claiming the bench, Brom acquiring food from a kitchen that had not offered any, Karis at the corner with the light, Ephram narrating the day's stage to anyone fool enough to sit near him, Cael at the wall-seat with the room in view — and Seln came in last with the day's pouch, and did not take the counter seat, or the end seat, or any of the margin's seats that a year of tournament tables had made his stations.

He sat at the middle of the bench. At the table. Among them, in the seat the configuration had been holding open without ever once saying so, and reached for the bread, and Lira passed him the stew as though he had always sat there, because as far as the table's true ledger ran, he had.

Nobody remarked on it, at that waystation or any of the sixteen after. Everybody counted it, every night, the way you count the members of a family at a door.

There was one mechanical fact underneath the arrangement that nobody said aloud either, and Cael filed it on the third night with the fire going down. The manifest listed the sixth man against *records and floor scheduling* — the same nine words that had listed him on a covered walk's notice board at the season's open, in a hand that had not been asked to change them. They were still true. They were still complete in form. Somewhere between a bluff road and a waystation bench they had stopped being empty, and no clerk anywhere on the continent had been required to amend a single character to make that happen. *The best paperwork in the world describes what a thing was,* the log noted. *It has never once been able to stop a thing from becoming something else while the description sat there being accurate.*

On the eleventh night Ephram gave the room his account of the third-place trial's second exchange, at length, with improvements, and from the end of the bench the delegation's assessment clerk corrected the sequence of two events in it from memory, flatly, without looking up from the pouch, and went back to the pouch. Ephram stared at the side of his head for some while. Nobody explained anything to him, because nothing had happened that required explaining.

Far to the west and south, at an altitude the season's papers never named, a chamber with no windows convened, and what escaped it, ever, was thirty seconds' worth — a scrap of minute, a participant's late account, the shape of the thing assembled afterward by people who spent years at the assembling: a formal convening, senior officials, most of them faceless by design — the faceless being the point of such rooms — and on the table, read into the record by a clerk whose voice had been chosen for its lack of qualities, the question:

*Whether the classification [SHATTERED] shall be designated an active threat category.*

The tournament record was cited. The unscorable entry was cited — the adjudication office's eleven words, traveled all this way to be evidence. Testimony was heard from the evaluator of record, who had come in person, and who gave it standing, with his name signed plainly to every word, in the register of a man spending the last of something:

"You are proposing to classify a boy as a threat category because your instruments cannot classify him as anything else. I have evaluated the instruments. The finding is theirs, not his."

He was heard with courtesy. The testimony was entered, and set aside, the way rooms of that altitude set things aside — without rebuttal, by continuing. An official far down the table cited, as precedent for elevated handling, the file's existing designation: Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4 — standing authority, the official noted, for treating the matter above ordinary process. And the room encountered, briefly, the small administrative discomfort it had been encountering for seven years without ever once holding it to the light: asked, for the minute's completeness, which office had authorized the designation, no one present could say. The registry's own sub-layer carried it; its authorizing instrument was not of record; the trace, a junior secretariat noted, had been attempted and closed. The room absorbed the absence the way institutions absorb absences — a pause, an entry, a moving-on — because the designation was *useful*, and useful things at that altitude did not require parents.

No vote was taken. The question was *entered for formal proceedings* — the machinery's patient phrase for a conclusion that had chosen its road and not yet its schedule — and the session adjourned, the officials dispersing to their unnamed offices, the clerk gathering the minute, the lamps going out in order. What remained, last, was the file on the table: the same file that had been growing since a Kindling in a border town three years ago and a flag no living hand had authorized four centuries before that — the watch, the referrals, the seasons, the entry with no number — sitting in an empty room with nothing decided.

Nothing decided, and everything entered. The machine's most patient sentence, waiting for its schedule.

The road gave the season its decompression at the season's own pace. The first days ran through tournament country still — waystations flying the silver-on-grey, innkeepers who recognized the plain colors and would not take full price, twice a rider overtaking the column with a broadside the city had printed after their leaving (the interpretations blooming on schedule, every one of them wrong, the word that mattered appearing in none of them). By the middle days the tournament thinned out of the landscape and the country went back to being country, and the delegation went back, mile by mile, to being what it had been before the continent learned its name: a company on a road, kit mended at evening fires, Ephram's harbor songs mangled into hill-country keys, Rooke and Gault conducting a seventeen-day argument about training calendars that both men plainly intended never to resolve. Cael's frameworks resettled a day at a time — the log tracked it at entry length, the Wind economy returning to rate, the left side unwrapping to yellow and then to nothing, the new mass seating itself into the architecture with the slow patience of masonry.

The waystations kept their own record of the season, and Cael read it the way he read notice boards. In tournament country the boards by the doors still carried the finals fixture in chalk nobody had bothered to scrape, and twice an innkeeper's wife came out to look at the plain colors without saying anything and went back in. By the sixth waystation the boards carried grain prices. By the tenth they carried nothing at all, and the man behind the counter asked, civilly, where the wagons had come from, and heard Norhold, and said he'd heard there had been something on down there this spring, and went to draw the beer. Ephram considered a correction and, to the table's visible admiration, declined to make one.

The watchers were back by the twelfth day, two at the far edge of a market town, rotation faces he knew, and he counted them from the wagon bench with something that was nearly nostalgia. *The apparatus resumes its old grammar,* the log noted. *Two men and a horse-change, after a city of eight thousand. It reads almost quaint now. Note, and keep the note where the warning lives: Vastin's whole hour said the old grammar is ending. Enjoy the rotation men. They may be the last watchers I get whose names I could learn.*

The district seat came on the ninth day, and the registry counter there held a summons with Brom's name on it that had been waiting eleven days for a wagon to bring the man it was addressed to.

He read it standing, at the counter, in the time four lines take, and then read it again, which for Brom constituted a scene. The clerk waited with the receipt-book open. Brom signed for it, folded it into thirds along its own creases, and put it into the inside pocket where the letter from Velmere lived, so that the two pieces of paper still capable of moving his life were travelling in the same place.

Outside, the column had the horses watered and nobody asked him anything, because three years had taught the delegation that Brom's news arrived on Brom's schedule and improved with waiting.

"Mandatory advancement review," he said eventually, to the wagon in general, buckling a trace he had not unbuckled. "Continental title triggers it. Panel sits at the district seat within ninety days of the season's close and considers formal advancement to Iron tier."

"*Considers*," said Karis, already writing.

"Considers," Brom agreed. "It isn't a promotion. It's a hearing about whether I get offered one." He checked the trace a second time, which he never did. "I've been Copper four years. I know what Copper costs, what it buys, and where every seam in it sits, because I put four years into finding them. Iron is a new floor with new seams and no map."

"You'd have ninety days to draw one," Lira said.

"I'd have ninety days to decide whether I want one drawn."

Nobody pushed it, because nobody at that column had ever pushed him and nobody had ever needed to. Karis wrote one line and did not read it out. The summons rode eight more days in the pocket beside the letter, unopened again and unresolved, which was the whole of what the year intended to say on the subject.

Karis closed the season's file on the fourteenth day at a waystation table, and made a ceremony of it that consisted entirely of declining to make one: the year's paper squared and sorted — the clause, the qualifying correspondence, the four meets, the protest rulings, the panel charts, the entry — tied into two bundles, a contents leaf written for each, both set into the case. "A hundred and forty sheets," she said. "Nine of them matter. I won't know which nine for two years, so all hundred and forty travel."

And Ephram spent the last three days of the road auditioning ways to tell it at Halcenvane and discarding every one of them, and arrived somewhere past the second-to-last ferry at the plain version, which he tried on the wagon bench and then kept: "We went, and we placed, and the arithmetic's on a mast where anyone can go and read it. That's the story. Anybody who wants it longer can buy me dinner."

The wagons crossed the last ferry on the seventeenth day, and Ostrand came up the valley the way home came up — brown-and-slate, the Ost running wide and slow, the bluff above it with its single architectural intention catching the late sun — and the delegation rode the climb quiet, the way you ride the last mile of a long year.

"I have seven things that aren't a Path," Cael said, eventually, to nobody in particular, the words arriving the way his best entries arrived — not planned, just finally true enough to say out loud. "A match record no instrument can hold, and five people who watched them fail to score me."

He turned it over once and let it stand.

"And somewhere above all of it, a room full of careful people deciding what I am. Again."

"They're behind schedule," said Lira. "You already decided."

"Years ago," said Brom.

Karis, writing: "Noted."

Down the table of the wagon's bench, the man who used to file reports about him watched the road and said nothing, which — from him — was agreement.

The standard was silver. He wasn't anything the silver could measure. The road home ran through a continent that now knew it — and somewhere behind them, in a room with no windows, the machine began deciding what to do about what it couldn't measure.

# Chapter 1 — Roster

He closed out the summer the way he closed out everything, at the window, with the pen, on the last night before the year began.

*Recess. Twelve weeks, four days. Closed.*

*Training: sixty-one floor sessions, logged separately. The working ceiling on Wind-adjacent held at six repetitions and did not move, which after a season of trying to move it is a finding and not a failure: six is what the architecture buys at my current cost structure, and the cost structure is the thing to work on, not the number. Landing beat unshortened. Year four of that entry.*

*The campus at quarter enrollment: quieter than I have ever heard a place I lived in. The fountain in the residence court runs louder when there is nobody to talk over it. I know because I counted the weeks by it.*

*Letters: nine from Hesk, which for twelve weeks is a rate. He has decided the tournament year deserves correspondence on a war footing. The last one closed: "Sixteen and a season coming. Sleep now. You won't later." I have taken half of that advice.*

*The watchers: rotation unchanged the entire recess. Two minimum at the bluff road, three on market days in Ostrand, the change at the second waystation holding to its schedule like a ferry. Whoever pays them pays by the year and expects nothing weekly, which tells me the budget line has stopped being anybody's decision and become somebody's habit. Habits are cheaper than decisions. They are also harder to cancel.*

*Seln: at his desk the whole recess. The assessment office runs a skeleton counter in summer and he was the skeleton, every working day, processing floor allocations for an academy with nobody on its floors. Nobody remarked on it. Everybody I live with noticed.*

*Karis: built the argument. That is the whole entry and it is a large one. The clause has sat banked since the last week of last term, exactly as agreed, and she has spent twelve weeks constructing the case around it the way Brom builds a defense — no single piece worth attacking, the whole thing worth avoiding. She says it will stand cross-examination now. She says it the way she says things she has already cross-examined herself.*

*Inventory at year's open, for the record, because the year is going to ask: six confirmed fragments. Wind-adjacent, Pressure-adjacent, Iron-adjacent, Compression-adjacent, Ember-adjacent, Shadow-adjacent. One anomaly, session nine, unreproduced, uncounted. One semester stamp, formally renewed, filed at a records hall on this bluff and mirrored at a registry I have never seen.*

*I am sixteen years old and I am enrolled at a chartered academy that intends to be measured against the whole continent this year, and the honest last line of the recess is this: for the first time since Denvash, I do not know what my part in the season is. Everyone else's part has a bracket. Open the year and find out.*

He read it back once, capped the pen, and went to bed with the window open, because the fountain had earned it.

---

Convocation at Halcenvane happened in the lecture complex on the first morning of the year, all faculty and the whole enrollment, and it was the only assembly of the calendar at which Chancellor Withrow spoke first rather than last.

Cael arrived early with the observation notebook and took the seat he had used all last year, four rows up on the north aisle, where the light from the tall windows crossed the stage at an angle and you could read a speaker's papers by the way she squared them. He counted the hall as it filled, out of habit and because the count itself was information: enrollment up perhaps forty on last year, and the new intake sitting the way new intakes sat, in a block, near the back, unsure yet which distances mattered.

Lira dropped into the seat beside him with her collar still damp from the yard. She had trained before convocation. She had trained before everything, all summer, and the certification had changed nothing about that except the direction of the work.

"Rooke posted the morning assignments," she said. "I'm running his speed line at second bell. Me, Ephram, two of the third-years."

"His speed line ran at dawn last year."

"His speed line ran at dawn last year for people he was still pricing." She said it without any weight, which was how she said the things that had cost the most. "He's done pricing me."

Brom arrived with bread from somewhere, because Brom had located the kitchens' soft perimeter within a week of enrolling and had spent a year cultivating it, and Karis arrived exactly when Karis arrived, which was on time to the minute with the notebook already out.

Withrow came to the lectern with one page and did not look at it.

"Welcome to the year," she said. "I am going to do the ordinary business in eight minutes and then I am going to tell you what this year is, so that nobody spends the autumn pretending they don't know."

The ordinary business ran eight minutes. Cael timed it. Term dates, hall assignments, two staff appointments, the fire-watch ledger — which drew a small sound from the enrollees who had been present for the inspection year, and which Withrow acknowledged with precisely one raised eyebrow and no pause.

Then she put the page down.

"Last spring I told you Halcenvane would contest the qualifying season of the Continental Academy Tournament. Some of you have spent the summer treating that as an ambition. Correct that this morning. It is a schedule — and it is a schedule this institution keeps with assets it has never held before: a formally Iron-tier Wind champion, certified at a registry station on demonstration and record; a repaired Iron Skin cohort standout the continental Copper bracket has not yet priced; an Iron Rank 3 Ember researcher who reads charters the way coaches read film; and the most-watched enrollee on the continent.

"The qualifying season opens in five weeks at the first regional meet. There are four meets in our region across the autumn and winter. Standing at those meets determines which academies cross the continental threshold, and the academies that cross it go in the spring to the finals, which this cycle are held at Norhold." She let the name sit for a moment. "Most of you have never seen Norhold. It is a crossroads city four times the size of Ostrand, and every three years it hosts the oldest surviving inter-institutional competition on the continent, and this institution has not qualified for it in eighteen years.

"Three sentences about that number, which is two more than it deserves. Eighteen years ago this academy fielded a side selected by seniority and sentiment, in a season it treated as a reward, and it finished where sides selected that way finish. I have read the file. We will not be conducting ourselves that way, and the difference will be visible from the first meet.

"The fielded side is drawn from the enrollment record under the tournament charter's own rules, which Registrar Bracken will administer to the letter — and I choose the phrase deliberately." She did not look at anyone in particular when she said it, which Cael filed, because Withrow's refusals to look were aimed. "Selection standards, meet schedules, and travel rosters go up on the covered walk's board at second bell. Coach Rooke has the training calendar. The season is long, the continent is watching the standings and not the intentions, and I will make the same request of you I made during the inspection, since it worked: be exactly what we say we are.

"Go to your assignments."

The hall emptied the way halls empty when they have been given something to carry. Cael stayed in his seat a moment, watching the stage, where Withrow was saying something brief to Rooke, and Bracken was already gone — which meant Bracken had somewhere to be, and Cael thought he knew where, because the enrollment record lived in the records hall, and the season had just been declared open.

Karis, standing, followed his look.

"Today," she said. "He files it today or tomorrow. The regional registrar's office closes intake at the end of next week, and Bracken is not a man who uses the last days of a window."

"You've talked to him."

"I've talked to him four times since midsummer." She put the notebook away. "Every conversation was about something else. All four of them were about this."

---

The delegation postings went up at second bell and the four of them read the board in the covered walk with half the academy pressed in behind them.

The travel roster for the first meet ran to eleven names: five competitors, Rooke, Gault, two third-year reserves, a wagoner, and a clerk of the assessment office, S. Seln, listed against the words *records and floor scheduling*, which was true, complete in form, and empty, and Cael read the line twice and did not smile, because smiling at a notice board was the kind of thing that got remembered.

The competitor list read: *L. Lira, Iron. B. Brom, Copper. K. Karis, Iron. E. Ephram, Iron. C. Cael — see demonstration provision, enrollment basis, filed.*

The crowd around the board did what crowds did with that last line, which was to read it aloud to each other in four different tones of voice, none of them hostile and all of them loud. A second-year Cael did not know asked the walk in general what a demonstration provision was. Somebody else said it was the thing from the assay hearing. A third voice, older, said with the confidence of complete error that it meant Cael fought exhibition bouts for the panel's entertainment and no standings, and had it half right, and Cael let it stand, because a rumor that was half right was a fence somebody else had built for free.

Ephram arrived, read the board once, and rendered the year's first verdict from the cohort's standing third.

"Eleven names and a legal citation," he said. "Every academy on the circuit gets a roster. We get a roster and a reading assignment." He shouldered his kit. "Good. Let them do the reading."

---

The roster submission itself happened on the fourth day of term, in the records hall, at the hour after the morning session, and it was the quietest large thing Cael had ever watched.

He was there because Bracken had sent for him, which Bracken did the way other men signed forms — as procedure, with a time on it. Karis was there because Karis had been there for every stage of the architecture since the charter had come off the shelf. Nobody else was there at all, which was itself a decision: Withrow had declined to make an event of it, and Cael understood the decline as strategy. Events invite witnesses. Procedures invite filing.

The submission bundle lay on the counter in a document wallet with the academy's seal on the flap: the tournament entry instrument, the fee note, the meet schedule acknowledgment, and beneath them, the thing the charter actually asked for — a certified true copy of Halcenvane Academy's enrollment record, complete, current to the first of the month.

Bracken laid the enrollment copy open to its last leaf, where the certifying language ran above his signature block, and read it through one final time at his counter's own pace. Cael read it upside down, which he was better at than most people were right side up.

*I certify this record complete and true, containing every practitioner enrolled at this institution on the date given, without omission.*

"Without omission," Bracken said, not looking up. "Four decades of certifying enrollment records and I never once had occasion to think about those two words. They're boilerplate. Every registrar on the continent signs them forty times a year." He inked the pen. "Boilerplate is just law nobody has needed yet."

He signed, sanded the signature with the small habitual motion of a man who had used sand thirty years past anyone's need for it, and closed the wallet.

"For the avoidance of doubt," he said, to Cael, "and because you are a person who keeps records, I will say to your face what the document says to the registrar's office. This academy's enrollment record contains your name because you are enrolled, on a lawful basis, adjudicated at public record, renewed at evaluation this spring. The tournament charter requires me to submit the enrollment record. I have submitted the enrollment record. At no point this morning have I made a decision about you, because no decision about you was mine to make, and I have been careful — " and here he looked up, and there was something underneath the dryness, old and entirely settled — "very careful, to make no decision anywhere in this file that was not mine to make. That is what they will look for, when they look. They will find a registrar doing his job in his sleep."

"Thank you," Cael said.

"Don't thank me for certifying a true record. The day that earns thanks, the institution earning it has larger problems." He tied the wallet's tape. "The courier goes at noon."

They watched the courier go, as it happened, from the top of the bluff road — not by arrangement; Karis had a free hour and Cael had a route he walked when he wanted the valley in front of him instead of walls. The rider took the switchbacks at a working trot, the document case flat against the saddlebag's spine, and was small and then very small, and the ferry took him across the Ost like any other item of a market day, and that was all. Somewhere downstream, in an office in the regional seat, a clerk would break the seal, and log the entry, and read eleven names, and stop at the fifth one.

"You know what I keep coming back to," Karis said. She had the notebook out but wasn't writing, which was rare enough that he waited. "Everyone is going to say we did something today. The registrar filed a record. That's the whole act. We didn't put you on the roster. We just declined to commit fraud by leaving you off. The clause does the rest."

Cael turned that over, standing at the rail with the river below carrying its barges through the arithmetic of an ordinary noon. He had spent a year at Greyvane learning what text could hold, and a year here learning what a record could carry, and the sentence had the load-bearing shape he had come to recognize the way a builder recognized a wall that would take a roof: no ornament, no reach. The record was true. The clause was mandatory. Everything that followed would follow from other people's language, faithfully applied.

"They'll call it a maneuver," he said.

"Of course they will. A maneuver is what you call it when the law lands somewhere you didn't want it." She wrote one line and closed the notebook. "The best part — and I say this as the person who spent twelve weeks proofing it — is that there is no step to object to. Object to the enrollment: adjudicated, twice, public record. Object to the record: it's true, and the alternative is asking Bracken to falsify it. Object to the clause: it's their charter. Every road into this ends with somebody explaining, in writing, which true thing they would have preferred to be false."

"Karis."

"Mm."

"Twelve weeks. The whole recess."

"Ten, honestly. Two were the tournament's procedural annexes, which needed reading anyway." She looked out at the river the way she looked at documents. "You'd have done it for me."

He had no answer to that better than agreement, so he gave her agreement, and they walked back up the road to an academy that had, by the plain operation of its own paperwork, just aimed him at a continent.

---

The assessment office's counter opened at the third bell, and Cael went down on the fifth day with the term's floor-allocation form, because enrollees requesting evaluation-linked floor time filed at the counter, and because the counter was where Seln was.

There was a queue of four ahead of him — the season had turned floor time into currency overnight, and half the academy had discovered a sudden need for supervised hours — and Cael used the queue the way he used every queue, which was to count the room. Two clerks working. Six form types in the wall rack, one of them new: a buff-colored sheet headed *Tournament Travel — Equipment & Floor Declarations*, which had not existed a week ago and which meant somebody in this office had spent the recess building the season's paperwork before the season was announced. He knew who. The knowledge went nowhere near his face.

Seln processed the queue at the speed of a man who had never once been asked to hurry and had never once needed to be — a Bronze-tier assessment clerk, Shadow Path per the registry's plain entry, which was the whole of what the registry knew and precisely as much as it was ever going to. When Cael reached the counter he took the form, read it entirely — he read every form entirely, from everyone, which was the camouflage; a man who reads everything is remembered for reading nothing in particular — and stamped the received line.

"Evaluation-linked hours are countersigned by the evaluator of record," he said. "Magister Gault countersigns on Fifth-days. The floor schedule posts the morning after."

"Thank you for the form."

"The office notes the travel roster has you at all four regional meets." Seln squared the sheet against the counter's edge, aligned it with the stack, and delivered the only sentence of the exchange that was not printed on anything. "Pack for the third one. The halls at the third one are cold."

"Noted."

That was the whole of it. Cael walked back up the covered walk with the copy-slip in his coat and filed the exchange where he filed all of Seln's exchanges, in the ledger that never touched paper: *the third meet is the one to watch, for a reason he can't say and I can't ask.* Twelve weeks at a summer counter, and the man had come out of it with the season already read. There was no one at Halcenvane — there was possibly no one on the continent — whose spoken sentences carried a higher freight-to-word ratio, and Cael had stopped being unsettled by it somewhere in the spring, and had never stopped being careful with it.

*Gratitude is the leak,* he had written last year, after the corridor. The entry still governed. He said thank you for the form, and nothing else, every week, exactly.

---

Gault's countersignature session that Fifth-day came with an item of actual news attached.

The evaluator of record had aged another year into the assay provision's machinery the way a man ages into a chair that fits him: what had begun, two years ago at Greyvane, as the most scrutinized procedure in the registry's regional history was now a calendar. Sessions logged, panel minutes filed, the semester evaluation scheduled for the spring — Cael's third, and the first that would have to be conducted somewhere other than this records hall.

"The panel travels," Gault said, signing. "That's the news. Withrow raised it with the registry's district office and the district office, wonder of the age, agreed without a hearing: the enrollment basis travels with the enrollee, therefore the evaluation machinery travels with the enrollment. If the season runs long — " he did not say *if you qualify*, which Cael noted, because Gault chose his conditionals like instruments — "the spring evaluation convenes wherever the delegation is. I have been issued a travel case for the seals. It has a handle. I find I am absurdly pleased about the handle."

"Will the observers travel too?"

"The Compact seats observers at tournament meets as a matter of course. Registry validation of tier results." Gault blotted the line. "They will not need to travel to see you. You are going to them."

Cael took that downstairs and stood with it in the yard for a while.

Two years of the arrangement's geometry, inverted in a sentence. Since Ardenmere the pattern had run one direction: he stayed put, and the apparatus came to him — wardens to a market town, assessors to a circuit hall, a delegation up a bluff road. The watchers outside the walls were the pattern's standing proof, paid by the year to sit at the bottom of a hill because the hill was where he was.

Five weeks from now he got into a wagon and drove into the middle of the instrument.

*They will not need to travel to see you. You are going to them.* He wrote it in the observation notebook under Gault's name and dated it, and beside it, because the honest ledger ran both columns, he wrote the other side: *and for one season, everyone they send is accounted for in advance — seated, badged, listed in a meet program. I have spent three years counting watchers from windows. This year the tournament prints me the list.*

---

Rooke's first full-squad session ran that evening on the second training floor, and Rooke opened it with the training calendar pinned to the boards and a speech that lasted six sentences.

"Five weeks to the first meet. The brackets are seeded by formal tier, which means the four of you fight in the boxes your paper puts you in, and the paper is right about exactly two of you." He did not say which two, and everyone knew, and knowing was the point. "Lira, Karis, Ephram: Iron bracket, regional field, profiles on the table by the door — learn them like coursework. Brom: Copper bracket. I have nothing to teach you about a Copper bracket except patience, and you already keep yours somewhere I can't reach it.

"Cael." Rooke looked at him for a moment with the expression of a coach reading a training problem no manual covered. "The meet hosts schedule demonstration exhibitions for you. Volunteer opponents, performance scoring, no standings. I can't scout volunteers who haven't volunteered. So your calendar says one word, and the word is: ready." He turned to the boards. "Floor work. Go."

The session ran two hours, and it told Cael more about the season than the convocation had.

He worked the evasion frames against Ephram first — who was faster than last year, and knew it, and made the knowing useful instead of loud. Three passes in, Ephram changed his entry timing without telegraphing it, caught Cael a half-beat deep on the recovery, and scored a touch that would have been clean at any tier. He stepped back, reset, and said, "That's the cohort's second-year entry. Everyone in the regional Iron bracket who trained at a continental-line academy has it. You'll see it forty times this season," and ran it again, slower, so Cael could take it apart.

That was the shape of the whole evening. The squad was not training; it was translating — Rooke and Ephram between them converting a year of cohort doctrine into the regional field's actual vocabulary, opponent by opponent, and the profiles table by the door was the dictionary. Cael read it during the water break the way he read everything, fast and twice. Forty-one names across the regional Iron and Copper brackets, each with a Path, a rank, an academy, and Rooke's marginal hand rendering verdicts at three words a fighter: *fast, brittle late. Honest, readable. Beat her twice, she learns.* Karis stood beside him reading the same pages and copying none of them, which meant she had decided the table would travel with the delegation and could be read properly later, and was spending the break instead on the two names Rooke had marked with nothing at all — a Wind Path Iron entry from the eastern academies with no notation, and a Copper from a school Cael didn't know. "The blanks are the interesting ones," she said. "A blank means Rooke hasn't priced them, and Rooke prices everyone."

Brom worked the far floor alone through all of it, unhurried, against the striking frame — not conditioning, Cael saw after a while, but inventory: he was running his whole defensive structure top to bottom, seam by repaired seam, the way a man checks a wagon before a long road. He finished, coiled the straps, and delivered his review of the training calendar to the room in general: "Five weeks is enough." Nobody asked what it was enough for. From Brom, the sentence was a completed thought.

Afterward, in the emptying hall, Lira sat down next to him against the wall with her wrists still taped.

"First meet," she said. "Five weeks. You realize this is the first season since Ardenmere where all of us are on the same card."

"I'd worked it out."

"Vell's circuit, you fought and I fought, different nights half the time. Greyvane, half of it was hearings. Last year was the ladder, and the ladder was yours and Brom's." She flexed her hands, watching the tape. "This is everybody. Same wagons, same halls, same season. Whatever it turns out to be, it happens to all of us at once."

"That's the part I keep arriving at," Cael said, and it was.

He wrote the log by lamplight with the residence court quiet below and the fountain running its one continuous sentence, and he kept the entry short, because the season was going to be long.

*Roster filed at noon, eleven names, mine the fifth.*

*Season's open. The tournament rules say an academy fields what it enrolls. I'm enrolled. Somewhere a committee is about to have a very bad week, and for once, none of it will be my argument. It's just their own text, arriving.*

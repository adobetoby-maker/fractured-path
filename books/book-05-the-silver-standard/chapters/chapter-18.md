# Chapter 18 — Daeva

She had been thirteen years old, and the Arbiter had come a year early.

That was the first fact of her file and the first fact of her life, and every other fact descended from it: the earliest verified Kindling in the registry's modern records — the involuntary hour arriving twelve months before the age the schedule had fixed for four centuries — in the garden court of an Auremont feeder school, a sigil appearing for a girl whose cohort had not been prepared for it because nobody prepares a cohort a year ahead of a law. The records preserved the attending instructor's account — the light, the stillness, the child asking the sigil, with a composure the account underlined twice, whether it was early or she was late. The hour itself she remembered in the garden court's own colors, and kept the memory the way other people kept heirlooms, because it was the last hour of her life that had belonged to nobody. She had been reading on the court's south bench — thirteen, tall for it, ahead of her cohort in everything and used to the aloneness that came with ahead — and the light had changed without the sky changing, and the sigil had been there, small and patient at the level of her eyes, older than the school and the city and the law that said it was twelve months early. The attending instructor's account, preserved in the file, recorded that the child had shown no fear and asked one question. The account preserved the question wrong, she had discovered years later, reading her own legend: it recorded *am I early, or are you late?* as composure, a marvel being marvelous. It had not been composure. It had been the first appearance of the only real question of her life — whether the schedule was wrong about her or she was wrong for the schedule — asked at thirteen, answered by nobody since.

The registry had treated the earliness itself as the first marvel — examined, verified, celebrated; a rarity its own tables listed in single digits per century — and the classification had come back inside the hour: Storm Path — the rare dual-aspect architecture, atmospheric pressure differential and discharge in one channel, a Path the registry's own tables listed at fewer than forty living practitioners — and the registry had celebrated the entry as much as recorded it, because the machine had found the thing the machine most loved, which was a marvel that fit.

Auremont had held her from the year before — recruited off the feeder school's first assessments, kept close, waiting for the hour — and the continental program had her within the season of it. And the ladder — this was the part the file recorded as triumph and she had stopped, somewhere in her teens, being able to read as anything but what it was — the ladder had come to her. Accelerated cohorts, assembled around her age and rebuilt twice as she outgrew them. Exhibition schedules, adjusted annually to keep her opposition meaningful as the meaningful pool shrank. Instructors imported, facilities extended, her advancement evaluations conducted by senior Arbiters at stations that opened early for the purpose. Iron at fourteen. Silver at sixteen. Gold at seventeen — the youngest in continental history, the certification ceremony attended by delegates from institutions she had never visited, her Kindling anniversary by then a minor Auremont holiday, observed with a half-day and a demonstration. Gold Rank 3 at nineteen. Youngest at that threshold too. Youngest at every threshold, forever, a row of records with her name filling a page of the registry's honors annex.

The cost accounting nobody at Norhold performed, she had performed herself, alone, for years, in the only ledger she kept off the program's books.

She had not fought an opponent whose outcome was in genuine doubt in four years.

Not one. She could date the last: a Gold Rank 6 border-guild veteran, an exhibition at the coastal circuit the summer she was fifteen, a man with thirty years of storms of his own kind — and for one exchange, one, he had shown her something her education hadn't priced, and she had spent the following two exchanges more awake than she had ever been on a floor, and had gone back to her quarters afterward and been unable to sleep for the pure singing strangeness of *not having known*. The program had reviewed the bout, identified the exchange as a preparation gap, and closed the gap. It had never reopened. She had been grateful, at fifteen, in the way of a girl raised to be. It had taken her years to name what the closing had cost, and when she named it she named it in the private ledger, in one line: *they fixed the last thing that could surprise me. Nobody asked me whether I wanted to be unsurprisable.* The arithmetic was simple and nobody ran it out loud: a Gold-tier at nineteen has no cohort. Her exhibition opponents were brave Silvers and career Golds twice her age managing their dignity; her program bouts were calibration exercises; her tournament appearances were coronations conducted at match length. She trained against projections, records, and the program's best committee-designs, and she was, professionally, gracious about all of it, because grace was on the curriculum and she had never failed a curriculum in her life. The system had built a ladder to her exact height and then congratulated her, sincerely, repeatedly, in ceremonies with delegates, for standing on top of it.

She was the machine's favorite anomaly — and the word she used for it, private, unspoken anywhere, was her own: *specimen*. Everything the machine had done TO the boy in the dossier it had done FOR her. It had feared his blank and celebrated her marvel, expelled him and accelerated her, built categories to contain him and holidays to display her — and the two operations, she had concluded years ago, without drama, were the same operation. The instruments talked about both of them instead of measuring them. Neither had ever once been *met*.

The program's week had a shape, and the shape had not changed in six years. Fifth-day conditioning against the facility's paired instructors. Sixth-day archive work — bout records, projections, the committee's studies of practitioners she would never be scheduled against. Rest-day observed by timetable and logged as observed. First-day discharge calibration in the grounded hall, where the readings were taken by two technicians who had never once addressed her by name and had never once needed to, because the readings addressed her adequately. Third-day pressure work, alone, which was the hour she liked. And Tuesdays, the design sessions — the program's flagship hour, the one visiting delegates were brought to, staged in the facility's largest hall with its chart-wall and its gallery of six chairs, of which three were generally occupied.

Her last program session before the filing had been the Tuesday calibration, and she kept it in the private ledger because it was the four years compressed into an hour. The program's designers had built her a committee-opponent — three senior practitioners running a coordinated scenario drawn from her projected Gold Rank 4 assessment — and she had solved it in eleven minutes, at three-quarter output, while the design staff charted her from the gallery rail. She could itemize the eleven minutes without notes, and had, that night, in the private ledger: minutes one through three spent finding the coordination's grammar, which was the same grammar the program taught, because the program had taught all three of them; minute four seeding the first lane against the coordination's own timing, so that its correction would arrive where she wanted it standing; minutes five and six taking the crescent apart at the joint between its second and third members, who had trained together for two years and therefore trusted each other a half-beat further than the design permitted; and minutes seven through eleven conducting an operation the chart would call economical and she called, privately and accurately, *tidying*. The chart went up before she had finished cooling — four columns, honest work by the best design staff on the continent — and the session note beneath it read *solution economical; recommend design complexity increased for the Rank 4 profile*, in the same words as the previous quarter's note, which she had also read. And afterward her lead instructor, a decent man who had taught her since thirteen and loved her the way builders love their best bridge, had asked what she wanted adjusted for the next design. She had looked at the chart of herself — the projections, the committee's best guess at a worthy problem, another opponent made of paperwork — and heard herself say *nothing, it's well designed*, which was true, and had been true for four years, and would be true forever, because the program could design everything about an opponent except the one property that had ever mattered, which was that she did not already know how it ended.

Then a boy at a corridor's mouth had looked back at her, and she had felt an ending she didn't know, for one second, in the middle of a trial she was winning, and the feeling had a name she had been too well-curated to ever need before.

She read the Cael dossier in one sitting, twice, in her program quarters on the night after the team trial, with the storm-rated windows dark and the city's noise coming up off the harbor — Norhold at tournament pitch, eight thousand voices' worth of it distributed through the taverns and the betting rows, a measurable fraction of it saying her name and another fraction saying his, the two facts of the cycle circulating through the city's night the way its locks circulated the harbor. The compilation was commercial, thorough, and wrong in the way all instruments were wrong about such subjects — accurate to every fact and blind to the finding. She recognized the blindness intimately, because her own file had it: eleven years of records that documented everything about her and contained nothing of her, a genre she had been the subject of since a garden court, reading now its only sibling. She was not reading it for the facts. She had stood at the mouth of her own collapsed corridor that afternoon and looked at a seventeen-year-old who had not been where her lane resolved, who had read the unreadable a half-second early and spent the half-second with an economy her whole education could price at a glance, and who had then looked back at her — that was the part; that was the whole part — looked *back*, up the lane, through the pressure-wash, with the flat complete attention of someone taking inventory. Nobody looked at her. People looked at her records, her lane, her file, her story, her academy's banner. The boy at the corridor's mouth had looked at *her*, the way she looked at things, and what she had felt, mid-trial, winning, was not curiosity.

It was recognition.

The only other practitioner of her generation the instruments talked about instead of measured. She turned the dossier's last page, and sat with it, and the feeling did not file itself away, and at some hour of the night she stopped waiting for it to.

She went to the quarters' glass instead, and stood at it a while. Norhold at three in the morning of finals week was not quiet; it was between shifts — the harbor's basin-locks working their patient arithmetic below the delegation quarter, the last betting rows shuttering their boards, a printer's boy going up the hill at a dead run with tomorrow's sheet under his arm because the broadsides paid extra for the hill. Every pane she had watched a city through since she was thirteen had been storm-rated, ordered by a facility, specified off her own assessment figures. Nobody had ever asked her whether she would like to be able to open one. She stood there until the printer's boy was out of sight, and then a while after that.

She wrote the challenge before dawn on the category's plain form, in her own hand, and gave the stated reason its one word, and filed it at the first bell in person — past her delegation head's office, which was not yet awake, which was not an accident.

Auremont objected by midday, and the objection had a room attached: the delegation head's borrowed office, herself standing, four of the program's senior staff seated in a crescent that had been arranged, she observed, before she arrived.

The delegation head opened it with the thoroughness the program gave everything. "The risk protocols were written for tier-matched bouts. The insurers have no schedule for this. The precedent — there is no precedent, which is itself the argument. And the finals calendar has you in ceremony in six days." He said all of it with genuine care, because he had known her since she was fourteen and the care was real, and that was what made the room hard rather than merely tedious.

One of the seated three — the program's risk officer, who had written her protocols for nine years and had never in nine years been on the wrong side of a table from her — put the only argument in the room that was not about the program. "The protocols exist because a Gold-tier discharge event in an unshielded venue killed four spectators at a coastal exhibition eleven years ago. That is why there are masts. That is why there is a setback. Against a tier-matched opponent I can price the risk to the fourth decimal, and I have, every time, for six years. Against an unclassified opponent I cannot price it at all — and my name goes on the sheet that says I did."

"Then order the masts," Daeva said. "Order the setback at three rows and the barrier raised and the surface rated, and price the whole floor as though you were pricing me against myself, which is the only honest instrument your office owns. I'll sign anything the office needs signed and I'll sign it first." She said it without impatience, because the man was right about his own profession and she had never in her life mistaken a colleague's competence for an obstacle. "You have a real objection. It's the only one in this room. It is an engineering objection, it has an engineering answer, and by the end of the week your engineers will have built it."

The risk officer, who had come to argue and been handed his own remedy, wrote something down. The delegation head watched him write it and understood, before anyone else in the crescent did, that the room had already ended.

"The program has invested — " began the newest of the four, and stopped, because the room's other three had winced in a way she was meant not to see and saw.

"Say it," Daeva said, without heat. "It's true. The program has invested eleven years in me. I'm aware of the size of the investment. I watched it being made from inside."

"Then you understand why the program asks you to withdraw the filing."

"I understand why it wants to." She let the distinction stand in the room a moment, curriculum-polite. "One question, and then I'll hear anything else you wish to say. Does any provision — the tournament charter, the program's covenants, the registry's schedule, anything any office in this city administers — give any person present the authority to withdraw a voluntary filing made by a practitioner in her own name?"

The silence conducted its own audit. The delegation head looked at the counsel's junior, who looked at her papers, which did not help her, because the papers said no.

"Then the objection is noted," Daeva said, in the program's own register, and thanked them for their care — sincerely, because they had built the ladder honestly; it was not their fault she had reached the top of it and found it standing in a garden — and went to her afternoon session and ran it flawlessly, because the curriculum held even when its subject had stopped being its product.

She read the objection's filed text that evening with the professional grace the curriculum had installed, and ignored it, and as the notice posted she permitted herself one reflection, without drama, because drama was for people whose lives contained doubt: she was nineteen years old, Gold-tier Rank 3, the youngest anything in continental history, and the filing was the first decision of her entire career that nobody had scheduled for her.

She read the posted line once, from her quarters' storm-rated window, at the distance the whole city read it, and went to her evening session, and ran it flawlessly, and slept better than she had in four years.

Cael went down to the exhibition ring two evenings before the match, in the last hour the Concourse admitted credentialed delegations, to learn the modified floor while it was empty.

It was not empty.

She was at the ring's far side, inside the expanded barriers, walking the new perimeter with her hand trailing the reinforced wall — learning her floor, the same errand, the same hour, the same instinct — and both of them registered the other's presence and the identical purpose in the same beat, and neither performed surprise, because the coincidence was not one: two practitioners the night crews had built this ring around, each arriving alone to read what had been built.

He came down to the floor. There was no etiquette for it; the season had used up all the precedents. She spoke first, across the fresh stone, without preamble, in the direct professional register the whole city knew from a distance and almost nobody had heard at range:

"Your read, at the trial. The lane's seed — you had it before the collapse initiated. That's not in your file."

"Your file says your lane can't be read early," Cael said. "Both files are having a difficult season."

Something crossed her face that the woodcuts had never once captured — quick, real, gone. She resumed the perimeter walk; he fell in at the professional distance, the two of them circling the ring that would hold them in two days, and the conversation ran the way the Reydan conversations had run, three years ago, at another ring's edge: no posturing, no theater, technical curiosity conducted between people for whom technique was the honest language. She asked about the exhibition floor's original dimensions and he gave her the paced figures. He asked what the arrestor masts' discharge rating meant about her working ceiling and she told him, flatly, accurately — *told him*, an opponent, two days out — and watched him file it, visibly pleased that he filed it rather than flinched.

The floor had numbers, and they traded them the way two surveyors trade a benchmark. Fifty-two feet on the long axis, he told her — paced twice, in opposite directions, and reconciled; forty-eight on the short. She had made it fifty-one and a half and took his figure without argument, on the stated grounds that he had paced it both ways and she had not. The barrier's inner face ran smooth to six feet and finished in a bull-nose coping, which meant nothing could be climbed and everything could be run along. And the surface had a crown.

"Half an inch," Cael said. "North to south. Drainage. The old exhibition floor drained east and they've relaid it to drain north, which is a decision, because nothing in here has rained since the Concourse was roofed."

"It's for the grounding runs," Daeva said. "Water finds the cable trench. If the trench takes water under discharge, four masts become one fault." She crouched, laid the back of her hand flat to the stone at the ring's centre, then again a full stride north, reading the fall the way he had read it, and stood. "Half an inch over twenty-six feet is nothing at all to stand on and everything to go down on. Whoever loses their feet in the north arc slides."

"You'll be in the north arc," Cael said. It was not a question and it was not a boast; it was the observation the floor itself made. "Your lanes want the long axis, and the long axis is where the fall runs. Every crossing you make on this stone is downhill by half an inch."

She looked at him across the fresh floor for a moment. "Nobody has ever told me a floor," she said. "Facilities tell my program the floor. The program tells me where to stand."

They walked the gate breaks after that, both counting, because gate breaks were where bouts went wrong and both of them had files that said so. Four breaks in the raised barrier: champion's side, challenger's side, and two service gaps at the east and west quarters for the crews — each a seven-foot section of lower wall with a coping seam a fighter could feel through a boot at speed. Cael paced the breaks against the masts. Daeva paced hers against the ring's centre, which told him something about the shape of her thinking that no compiled dossier had, and he said so, and she asked what it told him, and he told her that too, exactly, without softening it. She considered the answer for the length of four strides and said it was correct and that she would be adjusting it before finals day, and neither of them thought that a strange thing for one of them to have said to the other two days out.

The circuit had its stations. At the west quarter she stopped at the arrestor mast and laid two fingers against its grounded cable with the proprietary distaste of a person inspecting her own cage. "They rate these off my assessment ceilings," she said. "Every venue I've fought since I was sixteen has ordered them. Do you know what it does to a bout, fighting inside apparatus built to catch your mistakes?" — and answered herself before he could, because the answer predated him: "It teaches everyone watching that your mistakes are a civic event. So you stop making them. Not fewer. *None*. Four years, none." She released the cable. "The dossier says you lose sometimes. Circuit bouts, early. A fair-day fighter named Sarel." She looked at him with the first plain curiosity of the evening. "What is it like?"

"Losing?"

"Being allowed to."

Cael considered the question at the depth it deserved, which was total, and gave her the log's own answer: "It's where the data is. A win tells you your plan survived. A loss tells you what you are." He paced a step along the barrier. "I've been allowed to lose because nobody decided I was worth protecting from it. It's the one door they left open by accident."

"The one door they built me was a ladder," Daeva said, "and it only goes up." She said it the way she said everything — flat, exact, no bid for sympathy anywhere in it — and resumed the circuit, and Cael filed the exchange as the mirror completing its silvering: his deprivations and her endowments, arriving at the same room.

They stopped at the ring's north arc, where the new sub-frame's seams ran under the stone, and she looked at the floor built to hold her, and said the thing she had filed a challenge to be able to say:

"They measure you and get nothing. They measure me and get records. You know what's identical about that?"

"Neither number is us," Cael said.

She was still for a moment — the stillness he had charted at eighty feet in a procession and had no better read on at eight — and then she said it back, slowly, the way you handle an instrument you have just been handed and suspect of being calibrated better than your own:

"*Neither number is us.* Nineteen years, and I've never once said that sentence out loud." She looked at the ring. "I've thought it since I was thirteen. There was nobody it would have been true *at*."

They finished the circuit in a silence that had no strain in it at all — the rarest kind, the kind the table at the blue door dealt in — and at the barrier gate she stopped, and turned, and delivered the last item of business in the register of a colleague fixing terms, which was, he understood, precisely what it was:

"Don't hold anything back that matters. I'll know, and it will insult us both."

She left through the champion's gate side. He stood in the modified ring a while longer, alone with the arrestor masts and the reinforced stone and the two days, and wrote the log there, at the barrier, by the work-lamps' leavings, because some entries belonged to their floors.

*Gold Rank 3. The system's favorite. And the loneliest read I've taken since Vastin — except hers is lonely like mine. Two days.*

Below the entry, after the pen had rested a moment, one line more, because Hesk's volume kept the whole account or none of it:

*She told me her ceiling when I asked. Entered where the rarities live: the first person in four years to answer one of my questions without wondering why I asked it. The match is going to cost. The conversation already paid.*

Then he walked home through a city that had two days left in it and was spending them at full voice — the outer court's torches doubled for finals week, the betting rows running late markets on the brackets' finals, the broadsides' night printers already working tomorrow's sheet, and over every conversation he passed, in every register from tavern to tea-house, the same two names, his and hers, the city's whole apparatus of anticipation pointed at a bout worth nothing and priced above everything.

He took the long way, past the outer court, because the count was worth having. The betting rows had four boards up on the exhibition line and no two of them agreed, which was itself the finding: three of the four had given up quoting a result at all and were quoting *duration*, in exchanges, with the weight of the money sitting on two. The fourth had closed its line entirely and posted a hand-lettered card saying so, which in that quarter of the city was a confession. A tea-house he passed had a woodcut of her pasted inside its glass — six weeks old and already wrong about the shoulders — and beneath it, pinned crooked, a broadside column with his own name in it and no likeness beside it at all, because nobody had yet sold enough of him to justify cutting a block. He filed the asymmetry and kept walking. The city had spent three weeks learning to spell him and had not decided what to draw.

Lira was up when he came in — at the common table with tea and the trial broadside, not reading it — and she took one look at him and conducted the delegation's entire debrief of the evening in four sentences.

"You've met her."

"The ring. Both of us learning the floor. Unarranged."

"And?"

Cael considered the honest total of it. "She's the mirror," he said. "All the way down." And Lira nodded slowly, and asked nothing further — the found family's oldest competence, knowing the size of a thing from the shape of its wake — and poured him the tea, and said only, as she went up, with the grin kept small:

"Two days. Sleep like it matters."

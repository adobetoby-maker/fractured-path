# Chapter 15 — The Team Trial

The eligibility question lived for eleven hours, which was the shortest life any question about Cael had ever enjoyed, and it died in the morning, in writing, of institutional relief.

Withrow filed the squad list at the adjudication office's first bell: five names drawn from the academy's roster — Cael, Lira, Brom, Karis, Ephram — per the trial rules, section cited, the filing accompanied by nothing at all, because a filing that arrived with a legal memorandum announced that its filer expected a fight, and Withrow had learned the season's grammar as thoroughly as anyone. The office's response came back before midday, and Bracken, who collected it, reported the encounter to the delegation table with the dry relish he reserved for watching other registrars live his life:

"The trial clerk read the list, went to the rules, read the eligibility text twice, and took it upstairs. I am told — the adjudication office leaks the way all good offices leak, through professional sympathy — that the question spent one hour on the Chief Adjudicator's desk, and that the ruling conference consisted of Umber reading the section aloud, once, and saying: *the rule says roster. The roster says what it says. And the trial is scored on unit performance against the Standard, which means that for the first time all season we would be rating this practitioner inside a system built to hold him instead of improvising one around him. Approve it before somebody makes me invent something.*" Bracken folded the approval notice flat. "They approved it with *relief*, understand. The fielding clause forced them to accommodate you. The squad rule lets them *process* you. You have no idea what that difference means to people of my profession, and I include myself: I've been sleeping badly on your behalf for two years and I slept like a quarry stone last night."

The notice posted at the outer court by the second bell. HALCENVANE — SQUAD AS FILED. The city's reaction was the reaction the season had trained: the broadsides had their sensation, the betting rows had a philosophical crisis — the trial markets had priced squads on bracket arithmetic, and the bracket arithmetic had no field for the fifth name — and the Concourse's ticket office sold the semifinal to its rails by nightfall, the fastest sale of the cycle, because the whole tiered city had spent three weeks watching the unclassified enrollee fight strangers in a fossil category, and now, at last, the tournament's own rules had put him on a scored floor, in a counted match, beside his own.

Rooke ran the squad's final session that evening, and before the shortest team talk of his career he ran the longest tactical brief of the tournament, because Auremont at full squad strength deserved every minute of it.

"Five names. I'll take them in order of what I can plan for." The boards carried the trial floor's posted schematic and five profile sheets. "Their lattice-breaker: Karis, he's your problem again, and you know his economics now — he solves structure, so give him structure to solve and bill him for the solving. Their two Silver seniors: team-trial professionals, a decade of scenario floors between them; they will run the objective game correctly and without imagination, which is what Ephram's for — imagination is a margin, trade on it. Zerin: Lira." He didn't elaborate. The room didn't need it; the file was three lines and six years of doors, and Lira nodded once, the nod of a woman who had already lost this bout honorably and intended to spend the lesson.

He came to the fifth sheet and did the honest thing, which was to turn it face down.

"I have no plan for the fifth name. I have watched every reviewable minute of her tournament and I can tell you what she'll do — she'll do whatever the floor's geometry least survives — and I cannot tell you how to prevent it, because preventing it is not a thing five Iron-tier fighters do. So here is the actual doctrine." He put his hand flat on the face-down sheet. "The trial scores accumulated ledgers, not moments. She will win her exchanges. The trial is whether we win *ours* — the whole floor, all five lanes, every minute she isn't personally rewriting. Squads have beaten better fives by being a better one. It has never once been done against this squad, which is why we came all this way. To find out on the record." Then the team talk: "Two years this five has trained on the same floors. Tomorrow's the first time anything official counts it. The counting changes nothing about the floor. It changes everything about the record. Go to bed."

---

The scenario floor was the Concourse's main stage rebuilt overnight — the tournament's crews at their generational craft — into terrain, and Rooke had the squad walk it at the morning inspection the charter allowed, and Cael charted it whole: an irregular field ninety feet on its long axis, three raised objective platforms — two on the north half, a third south and east, each a stride high with ramped approaches — a long diagonal barrier-line cutting the field's middle third into two unequal countries, and sight-block clusters at the barrier's ends, head-high, killing the corner sightlines. A floor made deliberately unlike any drilled square, because the trial scored what brackets could not: unit behavior in country nobody owned. Objective control and engagement, twin ledgers, accumulated across exchanges. Five against five.

The inspection allowance was a quarter of an hour and Rooke spent it as five separate quarter-hours, which was the only competent way to spend it.

Brom walked the barrier-line end to end and back, once at pace and once slowly, running his palm along its face where the crews had joined the sections, and reported the finding to the squad at the north end in his own currency: "Two gaps. Here, and forty feet down at the second joint. Both wide enough for one at speed and neither wide enough for two. Anybody who wants the far country in a hurry uses one of them." Ephram wrote both down. Brom did not add that he intended to stand in the second one, because he did not need to; the delegation had watched him take a position and hold it for two years, and the sentence was already in the room.

Karis paced the sight-block clusters with her arms folded and her forearms carrying nothing, still favoring the heels of her palms in a way she had stopped bothering to hide — the raw ignitions were four days old and would be six by the trial's end — and priced the corners the way she priced clauses. "The blocks kill the sightline from either platform ramp to the diagonal," she said. "Which means a rotation called from the north platforms is called blind into the middle third. That's not a hazard. That's a *cost*, and it's on both squads, and the squad that budgets for it first gets a free exchange out of it." She looked at Ephram. "You'll be calling half of this floor from behind a wall. Practice the count."

Lira ran the long axis twice at three-quarter speed with the wrap tight under her collar, arrived back at the mark, and gave the floor's verdict in the shortest sentence available: "It's fast north and slow south. The southern platform costs double what it scores." Rooke, who had spent his own quarter-hour reaching that exact conclusion from the coaching rail, said nothing at all and marked his file, which from Rooke was the loudest possible agreement.

Cael walked it last and alone, and charted the whole of it, and what his charting kept was not the platforms but the diagonal — ninety feet of barrier that every squad on the schedule had inspected as a wall, and that ran, uninterrupted, from the floor's north-west corner to its south-east one, the single longest unbroken line in the terrain. He noted it as terrain the way he noted everything, filed it under *the feature nobody has priced*, and went back to his mark to warm up, and did not understand what he had written down until the third exchange, when it stopped being a wall.

Auremont fielded their filed squad, and the Concourse read the five names off the boards in a rising roar: their Iron lattice-breaker; Zerin; two Silver-tier seniors from the program's deep bench; and Daeva.

Cael, at the squad's warm-up line, read the roar's grammar the way he read everything. The city had come for two stories and gotten both on one card. He wrote neither down. The trial gave nobody time for literature.

The trial's format got its reading from the referee's dais before the first call — objective platforms declared, ledgers explained to the galleries, the two squads presented — and the presentation produced the day's first charged moment: the ten fighters at the center line, and the Concourse reading the matchup's whole architecture at a glance. Auremont in deep blue and silver, the reigning holders, their squad a decade of program depth. Halcenvane in plain colors, three weeks from unknown, their fifth man the only practitioner on the floor with a blank where every other name carried a tier. Daeva stood at her line's end, opposite Cael's, and did not look at him during the formalities — which he charted, and charted his own charting of it, because the not-looking was the first managed surface he had ever caught on her, and managed surfaces were his native language. She knew he was there the way he knew she was. The trial would conduct the introduction.

**First exchange.** The found family, counted at last, and the count came back the way two years of tables and floors and walls had built it to come back.

They were not the better five fighters. They were — for two exchanges, in front of the packed bowl and every scout on the continent — the better *unit*, and the difference declared itself from the first objective call. The compound gaze ran the floor entire: Cael at the unit's pivot, reading the terrain's traffic — not deployments, never deployments, only the ordinary reading that his file called peculiar timing — and feeding it out in the shorthand the five of them had grown like a dialect. Lira ran the economy on his reads: her speed spent only where the read said it purchased, arriving at contested platforms a half-beat before contention. Brom priced lanes — the scenario floor's barrier lines made corridors, and corridors were toll roads, and Auremont's two Silver seniors paid at every gate while the engagement ledger counted. Karis's lattice sealed what Brom priced, ignition architecture laid as *infrastructure*, denial written in advance across the routes the reads said mattered next. And Ephram ran point like Rooke's best product — the objective game's grammar native to him now, rotations called clean, the whole five breathing on his count.

The exchange's shape was set in its first ninety seconds and the setting was Ephram's. Auremont opened for all three platforms at once, which was the correct opening and the one every squad on the schedule ran, and Ephram declined the southern one before the first contest reached it — the call went out across the floor in two words and Halcenvane simply was not there to fight for it, the whole five rotating north while the holders' left-hand pair arrived at an uncontested ramp and stood on a platform that cost double what it scored. It was Lira's inspection verdict, spent within a minute of the flags, and Cael, running the floor's traffic from the pivot, watched the Silver senior on the southern ramp perform a full second of visible arithmetic before committing to hold a thing nobody wanted.

Then the barrier's western crossing began to charge. Brom had put himself in the second joint's gap the way water puts itself in a low place, and the holders needed the gap, because converting the southern platform required moving men north through the middle third, and the middle third had two doors and Brom was standing in one of them. He did not stop anybody. He priced them: the first crossing cost Auremont a redirect and half a stride, the second cost a redirect and a full one, and on the third the lattice-breaker declined the joint entirely and went the long way round the barrier's south end, which was forty feet of floor spent to avoid one Copper-tier fighter standing still, and which the engagement ledger counted at both ends.

Karis sealed behind him. Her ignition architecture went down on the ramps the reads said mattered next — not thrown, *laid*, denial written into the approach lines two rotations before anyone needed it, so that when Auremont's Silver pair finally came north through the long way they arrived at a north-east ramp that had been made expensive in advance by a woman standing sixty feet away with her hands at her sides. Cael charted the moment the senior recognized it: the stride broke, the man's whole approach re-formed to take the ramp from its blind side, and the blind side was where Lira had already been for a half-beat, because Cael had read the traffic and said one word and she had spent her speed exactly once.

They took the first exchange on both ledgers — the two northern platforms held from the second minute, the southern traded early at Ephram's call and taxed at the barrier's western crossing every time Auremont tried to convert it. The Concourse, which had come for stories, began to understand it was watching a system.

**Second exchange.** Auremont adjusted — a continental program's adjustment, structural and immediate, arriving inside a two-minute break, which was itself the most frightening thing about it.

The lattice-breaker went at Karis's infrastructure directly, and his method was the one she had priced off him in the Iron bracket and had studied since: he did not evade laid denial, he *entered* it, on the theory that architecture laid in advance is architecture that has already spent its decision, and he walked her north-east ramp twice at cost to prove the theory in front of a panel. It cost him. It cost her more, because a sealed route that has to be re-sealed is a route that is now being paid for twice, and she paid it twice and then, at the exchange's middle, stopped paying and moved the whole seal thirty feet to a ramp he had not yet insulted — an undocumented rebuild, mid-exchange, that Rooke's file afterward recorded with two words and an underline.

Zerin came off the leash on the flanks, and the read-economy met the thing it had met in the semifinal and had been rebuilt since to survive: ambient speed, no gather, arriving where the reads said she should not yet be. Lira did not race her. Lira had learned the whole lesson eleven days ago at a cost she had itemized in public, and she spent it now not on Zerin but on the ground Zerin needed, conceding every flank contest and taking every crossing behind her, so that the fastest fighter on the continent spent the second exchange running errands at the margin of a floor whose middle was being decided without her.

The two Silver seniors refused Brom's corridors entirely — the correct answer, a decade of scenario floors talking, and it worked, and it cost them the whole northern half to do it, because refusing a corridor means going where the corridor is not, and where the corridor was not was where Ephram had put his rotation. They were the best team-trial professionals in the building. They ran the objective game precisely, in order, without one imaginative motion in eleven minutes, and Ephram beat them with a trade nobody in a scenario-floor manual would have signed.

It bought Auremont the engagement ledger's margin back, and the exchange ran to the wire — and the wire came back Halcenvane, on the objective count, because Ephram traded a scoring position nobody thought tradeable for a platform rotation the reads had priced two moves earlier, the cohort's arithmetic conducted at unit scale. Two exchanges. Two years of found-family mechanics, paying out on a sanctioned floor, on the record, counted.

At the exchange break, Rooke's face did nothing and his hands squared the coaching file twice. In the Compact row, visible from the squad's reset line, Ilsev's pen had stopped moving. The delegations' gallery had gone dense and silent. The best squad on the floor, two exchanges running, wore Halcenvane plain, and every professional in the building knew it.

**Third exchange: Daeva.**

She had spent the first two exchanges as a piece on the board — present, efficient, contained, playing the unit game inside the unit's grammar, and Cael's floor-reading had tracked her the whole time the way you track weather on a far coast. Afterward, reviewing, he would understand that the containment had been her own choice, and that Auremont's coaches had not made it, and that what changed in the third exchange was simply that she stopped making it.

The stormlane opened at the exchange's first call, and it opened down the diagonal — the barrier-line's whole length, the floor's one uninterrupted geometry, the feature every unit had treated as a wall revealed in one second as a road.

He felt it before the floor saw it — pressure shifting wrong along the barrier's line at the terrain's middle distance, a corridor of collapsed air assembling itself at a speed that belonged to no tier's tables — and then she was *through* it, and the trial's geometry died. Not beaten: *voided*. The lane crossed the scenario floor's whole argument — barriers, corridors, priced lanes, sealed routes — the way a river crosses a property line. She took the north platform out of Ephram's rotation before the rotation's call finished — Ephram had the count half spoken, two syllables of a four-syllable call, and by the fourth syllable the platform's holder was on the ramp's floor and the platform was hers and the call described a floor that had ceased to exist. He called it again, correctly, immediately, from behind the sight-block where Karis had told him he would be calling half this floor blind, and the second call was also obsolete before it landed, and Cael, charting, recorded the only thing worth recording about it: the fault was not in the calling.

She was at the flank breaking Lira's economy before the read that should have warned of her had anything to read — the lane's collapse outran its own signature. Lira had spent two exchanges buying ground a half-beat early and banking the proceeds, which was the whole architecture of everything she had built since Ardenmere, and the architecture required one thing only, which was that the beat be knowable in advance. She conceded the flank, sold the position, and arrived at the ground she had bought to find the crossing already through it. She did the correct thing twice more and it was correct twice more and it did not matter twice more, and at the exchange's end she was still doing it, which the delegation's bench read from the rail with a particular kind of pride.

Brom's corridors tolled nothing; she did not use corridors. He stood in the barrier's second joint, the gap he had found at the morning inspection, wide enough for one at speed and not for two, and the lane went past him along the diagonal's face at a distance of four feet without ever entering the country his position governed — a toll gate on a road nobody was travelling. He moved off the joint at the exchange's midpoint, of his own judgment, to go and be useful elsewhere, which was the single most expensive admission Cael had ever watched him make.

Karis's sealed routes sealed nothing; she did not use routes. The denial architecture she had laid across three ramps and re-laid once under pressure priced approaches, and an approach is a thing that happens on a floor, and the stormlane was not on the floor in the sense her architecture was built to price. She watched a hundred careful decisions become scenery in under a minute, standing with her scorched palms open at her sides, and the thing Cael charted about her was that she never once looked at the ruined work — she looked at the lane, all of it, both crossings, the whole length, with the fixed attention of a woman taking notes without a notebook.

Five fighters who had been the best unit on the floor for two exchanges were, inside ninety seconds, five separate people being visited in sequence by a Gold-tier fact, and the engagement ledger counted what it was built to count, which was everything.

She is not better than five people, Cael's charting recorded, at speed, in the only line it had time for. She is orthogonal to them. A Gold-tier fact moving through an Iron-tier argument.

And then the lane crossed his position.

It happened once, held to a single exchange by whatever governed her choices — afterward he would spend a page on the question of whether she had saved him for last or simply reached him last, and conclude the page honestly: *unknown* — and it happened at full speed. The pressure-fold came assembling toward his ground, corridor collapsing, discharge gathering at its head, and the Iron-adjacent surface read — at its absolute limit, deeper than he had ever run it in public, the read that solved weaves and timed resets now drinking the atmosphere's own weight-shift — handed him the lane's arrival a half-second before the lane arrived.

Half a second. At her tempo it was a fortune and it purchased exactly one thing: he was not where the lane resolved. The crossing that should have scored — that had scored, four times, against four of the best fighters of his life — spent itself on his vacated ground, and Cael arrived at the lane's edge, upright, inside the storm's pressure-wash, close enough to read the discharge's leashed arithmetic, and for one full second, mid-trial, mid-exchange, with the whole Concourse roaring —

Daeva stopped.

Entirely. The tempo, the lane-work, the forward arithmetic of the most relentless fighter in the field: stopped. She stood at the collapsed corridor's mouth and looked at him — and Cael, who had been read at range by wardens and evaluators and a whole civilization's instruments, met, for the first time in his life, the thing itself: she looked at him *the way he looked at things.* Inventory. Recalibration. The naked, unhurried attention of an instrument encountering data outside its tables and electing, before eight thousand witnesses, to stop and read.

One second. Then the trial resumed, because the trial was still running, and she was gone back into it, and the exchange finished what the lane had started.

**The fourth exchange** was the trial's quiet masterpiece, and the delegation would keep it privately forever, because almost nobody else in the building understood what they were watching: Daeva contained herself again — the point long since made, the lane-work sheathed, a Gold-tier fact electing to play the unit game once more — and Halcenvane's five, who had just been structurally dismantled in front of the full Concourse, did not dismantle. They re-formed. The compound gaze came back up; the economy recalibrated to a floor that now included weather; Brom re-priced his corridors two hands wider; Karis rebuilt her infrastructure with its sealed routes hedged; and Ephram called the rotation with his voice level, and the last exchange ran units against units at full honesty, and Auremont won it, narrowly, on the engagement ledger — against a squad that had every excuse to be broken and came instead to collect.

Cael's own fourth exchange held one transaction the charting kept: Zerin, crossing his lane on a flank rotation, read him for two full beats at speed — the storm's near-miss had been the trial's loudest fact; the whole floor had recalculated him — and elected, visibly, not to test it, spending her speed instead on Ephram's platform, an economist declining an unpriced trade. *The file's first entry about me that Auremont wrote in real time,* the log would note. *Unknown cost. Route around. Even their speed is disciplined.*

**The arithmetic closed.** Auremont advanced to the final on accumulated objective points — the exchanges split two and two, the margins printed and honest, the trial decided not by an exchange count but by the objective ledger the third exchange had emptied. Halcenvane exited to the third-place trial, and exited to a standing Concourse — the full-throated, sustained acknowledgment the city gave perhaps twice a cycle, eight thousand people on their feet for the beaten squad, because Norhold had watched eleven cycles of team trials and knew what it had seen for two exchanges, and knew what it had seen in the third, and honored both.

---

The delegation floor, that evening, ran the honest ledger of a loss that felt like a finding.

Rooke opened it, file flat, verdict first: "We were the best squad on that floor for two exchanges. The third exchange wasn't a squad problem." He let the sentence stand exactly as long as it needed. "There is no squad answer to what happened in the third exchange. There is no *bracket* answer to it. I've coached three cycles and I have never seen a practitioner void a scenario floor, and neither has anyone else in that building, and the scouts of fourteen academies are writing my sentence in their own files tonight. So we will not be conducting a review of the third exchange. We will review one and two, which were the best team fighting this academy has ever produced, and four, which was the bravest." He turned the file around. "Ledger's open. Findings."

The findings ran long and warm and specific, the found family doing its native work — Lira's economy audited, Ephram's platform trade celebrated into embarrassment, Brom's corridor pricing itemized against Auremont's Silver seniors — and at the session's end, when the file closed and the table loosened, Ephram came and found Cael at the room's edge, and completed, without preamble, the arc that a covered walk had opened a year and a half ago.

"I waited for the semester evaluation." He said it in the cohort's flat honest register, a man auditing his own record aloud. "Then I waited for this — a scored floor, official, counted, where I'd finally see it myself instead of reading a board." He nodded back toward the table, the file, the trial. "She looked at you like the rest of us look at you. Middle of a continental semifinal, winning, and she stopped — because whatever you are was worth more to her than the exchange." He put out his hand, the second time in their lives. "I'm done waiting. Whatever you are, I'm glad it's on my side of the floor."

Cael shook it.

"That's the whole speech," Ephram said. "I've been drafting it since the wool town. It had four more sentences and Lira cut them."

---

The log, that night, in Hesk's volume:

*Team trial, semifinal: lost — exchanges split two and two, decided on accumulated objectives. The found family fought together on a sanctioned floor for the first time in any of our lives, and for two exchanges we were the best thing in that building, and it's on the record, counted, forever. Whatever else this tournament was for, it was for that, and it happened, and nobody can unfile it.*

*And the third exchange happened too. She stopped mid-trial to read me. Gold Rank 3, nineteen years old, and the first person at this tournament whose attention feels like mine. Filed under: things that are going to happen whether I schedule them or not.*

He capped the pen. Down the room, Lira was still at the table with the trial's broadside proof-sheet, reading the account of the third exchange for the fourth time, and she looked up and found him watching and said, with the grin she kept for weather worth having:

"Third-place trial first. Then your storm."

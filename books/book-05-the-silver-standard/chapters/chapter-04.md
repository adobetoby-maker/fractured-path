# Chapter 4 — Standard Measures

The wool town's paper caught them up on the road, as the season's paper always would: the meet record, posted the morning after their departure, reached the delegation by courier at the first waystation, and with it the ruling that gave the autumn its first quotable official.

The exhibition ratings had posted with the record — Cael's two bouts, rated against the Standard, landing in a tidy band just above the middle of Iron, exactly where a careful reading of what he had shown would put them — and the Bronze Rank 3, who had lost in three exchanges to a boy now rated below his own tier's floor, had read the posted sheet in front of a morning crowd and done the arithmetic in public. He filed a protest before the delegation's wagons had cleared the second ferry: the rating was inconsistent with the result; either the bout was misjudged or the rating was; the record could not hold both.

The presiding adjudicator — a dry, small woman on loan from the continental office — resolved it in two written sentences that Karis read aloud to the waystation table with something close to reverence.

*The Standard rates performance, not outcomes. The panel finds the performance was rated correctly, and observes that the protest's actual complaint — that the practitioner appears to have won without extending himself — is not a scoring error, and the remedy for it is not available from this office.*

"'The remedy for it is not available from this office,'" Karis repeated. "She means: *if you want him to try harder, make him.* A continental adjudicator read two bouts and a ratings sheet and landed, inside a week, on the exact question this circuit will spend its whole season circling." She looked across the table at Cael, not unkindly, with the researcher's flat clarity. "The category is going to hold you exactly as long as nobody makes you. You know that."

"I know that."

"Good," she said, and let it go, and the notebook got one more line he didn't see.

The same waystation morning paid another of the first meet's bills, because Rooke collected what he invoiced: Lira's warm-up, run in the innyard at dawn under the coach's flat attention, told him what four bursts over rate had cost — the burst-shoulder tight through the first quarter hour, the landing-leg hip reporting on the deep squats, nothing torn, everything taxed. "Two days at half work," was the ruling, entered in the coaching file while she pulled her coat back on. "That's the price of the wall you unpicked. Cheap. Pay it properly and don't buy two of them in one bout again until midwinter." She paid it properly, and the file noted that too.

The second meet's host town was a mill town in the hill country a week west, cold earlier than the river valley, and its meet ground was the mill guild's own training hall, which meant a level floor for once and a roof, and Cael spent the first hour of arrival day doing what he now understood would be the season's standing ritual: walking the ground, counting the apparatus, and finding his own name already there ahead of him.

The exhibition challenges were filed before the delegation's wagons were unloaded. Four of them this time — a retired Iron Rank 9 who trained the mill guild's yard crews, two academy Iron entries from the bracket's middle who wanted the measure of the story before the story got bigger, and a Bronze Rank 5 half-guild half-academy man whose filing named its reason in the space the form provided with a candor Cael admired: *curiosity.* The exhibition steward here had heard about the wool town and had a board ready, and the word DEMONSTRATION was pre-chalked, and there was a bracket of blank lines under it, waiting, like a menu.

"You're an attraction," Ephram observed, reading the board over his shoulder. "Three weeks. It took three weeks."

"It took three years," Cael said. "The three weeks were shipping."

---

He spent the evening before his first bout doing what he had been doing every spare hour since the map room: reading the instrument.

The Standard's public documentation ran to forty pages, and Bracken had obtained the delegation a copy of the adjudication office's own scoring manual besides — the examiner's edition, annotated, the one panel judges trained from — because Bracken believed in reading the version of a rule that its enforcers read. Cael worked through it at the residence table the hosts had given the delegation, with Karis across from him working through the same pages in the other direction, and between them, by the second evening, they could have sat the panel qualification.

The architecture was three ratings and a mark. Every scored performance was judged on *execution* — the technical fact of what the practitioner did, its difficulty and its cleanness; *control* — the margin the practitioner demonstrated over what they did, precision under pressure, economy, the absence of waste and accident; and *effect* — what the performance accomplished against the opposition actually present. Three numbers, rated by a panel of five, high and low discarded, the middle three averaged. And then the mark: the composite was scaled against the reference profile — the fixed, calibrated, cycle-locked performance model of a Silver-tier Rank 5 practitioner — and expressed as a rating relative to that baseline. A rating of the baseline exactly was written as par. Everything on the continent was a distance from par.

He watched the instrument work before he read how it worked, because that was the order he trusted. The meet's first afternoon he stood at the exhibition floor's rating table — public, by design; the tournament scored in the open — and charted the panel through four bouts that were none of his business. Five judges, each with a slate, each rating alone: no conferring, no glances, the deliberate mutual blindness of people who had been trained that agreement was something you discovered afterward, not manufactured during. The clerk collected the slates, struck the high and low, chalked the middle three, averaged, scaled against the reference tables, posted. Eleven minutes, bout to board. He timed all four. The spread between the eleven minutes was nine seconds.

*It runs like Vell's ledger with five hands,* he noted, *and the five hands never touch. Whoever built the procedure assumed the judges were honest and designed it so they didn't have to be — struck extremes, blind ratings, posted arithmetic. It's the first apparatus I've met in four years that I'd have designed the same way.*

"It's beautiful," Karis said, on the second evening, and she did not use the word loosely. "I've been trying to break it for two days. Watch what the three ratings do: you can't buy one with another. Overwhelming power scored against a weak opponent — high execution, high effect, and the control rating catches the waste and drags the composite. Perfect technique that accomplishes nothing — execution alone, effect drags it. It's a three-legged instrument. You can't lean it."

"You can lean it," Cael said.

She looked up.

"Not by pushing a leg up. By holding all three legs level, below your reach." He turned the examiner's manual around to face her, open to the annotation he had found an hour ago and had been sitting with since. "Panel guidance, page thirty-one. *Judges rate the performance demonstrated, not the capacity inferred.* It's the honest rule — the instrument refuses to guess. Which means the instrument scores exactly what you show it, and a practitioner who controls what he shows controls his rating." He said it flatly, because the log was where he kept the other tones. "It can't be leaned upward. Nobody can show more than they have. It leans downward without ever knowing it's leaning. The manual even names the case and files it as no case: *performance below evident capacity is not a scoring irregularity.* The remedy isn't available from that office either."

Karis was quiet for a moment, reading the annotation, and then she said the thing he had been declining to write for two days.

"The fairest instrument on the continent has exactly one blind spot, and you live in it."

"I built the spot before they pointed the instrument," Cael said. "Three years before. Vell taught me to fight to the record. The record just got better instrumentation." He took the manual back. "I want it written somewhere that I know what it costs. The instrument treats me exactly like everyone else, and I'm going to answer it like nobody else does, and that's the arrangement's price: the one honest measure I've ever been offered, and the doctrine says decline to be measured."

"The doctrine's right," Karis said.

"The doctrine's right," Cael agreed. "Note the tense we both used."

---

The bracket days belonged to the delegation, and the delegation used them.

The hill country's academies ran shallow, but shallow was not the same as soft, and the mill town's Iron bracket held one fighter who mattered: the host guild's sponsored entry, an Iron Rank 5 Force Path who had taken the regional title at this meet two seasons running and trained on this exact floor six days a week. Lira drew him in the final. The hall filled for it — mill town, guild champion, home floor — and she gave the home crowd a strange gift, which was a bout with no villain in it: four exchanges of two honest fighters at honest speed, decided in the fourth when she spent a burst on a re-entry angle his home-floor habits had never needed to defend, because nobody who trained here attacked out of the corner the support pillar crowded. Rooke's margin got a new line that evening — *pillar corner: she saw it in warm-up, said nothing, banked it two days* — and Lira's season stayed unbeaten, and the guild champion shook her hand and asked, with a professional's directness, what he'd missed. "Your floor has furniture," she said. "You stopped seeing it years ago."

Brom's Copper day ran to schedule — three bouts, patient, unmarked — and what the day actually held would surface at the delegation table later in the week, because Brom's days had a way of being about more than their flags said. Karis took her pool efficiently and lost the bracket semifinal to Lira in the politest bout of the season — a three-exchange family argument, Rooke called it — and came off the floor already writing, because losing to Lira produced data and Karis had long since stopped paying emotional duties on data. Ephram reached his final and won it, his first regional title, and stood at the flags with the certificate looking like a man rehearsing how to be somebody who won things, and the delegation let him have the whole evening about it, which he spent buying the table's dinner with ceremony and precision.

*The season's shape, mid-autumn,* the log recorded. *We are not the deepest delegation on the circuit. We are the best coached and the honestest priced, and the standings are beginning to say so in the only language standings have.*

---

His own bouts ran on the exhibition floor, and the retired Rank 9 was the best fighter he had faced since the ladder, and that bout deserved and got the season's fullest page.

The mill guild's hall gave the exhibition floor its geometry: a rectangle under the crane-beam, two long sides roped for the crowd, the short west end walled by the guild's tool racks — real racks, real iron, declared out of play and profoundly present — and the east end open to the cold light of the loading doors. The yard-master took the west end in the first exchange without appearing to choose it, and Cael understood the choice by the second: the man's entire style was built on denying ground by increments, and the racks anchored his flank the way a river anchors an army. Twenty years of training yard crews had made him an economist. He advanced in paid-for inches, cut off angles before they existed, and treated Cael's evasion frames not as a problem to solve but as a budget to exhaust.

So the first exchange went to him on flags, and the second went nowhere — four minutes of Cael refusing the shrinking floor, circling out the open east end, resetting, and the yard-master allowing it with the patience of a man who had drilled patience into three hundred apprentices — and the crowd began to understand it was watching a siege, and sieges are arithmetic. The Iron-adjacent read gave Cael the man's weight-language early and completely, and it was not enough by itself: knowing where an economist will spend does not refund what he has already bought. By the third exchange the usable floor was two-thirds of its start, the racks a step off Cael's blind shoulder every time he cycled left.

He solved it with the floor's one asset the yard-master had spent twenty years learning to ignore: the crane-beam's shadow. The loading doors threw the morning in flat cold bars across the boards, and the beam's shadow crossed the floor at a diagonal, and a man who trains on a floor for two decades stops seeing its light the way Lira's guild champion had stopped seeing his pillar. Cael spent the fourth exchange herding the fight's rhythm — not its position; the yard-master owned position — so that the man's forward step landed him blinking into the door-glare at the moment the exchange turned, and spent his one full-rate Wind-adjacent sequence of the bout inside that blink, and took the exchange at contact. The fifth was the yard-master testing whether it had been luck, from a stance rebuilt to quarter the light, and finding that it had not been luck, and being unable to rebuild twenty years in four minutes, and losing the bout's last flag on the same diagonal with the grace of a man filing a lesson.

Five exchanges, one more than the fight needed — the extra bought deliberately, the doctrine's tax, because a five-exchange win against a respected local Rank 9 rated as *strong Iron performance, composite below par by the expected distance*, and a three-exchange win would have rated as something the band was not built to hold. The win-sentence went in the log with the bill beside it: *I took away the last thing on his floor he'd never priced — its light. Cost: the full evasion rate spent in one exchange, the left calf tight where four minutes of contested footing put it, and one more entry in the growing file of opponents who shook my hand while visibly recalculating the story.*

"You fight like an auditor," the yard-master said at the ropes, meaning it as a compliment, and Cael accepted it as one, and did not say that the audit had two sets of books.

The mill-town panel scored it exactly as constructed. He watched the flags, and watched the rating clerk's chalk, and felt the strange doubled satisfaction that was becoming the season's signature feeling: the craftsman's pleasure of a tolerance held, stacked directly on top of something that was not pleasure and that he declined, for now, to name.

The two academy entries went the same way at lower intensity. The Bronze — *curiosity* — earned his answer honestly: he fought a clever, over-extended, generous bout, was managed into a four-exchange loss he plainly enjoyed, and stood afterward at the ropes asking Cael technical questions about the third exchange with the fervor of a man who had paid his fee and intended to get his money's worth. Cael answered them. All of them. Accurately, about the public suite, completely, and the Bronze went away satisfied, and the log noted the transaction that evening with the precision it reserved for the doctrine's edge cases:

*Everything I told him was true. Everything I told him was in the meet record already. He thinks he got the fight explained. He got the record recited by the person it's about, which felt like candor because it was delivered in the first person. I am getting better at this in ways I want tracked: the mask has stopped being a thing I hold up and started being a register I speak in. Quenna's question, third year of it: can you not-do it in front of people who wish you harm? Current answer: I can not-do it in front of people who wish me well, which is harder, while they thank me for doing it. She never asked me that one. I'd like to know why not, because I think she knew the order of difficulty and I think she asked me the easy one on purpose.*

---

The ratings posted on the last morning, and they were tidy, and the tidiness was the thing Seln flagged.

He did it in the only register he had: administratively, at the delegation's document table, sorting the meet's paper into the travel file while the hall emptied. Cael was the only one in earshot, which with Seln was never an accident.

"The office notes a statistical matter," Seln said, to the file. "Four exhibition ratings this meet, two last meet. Six bouts, six different opponents, four tiers of opposition, two different panels in two towns." He squared the ratings sheets. "Composite spread across all six: inside four points. The bracket competitors' spreads run thirty and forty points across a season. Yours runs four." He clipped the sheets in. "Instruments produce spreads. Consistency at that grade is produced by something else. The office files no conclusion. The office observes that other offices also employ statisticians."

He closed the file, and added the sentence he had come to deliver, in the voice of a man reading a form's instructions aloud.

"Variance is cheap. Buy some."

Rooke, who missed nothing that happened at a document table within forty feet of him, arrived at the same finding by his own road that evening and delivered it as coaching — one observation, flat, over the file, and nothing after it. "Your ratings sheet reads like a metronome. I've coached twenty years and I've never produced a metronome. Fighters breathe." He stood, and left the rest where he left everything he had decided not to know: on Cael's side of the table. *He saw the same flaw Seln saw,* the log noted, *and handed it to me as a coaching remark and walked away, which is the whole shape of the man's discretion: he corrects the sheet, not the cause. The fix is mine to build. Engineered scatter, self-assigned: a slow first exchange against grapplers, a point conceded when the light's behind me, costed and rotated, no two meets alike.*

*He's right,* the log conceded of Seln's point, *and it's the first flaw anyone's found in the tournament form of the doctrine, and of course it was him. The band isn't the tell. The band's width is the tell. A real Iron fighting six different opponents wobbles — has bad mornings, over-performs at home, drops points to styles that don't suit him. I have been printing the same number like a mint. Correction from next meet: engineered scatter. Two points down against the wrong style, three up on a good day. It offends something in me to plan my own bad mornings, and I note the offense with interest, because it's the same thing that flinched at the manual's page thirty-one. Inventory that flinch. It's load-bearing somewhere.*

The cold arrived properly on the meet's middle night, the hill country's first hard frost, and the delegation's residence — the mill guild's visiting-masters' house, stone below and timber above — held the heat of exactly one stove, and the season's found-family arrangements assembled themselves around it without a word of planning.

Cael came down late from the log to find the common room reorganized: the table hauled stove-ward, Karis at it with the notebook and a candle she'd rationed to herself, Brom asleep upright in the room's one good chair with the specific totality of a man who could sleep in a bracket hall, Lira on the hearth-edge working oil into her boots against the frost, and Ephram — regional champion of eleven hours' standing — copying Rooke's margin notes into his own book, because Rooke had told him champions kept their own files now.

He took the bench end that had, by the silent allocation these rooms always performed, become his: the one with the wall at his back and the window in view. Nobody looked up. Lira moved the oil-tin so he'd have elbow room. It was, he had concluded across three years of such rooms, the whole of what the word family had ever reliably meant anywhere he could verify it: a configuration that adjusted for you before you arrived.

"Question," Lira said to the room, working the boot-heel. "Anyone else notice we're rich?"

Brom, without opening his eyes: "Define it."

"Fourteen months ago we were four people and a legal theory. Tonight the delegation standings have us second on the circuit, the regional champion's doing his homework at our table, and the strangest one of us is famous in towns he's never entered." The boot got a final pass. "I keep waiting for the bill. That's the Fenmark in me. I'd like it noted that I noticed the waiting, which Karis says is progress."

"The bill comes," Karis said, not looking up. "Bills always come. The difference between now and Fenmark is solvency."

Cael wrote that down. She saw him do it and let him, which was its own line in the accounting.

---

The assay evaluation convened on the meet's middle day, in a mill-guild committee room requisitioned for two hours, and it was — Cael timed it, because timing it had become the joke he kept privately with himself — the most boring ninety minutes of the meet, which was its entire triumph.

Gault chaired with the travel case of seals open beside him — he had been right about the handle — and the panel's visiting second was a registry officer from the district seat who had plainly read the whole file on the road and asked four competent questions about deployment ceilings. The procedure ran the semester machinery it had run twice before: the enrollment basis reviewed, the term's supervised floor-hours certified, the evaluation records sighted against the demonstration provision's requirements, the renewal minuted. Cael answered what was asked, exactly, in the register the panel's own documents used. Fifty pages of the strangest legal architecture on the continent, ticking over in a borrowed room between a wool merchant's committee meeting and somebody's guild lunch.

The juxtaposition was the day's actual content, and he wrote it while it was fresh:

*Two instruments, one year, both managed. In the morning I calibrated a performance so a five-judge panel would rate me just above Iron against a fixed Silver mark. In the afternoon a two-officer panel certified, from documents, that my enrollment basis remains sound — an evaluation that exists because no instrument can rate me at all. The tournament measures me and gets a number I chose. The registry measures me and gets a lawful shrug. Between the two of them stands the entire apparatus of the most classification-governed civilization in the world, and the honest entry is that neither instrument has ever touched me. One reads a mask. One reads a file.*

*Gault packed the seals into the case and said "routine" to the visiting officer, and the visiting officer agreed, and they were both right, and I sat in a committee room and watched the word routine get applied to me by two men whose predecessors would have called the same file an emergency. That's the year's real motion, and no panel rates it: the apparatus is getting used to me. Used-to is a rating. It just doesn't post.*

---

The delegation took the standings — first of five this meet; the hill country's academies ran shallow — and took the road home through the first real cold of the season, and on the second night, at a coaching inn with the wagons in the yard and the company spread across two rooms and a landing, Cael sat up late with the log and wrote the entry the meet had been assembling toward all week.

He wrote the inventory first, because the inventory was the frame: six exhibition bouts this season, six wins, ratings banded where he had banded them — the sentence tested against Vell's circuit, the ladder, two academies, and six volunteer opponents this autumn from Iron Rank 2 to a retired Rank 9; every test available at his price, bought and passed. Bracket record across the delegation: Lira unbeaten, Brom unbeaten, Karis and Ephram placed and pointed. The season's arithmetic running ahead of even Withrow's schedule.

Then the entry:

*Rated again. In-band again. The judges keep writing numbers that mean "strong Iron, ceiling unknown," and the honest entry is: I can beat anyone at my tier. I've tested that sentence for three years and it holds. What I haven't tested is what tier means when the word points at me. Not this season's problem.*

He read it back, capped the pen, and it was done and true and filed.

Down the landing, a floorboard registered Brom's weight once and went silent. Outside, the inn's yard-lantern burned over two wagons whose manifests were printed in a program, and the cold came down off the hills onto a country that had measured everything in it for four hundred years and had, for six bouts running now, written down about one boy exactly what he had decided it would.

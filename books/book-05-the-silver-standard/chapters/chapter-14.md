# Chapter 14 — Seventeen

The tournament's charter mandated a rest day at its midpoint — two centuries of experience with what happened to fighters, panels, and cities that ran three weeks without one — and Norhold kept it the way port towns keep harbor-holidays: completely, and with relief. The Concourse stood closed and quiet, its flags up and its floors empty, the great boards under their canvas. The betting rows shuttered. The broadsides printed a single thin sheet of retrospection and speculation and went back to bed. And the delegations scattered into the city to be, for one day, people.

Cael woke late — a first for the tournament; the season's ledger recorded sleep like everything else, and the column had been running thin since the Shield captain — and found the guesting-house floor already half empty and a note on the common table in Lira's decisive hand: *Dockside. The cookshop with the blue door, past the third wharf. Midday. Attendance mandatory. It's your birthday, in case the log hasn't told you.*

The log had told him. The log had been telling him for a week, in the small way of a date approaching down a page. He had turned seventeen at some hour of the night between the rest day's eve and the rest day, in a guesting-house in a city of eighty thousand strangers and eleven of his own people, and he took the note and the new-turned fact down through Norhold's holiday streets with his coat open to the spring, and gave himself the walk as a present, which meant cataloguing without obligation — the instrument run for pleasure, the way Hesk played his workshop's finished fiddles.

The city at rest was a different document from the city at tournament, and better reading. The harbor's rest-day rhythm ran slow and true, barges warped along the quays at holiday pace, the basin-locks working their old arithmetic for an audience of gulls. The tournament flags hung idle overhead, and under them Norhold conducted its actual life: a wedding party crossing the fish market in their best clothes, the bride's uncles carrying chairs; a school of children being taught to scull in the inner basin by a woman who bellowed like a bracket coach; two Rhagen fighters buying pastry at a stall like ordinary mortals, doctrine suspended, arguing about jam.

The holiday did not extend to the city's arithmetic. The hiring board at the second wharf carried the next morning's crews in chalk — *stone barge, four hands, Iron or better, day rate and dinner* — and a dozen men stood reading it who had never seen a tournament floor and who priced a rank by what it could lift and for how long. The public house on the corner of the fish market kept its front room for the wharf and its back room for ranked custom, the door marked with a small brass plate carrying a rank line, and the plate was old enough that the rank engraved on it had been superseded twice by the registry that issued it. At the registry annex on the coopers' row the shutters were down for the rest day and the queue had formed anyway — thirty-odd people with their result slips, standing a day early outside a closed office, because the tournament made ranks and the registry made them real, and the distance between those two facts was where a whole street's worth of patience lived.

A woodcut stall at the basin steps was selling the tournament's faces two for a copper, pegged along a line like drying fish. The Auremont Gold hung at the middle in three-quarter profile and was down to her last two sheets by midday. Zerin next to her, then the Rhagen striker, then a plate of the Iron bracket's semifinalists on which the cutter had got Lira's chin badly wrong and her shoulders exactly right. His own hung at the line's far end with the eyes cut too old. He bought it, because Hesk would want it and would say something withering and keep it, and the stallholder took his copper and made change without a flicker of recognition, which Cael priced as the best purchase available in Norhold.

At the third wharf a street fiddler was working the holiday crowd, and Cael stopped for her the way Hesk had taught him to stop for anyone competent with an instrument, and put a coin in the case, and got, because the woodcuts had made his face a public document, a grin and four bars of a quick-march played at him as he went — the city's own register of fame, cheerfully illiterate in everything the dossiers thought they knew.

*Seventeen years old and the continent's strangest case file,* he noted, walking on, *and to a fiddler at the third wharf I'm worth four bars of a march and a grin. Norhold has been pricing famous strangers for six centuries. It prices them low and kindly. I find the rate very restful.*

He arrived at the blue door at midday exactly.

Lira had found the place the way Lira found places: infallibly. A dockside cookshop of the working kind, low-beamed, smelling of frying fish and tar and bread, its custom half wharfmen and half tournament strays, with a long scarred table at the harbor window that she had somehow annexed entire. She was instigator emerita and in full office: the table was set, the bench cushions extorted from somewhere, and she directed arrivals with the crisp economy she had once spent on bout entries.

Brom acquired food. It was never clear, at any table the delegation had kept in three years, quite how this happened — no order was ever heard placed — but dishes began arriving within minutes of his sitting down and did not stop: the fish, the bread, a pot of something dark and excellent involving mussels, fried potatoes of a local cut, and, eventually, with a wink from the cookshop's proprietor that suggested negotiations had occurred at some earlier hour, a flat honey-cake of the northern kind with no ceremony about it except its arrival.

The table showed its season the way rest-day tables did: Lira's shoulder wrap visible under her collar when she reached across for the bread, the last two days of Rooke's mandate worn like an inconvenient scarf; Brom's forearms yellowing where the final's bruises were finishing their business; Karis's palms still faintly marked at the heels from the raw ignitions, her cup handled a shade more carefully than usual; Cael's left hip flexor on its fourth of four careful days and the channel-burn on his right hand fading to a dry pink he caught Brom pricing across the table; and everyone at the table cataloguing everyone else's bills with the found family's oldest arithmetic — pain accounted, nothing remarked, the season's ledger reconciled in glances over fish.

Karis came with nothing. No notebook. No document wallet, no charter extract, no panel study. She sat down at the harbor window with her hands empty and kept them that way the whole meal, and wrote nothing, all afternoon — and everyone at the table understood the grammar of it, because from Karis an afternoon of unrecorded time was not a rest. It was a gift, and it was the most expensive kind she had.

And Seln came to the table and sat down at it.

He arrived last, in his plain off-duty coat, carrying the day's post from the delegation pouch because the pouch was his office and offices did not keep rest days — and then, having handed Cael the one package that mattered, he did not withdraw to the counter with a cup and a vantage, which was the Seln arrangement at every meal of the tournament and every meal before it, the seat at the margin, the position with the sightlines. He pulled out the bench's end seat, at the table, among them, and sat, and reached for the bread.

Nobody made anything of it. That was the point of it — the whole table understood in the same half-second that the correct response was none, and delivered none in five distinct registers: Lira passed him the fish as if he had always sat there; Brom shifted six inches with the unhurriedness of geology; Karis, handless and writing nothing, noted it with her eyes only; Ephram kept talking about the harbor. One step past a wall two years thick, taken the way he took everything: without announcement, at his own hour, in the middle of an ordinary noise.

Cael filed it where he filed the season's largest facts, and kept his face on the fish.

The table ran three hours, and it ran the way the delegation's best tables had always run, which was on the found family's native fuel: shop talk that wasn't, stories with their edges worn friendly, and the particular luxury — rarer this year than any — of nobody at the table being measured.

Ephram held the first hour with the harbor. He had discovered, in the way of a man whose curiosity had finally found its leisure, that Norhold's wharfs ran on a basin-lock system two centuries old, and he narrated its mechanics with the enthusiasm the cohort usually reserved for entry timing, and the wharfmen at the next table, overhearing, corrected him twice and then adopted him, and by the meal's middle he was three benches away being shown a lock-keeper's log, entirely happy, a continental Iron competitor on his rest day apprenticing himself to a harbor for the afternoon.

"He's going to come back knowing how to drain the harbor," Lira said.

"He's going to come back knowing the *duty roster* of the harbor," said Karis, handless, at ease. "He learns systems now. He caught it from this table. We should quarantine."

The second hour belonged to the year — the table's own retrospective, unplanned, assembling itself the way such things did out of one story calling up another. Lira told the board-man story again, with the hand gestures, for Seln, who had been at the counter the first time and was at the table now; and the retelling turned into the wool town, and the wool town into the cold halls, and Brom, who narrated perhaps four stories a year, was induced to give the table the full account of the southern coach and the dossier — pausing at the hinge of it, four pages held out across a warm-up floor, to deliver the line that had become his private summary of the whole scouting economy: "He handed me the bracket's homework and thought he was returning a glove." And Seln, from the bench's end, in his flattest register, holding a mussel shell as though minuting it: "The office wishes to note, for completeness, that the glove metaphor was also purchasable. Three towns later, two academies were trading warm-up-floor courtesies in visible imitation. You started a fashion in intelligence laundering." The table's laugh went up whole, and Seln inclined his head a quarter inch, a man acknowledging applause at the correct administrative rate, and reached for the potatoes.

The table had registers, and they were as fixed as bout stances. Lira told forward, at speed, with her hands, arriving at the end before the middle had been laid and doubling back for it without apology. Brom told in inventory — the room, the number of people in it, what each of them was holding when the thing happened — and the whole tension of a Brom story came from how long he took to reach the verb. Karis did not tell stories at all; she cross-examined them, and the table had learned over two years that a Karis question dropped into the middle of somebody else's account was not an interruption but a repair. Ephram told his against himself, always, which the cohort had taught him and which he had since improved on until it was his own. And Seln's, on the rare occasions he issued one, arrived without a first-person pronoun anywhere in it, a consistency so total that Lira had once spent an entire evening in the cold halls trying to make him produce one and had failed at a cost of two cups of the good cider.

It was Lira, therefore, who went after Karis, because it was always Lira who went after Karis, and because a rest day was the only terrain on which the attempt had ever worked.

"Your first notebook," she said. "Not this one. The first one. What's on page one?"

Karis considered the harbor for long enough that the table assumed a refusal, and then gave it, in four words, flat: "*Things adults say that the paper does not support.*"

"How old were you?"

"Eleven. It ran to nine entries before the end of that month and I have never once had to revise the heading." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the scarred wood, empty-handed, writing nothing, and added the part she had plainly not intended to add, which was how the good ones always arrived. "Six of the nine were about me."

The proprietor came through with the second pot and stopped to establish, in the manner of dockside proprietors everywhere, exactly who was eating in her house. Tournament people, she was told. She said she had gathered that from the way they were sitting, which was the way fighters sat, all facing the room, and she informed them that her father had rowed the inner basin the cycle Norhold last hosted, and that the tournament was good for trade and bad for sleep, in that order, and that the cake had been ordered three days ago by the large one, who had also paid for it three days ago, which Brom received without expression and without denial while Lira looked at him down the length of the table for a considerable time.

In the third hour the table went quiet in the good way, the harbor light moving on the water, and Cael did the thing he had learned to let himself do perhaps three times a year, which was nothing at all. No cataloguing. No count. The instrument down, the log shut, seventeen years old at a scarred table by a window, in the company of the only five people on the continent who knew both his ledgers, while the proprietor's honey-cake was cut by Lira into pieces of aggressively equal size and the tournament, for one mandated day, declined to exist.

*The first birthday was staged for the record,* he wrote later — because the comparison had sat with him all afternoon and the ledger deserved it. *Sixteen was a wall above a river, a delegation four days gone, a year that had to be closed out like a case file. It did structural work: it proved the day could be kept at all. Seventeen didn't have to prove anything. It just had to be true, and it was — smaller, warmer, unwitnessed by anyone but its own table. Note for the general theory of instruments: the first measurement calibrates. The second one just reads. This one read.*

---

Hesk's package was the size of a book because it was one.

He opened it at the table, because the table had earned the opening: brown paper, instrument-maker's twine, and inside, a bound volume — blank, a ledger of the working kind, but of no working grade Cael had ever handled. The boards were quarter-bound in leather the color of good tea, the corners metaled, the paper heavy and faintly toothed, sewn in signatures that opened flat at any page and would still open flat, he understood, examining the spine's construction, in forty years. The endpapers were marbled in grey and silver. It was the most beautiful object anyone had ever given him, and it was also, exactly, a tool — the two facts not in tension anywhere in its construction, which was the entire grammar of the man who had made it.

The note was in the front, in the upright letters that had taught Cael his own:

*Seventeen. My trade says an instrument is finished when it measures true — not when it looks done. You measure true. Let the rest of them look. — H.*

He read it twice at the table and did not manage to say anything for a moment, and the table, which had its own competence in such moments, filled the gap with the honey-cake.

Lira read the note — he passed it to her; the found family's post ran open by old custom — and then read it aloud, because it deserved the air, and the table took it in with the respect of people who had all, in their different years, been measured wrong by instruments that looked done.

"Instrument-maker's grade," Brom said, turning the volume once in his big hands with a craftsman's son's assessment, handing it back. "That binding's a hundred-year book. He's telling you the log's going to matter longer than you think."

"He's telling him it already does," said Karis.

The volume went round the table the way tools went round the table, which was thoroughly and without ceremony. Lira opened it flat at the middle, laid her palm along the gutter, and pronounced it a ledger you could write in one-handed while holding something in the other, which was the highest sentence available in her vocabulary for an object. Karis found the ruling — faint, grey, twenty-eight lines to the page, and down the outer edge of every leaf a narrow second rule that no mass-bound register on the continent carried — and looked at it for some time.

"He ruled you a margin," she said.

"For annotations."

"For arguing with yourself. He's read your log."

"He's never seen a page of it."

"Then he's read *you*, and worked out that whatever you write down, you'll want to take issue with it in eighteen months." She closed the volume and pushed it back across the scarred wood with two fingers, the way she handed over documents. "It's the correct design. I'd have specified it myself and I'd have taken four pages to justify it."

Seln, from the bench's end, supplied the parcel's provenance in the register of a man reading from a manifest, which he was.

"The pouch listed it as stationery, bound, one," he said. "It came up from the coast on the ordinary post, in a crate of instrument varnish, insured at a valuation the office considers instructive." He aligned his cup with the table's edge. "The office has handled four thousand parcels this tournament. That is the first one it has been sorry to hand over."

The rest of the pouch's personal post got its reading at the table too, by the open custom. A card from Quenna — the first since midwinter — in the Greyvane scout's economical hand: *The broadsides reach even here. Two Silver volunteers. I recall a boy who needed persuading that a sanctioned floor was worth the exposure. Tell him the persuading is the part I put on my own ledger. — Q.* A letter from Vell that was, characteristically, a receipt: she had entered the tournament's published exhibition ratings into the Ardenmere ledger against his old circuit record, because continuity of record was continuity of record, and the letter enclosed the ledger's cross-reference number *in case the tournament's filing proves less durable than mine.* Lira read that one aloud twice on request, and pronounced the cross-reference number "the most Vell birthday greeting ever issued," and the table drank to the ledger-keeper of Ardenmere, who would have entered the toast itself as an item had she been present, with a stamp.

The table broke up at the turn of the afternoon, the way the delegation's tables broke up, which was over money and took twelve minutes. Lira had the proprietor's reckoning in her hand before anyone else had noticed it arrive, on the grounds that she had annexed the table and annexation carried obligations; Ephram, back from the harbor with lock-grease on his cuff and a diagram he had been given, said that a birthday reckoning was the cohort's and produced a purse; Karis observed, without heat, that Lira's argument was procedurally sound and substantively self-serving, which was a Karis compliment; and Brom, who had settled the cake three days earlier and the rest of it that morning, let the proceeding run its whole course before mentioning either fact. Seln paid for the cider on his own account, alone, quietly, at the counter on the way out, which nobody remarked on, and which the whole table saw.

---

He wrote the first entry that evening, back at the guesting-house, at the window, in the old volume's last pages first — the closing of an interval; open it, enter what happened, enter what it cost, close it — and then, on the new volume's first heavy page, in his best hand, the inventory, because a new ledger opened with a statement of account.

*Seventeen. Written at Norhold, on the tournament's rest day, in the volume Hesk made. The date witnessed: the table at the blue door, all of them, and the day itself written into the log in my own hand, which he taught me to do, for numbers nobody was tracking on my behalf. Somebody is tracking this one. That is the change the ledger opens on.*

*Inventory, current and confirmed:*

*Six fragments. Wind-adjacent — Lira; the oldest; working ceiling six repetitions, deployment notes current through the Shield bout's eleven relocations, which taxed the ceiling's economics a new way and taught me displacement is cheaper than evasion at scale. Pressure-adjacent — Feryn; reliable, restricted, the restriction now three years old and load-bearing in the record. Iron-adjacent — Brom; the surface read; the cheapest thing I own and the one that has paid for every Silver exchange of this tournament. Compression-adjacent — Reydan; contact absorption; banked in public since the day it arrived, held banked at a handspan's range twice this fortnight, at a price the old volume records honestly. Ember-adjacent — Karis, in her handwriting; ignition point, contact, both licensed deployments spent per bout at documented rates. Shadow-adjacent — source protected, here as everywhere; sixteen months; deployment: none, ever, anywhere public; the cost continuous and the count per week still falling; useful nowhere yet, third year of that answer begins.*

*One anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent. Turned over once tonight, per the ledger's custom at openings and closings — to check that I still cannot reproduce it and still cannot explain it. I still cannot do either. Still open. Still real. Patience.*

*One semester stamp, renewed. One enrollment, lawful. One tournament, half spent. One question, load-bearing, opened at the midpoint and not closed tonight either: which tier is mine. The new volume inherits it the way it inherits everything — entered, dated, honest, unresolved.*

He sat with the page a while when it was done, the ink drying in Hesk's paper's patient tooth, and let the volume's heft say the thing it had been built to say. An instrument is finished when it measures true.

The team trial draw posted at dusk.

It went up on the outer court's boards with the rest of the resumed schedule — the Concourse's canvas coming off in the evening light, the city stretching itself back toward tournament time — and the delegation walked down together to read it, because nobody read draws alone, and the custom had carried them through a season of pool lines and seeding sheets and had one more thing to carry.

The team trial bracket was eight lines: the eight academies whose accumulated standings had qualified squads. Single elimination. Scenario floors. And on the semifinal line, in the tournament's flat type, the pairing the season's whole arithmetic had been assembling:

HALCENVANE — AUREMONT.

The table's quiet at the cookshop had been the good kind. The quiet at the boards was the other kind — the specific quiet of people reading the thing they had hoped for and feared in the same line, because Auremont in the semifinal meant the reigning champions at full squad strength, the program that had eliminated both their Iron semifinalists, the deepest roster on the continent.

The bracket's other half ran RHAGEN — the institute had qualified its squad on doctrine depth, Marek and the Shield captain anchoring — against the river academy's methodical five, and the geometry was legible to everyone at the rail: the winners met in the final, the beaten semifinalists met in the third-place trial, and every plausible route through the draw ran Halcenvane into one of the two academies whose best fighters the delegation had already fought once each, in single combat, with the surveys filed on both sides. "The tournament's a small town by the third week," was Ephram's reading, the harbor diagram still folded in his coat. "Everyone's already met. Now we meet in fives."

The outer court filled around them while they stood there, because a draw posts once and a city reads it all evening. The stewards had the canvas off the main board and were chalking the resumed schedule beneath the trial lines, and the chalk could not keep ahead of the reading; the sheet went up, the front rank read it aloud to the rank behind it, and the pairing crossed the court in something under a minute by Cael's count, in three languages, arriving at the far colonnade compressed to a single word. Rhagen's people read it and were, unmistakably, relieved — the institute had drawn the river academy, and a doctrine squad would always rather meet a methodical five than the holders — and nobody at Rhagen had the poor taste to say so within earshot of anyone in plain colors. The river academy's coaches read it twice and went away talking fast and quietly. Two of Ternhall's Iron entries, eliminated three days before and staying on to watch, read the semifinal line, turned round to find the Halcenvane party in the crowd, found them, and one of them lifted her cup an inch: the tournament's oldest gesture, meaning *better you than us, and go on anyway*.

The betting rows had reopened with the canvas and were already crying prices on the trial, four criers within thirty feet of the board, and Cael listened to them the way he listened to any market, which was for the gaps. A price on Auremont. A price on the final. A price on the number of exchanges. No price at all on the fifth Halcenvane name, and two of the four criers were saying so at volume, as a feature, because a market that cannot quote you will sell the fact that it cannot quote you.

And it meant her. Everyone at the boards was too disciplined to say it, and everyone at the boards was thinking it, and the broadsides would print it by morning: Auremont's squad had five names, and the first of the five was the tournament's Gold-tier fact, and a scenario floor was not a seeded bracket. There was no tier line in a team trial. There was just the floor, and whoever the academies fielded on it.

Which was when Karis — who had brought nothing to the cookshop and carried nothing all day, and who had, it now became apparent, spent the walk down to the boards retrieving her working self from wherever she had parked it — took from her coat the notebook, and opened it to a page banked five days into the tournament, and laid it flat on the board-rail where the delegation could read it in the torchlight.

*Squads shall be drawn from the academy's roster.*

The trial rules' section number. The cross-reference to the sixth part. The one word beneath: *Again.*

She looked at the space where Withrow would stand when the filing question was put — the chancellor was at the delegation head's evening session; the decision would be hers by morning — and then she looked at Cael, and the torchlight did what torchlight did, and the season arrived at the sentence it had been walking toward since a courier took a document wallet down a bluff road in the autumn.

"You're on the roster," she said. "The clause does the rest. Again."

The log, that night, in the new volume, second entry, the hand steady:

*Seventeen. Six fragments. Tomorrow I find out if the second technicality holds, and if it does — the found family fights together, on a sanctioned floor, for the first time in any of our lives. Whatever else this tournament is for, it was for that.*

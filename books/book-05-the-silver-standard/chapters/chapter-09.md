# Chapter 9 — Opening Rounds

The Continental Academy Tournament opened at midmorning on the first day of the spring's second month, and it opened the way institutions of six centuries open things: with a procession that was also a ranking, a ceremony that was also a schedule, and a full city in tiered stone watching a liturgy most of them could recite.

The delegations processed onto the main floor in qualifying order, to music the tournament had used so long that nobody now knew its composer, which the crowd read fluently — early entrants first, the great academies later, the whole arrangement a sentence with its emphasis at the end. Halcenvane walked eleventh of fourteen, and Cael walked it with the observation notebook's discipline running even where the notebook couldn't: counting the tiers as banks of faces, cataloguing the delegations ahead and behind, logging the pressure of the tiers' massed attention as a physical datum — a weather he had never stood in, which had to be learned like footing. Withrow led them, in the registry-official's plain formal grey she wore perhaps twice a year; the four competitors walked in bracket order; Cael walked where the marshals had, after visible deliberation, put him — last in the file, unbracketed, which suited both the marshals' logic and, though they could not know it, the record's.

The crowd's sound changed when Halcenvane's block reached the mid-floor. Not louder: *specific*. A wave of recognition running the tiers ahead of the delegation's own pace, the whole stone bowl performing the elbow-tap at once, and he heard his own case reach him from the stone in scraps — *the unclassified — that's him, the young one at the back — the clause, the one with the clause* — and walked through it the way Rooke's floor doctrine said to walk through weather, which was: acknowledge, price, continue.

Auremont came last, behind the Silver Standard.

The banner entered on its ceremonial staff, carried by the tournament's own color-guard — the champion academy did not carry the Standard; the champion academy walked *behind* it, in the place of honor that was also, Cael noted, the place of custody — and the Concourse rose for the object with a completeness that taught him more about the tournament in four seconds than the charter had in a season. Then Auremont's file, forty strong, bracket order, and at the file's head, walking beside the standard's shadow at an unhurried processional pace:

Daeva.

First sight, at last — held, by the procession's own geometry, at a hundred meters and no nearer; the files wheeled at the mid-floor and came no closer to the delegations' blocks — and Cael did what he had waited a week to do, which was to point the whole instrument at her and read.

He got almost nothing, and he got it very clearly.

Nineteen, tall, carrying the walk fighters carried when nothing in the vicinity outranked them — except that every fighter he had ever read carried that walk *at* something: at a crowd, at an opponent, at a mirror somewhere. Hers was aimed at nothing. She walked beside the most venerated object on the continent, through the largest crowd of her life, wearing the attention of eight thousand people and an entire watching civilization's favorite story, and her carriage reported none of it — no performance, no suppression of performance, which was itself usually a performance. The compound gaze, at a hundred meters, across a procession, took its measure and returned the reading he entered in the notebook that evening, verbatim, because it deserved its own line:

*Daeva, first read: too little data. Which is itself data. I have read every fighter I've ever seen at this range — stance, weight, tells, the aim of their attention. She has either none of the ordinary surfaces or manages them past my range. Both possibilities are the same finding: whatever the instruments say about her, the instruments are not why she's dangerous. File open. Primary data at the procession's end: as the delegations dispersed, she looked at exactly one thing that was not architecture. It was our file. It was brief, and it was not curiosity. It was inventory.*

The procession's last act belonged to a grey man with a ledger, and Cael, primed by Rooke's briefing, watched it the way the rest of the Concourse watched the banner.

Chief Adjudicator Umber came to the main floor's center with two of his tower's clerks and no ceremony of person at all — a man in his sixties in the adjudication office's plain grey, unhurried, looking exactly as advertised like a senior clerk — and performed the cycle's founding ritual: the locking of the Standard. The reference profile's master tables were carried out in their case; Umber verified the seals of the last cycle's closing in view of the crowd, spoke the attestation — his voice, through the Concourse's built acoustics, dry and exact and entirely unperformed: *the mark is the mark of the last cycle and every cycle; no hand has adjusted it; against it all are measured alike* — and affixed the current cycle's seal, and eight thousand people watched a man stamp wax on a table of numbers with an attention they had not given the delegations.

"They cheer the banner," Rooke said quietly, at Cael's shoulder. "They *trust* the seal. Watch the difference in the sound."

The difference was audible: the banner had gotten the roar, and the seal got something lower and more valuable, a rumble of assent from tiers of people most of whom could not have read the tables and all of whom knew what the seals were for. Cael filed the acoustics of institutional trust beside the year's other findings about instruments, and added the note that mattered: *Umber verified the old seals before setting his own, in public, though nothing required it and nobody would have checked. The man audits himself in front of eight thousand people as a matter of routine. Rooke says he can't want anything from me. I'd sharpen it: he's the first person in this apparatus whose wants I've been able to read at range. He wants the mark to be true. It's all over his hands.*

---

The opening rounds began the next morning on four floors at once, and the tournament taught its first structural lesson inside the hour: everything Rooke had said about a bracket of ceilings was arithmetic, not caution.

Lira's opening Iron bout ran long, and the length was the lesson.

Her first-round opponent was a mid-seed from the southern academies, an Ash Path skirmisher whose regional file would not have troubled a semifinal — and the tournament's second floor, a true fast square under the Concourse's high glass, gave neither fighter anything but each other. First exchange, Lira ran the season's standard survey and the survey came back wrong-footed: the skirmisher's Ash-screen work — brief occlusions, drifting veils that broke sightlines for a half-beat at a time — was regional-ordinary in kind and continental in *discipline*, every screen placed to cover an actual intention rather than to look like coverage, and her reads, built all season on seeing more than opponents managed, spent the whole exchange seeing exactly as much as he permitted. He took the first on flags. Nobody had taken a first exchange off her since the quarry town.

She adjusted the way the file said she adjusted, which was structurally: if the screens broke sightlines, she would stop buying with sight. Second and third exchange she fought him increasingly by rhythm and floor-feel — the skirmisher's feet made honest sounds his veils couldn't cover; the burst-economy ran on hearing for whole half-beats at a time — and the exchanges came back to her one flag at a time, expensive, real. The fourth she won going away, his screen-budget spent, and the flags ended it, and she came to the rail afterward not tired so much as *informed*, and delivered the finding to the delegation in one line: "Regional bouts test whether you're good. These test whether you're good *everywhere*. He found the sense I lean on and taxed it. Round one."

There were no curious locals in this field, no fifth bodies making up delegation minimums. The floor of this bracket was the ceiling of every bracket she had toured all year — and the file's honest margin, which Cael wrote and showed her, and she approved with a nod: *she won on adaptation, not on margin. At regional scale she had both. Here the margin's gone and the adaptation is the whole purse. Zerin sits two rounds away, and Zerin's file says her margin travels.*

The draw's geometry got its on-page confirmation the same session, because Karis's opening bout ran on the adjacent floor in the same hour, and the delegation split its rail to cover both: separate pools, the two of them, by the seeding office's ordinary practice of distributing an academy's same-bracket entries — they could meet in the final and nowhere earlier, and the delegation's own briefing had underlined it, Rooke tapping the two pool columns: "You're not each other's problem. Solve forward." Karis solved forward in three exchanges, lattice-and-ignition efficiency against a Rune Path methodist, and was back at Lira's rail in time to chart the fourth.

Brom's Copper opener was the morning's brief comedy — his opponent had read page three of an old edition; the funnel collected its first continental admission fee in ninety seconds — and his second-round bout that afternoon was the real news: a hill-country mispricing of genuine quality, the buried kind, who took Brom to a fifth exchange for the first time all season and left the floor having earned the handshake the gallery gave him. The Copper bracket at Norhold, the file recorded, was exactly what the compiler's advisory had promised: a conference of the system's pricing errors, upward and downward, meeting each other at last.

Ephram beat the coastal column's second entry in a bout Rooke used for the evening's teaching — "watch the third exchange; he spent two flags to learn the man's reset and bought the bout with the knowledge; that's the trade, that's the whole trade" — and stood, by the second evening, in the round of sixteen, at his stated price of admission, trading.

*The delegation's opening ledger,* Cael wrote. *Four for four, no exchange lost that wasn't tuition, and the tournament's answer already legible: everyone here is somebody's best. The season taught us to beat regional fields. The next eight days find out what it taught us about ceilings.*

---

His own slate posted on the second evening, and the Concourse's rumor engine met it with the sound of a city getting the entertainment it had budgeted for.

Two challenges filed. Both Silver-tier.

The first was a Silver Rank 6, a Shield Path squad captain from Rhagen — the institute's team-trial anchor, a doctrine fighter of fifteen years' standing whose filing, per the category's custom, stated its reason: *doctrinal interest.* Rhagen's coaching floor, said the delegation's collective intelligence within the hour, had read the dossiers like everyone else, and had concluded, with Rhagen's characteristic directness, that the interesting question about the unclassified enrollee was whether structured doctrine could do what a season of volunteers hadn't; and they had sent their best structure to ask. The second was a Silver Rank 4, a Blade Path duelist from the lake academy, whose filing stated, with a candor the category's clerks reportedly appreciated, *the crowd.* A duelist's economy: the largest galleries of the cycle would attend these bouts, and a fighter whose craft was performance wanted the performance's biggest room.

The scheduling clerk set both for the tournament's middle days — the card's natural theater slot — and the Concourse's boards, where the exhibition frame's blank lines had sat for a week like an unanswered question, filled their first two ruled lines with Silver-tier names, and the rumor engine celebrated. Cael heard the fact repeated around him for two days in the tone of citizens reporting a wonder: the demonstration category had not drawn a Silver volunteer in living memory. It had now drawn two in an evening, for the same opponent, who held no rank at all.

"You understand what happened," Karis said, at the table, in her audit voice. "The category's economics inverted. All season the calculation was: a ranked fighter risks standing against you and gains nothing the sheet can hold. That held exactly as long as you were a regional curiosity. At Norhold you're the cycle's story — so now the calculation runs: a Silver who fights you well is *in* the story, win or lose. Rhagen's captain gets his doctrine written about on every gallery sheet on the continent. The duelist gets the crowd of his career." She turned her cup. "You've become the thing you spent three years studying. An information asymmetry. Everyone else fights for points. People fight *you* for meaning."

"And the meaning's priced in Silver now."

"The meaning's priced in Silver *this week*," said Karis. "Mind the trend line."

Rooke arrived at the table with the two filings copied into the coaching file and ran the delegation's preparation council on the spot, because Rooke did not let novel problems age overnight. "The duelist first, because he's simpler: Blade Path, Rank 4, performance fighter. Dangerous the way theater's dangerous — he'll fight *at the gallery*, tempo built for effect, and effect-tempo has real teeth if you let it set the bout's clock. You won't. Next." He turned the page. "Rhagen's captain is the actual examination. Shield Path, Rank 6, fifteen years in doctrine, and Rhagen doctrine is the best-documented fighting system on the continent — which cuts both ways: I can get you his structure to the half-beat, and he knows I can, and he's coming anyway. Understand what that means. He's not coming to surprise you. He's coming to run the doctrine at full depth and find out whether *anything* you do breaks it. It's not a challenge, it's a — " Rooke searched for the word and landed on it with distaste — "a *survey*. Rhagen surveys. Then Rhagen's whole institute reads the survey."

"So the bout's real audience is Rhagen's coaching floor," Cael said.

"The bout's real audience," said Rooke, "is everyone who will ever plan against you off Rhagen's findings. Which at this tournament is everyone." He closed the file. "Ten days. We'll build the answer the way we built the season: you'll show the survey exactly the country you want mapped."

Seln, sorting the evening's papers at the table's end, contributed the council's closing datum without looking up: "The office notes the Rhagen filing was countersigned by their delegation head — institutional, not personal. And that the adjudication office assigned both bouts its senior exhibition panel." He clipped the schedule into the file. "Somebody in Umber's tower is also treating the fossil category as load-bearing this cycle. Scheduling is opinion."

*Scheduling is opinion,* the log repeated that night. *The category that waited three hundred years is getting the senior panel and the theater slot. The tournament's own machinery has started making room around me — quietly, procedurally, the way this year does everything. Somewhere between a squad clause with no qualifier and a fossil provision drawing Silver, the question is assembling itself in the tournament's own documents: what, exactly, is the instrument for, if not this?*

---

The team-trial rules posted the same week, in the adjudication office's methodical sequence — brackets first, trial second, ceremonies last — and Karis read them the evening they went up, at the guesting-house table, in preparation for the squad phase, because reading governing documents before they governed anybody was the reason she existed.

Cael was across the table with the Rhagen captain's bout records. He had learned, over two years, to work beside her reading without watching it, and he had also learned exactly what it sounded like when the reading stopped.

The reading stopped.

He did not look up. Forty seconds. She read whatever it was twice more — he could hear the return passes in the paper — and then she took the notebook, and opened it not to the tournament section but to the front matter, where a certain family of citations lived, and copied one line with the unhurried care she gave to load-bearing text. Then she wrote a single word beneath it, and closed the book, and said nothing at all, and went back to the trial rules, and finished them, and made tea.

He retrieved it from her at the evening's end, because the protocol between them had long since dispensed with pretense: she handed him the notebook open to the page, unasked.

The copied line read: *Squads shall be drawn from the academy's roster.*

Below it, in citation form, the trial rules' section number. Beside that, cross-referenced in her tidy documentary hand, the older entry it now stood next to — sixth part, participation, subsection four, *shall field any practitioner appearing on their enrollment record* — and beneath both, the single word:

*Again.*

"No tier qualifier," Cael said.

"No classification requirement. No seeding reference. Drawn from the roster; the roster is the enrollment record; the enrollment record is the law." She took the notebook back and squared it. "And that is the entire discussion we are having about it tonight. It's banked. The brackets run first, and a question you ask early is a question somebody else answers first — we know this; we have the season to prove it. If the trial draw makes it matter, it's ready. If not, it sleeps." She stood, collected her cup, and paused at the stair with the day's last finding, delivered as lightly as she ever delivered anything, which was not very:

"Second time, Cael. The same drafters, the same stratum, the same silence where the qualifier should be. Once is an oversight. Twice is starting to look like a door somebody built on purpose. A note for winter. Good night."

The city, meanwhile, conducted its tournament, which was a different tournament from the fighters', running on the same events.

Cael walked it on the third evening with Brom — the delegation's crowd-formation now automatic, the mountain at his outside shoulder — and catalogued the parallel apparatus at work: the betting rows along the outer court doing a cycle's business in a week, boards dense with brackets, and his own name present after all, not as odds but as an exhibit — *no book on the demonstration bouts*, chalked and framed, the wool town's precedent gone continental; the gallery-sheet printers running damp broadsides of the day's results with commentary attributed to initials; the food rows; the color-sellers; two enterprising children conducting a brisk secondary market in delegation buttons; and everywhere, in every register from tavern argument to printed analysis, the cycle's story being assembled in public out of its available parts — the champion academy and its prodigy, the eighteen-year absence returned, the mispriced Copper destroying his bracket, and the boy the registry couldn't name, whose two Silver challenges had turned the tournament's fossil category into, the broadsides agreed, the hottest card of the middle week.

"You're reading the whole city," Brom observed, at the court's rail.

"The city's reading me. It's only polite." He watched a broadside change hands below, his own woodcut face upside down in a stranger's grip. "Three years ago the whole apparatus watching me was two rotation men and a budget. Now it's a civilization with a printing industry, and the strange finding is it weighs *less*. The rotation men were reading my file. These people are reading their own story, and I just have a part in it. Nobody down there wants the mechanism. They want the theater."

"And when the theater and the mechanism are the same thing?" said Brom, who asked the load-bearing question about once a season, always mildly.

Cael was quiet for a moment.

"Then I'd better be sure which one's on the floor," he said, and Brom nodded and let it stand, and the notebook got the exchange verbatim that night, filed under: *things Brom asks that I answer too quickly.*

---

He went up to the gallery on the fourth evening to chart the Compact.

The observation row sat above the main floor's east rail, a reserved bank of twelve seats with the registry's plain sigil on the rail-cloth, and Cael had been cataloguing its occupancy since the opening the way he had once catalogued rotations from a boarding-house window — by schedule, by face, by the arithmetic of who sat where. The tournament observation seat proper: **Ilsev**, senior evaluator of record, four days running, files squared on her lap-desk at angles that had not varied by a degree all week, working the bouts below with the procedural scruple he remembered from a hearing room two years ago — a woman who had read his first case at the counter of his life and had been, in every recorded act since, exactly correct. Behind her, second seat of six: **Havel** — older than the assessor Cael had first charted in Ardenmere, carrying the same private carefulness, a man Cael had never spoken to and whose file of quiet appearances in his life now ran to three postings and two academies. Around them, the monitoring rotation's ordinary staff, badged, listed, unremarkable.

And at the row's end, one seat apart from the working seats, an empty chair.

It had been empty all four days. It bore no name-plate — every other seat in the row bore one — and it was not a spare: the rail-cloth ran to it, the seat was maintained, and twice Cael had watched Concourse stewards decline to seat gallery overflow in it with the particular firmness of men executing an instruction they had not been given reasons for. He worked the question the way he worked everything. Not Ilsev's superior from the district: that officer had a plated seat, third position, occupied on opening day and empty since, per the ordinary economics of ceremonial attendance. Not the adjudication office: Umber's people sat their own tower. Not Auremont's or anyone's academy liaison: wrong row, wrong protocol. The chair belonged to the observation row's own hierarchy, was held against an occupant the stewards expected, and carried, in place of a name, an instruction.

He could not assign it, and he logged the failure honestly, which was the notebook's oldest law.

*Compact row, seat twelve: reserved, unplated, unfilled, defended. Somebody is expected at this tournament whom the seating order declines to name in advance. I have run my file of candidates twice and the file returns nothing at the right altitude — nameplates are for people whose attendance is a fact; the blank is for someone whose attendance is a decision. Being made where I can't see it, by someone the stewards answer to at one remove.*

*The chapter of people who watch me has a new page, and the page is blank, and I have learned what blank pages in this story are.*

*They're the ones that get written last, by the person the file is actually about.*

He closed the notebook on the empty chair and went down to the floors, where the brackets ran on under the lamps, and the tournament's first week gathered itself toward the fights the whole season had been for.

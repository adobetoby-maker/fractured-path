# Chapter 24 — Alignment

The snow came in the night before the ferry's last run, and he catalogued Lowmarch under it from the landing because he had catalogued it from a rise in six weathers and wanted the seventh to be the last one honest.

The river running black between white banks, with skin ice along the shallows that the ferry's bow would break on the way out and would not break on the way back, because there would not be a way back until the thaw. The landing with its bales gone, all of them, and the broker's mark under a hand of snow on the one crate that was left. The meadow white and empty, the tent-rings under it. Smoke from every roof. The board's room at the lower inn with both doors shut against the cold and the road door's hand's-width gone, so that a man who wanted the smoke out would have to want the cold in, and Pike, who kept the room, had chosen the smoke. The ferryman at his ropes with a boy, and the tarred sack on the deck with the Line's lead on it, waiting for the last of what was going west before the ice shut the door.

Five of them, on the hundred and twenty-ninth day past the Line, at the landing, in the snow, with a mule and three boxes and a survey kit and a girl with her arm across her chest, because first snow was first snow and a healer had said she would say when, and had not yet said.

He catalogued the five of them at the landing as he had catalogued them at every halt of the year, because it was the last halt and he wanted the count. Lira, one-armed, the sling gone grey with six weeks of road, the taped foot put down flat in the snow, Oryn's crossbow across her back on its strap; she had walked six days from the fourth site on that foot and had not once said the word *ankle*, and would not until a healer said the other word first. Brom, with the kit on one shoulder and the middle box on the same one, and the other shoulder carried in the same guarded set it had kept since a hall — still, and slightly forward — though the healer of no Path at Teague's fire had said it wanted binding and not hands, and it had been bound, and had healed under the binding as a shoulder heals when it is left alone by a man who cannot leave things alone. Karis with the three copies rolled under her arm in oilcloth against the snow, the ledger inside her coat where Seln had let her carry it for the last two days, and the straightedge in her hand, because she had not once put it back in the box since the marsh. Seln with the case on his back and his eyes on the landing door, counting who came out of it. And himself, with nothing in his hands, and ten things held to nothing, and a still place at the middle of them that he had read three times and had not touched since, because a healer had said *stop* and he had stopped.

---

Pike read the season's line before he entered it, because the procedure required it, and the procedure was the only thing at Lowmarch that had never once bent for the weather.

He read it at the plank, to the book, in the voice he used for a price, with the room full because the room was where the warm was and the five of them at the plank because it was theirs, and he read the whole of it at the size a line was read, no larger.

"*Crew of six. Ford cleared, traverse mapped, Ring entered, Stair entered and instruments recovered, Hall entered. No losses. Returned every time.*"

The pen stopped. "That's said."

"That's said," Cael said.

He read the page upside down across the plank, because he read everything upside down and had never once stopped: nine completed lines under the five names, the last three of them the traverse, the Ring, and the Stair, in Pike's hand, each with its outcome and its price, and the Stair's price column saying *three hundred; taken; not paid*, in the small hand Pike used for a thing that was true and was not going to change. Pike put two fingers flat on the ledger and turned it, with one push, so that it faced across the plank, which he had done twice in a season, and Cael read it the right way up, and it said what it had said.

Pike did not write a number.

He had the wall sheet on the plank beside the book — the thirteen crews in the one-armed hand, top to bottom, Teague's four at the top where they had been for three years, and the five names eleventh, where he had put them after the Ring — and he had the pen over it, and he did not put it down, and the room, which had been not-looking at the five of them since the Stair in the way a room does not look at a thing it has decided about, stopped pretending it was not looking at the pen.

Pike said, to the sheet: "Teague says you need a column I don't have. I've kept this ledger thirty years. First time it's been wrong about a crew by being too small."

He put the pen down. He did not enter a line number. The five names stayed where they were, eleventh, on a sheet that had thirteen crews on it and a column for outcome and no column for whatever it was that walked into three floors and came out, and Pike pinned the sheet where it went, by the road door, unchanged, and stood back from it as he stood back from a price he had just said, and that was the saying, and the saying was for the room, and the room had heard it.

Cael filed it beside a note in a records hall on a bluff that had closed a file with *the instruments will not see its like*, and beside a captain on a road at dark saying *no column*, and found that the shelf had three ledger-keepers on it now, and that the third had just done the only honest thing a ledger can do with a thing it cannot hold, which is to say so aloud and leave the line blank.

The room had heard it, and the room did what a room does with a thing it has decided about, which is nothing, aloud. The hide-woman at the middle table with her ledger shut on her thumb, who had looked at the five of them in the autumn as at a thing that would be gone by the snow, looked at them now with the snow on the window and did not open her ledger. The crew of three by the road door, whose canvas had gone from new to grey to patched, had stopped eating. Two carters at the fire, one of whom had walked the Thornwater road behind them in the first week, looked at the wall sheet and at the five names eleventh on it and at the one-armed man standing back from it, and one of them said, to the other, at the size a carter says a thing about a road, "Eleventh," and the other said, "Aye," and neither of them meant the number.

---

Teague wintered at Lowmarch, as Teague's crew wintered every year, at the best table, with the jug, and he came to the plank while Pike was still standing back from the sheet, because a captain who has set a term does not wait for a better moment to say it.

He stopped a stride and a half off. He did not look at the sheet.

Teague said: "Spring. Whatever's on that bearing. My crew comes."

It was not an offer. It was a term, stated once, by a man who kept his own ledger and had put a line in it at a rim and had come three days along a bearing with a healer of no Path to bind a shoulder, and who now, at a plank in the snow, with the tall woman and the broad man and the young one at the table behind him not looking at the plank, as a crew does not look at its captain setting a term, told the crew at the bottom of a wall where he would be when the ice went out.

"Noted," Cael said.

Teague went back to his table, where his three sat with the jug and the fifth — the older woman with the cloth bag, who had bound a shoulder at a marsh and had walked back to Lowmarch with them and had not once said a word to any of the five since — and who looked up as her captain sat down, and at Brom's shoulder across the room, once, the way she had looked at it at the marsh, and went back to her cup, which was a healer's whole verdict and Brom took it as one.

Nobody at the plank said anything, because there was nothing to say that the word had not said, and Seln, at the door end, who had been watching the rival clock since a board's room in the autumn, looked at the man's back for the length of a breath and then at the snow through the landing door's crack, and Cael saw the look and did not have a column for it either, and let it stand.

---

The winter's contract went on the plank before the inventory went in the satchel, because that was the order the year kept: the board first, then the book.

Pike had one slip that had waited since the autumn — the axe-head hold's long fence, forty marks, HOLD, Teague, which Teague's crew had walked in the autumn and had not finished because the Stair had taken them south — and Pike said, to the book, "Fence. Forty. Teague says it's yours if you want it; he's got the winter road," and Brom, who priced fences, said, "Ours," and Pike wrote HOLD and the five names under it, and the winter had a job in it, which was the thing a winter needed. Karis took the inn's back room for the sheets, at a price the woman who kept the inn named and Karis paid without counting, which Cael had never once seen her do with money. Seln bought nothing, and sat at the door end, and watched the landing, and when the ferryman came in to say the sack was closing at noon, Seln looked at the sack and at his own coat where the ledger had been and was not, and said nothing, because he had given the ledger to Karis on the road and had not asked for it back and was not going to.

The inventory went into the satchel by the inn's fire that evening, by lamp, with the five of them round it and the sixth on a route, and it was the fullest the log had ever carried, and he wrote it as an inventory and not as anything else, because the ledger of the year wanted its forms kept.

Hesk's satchel. He had carried it out of a residence room on a bluff a year ago with the log in the pocket built for it, and it had been on every road since, waxed leather over a frame built for forty years of weather, the spare buckle still sewn flat on the strap's underside where it would not chafe, and the leather had gone dark along the strap where his shoulder took it and the brass had gone dull at every corner and not one seam had opened, because a man who had watched a strap fail on a road had built it so that it would not fail on anyone he liked. He set it on his knee by the fire, and opened the flap, and took the log out, and the four of them let him, because the inventory was a thing they had watched him write at the end of every year since Greyvane and had learned to sit still for.

*Hundred and twenty-ninth day. Lowmarch, the lower inn, by the fire. First snow. The ferry's last run tomorrow. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned since the Ring, and three hundred entered as taken and never to be paid. Nineteen.*

*Ten confirmed fragments.*

*Wind-adjacent — Lira. Pressure-adjacent — the Bronze from Ardenmere. Iron-adjacent — Brom. Compression-adjacent — Reydan. Ember-adjacent — Karis. Shadow-adjacent — Seln; source protected; the seal held past the Line as it held inside, and was used twice out here where no one who could carry it west could see it, and that is the seal's whole logic and it is unchanged. Storm-adjacent — Daeva. Anchor-adjacent — unnamed; the hand with no face; opened a door on a rim and held a floor in a hall, and I own a piece of the craft the system uses to hold people and have used it twice this year, once each way. Blade-adjacent — Ephram. Tide-adjacent — Oryn; partial; Iron; self and contact; the tenth, and the first that does nothing in a fight, and the only one whose engagement field says clinical, and the only one with a rule on it in her hand and mine: ask first, every time. Deployment notes current on all ten.*

*One anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent. Not an entry any more; a location. The still place. Read three times. Doesn't flow. Doesn't change. Everything goes around it. Not the Quiet. Not a fragment. Older than the log. Observation continues.*

*Reydan will get his answer. Daeva will get her rematch. Both are going to need a map.*

He read it back once, and closed the book on the pencil, and put it in the satchel's pocket built for it, and closed the flap, which closed against nothing, because the snow was outside.

---

The ferry's last run took the mail west and brought the mail east, and the ferryman sorted it aloud at the plank by name, as he had sorted it four times since the autumn: the tarred sack opened on the plank, the Line's lead broken by Pike with the knife he kept for the pen, and the pieces taken out one at a time and the name on each said at the size a price was said, so that the room could hear who had been written to and who had not. Nine pieces. Six for carters who were wintering at holds and would have theirs carried out by the last carts. One for the inn. And two for the five of them, and one of the two was not a letter.

Hesk's package was the size of a hand.

He had known it by the wrapping before the ferryman said the name — the oiled cloth, the twine tied the way an instrument-maker ties twine, so that it comes off in one pull and can be tied again — and he took it to the fire and did not open it at the plank, because he had never once opened a thing of Hesk's at a plank. He opened it on his knee with the log beside it, because that was where things went when he had read them.

A straightedge. Brass, folding, three sections on two pins so that it lay in the hand the length of a palm and opened to the length of a forearm, and the edge true — he sighted along it against the fire and there was no light under the line — and calibrated, in the small struck marks he had known before he could read, along its whole length, in a hand that had cut the marks on every gauge in a shop in Denvash for forty years. Instrument-maker's work. Six weeks late.

The note was in the fold, in the upright letters:

*Nineteen. I'm told it's late; the ferry's fault, not mine. Your grandmother said a man who catalogues everything eventually needs to draw a line through it. This one's true to a hair. Mind the shape. — H.*

He read it twice. Then he looked across the inn's table at Karis's map, which was under her hands with three circles on it and a fourth and a line through all four, and at the brass straightedge from Ternhall lying along the line, and at the brass straightedge from Denvash in his own hand, and he did not say anything, and Lira, who had read the note aloud per the family's open post and had watched him look, did not say anything either, and Karis looked up from the map, once, at the thing in his hand, and went back to the map, which was, from Karis, a whole sentence.

He wrote west that night, by the fire, on the inn's paper, to a shop in Denvash, in the plain hand, short, because the ferry would carry a paragraph and would not carry a page:

*Nineteen, past the Line, and there's a roof. It's an inn. The shape you said to mind is a line, and I've got the tool for it now. — C.*

And to Vell, at Ardenmere, because her ledger held the first honest record of him that had ever been written and Pike's held the newest, and she would want to know the thing that a ledger-keeper wants to know about another one:

*Vell. There's a second ledger on the continent with my name in it, kept by a one-armed man in a town on no map, and this week he stopped numbering me. He said his book was wrong about us by being too small. You said once that somebody should have a record that doesn't answer to the Compact. There are two now. The session still stands. — C.*

He sealed both, and put them in the tarred sack himself, at the landing, in the snow, and watched the ferryman put the Line's lead on it.

---

The second piece for the five of them came by carter, not by ferry, the morning after, up the void road from the east with a hide-load and a mule, and the carter said the name at the plank and Pike said, "That one's here," and the carter gave it to Cael because Cael was the one who had come to the plank.

Thornwater's paper. A hand that had set a great many bones, square and plain, written to be read by people who did not read much, and it was four lines, and he read it at the plank, upside down first, because he read everything upside down, and then the right way up, and then aloud, to the four of them, because it was addressed to the five and the five kept an open post.

*Route's whole. The knee held. Nobody died of eleven days. Spring: bring the map, and lie still when I say. — O.*

Six, by letter.

He read it a fourth time to himself, because it was four lines and he wanted them: *Route's whole* — the seven holds walked round, the circuit closed, the thing she owned kept. *The knee held* — the old woman at Thornwater, up her ladder. *Nobody died of eleven days* — the cost she had laid flat on a bank at Millrace, paid, and found to be what she had said it might be and not what she had feared. *Spring: bring the map* — the same sentence as the fork, and the fork's sentence had meant the opposite of Oxhollow's, and this one meant the same as the fork's, and he had the whole of her in four lines and did not need a fifth. And *lie still when I say*, which was her terms of service, restated by post, so that the winter would not soften them.

He put it in the satchel, flat, against the leather book, where the others were, and Lira, one-armed, said, "She'll say when," meaning the arm, and meaning the spring, and Brom said, "Spring," meaning both, and that was the sixth chair filled, by post, at a plank, in the snow, because that was the only way she would ever have filled it, and everyone at the plank knew it, and nobody said so.

---

The map was on the inn's table, and it was the last thing.

Karis had laid it out at dusk without saying she was going to, the way she had laid the ring flat on Thornwater's table and the walls flat on the Stair's floor: the carter's sheet, with the four circles on it — the Ring, the Stair, the Hall, the fourth — and three of the centres marked to a stride in her small hand and the fourth as the carter had drawn it, near enough, and the kit's bearing drawn in pencil from the first two and running on, and the brass straightedge from Ternhall laid along the bearing, and now, beside it, the brass straightedge from Denvash, which Cael had opened and laid along the same line without being asked, and which touched the three centres without a hair of daylight and the fourth as near as the sheet allowed, exactly as the other did, because a thing that is true to a hair is true to a hair whichever hand cut the marks on it.

The five of them stood round it. Nobody had called them to. The lamp was on the table's corner and the fire was down to a working red and the snow was on the window, and the four circles lay on the sheet in a line, and the line went on past the fourth toward the map's edge and off it.

The sites were on an alignment. Not random.

Karis looked at it for a long time. She had the pencil in her hand and she did not use it, and she had the notebook under her other hand and she did not open it, and when she spoke it was the only sentence the evening permitted her, and she said it in the small voice, and once.

"Someone made this pattern."

Nobody answered. Nobody said who, or why, or how old. Lira looked at the line. Brom looked at the straightedges, both of them, brass on brass. Seln looked at the map's edge where the line went off it. Cael looked at the fourth circle, twice the size of the others, with a cordon round it that nobody had drawn on the sheet because nobody had needed to. The lamp's light lay along the brass and the brass laid its shadow along the line, a hair's width, the length of the map, and the five of them looked at the shadow for four seconds, by his count, and then Karis put her hand flat on the sheet, and the evening closed.

---

"I have ten things that aren't a Path," Cael said, eventually, to nobody in particular, "no tier, no rank, no file I can see — and five people, one of them on a route, who've walked into the quiet three times and come out." He turned it over once and let it stand. "And a line on a map that somebody drew before there was a registry to draw one."

Lira, one-armed, feeding the fire: "Then we follow it."

Brom: "Spring."

Karis, writing: "Noted."

Down the fire, the man who used to file reports about him watched the snow on the ferry landing with evident professional approval and said nothing, which — from him — was home.

Nothing out here had read him. At three floors older than any registry, every Path in the party had stopped at a line and his had not; a fourth had been paced from outside and not crossed. Nobody alive — least of all him — knew why. Someone had made the pattern. That was as far as anyone could say.

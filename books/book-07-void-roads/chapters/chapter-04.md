# Chapter 4 — What the Board Prices

The calf held for eleven paces at a time.

He learned the number on the second morning, crossing the yard from the loft to the well, because the leg was a thing he owned now and he inventoried what he owned. Eleven paces on the flat; then the bite reminded him, in and out, where the teeth had gone, and he put the weight forward onto the other foot for a pace and started the count again. Twelve if the ground fell away. Nine if it climbed. By the third day he had the yard mapped in elevens.

Inventory, taken at the foot of the loft ladder each morning, because the ladder was where everyone passed.

Brom's forearm: bound wrist to elbow in the woman's boiled cloth, the cloth gone dark at the wrist by the second day and cut off on the third. The woman came up the ladder with the pot and did it without asking. Brom held the arm out and looked at the wall while she washed the thing under the cloth and wound new cloth over it, tighter, and when she said "Don't use it," he said "No," as if it were the same conversation continued. He carried nothing. It was the first time Cael could remember seeing the three boxes on a floor with Brom standing beside them and not under them.

Lira's ankle: off the ground three days on the woman's order, and on the fourth a stick, a hay-fork's handle Seln had cut down to her height without being asked. The swelling had gone down far enough to show the shape, and the shape was not the shape of the other one. It bent where an ankle bent, but less, and later. She walked the yard on it with the stick and her mouth shut, and on the fifth day she came back from the gate and said, to nobody, "It's setting stiff," and the woman, passing with buckets, said, "It'll set how it sets," which was a verdict and not a comfort.

Karis's hands: wrapped still, the fingers free from the second knuckle out, and the palms under the cloth a thing she did not look at when the wrapping was changed. She could hold a cup between the heels of both hands. She could not hold a pen — and then, on the fifth day, she could, if holding was the word for it: the pen laid across the wrapped palm and pinned there by two fingertips and a thumb, the whole hand moving from the elbow because the wrist would not close. She took the notebook back from him that morning without saying anything about it, and he let her, and read over her shoulder what came out. The letters were twice their size and leaned two ways at once. Above the day's first line she had written, slowly, and it was still bad: *Hand poor. Fifth day after. Entries stand as written.* Then she had gone back and underlined *stand*.

And the calf, in elevens.

Four of five. He had written it the first night and Pike had written it on the slip and the yard confirmed it every morning at the ladder: four people healing slow or healing wrong, and one who was not hurt and went down to the ford at every dusk and came up at full dark and said "Nothing."

Nobody mentioned the limp. He had expected Lira to. She looked at his leg once, on the second day, at the well, and then at her own foot, and did not.

---

The steading had a table.

It stood at the barn's far end, a long plank on trestles where the woman set out bread for the carters at harvest, and there was no harvest and no bread on it, and Karis asked for it on the third day the way she had asked the woman for the ford's lore: plainly, with the notebook open under her arm. The woman looked at the wrapped hands, and at the three boxes at the ladder's foot, and said the table was a table, and went away.

Brom could not lift the boxes and did not try. Seln and Cael brought them down one at a time and set them on the plank in the order Karis named — left, middle, right — and she opened them with her fingertips, one lid, then the next, then the third, and stood back.

He had seen inside them a hundred times and catalogued them anyway, because it was the first time all three had been open at once, in daylight, on a surface that was not a floor. Paper, mostly: copies in her hand, in Prynn's, in hands he did not know, tied in bundles by subject, the subject on a slip under the string. A folding rule. Chalk. A tin of pencils. Two bound books carried out of three academies and never once opened on a road. Three years of a researcher's life packed as a carter packed grain: tight, by weight, nothing loose.

She could not lift the bundles, so she pointed, and Seln lifted, and by noon the boxes were empty and the plank was full, and it was — he looked for the word and found the one she would have used — a library. Shelves with no wall behind them. The bundles stood on end in rows with their slips facing out, and she walked the length of the plank with her wrapped hands held behind her back and read the slips like a woman reading a street she had lived on for years and never seen from above.

"There," she said. "That's everything I know."

"It's a lot," said Brom, from the door.

"It's everything I know about the wrong country." She had reached the right-hand end. "Every line on this table was written inside. Registry stone, academy stone, copies of copies from archives with doors on them. All of it is *before the Line*. I've walked eight weeks in a place where nothing is verified and every cart that passes me has a man on it who knows something, and I have not got one line out of any of them I could set on this table under a slip." She looked at the hands. "And I couldn't write it if I had."

"You've got the woman's," Lira said, from the bench by the door, the stick across her knees. "Dusk. Out of the water. River's their colour. That's three lines."

"That's lore. It's good lore. It kept us alive. It's under a slip." Karis nodded down the plank at a bundle at the far end, thin, one page. "That's the shelf. One page, dictated, in his hand. There's no *record* out here. There are people who know things and a road, and the people get on the road and go, and what they know goes with them." She said it without heat, as she said a finding. "I'm a researcher in a country that keeps nothing. I'd like to write that down, and I can't hold the pen."

She asked everyone anyway.

The grain moved on the fifth day, because the ford was clear and the rain was a day off. Six carts came up from the Lowmarch road empty in the morning and stood in the yard loading, and with the carts came carters, and with two of the carters their wives, who drove; and Karis went out into the yard with the notebook against her forearm and the pen pinned in the wrapped hand and asked every one of them the same thing.

The question was the void roads. Not the dogs — the dogs were under a slip. The roads: which ones, and where they went, and what a carter knew about the stretches that the map on Pike's plank drew as a dashed line stopping in nothing.

Most of them told her which roads. Two told her where they went. One, a man with a clouded eye, told her he did not talk about roads to paper, and she wrote that down too.

The carter's wife was on the fourth cart, a woman of forty in a coat cut for a bigger person, the reins once round her fist and a boy of ten beside her on the board. She listened to the whole question with her head to one side, and when Karis had finished she said:

"You mean the quiet ground? Everybody knows the quiet ground. Nobody goes in."

Karis stood very still.

Cael, at the barn door with the calf on its ninth pace, watched her not ask. He watched her want to — the whole body leaned toward the want, the pinned pen coming up — and then the woman clicked at the mule and the cart went forward a length in the line, and the boy on the board looked back at the two of them once, with no expression at all, and that was the end of it. The woman had said it the way the steading's woman had said *it's because you die*: as a stair is a stair. There was nothing under it that a question could reach.

Karis wrote it down where she stood. Three sentences, the letters twice their proper size and leaning, the hand moving from the elbow. He read them upside down as they went in. Then she went back over the second sentence and underlined, twice, the two words that were not a place and not a rule but a claim: *everybody knows*.

"That," she said, "is a source." She closed the book on it. "Not a good one. The first."

She did not say anything else about it, and neither did anyone, that day or the next, because the grain was moving and they were moving with it, and a rumor with a line under it was a thing that kept.

---

They went down with the grain, because there was no other way to move three boxes with one man who could carry them and could not lift his arm.

The boxes rode on the second cart, roped, with Karis walking at the rope's end with her wrapped hand on it, not holding, touching. Lira walked the first mile on the stick and then got up on the tail of the lead cart and announced that it was her decision, and it was. Brom walked beside the wheel with the bound arm against his chest and the other hand on the cart's rail. Seln was ahead. He was always ahead. The stretch came at noon, at the hour he had named, and the scrub made its ordinary noise the whole two miles, and the carters, who had not walked this road in a month, talked the whole way through and did not know they were doing it.

Cael walked in elevens.

The steading's woman had said the last of it at the gate, to Lira and not to any of them, with the buckets down for once. "It's set stiff. It'll set stiffer. Healer would've had it straight in an hour." Lira, from the cart's tail: "There isn't one." The woman: "There's not. They stop inside, where the tiers are." And she had gone back through the gate, and the count of it — one healer for a country, walking a route, not due — went into his head beside the ledger with no tier column in it, and he did not put a verb between the two, because it was not his sentence to write yet.

They camped the first night at the road's turn above the wash, six carts in a ring and the mules inside it, and a fire the carters lit without asking anyone, because the stretch was behind them.

Brom's cloth had gone dark again at the wrist.

He unwound it himself, one-handed, teeth on the end when a hand was short, and did not ask for help, and Cael got up and held the loose end without being asked, because that was the arrangement. Under the cloth the forearm was closed along most of its length and open at the wrist, where the bone had been nearest the air, and it was clean, and it was not right, and Brom looked at it with the face he had for a lock somebody else had built. He wound the new cloth from the wrist up. "Tighter," he said, once, and Cael pulled, and Brom tied it off with his teeth and the good hand and put the arm back against his chest.

Lira had watched from the cart's tail. She had the boot off and the foot in the firelight, turning it as far as it turned, which was not far.

"At Norhold," she said, "I told them I wanted to become something they hadn't seen yet."

She said it to the fire. Not to Cael; not to any of them. Karis, across the ring with her hands in her lap, went still the way she went still for a source.

"They wrote it down. It was the thing you were supposed to say, and I meant it, and they wrote it in the column for things people say." She turned the foot the other way. "The Silver bracket was the ladder. Every rung has a number on it and the number is how you know you've climbed, and a panel at the top of every flight tells you what you've become since the last one. I walked out of it." A pause, with the fire in it. "On purpose, at full price, and I'd do it again. I'm not saying anything about that."

"You're saying something," said Brom.

"I'm saying that on the bar I was fast." She put the foot down. "Faster than I've been with Wind. I don't know if that's true. Nobody was counting. I know it's what it felt like, and I know I did it on a floor I can't run on anymore, and the next time I run there's going to be nobody at the end of it with a number." She found the exact sentence and said it to the fire. "So what am I becoming, exactly, if nobody's measuring?"

Nobody answered at once.

"Fast," said Brom, eventually, because it was the word he had.

"I know I'm fast. I was fast at fourteen. Fast was the *floor*." She reached for the boot. "Something they haven't seen yet, I said. Well. They haven't seen it. They're not here to see it. That isn't the same as becoming it, and I don't know what would be."

Karis said, quietly, "Neither do I. I'd write it down if I could."

"Write it when you can."

Cael did not answer, because it had not been asked of him, and because he had spent four years being the thing no instrument could read and had never once heard it put as her question. The same fact could be his relief and her want. He filed that, and did not write it down that night, because it was hers first.

Lira got the boot on. She did not ask it again. It was the ankle she rubbed, afterward, not the question.

---

Lowmarch from the shelf above the ford, on the sixtieth day past the Line, catalogued because it had been eight days: the river lower again; the ferry on the far bank and not idle, the flat coming across with three men on it and a sack; the smith working; the upper inn with smoke; the meadow turned over another quarter. The ash still had the same carter under it with the same two mules. Brom looked at that, and at the ten paces on, and put nothing down, because he had nothing to put down. Seln took the boxes off the cart. The carters went on to the ferry with the grain and did not look back, and the five of them stood in the meadow with three boxes and the look of people who had walked somewhere and come back worse.

The board was under its roof by the landing, and Pike was under the roof with it, and looked at the five of them for the length of time it took to count five, and went back to the page.

"Thornwater," he said, to the page. "Read it at the plank a week ago. Saying's tonight, at the book, if you want to hear it said."

"We do," said Cael.

"Then tonight." The pen did not stop.

Cael read the board. Fourteen slips where there had been sixteen. The ford was gone from it. The autumn road was gone — HOLD, Teague, sixty and fed; walked, then, or being walked — and the grain escort at thirty, and in their places two escorts and a fence-line and a well to be cleared, priced at four. He read all fourteen twice and sorted, and while he sorted Seln read them beside him, and Cael stopped sorting and watched Seln read.

He had watched Seln read rooms for four years. He had not watched him read a board. The eyes went down the fourteen slips once and came back up them once and stopped on three, and the three were the ones Cael would have marked as the only ones that paid for what they asked, and Seln had not been counting marks. He had been placing them. The well was on the south road, which he had walked; the fence-line was the steading's that had called them cheap, which he had walked round. He had walked every road out of Lowmarch in the four days before the ford, the way he had walked every road out of Thornwater, and had told nobody, because nobody had asked.

Seln put a finger on the well slip and moved it a quarter-inch on the plank, which was how he took a job.

Then he said, not looking round, level as the road:

"I keep waiting for someone to ask for my report."

Cael looked at him.

"Fifteen years," Seln said. "Every road, every ford, every door. Somebody asked. Somebody always asked; that was the whole of it; the asking was the job and I was the answer, and I never liked one of them." He took the finger off the slip. "Eight weeks. I've walked every road out of two places. Nobody's asked."

"Is there one?"

"There's always one."

"Then—"

"No." Seln turned, finally. "Not if you ask. That's the thing I'm finding out. I don't want to be asked. I've been asked. I wanted to see what I did with it when nobody did." He looked at the board a moment longer. "It turns out I take the well, and I walk it first, and I say nothing. It's the same craft. It weighs less." He went back to the slips, and that was the report.

---

Brom had gone to the plank's near end while they read, and stood there with the bound arm against his chest, and waited for Pike to reach the end of a line.

Cael was at the plank's far end with the fourteen slips and did not move, because Brom had gone there to say something without the crew, and a man who wanted the crew went where the crew was. He heard it anyway. He heard everything at a plank, and Brom did not lower his voice so much as flatten it.

"What does the board pay a man with no Path at all."

Pike finished the line.

"Same as anyone," he said, as he said a price. "What'd he do?"

Brom did not answer that, because it had not been a question to him; it had been the answer. He stood a moment with the arm against his chest. Then he put the good hand out, palm up, across the plank — not to Pike — and said, "Pencil," to the far end, and Cael put his own in it.

Brom took a slip off Pike's stack of old ones, the carters' stack, backs of backs, and Pike let him. He wrote on it, on the plank, with the wrong hand, because the right one was in cloth. Five words on one line and three on the next. He read it once, folded it twice, and put it inside his shirt against the skin, and gave the pencil back, and said nothing, and went to stand with Lira at the roof's edge.

Brom had written down what somebody said to him once before in his life that Cael knew of. Rooke, on the third day of a season that had made him worse on purpose. He had said so at the time, and had said he was quoting, and had quoted, and it had been the only time in four years Cael had seen a pencil in that hand.

That made two.

Pike had not looked up. "He had a number, then," he said, to the page. "Inside."

"He found it," said Cael. "They never wrote it in."

"Then it's the same as mine." Pike wiped the pen. "Copper. Thirty years on the roads. I stopped carrying the paper the second year, because it weighs, and out here nobody's buying the weight." A pause, at the pen. "Held a dog with a shoulder and a river, that one. That's on a line in my book. There's no line for the other thing, and nobody's ever asked me to make one."

---

The ferry came in at the fourth hour after noon, with the sack.

Canvas, tarred, sealed at the neck with the Line's waystation mark in lead: mail, once a month, carried to the Line by the ferry and back with whatever had come the other way. The boy brought it up from the landing on his shoulder and set it on the plank's end, and Pike cut the lead with the pen-knife and turned the sack out and sorted, aloud, by name, because the board was where the names were.

Nineteen pieces. Eleven for carters, by the holds' brands. Four for crews. Three for the inns. And one more, and Pike read the name on its face as he read a price and held it out across the plank without looking at who took it.

"Cael."

Paper folded to a palm's width, and a hand on the outside that he had known before he could read: the cramped, exact, instrument-maker's letters that had written every label in a shop in Denvash for twenty years, as if a letter were a gauge and the address its calibration. *Cael. Past the Line.* Nothing else. It had come through three hands and a ferry to exactly the man it named, because past the Line there was one board where names were kept, and Hesk had counted on that without ever having seen it.

He did not open it at the plank.

He opened it under the ash, ten paces on from where the ash had been theirs, sitting on a box, with the log open on his knee because that was where letters went when he had read them. The date at the top was three weeks gone — written, he worked out, while they were still walking the first road jobs. Before Thornwater. Before the ford. Hesk had known where they were and nothing of what they had done there, and had written anyway, into the space where the knowing would be.

*Past the Line. Your grandmother crossed it once, before you were born, on a trade run; she said the roads were honest and the food wasn't. Mind the roads. Send word when there's a roof. — H.*

He read it once. That was the whole of it, and it was, for Hesk, long.

He put it in the satchel, flat, against the leather book, where the others were. Then he sat a while with the log open and the pen not moving and looked at the ash, which was not a roof, and at the meadow, which was not one either. The ferry went west again in a month. He had a month to find a roof or to write that there was not one, and he found that he did not know which he would rather send.

He had not known about the grandmother. He filed that as what it was: one sentence, from a man who did not spend sentences, and a new fact about his own life arrived by ferry three weeks old. He did not ask it anything. Hesk had said *mind the roads* and underlined nothing, because Hesk did not underline, and it landed as an instruction that was also the whole of what the man could say.

Then he wrote, because the letter was in the satchel and the log was on his knee and it was the sixtieth day.

---

*Sixtieth day. Lowmarch. The meadow, under the ash, ten paces from where it was ours.*

*Inventory. Nine confirmed, one anomaly, patience. Deployment since the Line: one, the ford, paid. Shadow tried at the landing when the ferry came in and stopped when I did nothing about it. Anchor wants the inn door. Eighteen. Calf: eleven paces on the flat, nine uphill, twelve down. Nobody mentions it, which I have decided to count as a kindness rather than a finding. Fifty-eight marks earned. Forty-eight in hand. No roof.*

*Hesk wrote. Three weeks old, written before the ford, into where the ford would be. Filed. I have a grandmother who crossed the Line on a trade run and said the roads were honest. I did not know that this morning.*

*Everyone I came out here with is finding out what they're worth without a number on it. Lira's fast without Wind — faster, she thinks, and nobody was counting, which is the whole of her question. Brom's a wall without Iron Skin, and he asked Pike what the board pays a man with no Path, and Pike said same as anyone, what'd he do, and Brom wrote it down. Second time in his life. Karis found a rumor in a cart-yard and underlined it, twice, and I watched her not ask the next question, which cost her more than the hands. Seln keeps waiting to be debriefed. He took the well. He'll walk it first and say nothing, and he's finding out that's what he does when nobody asks, and I think he minds less than he expected to.*

*The board prices what we did. Tonight it says the ford out loud, and moves us or it doesn't. I'd rather be priced by the man who's better than me than by anyone who isn't. That's the whole sentence. I notice I'd rather it were Teague than a panel, and I notice that's new.*

---

The board's room, with the light gone off the water.

Pike carried the book up from the landing under his arm and set it on the table by the fire and put the stone on it, and the room did what the room did, which was go on drinking with half its attention on the book. Six tables. The carters at the two by the road door. A crew of three at the middle table, new faces, new canvas. The best table by the fire empty, the four chairs pushed in, the jug on it full and untouched — the autumn road, sixty and fed, and its crew somewhere on it with a cart that was not in a river. The five of them at the table by the road door, because Brom could see both doors from it. Lira's stick against the wall. Karis's notebook open, the pen pinned. And on the long wall between the fire and the door, at head height, on four wooden pegs: the sheet.

He did not read it. He had read it. He counted it, from where he sat, to be sure it was still thirteen lines, and it was, and the last line was still five names in a row.

Pike opened the book. He turned the pages back to a place a week old and put two fingers on a line and read it aloud, evenly, to the room — not to them, to the room, which was the procedure — and the room listened, because the room always listened to this part.

"Stillhound pack, Thornwater ford: cleared. Five confirmed. Crew of five, four injured, none lost. Price paid."

He said it once. The carters by the road door had stopped talking for it and started again after. The crew of three at the middle table looked over, at the stick against the wall and the arm against the chest and the wrapped hands on the notebook, and looked at each other, and that was a verdict too, of a kind, and Cael filed it beside the others without needing to hear it.

"That's said," Pike told the book. He shut it. Then he got up, and took the pegs out of the sheet on the wall one-handed, top left, top right, and let it roll down into his hand from the bottom two, and brought it back to the table and laid it flat under the stone, and took a clean sheet from under the book's cover, and sat, and began to write.

He did not hurry it and it was not slow; it was the pace of a man copying a thing he had written before and did not need to think about, until the twelfth line, where he stopped, and looked at the old sheet, and looked at the ledger's shut cover, and wrote five names.

Cael watched the five names go in from across the room, upside down, in the same hand and the same ink and the same size as the eleven above them. Then Pike wrote the thirteenth line, which was the crew the old sheet had named by its mule, and set the pen down, and wiped it, and got up, and pinned the new sheet to the wall on four pegs in the same four holes, and came back and sat down behind the book.

The carters had looked at the wall while it was bare, and looked at it again now that it was not, and one of them said something to the other that Cael could not hear and did not need to.

He counted. Top line, one word. Ten names between. Then theirs. Then the mule.

Twelfth. One line. He had been placed by panels and by seedings, and he had never once been placed by a man who unpinned a sheet in front of the people it named and wrote the whole of it out again by hand so that one line would be in a different place, and read nothing aloud while he did it, because the reading had already been done.

Lira had her hand on the stick. Brom had not moved. Karis had written something and stopped. Seln was looking at the door.

Pike looked at the sheet a moment longer, the way he had looked at Teague's name on the slip before he drew the line through it, and then he said the rest, to the room, at the pitch of a price, asked by nobody.

"Teague's still eleven up. He'll speak to you when you've cost him something. That's not rudeness. That's the ledger."

# Chapter 12 — What Seln Remembers

The light came onto the floor first, as it had the morning before, and he watched it come from the rut.

Positions, catalogued at first grey, because he had been awake the whole of the dark and a count was what he had. Lira on her back on the road where Oryn had left her, the left arm bound across her chest from the shoulder, the right hand flat on the rut beside her, the taped foot turned out; she had not moved in the night. Oryn a stride beyond her, on her side now, the leather roll under her head and her hands tucked into the carter's coat. Karis against the middle box with the notebook shut on her knees, asleep sitting, charcoal still on her fingers and the dark going up both sides of her throat into the collar. Brom on the box with the crossbow across his thighs and the right hand wrapped in Oryn's linen, awake, looking not at the ring but at the scrub. Seln at the stone, standing, with his back to the camp, where he had stood since full dark, and the locked case on its strap across that back, where it rode on every road — the cache, shut, not once in two days off him.

The mule, hipshot, at the road's edge. The lantern out.

One dead hound in the rut against the stone, its forefeet across the line and the rest of it inside, the bolt standing out of its chest. The other was on the floor's west lip, two hundred meters back, and nobody was going to go and get that one.

The floor went white. The eleven uprights stood up out of the grey on it, shortest to tallest, and he counted them, because he had counted them every morning, and it was eleven.

He had not slept. He had written instead, twice.

---

The first entry he had written in the rut by the lantern's edge before Lira turned her head. The second he had written in the dark, by nothing, two hours after Oryn took her hands off the arm and lay down on the road — the lantern put out to save the oil, Seln gone to the stone, the camp settled into the breathing of five people who had all been hurt in the same hour. He could not have slept with it unwritten, and had not tried.

He read it now, in the first light, to see whether it was still true.

*Ninety-sixth day, later. The stone, outside the line. Lantern out.*

*I'm not keeping it secret. I'm keeping it unfiled. There's a difference. Lira said later. Later is when I have a sentence. The answer might be: because whatever I am isn't a Path. I've known that since I was fourteen. Out there it was a flag. In there it's the only thing that works. I don't have a word for what that makes me, and I'm not going to invent one at a camp with Lira's arm two hours old.*

*Unfiled. Not filed wrong. Not filed under a word I invented so the page would have one. The registry filed me under a word the day I was fourteen, and I've spent four years being the case the word didn't fit. I'm not doing it to myself on a road in the dark.*

It was still true. He put a line under *unfiled*, because that was the word, and closed the book on the pencil.

---

Oryn sat up when the light reached the road, and looked at her hands, and then at the camp, in that order.

"Water," she said, to Brom, and Brom got up off the box with the bow in his good hand and went to the mule. "Not the skin. A pan. And the lantern's oil, if there's any; I want it for the hand."

She did the arithmetic before she touched anyone, aloud, because that was how she kept it. Cael sat up in the rut to hear it, and his own bill came up with him: the ribs stopping his breath at the tape as he turned; the right calf gone stiff and hot inside the stuck cloth; the nine gone to nothing with a hollow where each had rung, faint now. Nothing wanted anything, except Anchor, which since the Ostrand road had wanted to fix points in doorways and now wanted the survey stake sixty meters south along the line. He let it want.

"Three of you," Oryn said. She had her hands turned over on her knees, looking at the palms as a carter looks at a skin to see what's left in it. "The throat first, because she can't tell me if it's worse and I'd sooner know. Then the hand, because the hand's the bow, and the bow's the only thing on this road that's killed anything. Then the calf." She looked at Cael when she said it, and away. "Fed and slept I'd do three and stand up. I've not been fed, and I slept on a road with the worst arm I've had in two years four hours behind me. So I'll do them at the rate I've got, and nobody talks to me while I do, and if I sit down between them it's not a conversation."

"Yes," Cael said.

"That wasn't to you. That was to everybody."

Karis had woken at *throat*. She had the notebook open before her eyes were properly open, and wrote, and turned it: *Worse in the night. Swallowing. Can breathe.*

"That's what I thought," Oryn said, and knelt in front of her.

---

He watched, because watching was the thing he did instead of the thing he could not.

Both hands, either side of Karis's throat, the thumbs under the jaw where the dark was darkest, Karis's chin up because Oryn had put it there. The count went to ten and Oryn's eyes went to a place, and then it was not a reading; it was the other thing, and from outside it looked like nothing at all — a woman kneeling with her hands on another woman's neck, still, in the cold, her breath going out white and even. What moved was Karis. The breath changed first, the swallow that had caught in the night going down in one piece. Then the dark under the jaw went from the colour of a plum to the colour of a plum's skin, and stayed there, and Oryn's face went grey by the same amount, as if the colour had gone across.

A quarter of an hour, by the light on the floor.

Oryn took her hands off and sat back on her heels.

"The throat's mended. The bruise'll come out through the skin over a week; that's blood with nowhere to go, and I don't chase blood." She swallowed herself, and it cost her something to do it. "The voice is the throat's business, not mine. Days. Hoarse before it's anything. Don't try it before it's ready; you'll only frighten yourself."

Karis wrote: *How many days.*

"I said days." Oryn was already looking at Brom's hand. "That's one."

She drank from the pan for a while first, and nobody talked to her.

---

The hand was the pads of four fingers laid open by a string in the dark, bled into the linen and dried there, and she soaked the linen off with the pan's water and a corner of the lantern's oil, slowly, while Brom looked at the scrub. When it was off she turned the hand over in the light and said, "String," as a verdict, and put her palms round it.

Shorter than the throat; less than half, by the floor. The fingers, when she opened her hands, had four dark lines across the pads where there had been four open mouths, and Brom closed the hand and opened it.

"Nothing on the string for three days," Oryn said. "Span it with the stirrup and your foot, like a man who's had his fingers opened once, which you are now. You'll not lose the lines. That's two."

"Hand," Brom said, meaning the whole of thank you, and went back to the box, and picked up the bow with the mended hand first, and put it in the other.

Oryn sat down on the road and put her head between her knees, and stayed like that for the time it took the light to come off the floor onto the grass at its lip. When she lifted it her face was the grey of the fused stone, and wet in the cold, and she wiped it with the coat's sleeve and looked at Cael.

"Calf," she said. "Where I can see it."

---

He got the trouser leg up, and the cloth with it, because the cloth had dried into the wound, and she did not tell him to stop. The ford's line, in and out — the old scar opened its whole length along the inside of the calf where the rim's edge had caught it, black at the edges from the night and clean under the black, which she said aloud, "Clean. You bled it clean running," and washed with the last of the pan's water.

Then both hands round the calf, the palms over the line.

He watched her face. The count to ten. Her eyes going to the place. Her eyes not finding it. The count going on past ten, which it had never done on Brom or Karis or Lira, on into a silence he could see her hearing. Her hands moved on the calf, once, a finger's width, as if there might be a bank a finger's width over. There was not.

"I can feel the cut," she said. Her voice had nothing left under it. "I can feel that it's a cut, and it's deep, and it's along an old one, and it's clean. That's a wound. I could tell you that with my eyes." She took her hands off. "I can't feel you. Nothing under it. The ribs, twice; the wrist; this. Four times. I don't say a finding four times to a patient and change it on the fifth."

"No," Cael said.

"I can't mend what I can't read. That's the rule. I didn't make it, and I'm not going to argue with it on a road with nothing left to argue with." She had the linen out already. "So it gets what the rest of the country gets when I'm eleven days off: washed, bound, and left alone. It'll close on its own. It'll close faster than it should. It always has, hasn't it?"

"Yes."

"Then it's a finding and not an answer, and I've got no third thing to call it." She bound the calf tight from the ankle up, as she had bound it on the walk down from the mountain, and tied it off, and put her hand flat on the binding for a moment, and got up.

She did not say *That's three*. She went along the line, south, into the scrub, slowly, with a hand on the mule's flank as she passed it and nothing in her hands after, and he understood where she was going only when she got there.

---

The stake stood a hand's height out of the ground, sixty meters south of the road along the line, where Karis's cord had found it with the bush grown round it and Brom's marker-stone beside it. Oryn sat down in front of it with her back to the iron and her knees up and the carter's coat pulled round, and looked west, away from the ring, at the country the road had come from.

He went after her, because the calf would take the sixty meters and he wanted to know what it counted. Eleven, on the flat, favoured. He sat down on the marker-stone, a stride off, and said nothing, and she let him.

"Three in two days," Oryn said, after a while, to the country.

He waited.

"The arm. That was last night, and it was one, and it was the size of two. The throat. The hand. Three mendings in two days." She turned her hands over on her knees and looked at them, and let them lie. "That's a route. Near enough, that's what I carry between a hold and the next one, for the people who feed me and shoe the mule. Three serious hurts and a road's worth of small ones. And I've spent it on a road two days from the nearest roof, on a crew that's not on my circuit, sitting against a piece of iron somebody drove where they'd measured to."

"Yes," Cael said.

"The route's eleven days north of here." She said it as she had said *Twelve, in two days* at Thornwater's gate: a count, not a complaint. "It was nine when I sat down at your fire at the bend, and it was a day I owed. Now it's a day and two nights and three mendings and a mule that's eaten what a mule eats in three days. Every day I sit here the route doesn't get shorter. It gets longer at the far end, where I can't see it."

She put her head back against the stake. The struck-flat top of it stood a finger over her hair.

"I can't move Lira today. I could move her tomorrow, if she walks the way I tell her and not the way she walks. That's the finding. The rest is arithmetic, and I keep it aloud because a thing I don't say I start to think I've got, and I haven't." She shut her eyes. "You can sit. Don't talk to me."

He sat. Anchor wanted the iron behind her head, a fixed point at the perimeter's exact edge, and he did not give it one.

---

Seln came along the line from the stone.

He had not left it since full dark. At first light he had read the ground from where he stood — the line's outside, the scrub either side of the rut, as far as the stone let him see and no further — and now he came along the line toward the stake with his knife put away and his eyes on the ground until the ground brought him to it. He stopped. He looked at Oryn against it, and at the flat top of it standing a finger over her hair, and at the number punched under the registry's mark, thirty-one.

Cael had never once learned Seln's face, and did not learn it now; but he saw something happen to it. It was not a change. It was the absence of one — the face going still in a way it had not been still a stride before, the stillness of a man at a shelf with his hand halfway to a book he had not remembered owning.

"Karis's map," Seln said, to the stake, with no change at all in the voice. "The carter's. Not the sheet."

Cael got up off the marker-stone and went and got it.

---

Karis came with it, because Karis went where the map went — along the line with the notebook under her arm and the charcoal in her fist and no voice in the mended throat — and Brom came behind her with the box, because the box was where the map lived. Lira, who had been told not to move, stayed on the road and turned her head to watch. That was the whole of the camp except the mule, sixty meters south of a stone in a rut, round a piece of iron with a healer's back against it.

Seln laid the carter's map on the box's lid.

Karis's whole copy of Pike's plank, no seal, the roads solid, the Fallow road leaving Thornwater's east gate and running two days and bending, at the second day's rise, round a circle the carter had drawn as a circle, blank, and coming back to its line beyond it. Two other roads on the sheet did the same round two other blanks.

Seln put his palm over the bend.

"Charcoal."

Karis gave it to him. He did not look at what was under his hand. He looked at Thornwater's fork and the road's straight east of it before the rise, and he put the charcoal to the map beyond his own thumb and drew — not quickly; Seln did nothing quickly — a road in a hand Cael had seen on the corner of every registry sheet since he was fourteen: dashed, unlabeled, going east and stopping in nothing. Then he lifted the palm and drew a circle over the place it had been. From memory, the charcoal going round once and closing.

It lay on the carter's circle. A finger's width off at the north, where the carter's hand had wobbled, and nowhere else.

"That's theirs," Seln said. "Not the sheet you carry; that's the printed one, and it stops at the Line because the standard wants a waystation at both ends of a road. The ones we carried past the Line were drawn the same way — dashed, nothing at the end of them — and they had that in them." He put the charcoal's end on the circle. "Blanks. Not dashes. A circle of nothing, no name, no mark, no line through it. Three postings, the same maps on all three, the same holes in them, at the same spacing as his." He moved the charcoal's end to the carter's second circle, and the third. "Here. Here. More, further than his goes. I never once asked what they were. You don't ask what a hole is."

Karis was writing before he stopped, and turned the book: *Who drew them.*

"They surveyed it," Seln said. "Five years ago by the stake, maybe six. They paced the circles and stamped the stakes and drew the maps with holes where the circles are. That's not ignorance. Ignorance doesn't stamp iron."

Nobody said anything. Oryn had opened her eyes and was looking at the map upside down, with the iron against the back of her head.

Seln straightened up off the box.

"The Compact knows what quiet ground does," he said. It was the voice he had used over Pike's plank with a finger on the Line, the voice for an inventory; it had no more in it than that. "And it's filed somewhere we can't reach."

He did not say it twice. He put the charcoal down on the map, and it rolled a little and stopped against the carter's line.

Karis wrote one word and turned the book, and it was *Where*, underlined.

Seln read it, and gave it back.

"No," he said. Not a refusal. A finding, in a register Cael knew from a table in Ostrand: a professional saying the extent of what he had. "I carried the maps. I never saw the file. I'm not going to draw you one."

She looked at him a long moment with her hand on the pencil, and then wrote *Entered as said* in the margin, and nothing else, and Cael watched her not write it, and knew what it cost her, because he had a page in his own book with a word on it he had not let himself put a verb after.

---

"The contract," Seln said.

Cael had it. It had ridden in the satchel against the leather book since the ninetieth day, thin broker's paper, the hook-and-bar seal in dark wax, and he unfolded it on the box beside the map. Seln did not pick it up. He read it where it lay, the whole of it, though it was four lines.

*Recovery. Surveyor's instruments abandoned at a site on the Fallow road. East of Thornwater; the second day; where the road leaves its line at the rise and comes back to it. One hundred marks (one hundred).*

"Written inside," Seln said. "Registry form. *Abandoned at a site.* A carter would say *left at the Ring*. This says *site*, because inside, a place with a stake in it is a site." He put one finger on the word. "For a kit that was never here. There's nothing inside that circle a person left except the stake, and the stake's where they measured to, not where they lost anything. The man who wrote this knew that. The man who wrote this drew the hole."

Karis had stopped writing. She was watching his finger.

"So it's not a recovery. It's a question. A hundred marks on a board, priced so nobody who knew the road would take it, and one crew on the wall that didn't know the road and needed the marks. Written from inside by someone who wanted to know which crew would go in." He straightened. "We just told them. The report goes west on the next ferry. It'll say we came out."

"Pike writes it," Cael said. "Posting, taking, witnessing, completion. He enters it, and the broker gets the copy, and the broker sends it where the marks came from."

"And it says: took the slip, went in, came out. Instruments not recovered. Crew of six." Seln looked at the map, and at the circle, and at no one. "That's the whole of what they asked. We've answered it."

Oryn, from the stake, without opening her eyes again: "Six is wrong. I'm a stop."

"They'll write six," Seln said.

Nobody said anything after that, and it was Brom who picked up the box.

---

The rest of the ninety-seventh day was the day it took a camp of six to be still, and he catalogued it because cataloguing was how the day went by.

The dead hound in the rut, dragged into the south scrub by Brom and Seln with a rope round its hind legs, the bolt cut out of it first and put back in the sleeve: five. The ration, said aloud by Seln because somebody had to say it: two days' bread for six at a day and a half's eating, the cheese finished, the dried meat meant for the walk back cut into sixths; two skins of water for six and a mule, which was the reason they would walk tomorrow and not the day after. Lira, eating with her right hand and putting the bread down to break it against her knee because she had no second hand to break it with, and not asking; and Brom breaking it for her the second time without being asked, and neither of them saying anything about that.

Her ankle, re-taped at noon. She could not do it one-handed and did not try; she held the foot up and looked at the ring while he did it, the boiled-linen strip going round in the pattern Oryn had shown them on the mountain, and when he tied it off she said, "Tighter than Oryn," and he said, "Yes," and that was all she said to him in the day. It was enough. *Later* held, on both sides, without anybody touching it.

Karis, on the middle box with the carter's map, copying Seln's circle into the notebook beside the stake's number, and writing under it three words he read upside down: *Blank. Stamped. Filed.* Then a line, then nothing, then the book shut on her knee and her hand flat on it for the rest of the afternoon, the throat going yellow at the edges of the dark, and no sound out of her at all.

Oryn, asleep against the stake in the sun until the sun went off it, and walking back along the line with a hand on the scrub as she came, and reading Lira's arm at the road — the count, the ten, the place found — and saying "Holding" to nobody, and lying down.

Seln at the stone, facing the scrub, with the case on him, for the whole of the light and then the whole of the dark.

Nobody asked him anything. Nobody asked Cael anything either. Five people had seen all nine, and not one of them turned to the sixth in the whole of the day and said the word *yours*. He watched them not do it, and did not file it as kindness, because it was not. It was a unit lying still in a cold camp with the one thing that could be done about anything done, and the one thing that could not be done left where it lay, where it would keep.

---

He wrote the second night's entry with the lantern lit for the length of it, because Oryn had said the oil would do for one more night if the night was short, and he made it short.

*Ninety-seventh day. The stone, outside the line. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Eighteen.*

*Inventory. Nine confirmed, one anomaly. Nothing used. Ringing this morning, faint, all nine, where last night there was nothing; not held, not spent. Anchor wants the stake. It can want.*

*Injuries, all six, after Oryn's morning. Lira: left arm slung, second day, not used for anything — she ate with the right and let me tape the ankle; Oryn says holding. Karis: throat mended, voice none; Oryn says days, and hoarse before it's anything. Brom: right hand closed, four lines across the pads, nothing on the string for three days. Me: right calf washed and bound, not mended; nothing under her hands, the fourth time; closing on its own, faster than it should. Ribs, tape twenty-one days. Oryn: three mendings in two days — the arm, the throat, the hand — and she says that's a route, and the route's eleven days north. Seln: nothing.*

*Seln remembered something today, against the stake. Karis has it in her margin, entered as said. I've entered nothing about it here, and I'm not going to. He said it once. That's its number.*

*The contract, read again. Registry form under a broker's seal, for a kit that was never here, by someone who wanted to know which crew would go in. Seln: we just told them. The report goes west on the next ferry and it'll say we came out.*

*I'm not going to write what that means. I don't know what it means. I know a hundred marks sat on a board for a fortnight so the wrong crew would take it, and we were the wrong crew, and the answer's on a ferry in a month with my name on the slip.*

*Five people saw all nine. Nobody's asked. Not because nobody wants to. Because Lira said later, and this camp keeps its word. Still unfiled. Still no word for it. Tomorrow we walk to Thornwater, Lira the way Oryn says and not the way Lira walks. The healer said she'll read me when I'm still and whole. I'm neither. Two days.*

He put the lantern out.

---

He woke before the light, and lay in the rut with the book on his chest and his hand flat on the cover, and listened to the camp be asleep. Seln was not at the stone. Seln was on the road beyond the mule with his back against a pannier and his chin down, which Cael had never once seen, and filed.

Oryn was at the line.

She had made no sound getting there. She stood on the road side of the two-fist stone with the carter's coat on and her hands at her sides, looking at the ring, where the first grey was finding the tops of the uprights and had not yet found the floor — and she stood there for the time it took him to understand that she was not looking at the ring. She was looking at the stone.

Then she raised her hands.

Both, in front of her, a little apart, palms in, the way she raised them for a reading with nobody between them — the count, he knew, running in her own hands. She held them there. Then she stepped over the stone.

One stride. Inside. Her hands stayed up, and her face did what it had done at the rim over Lira's pouring arm and over Brom's forearm on the first morning: the count stopping at the stride. Not ending. Stopping. She stood inside the line with her hands raised to nothing, and looked at them, and looked at the stone behind her heel.

One stride. Out. Her hands had not been lowered; they had been held, and her eyes went to the place, and found it, and her mouth moved, and he knew the word without hearing it because he had heard it on this road before.

She did it again. In: nothing. Out: back.

The third time, slower, with a pause inside long enough for him to count twelve — standing on the grass with her hands up in the grey and the whole of a dead-Path circle in front of her and a den at the far side of it, alone, checking whether a rule that had never once failed anybody who kept it would fail her if she stood in it long enough; and it did not, and she stepped out, and it came back. And the fourth time, as if three were an anecdote and four were a finding: in, hands raised, nothing; out, hands raised, the current back at the stride, exactly, the clock starting again without having lost the time — being checked, on the second dawn, by the one of them who had walked past this bend eleven times in two years and never stepped off the road, and had now stepped over its stone eight times, four in and four out, the way Karis had paced the circle: with a count, and nobody watching, and the count kept in her own hands.

He did not write it down.

The book lay on his chest with his hand on it and the pencil in the spine, and he kept it shut. It was hers. She had not said it, and she would not want it said yet; she would say it when she had it as a finding and not as a morning, and until then it was not his to file. So he lay in the rut with the calf bound and the ribs stopping his breath at the tape, and watched the healer stand at the line with her hands up in the grey and the stone at her feet, and let her be the only one awake, until the light came onto the floor behind her and went white along its whole width at once, and she lowered her hands, and stood a moment longer looking at them, and then came back along the road to the camp, quiet, as a carter walks quiet ground, and lay down in her place by Lira's arm.

# Chapter 7 — The Healer's Route

She taped his ribs on the eighty-first morning, before the sun was on the bench, and told him what it was while she did it.

"This is cloth," Oryn said. "Anybody can do cloth. Breathe out. All of it." She took the linen round him under the arm and over the place three times, pulling at each turn until the breath he had left stopped where she wanted it and not where the shale-back had decided. "There. Now it stops at the tape. That's the whole of what tape does. It doesn't mend anything. It tells the ribs where the edge is so they stop looking for it." She tied it off at the front, flat, with the knot where a strap would not lie. "You'll hate it by noon. Leave it."

He breathed in and the breath stopped a finger's width sooner than it had for three days, at a place he could feel from the outside, and it was better. He did not say so. She had not asked.

Then the lip.

Seln went first, one hand on the mountain, and stood at the far end where the bench opened and the mule was, and waited. Lira went second. The bone was whole, Oryn had said, and new, and today it took weight; she stood up off the shale on it with her face doing nothing and the bare foot under the taped ankle going grey in the cold, and she walked the lip as she had walked the ledge, feet first, reading it, slower, with a hand on the rock the whole way. Oryn went behind her. Not close. A stride and a half — near enough to take a hand, far enough not to be taken by one; and Cael, who had catalogued where people stood since he was fourteen, catalogued it. She had put herself where a fall would come. Not in front, where Seln was, and not at the back, where Brom would be. Where the hurt would arrive, if it arrived.

The boxes went next, one at a time, on backs. Brom put the strap of the first over his shoulders and Oryn looked at the mended arm inside his sleeve and said "Carrying," and Brom said "Not pulling," and she said "Hm," and it stood. Seln came back for the second. The third went on the mule.

Cael went last, with Seln behind him, because Seln had come back a third time without being asked. Thirty paces of the mountain's edge with nothing under the outer foot; a hand on the rock and the next foot, and the breath stopping at the tape, and the calf saying what it had said since the shale. He counted the paces because there was nothing else to count, and got thirty-one, and stepped off onto the wide bench, and the mountain was behind them.

The mule stood where it had stood for two days, tied to nothing. It looked at six people and three boxes as a mule looks at anything, and put its head down.

---

Four days down.

He wrote that at the first halt, because he had the log and the satchel and nothing else — the first time since the Line that he had come off a job carrying nothing but paper — and the sentence was the shape of the thing he was in. Four days: the bench going down and widening and losing its edge until it was a slope and then a hillside; the shale giving way to turf in patches, and then to turf with shale in it; the gorge opening below into a valley with Oxhollow's river at the bottom of it. Lira on the mule, the bare foot in a sock of Oryn's linen, saying nothing about being on a mule. Brom under a box. Seln under a box, ahead, and then not ahead, and then ahead again. Karis with the packs and the map, drawing the bench down as she had drawn it up. And the healer.

He catalogued her walk on the first day, because it was the first thing she did in front of him that was not her hands.

She walked like a carter: the pace set once at the start of a road and not changed for a slope or a narrow or anything short of an animal in the road; the feet going down whole and staying down; the weight never once ahead of them. Lira moved as if the ground were a thing she could leave. Oryn moved as if it were a thing she had an arrangement with. She was never fast. In four days he did not see her hurry, and he was watching for it — at the mule's stumbles, at the place on the second day where the ground went out from under Brom's box — and she came to each at the same walk, and was there when she was needed and not before. Item. She walked on the down-slope side of the mule, where a rider would come off. Item. She looked at the four of them who could walk as Seln looked at a road, every halt, once, all together, and then at whichever one had changed. Item. She had a heel gone raw in her boot from the mountain, and at the first halt she took the boot off and washed it and taped it with the same grey cloth she had put round his ribs, and put the boot back on, and walked. She did not put her hands on it and go still. She could not do that to herself. She had said so on the bench, and here it was, in a boot.

Lira asked her about the ankle at the second day's noon.

They had stopped where the slope flattened into turf, and Lira sat against a stone with the leg out, and said it as she said everything, as a fact for checking: "You could do the ankle."

"I could."

"But."

"Three." Oryn had her skin out and was drinking, not much. "Three a day, if I'm fed and I've slept, and that's a good day; two's more like it on a road. That's what I've got, all told, for everybody I put my hands on between here and the next place I sleep." She capped the skin. "I spent three on your mountain. A leg, a wrist, and a leg's worth of walking up. Oxhollow was owed a visit six days ago and I was on a bench instead. Somebody at Oxhollow's got worse in six days. I don't know who yet. There's always someone." She looked at the ankle, once. "That's a tape and a season. It'll set. Somebody at the ford's a leg, or a chest, or a child. Your ankle's not going to be why they didn't get me."

Lira looked at the ankle, and then at the healer, and said, "Right." She did not ask again. He had known her four years and could count on a hand the things she had asked for twice.

He put the arithmetic on the shelf beside Vell's. It was the same shape. A finite thing, kept in a head, spent by a rule the keeper had not made and would not break, and the rule was not who deserved it. It was who was next on the road.

At the same halt Oryn came round the stone to him and said "Calf," and he gave it to her: Seln's binding, four days old and stiff with the mountain, and the wound under it, the ford's two mouths opened along the shale and closed again, mostly, as things closed on him. She looked at it as a carter looks at a wheel. She washed it from her skin and bound it fresh in her cloth, tight, and did not put her hands on it and go still.

"I've a finding on you," she said, to the calf. "I'll not run it again on a hillside. At the ford, when I've a table and I'm not counting days." She tied off the cloth. "You'll keep till then. You've been keeping for years."

---

The route came out on the second night, at the fire, because Karis asked for it, for the map.

Brom had lit it, with a flint. Karis sat by it with the book on her knee and the pen in the scarred hand; she had been looking at the healer for two days with the look she gave a primary source, and had held the question, which Cael marked, because Karis holding a question was Karis paying for something. What she paid for was the map. "Where does it go," she said. "The route. For the map. I've Oxhollow and Thornwater and nothing between."

Oryn named seven.

She named them in order, as a carter names the fords on a road, and Karis wrote them as she said them, and two of the seven Cael knew. Oxhollow, where they were going. Thornwater, where he had lain in a grain loft with a calf that said eleven. The five between he had never heard of, and he did not ask, and the healer did not describe them; they were names, and then they were ink, and Karis drew a line through the seven that came back round to the first.

"Twenty days," Oryn said. "Round. Two at a hold, near enough, and the road between, and the road's the part that doesn't move. A hold can wait a day. The road can't be walked faster than it's walked." She had the mule's lead in her hand and was working a burr out of it. "I've walked it round thirty-odd times. I've been late to a hold eleven of them. This is twelve."

"What do they owe you?" Karis. The research voice; the question meant exactly.

"Oxhollow shoes the mule and keeps a table I don't have to carry. Thornwater feeds me. One of the five gave me this coat two winters back and won't hear about it." She did not look up from the burr. "And I owe them. There's a hand at Oxhollow I said I'd look at again and haven't. There's a child three stops on that had a fever the day I left, and I don't know. That's what late is. It's not a number. It's who I don't know about."

"You keep that in a book."

"No."

Karis waited, pen up. Oryn got the burr out and dropped it in the fire.

"I remember who I've had my hands on," she said. "That's the book. A healer who needs a page to know who's owed has stopped looking at them."

Karis wrote that down too, and Oryn watched her do it and said nothing about it. Seln, across the fire, was listening as he listened to a road.

"Why," Karis said then. It was the whole question, and she let it be.

The healer was quiet a moment. Not thinking; this was a woman deciding whether a thing was worth the breath, and deciding it was, once.

"They certify you," she said, "and they hand you a list. The list is by tier. Bronze and above first, and there's always a Bronze." That was the whole of the city, and she gave it one sentence's room. Then, to the fire, without heat, as a list: "In the city I healed Bronzes with bruises. Out here I set legs. I'm not going back and I'm not stopping. The route is the only thing I own."

Nobody said anything. Karis wrote it. Lira looked at the healer and then at the fire, and Cael thought — inference, kept as inference — that she had heard the shape of her own sentence in it, the one she had said to a different fire at Lowmarch.

He catalogued the want because he catalogued wants; it was the first thing he learned about anyone and the last thing he trusted. Seven holds, in order, and a clock. A table at a ford she did not have to carry. A child three stops on. He had been in rooms since he was fourteen where people wanted things and he was somewhere in the wanting — a file to close, a fighter to beat, a phenomenon to write down. She wanted the road walked at the pace it walked. He was not in it anywhere. It was simply true, and it was the first true thing about her he had not had to work for.

---

The third day they came down onto the low road.

It was hoof-cut and plated for a hundred paces where the herd had come off the fan and crossed it — shale on turf, as if the mountain had walked a piece of the way to Oxhollow and thought better of it — and then it was a road again, and empty. Seln read the ground and said twenty-seven, in a voice that meant the carter had been right. Karis lit the fire that night: one point, on the first try, the first thing she had lit since the road, and sat down after it, not hard. Oryn looked at her and said "Eat," and Karis ate.

The fourth day was river and turf and the valley opening; and Oxhollow, in the afternoon, at the gorge's mouth.

He read it as he read a board. A river coming out of the mountain grey and fast and going brown as it slowed; a ford where a gravel bar split it, the water over the bar the height of a knee and moving; on the near bank, under the last of the bench, a stockade of grey poles the height of a man and a gate in it standing open; inside, eleven roofs and a yard, carts drawn up nose to tail along the water, mules, the smell of wet rope and dung and something cooking; and against the stockade's inside face, by the gate, a lean-to with a table under it and nothing on the table.

Nobody sat at that table. He had it before anyone said it: the lean-to was the ford's, and the table was hers, and the place stayed empty twenty days at a time because the one person who used it walked a circuit and the ford had no one else. A city had a healer on every street and a list by tier. Oxhollow had a table.

There were already people at it. Someone had seen the mule come down the road.

"You're last," Oryn said to the five of them, at the yard's edge, and it was not unkind; it was the order. "You're the only ones I've had my hands on this month. Sit." And went to the table at the walk she had come down the mountain at, and the line closed round her.

He saw it from the yard, which was as near as he went; it was hers, not a crew's. An old man with a chest that whistled. A girl with a burn up one arm that had been dressed by somebody who did not know how. A carter with a hand under a cloth — the hand, Cael thought, that she had said she would look at again. She took them in the order they were worst, not the order they had come, and nobody in the line argued the order, which told him how long the table had been hers. She went still over the chest for the seconds and then for the quarter-hour, and the whistle stopped. The girl's arm took the time an apple takes. The carter's hand she unwrapped and looked at for a long while and put both hands on, and said something to the man, low, and he nodded and went; and Cael did not know whether that had been the third until she sat down on the bench behind the table and drank, with the grey in her face, and said, to nobody, "That's three," and the line, which knew what that meant, thinned to the ones who only wanted looking at.

While she worked, he and Karis took the map to the man who kept the ford.

A factor's hands, ink and rope; a face that did not change at nine widths, or at the eighth narrowing's drawn break, and changed at the tenth, which was not a width. Karis had drawn the absence as she drew everything, exactly. He read it twice. Then he counted seventy marks onto the table in fives, and said, "Then they go round," and, because Cael had not moved: "That's worth seventy to know before the first cart's in the gorge. I'd sooner pay it for this."

Cael took the marks. One hundred and sixteen in hand, the smith's six still owed at Lowmarch for a cart that would not be coming back to him. He did not take the log out; the healer's line was thinning, and she had said last, and last had come.

---

She read him first, at the table, with the yard going quiet round them as the inn had gone quiet round Teague.

"Ribs," she said. "Sit there. Don't breathe for me. Breathe how you breathe."

She put a hand on the tape over the place and a hand on his back behind it, as on the mountain, and went still. Seconds. He counted them. On the bench she had stopped in the middle of them; this time she held to the end, and her face did the thing, smaller — a woman who has heard a thing twice and is confirming the count.

"Six days," she said. "Those feel like a fortnight. They're knitting. They're knitting as bone knits, only faster than bone does, and there's nothing doing it. No current. Nothing I can feel going along them and telling them to." Her hands came off. "They're doing it themselves. I've had my hands on a hundred ribs. Ribs don't."

"They always have," he said, because she had asked that on the bench and it was still true.

"I heard you the first time." She turned. "Leg."

Lira put the leg on the bench, and Oryn put both hands on it above and below where the angle had been, and went still, and it was over in the seconds. "Whole. New. It aches." Lira said it did. "That's the right amount of ache. Don't run on it for a week. You will. Don't." She did not look at the ankle. "Arm."

Brom gave her the forearm, the raised dark line from wrist to elbow, and she took it in both hands and went still, and this time Cael watched her face instead of the arm, because he wanted the face at the end of a reading that worked. It was the face of a woman reading a page she had read before. "Iron Skin," she said. "Dense. Everything where it goes." She let the arm go. "Ordinary." She said it as the best word she had, and Brom, who had spent years wanting a number, looked at the arm as though ordinary were one, and Cael kept that for the log and did not smile.

"You again."

She took his wrist, and put her other hand flat against the side of his neck under the jaw, where — he had learned it from watching four healers, never from being told — a Path was loudest in anyone; where nothing was broken and nothing bound. And went still.

Longer. He counted to fourteen. Her eyes were on the stockade over his shoulder, and then on nothing, and the face did not do the thing; it had stopped expecting a number. It held, listening, as a woman holds who has put her ear to a wall and is waiting to be sure the room behind it is empty.

Her hands came off. She sat back on the bench.

"That's three times," she said. "Three, and the third on a place with nothing wrong in it, so it's not the hurt getting in the way. I wanted to know that." She looked at her hands, and then, deliberately, at him instead. "It's like putting my hands in a river in the dark. Something's moving. I can't find the banks, so I can't read it, so I can't touch it. That's the rule. I didn't make it."

The yard was quiet. Karis had the pen up and was not writing, which was rarer than writing.

"There's two ways to read." She said it to him, but it was the voice she used to a room. "The one I've run on you three times is the short one. Hands on the hurt, a count of ten, and I get the hurt and what's under it — Iron Skin, Ember, Wind. Every healer runs it. It costs nothing worth counting; it's the one you run on a road with a line waiting. The long one's the other thing. Both hands, and all of me, run through all of you, slow — not looking for a hurt, looking at how you're built. It takes minutes, not seconds. You have to be still, and you have to be whole, because a hurt shouts and I can't hear the rest over it. And it costs what a mending costs. I don't run it on a road." She turned the skin over in her hands and did not drink. "I've never run it on you. You've never once been still and whole in front of me. I met you on your back with three ribs gone."

"And if I were."

"When you're not broken, I run the long one. It won't read you either. I want to feel it not read you properly." She said it as she had said ordinary — a finding she was willing to be wrong about and did not expect to be.

He did not answer that. There was nothing in it that asked to be answered; it was a woman telling a patient what the next visit was.

"Three things," she said, "and then I've a mule to load. When you heal — when you've done it on your own, the way you say you always have — does it hurt in the right places?"

He had never been asked. Four healers, three assessors, a panel, and Coss with a file, and none of them had asked whether it hurt in the right places. He answered it honestly, because the only strategy this woman would not see straight through was the truth. "Yes."

"Faster than other people, or slower."

"Faster."

"Has any healer ever got anything? Under. Not the ribs. You."

"No."

She nodded, once, the nod of a count coming out as expected. She did not write it down. Karis's pen was still up; the healer did not look at it, and Cael understood — it arrived as findings arrived, whole — that she was not going to. Karis kept what she learned on a page so that it would outlast her. This woman kept it where she kept the seven holds and the carter's hand and the child three stops on, and nowhere else. She would remember his ribs at Thornwater in nine days as she remembered the coat. That was the book.

"That's the visit," Oryn said, and stood up, which took her one try, and went to the mule.

---

She loaded it herself, at the gate, with the sun going off the bench above the roofs. The roll under the strap; the panniers, which had carried a box for four days and now carried the kit. Lira, on two feet with a hand on the stockade, watched her and said nothing; Brom held the mule's lead while she tightened a girth and gave it back without being asked, and she said "Hm," to him, and it was thanks. It was not a leaving that had been arranged. It was a healer at the end of a stop.

"What do we owe," Cael said. He had the marks in the satchel, and he had priced a leg on the board and did not know what it was worth at a table.

"Oxhollow feeds the mule. That's Oxhollow's." She took the lead, and looked at him, and it was the look from the third reading, the one with no number in it and no awe. "Thornwater's on my route in nine days. You'll be on the board by then. Come find me. I want to read you when you're not broken."

He had a sentence. He had had it since the second night's fire, and it was a good sentence, and it asked her to walk south with five people instead of north with a mule, and he did not say it. He catalogued not saying it; that was the whole of what he did with it. She had seven holds and a child she did not know about. He had a board. She had told him at a fire what she owned, and it was not a thing you asked a person to set down for a crew that had known her four days.

"Nine days," he said.

She went out through the gate and up the north road at the carter's walk, and did not look back, because looking back was not a thing the walk had in it.

---

Teague's crew came in off the east road at dusk.

He heard the cart before he saw it, and the first thing he catalogued was that it was a cart. Two wheels, a bed, a load under canvas, and it was not in a gorge. Four with it. The tall woman walked ahead with the spear across her shoulders and her hands hung over it. The broad man with the crossbow and the limp had the cart's tail. The young one was between the shafts, pulling, and pulling it well. And Teague came last, as he had come last through Lowmarch's door, and stopped at the yard's edge where the water began and did the thing Cael had watched him do once: he looked at the woman, and at the man with the limp, and at the young one at the shafts, each for the length of a breath, and only when he had them did he look at the yard, and the yard had five people in it he had read across a room thirty-five days ago and not spoken to.

He had heard, then. The ford was on Pike's page with his hold struck through and their five names under it, and Pike read the page aloud; the traverse had come down the mountain ahead of them on a carter's tongue. Pike had said it, at the plank, asked by nobody: he would speak to them when they had cost him something. The ford had been his slip.

Teague crossed the yard at the pace of a man going to a table he knew. He stopped a stride and a half off, which was Oryn's distance, and Cael marked the coincidence and did not trust it, and the man looked at him — at him, not at the tape under his shirt or the calf he was standing wrong on, though he had seen both from the water — and spoke.

"You cleared Thornwater's ford with five and lost a cart on the high bench with five."

"Yes."

"The ford was good work. The bench was a cart."

He took it apart, because that was what he did with a sentence that had been aimed. It was not unkind; he listened for unkind and it was not in it anywhere. It was a claim, with the evidence in it, and a ruling: two jobs, five people on each; one done well; one that had cost a cart. Every word was true, and the ruling was the one he would have written himself over the traverse if he had been Pike, or Vell, or a boy in a boarding house with a notebook and a circuit to price. He had been on the far side of this a thousand times. He had never once been on the near side of it from a man who did it as he did it — no seal, no bracket, no tier; a ledger in a head and a sentence that said what the ledger said. He had no argument. The bench had been a cart.

Then, the rest of it, in the same voice: "The board's going to have you and me on the same contract inside a season. Be good enough that I'm glad."

He did not wait to be answered. It had not been a question. He went back across the yard at the same pace and said something short to the tall woman, and the young one let the shafts down, and the man with the limp hung the crossbow on the cart, and the four of them stood at the water and let the mule drink and did not look at the five of them again, because the ledger had been read and there was nothing else on the page.

Cael sat down on the bank, with the tape telling his ribs where the edge was, and took the log out.

*Eighty-fourth day. Oxhollow. The map's paid; a hundred and sixteen in hand, six owed for a cart. Lira's on two feet. The healer's gone north.*

*Teague priced us. He's right about the cart. Oryn read me three times and got nothing three times and wants a fourth. Two people out here measuring me, and neither of them has a seal. I keep waiting to mind.*

He closed it and did not write anything else, because the rest of what he had was not a sentence yet.

Across the yard the young one picked the shafts up. The tall woman went into the water first, with the spear across her shoulders, and the cart went in after her with the broad man at its tail, and Teague last; and the four of them walked Oxhollow's ford at dusk with the river to their knees and the cart's wheels finding the bar, and nobody spoke, and nobody splashed, and the crossbow hung where the man could reach it without looking. They walked it the way carters walked the void roads. Quiet. A crew that had walked this a hundred times and had never once lost the count of itself in the water. They came up onto the far bank and the cart came up after them, streaming, and the south road took them, and the light went off the river.

He turned on the bank. Behind him, above the roofs, where the north road climbed along under the bench, there was one more thing in the last of the light, and he found it because he was looking. A mile up, by the eye. A mule, and a woman walking on the down-slope side of it at a pace that had not changed since the gate, small against the bench, and then smaller, going the way the route went.

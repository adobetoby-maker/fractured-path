# Chapter 6 — Shale

The herd had come round the shoulder in the night.

He counted them from the root of the ledge in the grey before the sun, because counting was the first thing he did with anything, and got thirty. Thirty backs on the upper shelf, two hundred meters up by the eye, along the last of the green above the bench. At dusk they had been half a day behind. Now they were above, and moving: not the drift of four days but a walk, heads down, the line going along the shelf at a man's pace toward the bulge, where the upper shelf pinched out against the mountain as the lower did. The green ended there. The grinding came down to the bench and did not stop.

"They're eating toward the end of it," Karis said. She had the cord in her scarred hand and her eyes on the ledge, which the sun was not on yet. "When it ends they turn round and come back over us. I'd like the cart across first."

"Then I'll run it," said Lira.

They had walked the last of the bench in the dark for that. She had taped the ankle at camp by feel, put the boot on over it, and said nothing about either. Now she stood where the shelf became a lip along the mountain's bulge: a cart wide for ten paces, then less, then a ledge the width of a door running out over nothing toward where the bench opened again and went on to Oxhollow. Below it the gorge was wider than anywhere they had seen it.

Brom had the strap on and the cart's tail a length back from the ledge's root, the three boxes roped at its front, the down-slope wheel a hand from the edge. Seln was not looking at the ledge but back along the bench, at a thing Cael had walked past in the dark and catalogued as the one object on the traverse that was not a plate: a fin of darker stone standing out of the mountain at the bench's inner edge, forty paces behind them, a man high and three carts long. Seln looked at it as he looked at the places they would sleep.

Lira went out.

Not a run; she read it with her feet first, ten paces at a walk with a hand on the mountain, then the hand off and the walk going to the thing she did between a walk and a run, the ankle taking it. The ledge held a walker. At thirty meters she turned to come back at a run, because shale that held a runner held a wheel, and that was the word Oxhollow was paying for.

She took two strides.

He heard it before he saw it. The whole ledge under her, from her boots to a length ahead, made one dry note, a plate going in a kiln, and then it was not a ledge. It was three carts' length of the mountain's edge going down into the gorge together, slowly and then not, with her on it.

She did what her body had been doing since she was fourteen.

He had watched her burst four hundred times and owned a piece of it. It was not loud to the ear, or not only: air went somewhere all at once, a clap without the hand, and she was not where she had been. This was not that. This was Wind, hers, Iron Rank 1 and everything the rank had, the whole declaration spent at once, upward and back, and it crossed the gorge. He felt it cross. The far wall gave the clap back a breath later, smaller, and by then she was on the bench beside him on her hands and one knee, the ledge gone from under where she had stood.

Two hundred meters up, thirty heads came off the rock at once.

---

He did not see them turn. He saw the line be a different line, every back on the shelf pointed away from the ledge, away from the loudest thing that had happened on this mountain in three seasons, and then the line was running, and Karis had been right and wrong in one sentence. A step went uphill. A run did not: the shelf could not hold thirty animals the size of the cart at a run, the outer plates went out from under the first of them, and the herd came off the upper bench down the face in a front two hundred paces wide, on its feet, with the shale coming with it.

A landslide with legs. It was the exact sentence.

"There." Seln, already moving, the arm out along the bench behind them at the fin. "Thirty seconds. Lee side."

He ran. They all ran, and the arithmetic ran with him: forty paces on roof-pitched shale, thirty seconds, and above them a sound that had stopped being a grinding and was the mountain coming down at a walk. Brom was past the cart, and his head turned, and he had done the sum.

"Cart's gone," Brom said. Not loud. He was running when he said it, and the cart stood where it stood, at the ledge's root under the herd's line, the three boxes roped to its bed. "It's between. No time."

Nobody argued. Karis, ahead, did not look back at the cart, which was how he knew she had heard.

The fin came up. He got into the shale in its lee with his shoulder to the stone and Seln's hand on his collar putting him lower, and Brom came in over him, and Karis did not come in. She stopped at the fin's up-slope end, in the open, back to the rock, face to the mountain, and put both scarred hands up in front of her.

"Karis—"

"They go from it." She said it to the mountain. "Seln. Do they."

"They go from it."

"Then they'll go from this."

She lit the line.

Not a point, the single ignition he had watched her make a hundred times. She laid the lattice out in a line, the whole length of the face above the fin and past it either way, twenty paces, thirty, a row of points so close they were one thing, a wall of ignition standing across the mountain at the height of a shale-back's head, between the fin and everything coming down. It was a declaration the size of a road.

The herd hit it and split.

The front came down the face at a gallop with the mountain under it and met a line it had to turn from, and it turned, as water turns at a stone, half left and half right, at a distance he measured afterward at eleven paces from the fin's face and could not have measured then, because there was no distance; there was a grey wall going past his shoulder at the length of his arm with plates the size of doors coming off it. The noise was inside the stone at his back. The down-slope half had nothing under its outer feet but the bench and then the edge, and some of it went over. He heard that: a different sound, and the far wall not giving it back. Most of it did not, and turned along the bench, and went.

He counted eight seconds because he was counting, and at the eighth Karis's hands came down.

The line went out with them. She sat, all at once, as a rope-end drops when the hand lets go, and put her back to the fin and her scarred hands in her lap and looked at nothing with her mouth open. He had seen her spent before. This was not spent. Spent was a jar with a little left in it. She had lit a road with a jar.

The first half was past. The sound had gone along the bench and away; the face above the fin was, for a breath, empty of anything but sliding shale; and Brom was already up.

---

"Two are mine," Brom said, and went.

The cart stood forty paces on at the ledge's root, where the first half had not reached. The second half was coming down behind it at the slant the line on the shelf had run: further along, later, and further along was the cart. He could see them, fourteen or fifteen, a front coming down the face at the cart in a sheet of moving plates, and Brom under them, running down the bench with the bound arm loose, and he could count the seconds.

He went after him. It was not a decision he watched himself make; it was his feet. It was the stupid thing, done on purpose, by people who did it for each other, and he knew it, and it did not slow his feet.

Brom hit the cart's tail and did not untie anything. He put the good hand under the front box and the bound arm under it too, and pulled, and the rope held for half a second and then did not, because the hand that was allowed to hold and not pull had pulled. Brom turned with the box against his chest and heaved it up-slope out of the herd's line, a length, two, and it landed on its side in the shale and stayed. The second came off the same way. Cael heard the wrist go; he knew the sound of a thing coming open under cloth. Brom did not heave this one. He ran it.

The third was the middle box, the one with the corner worn white, the one Karis had put her hand flat on at Lowmarch to see whether it was warm.

He had it by the rope and the rope did not give and he had a knife and used it, and the box came into his arms heavier than it had any right to be, paper being heavy, and the first animal of the second half came onto the bench a length from the cart.

Compression wanted him. He felt it want, where it had been wanting since Reydan: take the force, turn it, walk away from it. Anchor wanted the cart, a fixed point under the axle, the cart nailed to the mountain and the boxes with it. He had a hand's breadth of a second to price both. A declaration under a herd that went from declarations, at the one place on the bench where the split was still holding, and the split was Karis's; it stood on her line and nothing else. One more loud thing under the second half, here, and it turned again, and the fin was what it turned toward.

He held. He took it raw.

The shale-back did not see him. It was not built to. It came past the cart at a gallop with its head down and its shoulder came round with it, a plate of armour the size of a tail-board moving at the speed of a falling thing, and took him under the left arm with the box in front of him, and he went where it put him.

He was on the ground in the fin's lee with the box under him and his face in the shale, and he was catalogued before he could see.

Three ribs. Left side, low, under the arm. He knew the number as he had known Lira's count at the circuit, not by looking, by the shape of what the breath would not do: the first breath stopped at a place and the second at the same place, and there was a give in the wall of him that was three wide. Not four. Three.

And the calf. The right calf, bitten through at the ford and mostly closed, had opened along the shale where he had gone down on it, and he felt that only afterward, as he had felt the bite only when the water went cold.

Behind him the cart went. He turned his head, which he could do, and watched it.

The second half came past it, and the cart did what Brom had said a cart did on scree, which was float: a finger's width at every turn of the wheel, and then not a finger's width. It went sideways along the bench in the shale moving under the animals, and its down-slope wheel went out over where the bench was no longer, and it tipped as if considering it, and went into the gorge with the shafts up.

He counted the second half past the fin: fourteen. Then the sound going down the mountain toward the fan, and the shale still coming down, plate on plate, settling, and the gorge giving the last of it back, late.

---

Lira came in last.

He had lost her at the ledge's root when he ran; everyone had. Now the bench was a different bench, plated over new to the edge, and she was forty paces off where she had landed, coming on her hands.

Seln went out to her. Cael saw him go and could not have.

She had gone for the fin when they went for it, on the ankle that had gone at the ford and gone again when she landed; he pieced that afterward from the marks in the shale. What had come down on her had not been an animal. It had been the bench: a plate going out under her foot as the second half came down and the whole shelf shifted, the leg going into the gap it left, and the plate above coming down on the leg with the mountain behind it. The plate was still there. Seln lifted it off. Under it the leg below the knee was the wrong shape. Not bent; the wrong shape, a line with an angle in it where there was not supposed to be an angle. The foot had no boot. The ledge had taken the boot. A strip of tape was still round the ankle above the bare foot, tied off at the shin.

She did not make a sound coming in. Seln carried her most of it. She looked at the leg as she had looked at the ankle at every fire on the bench: turned it in her head as far as it would turn, and then looked.

"That was me," she said, when Seln had put her down. Not to any of them. To the bench. "The loud thing."

Nobody said it was not.

"It was the ledge," Karis said, from where she sat with her hands in her lap, in a voice with nothing behind it. "The ledge went. Then it was you."

"Then it was me." Lira put her head back against the stone.

She did not say it again. She had put it in its order, and the order was right, and nobody on the bench took it from her.

---

Inventory, taken in the fin's lee on the seventy-eighth day, in his head, because his hands were under him.

Lira: the right leg broken below the knee, the shape wrong and the skin whole, the foot bare, the ankle under the break gone twice and no longer the largest thing in the leg. Seln straightened it as far as it would go, with Brom holding her and her own hand in her mouth, and it was not straight. Two plates of the mountain went either side of it, and Karis's cord round the plates from knee to ankle, tight, so that the leg was a thing in a box. She did not faint, and he had never seen her work so hard at anything.

Brom: the right forearm open again at the wrist under the cloth, the cloth dark and getting darker, the hand that had pulled two boxes off a cart hanging useless. He rewrapped it himself over the old cloth, one-handed, with his teeth. He said the boxes were fine. He had checked them first.

Karis: nothing broken, nothing cut. Empty. She had sat down at the eighth second and not stood since. She slept where she sat before noon, in the sun, with her head on the middle box.

Seln: unmarked, which from Seln was a report in itself.

Cael: three ribs, left side, low. Eighteen. Nine confirmed, one anomaly, none of it spent, all of it wanting. The calf Seln washed and bound with the last of the boiled linen from Thornwater, and he did not count paces, because he was not walking anywhere.

Five. Two of them could stand.

The cart: in the gorge, with the road food that had ridden under the boxes and the smith's six marks. The boxes: three, in the fin's lee, on their sides where they had landed and been carried, roped shut still, not one lid sprung. Karis had checked that before she checked her own hands.

The herd: sixteen past the fin in the first half, fourteen in the second, three he had heard go into the gorge and could not swear to. Twenty-seven down the mountain toward the fan.

The map: in Karis's book, in Karis's satchel, on Karis. Nine narrowings and their widths, and now a tenth, which was not a narrowing. It was an absence. The ledge was gone for a cart's width along three carts' length; what remained was a lip against the bulge that a walker could take with her hands on the rock, and a mule could not, and Oxhollow's carts would not, ever. Seln walked out along the lip in the afternoon and came back at dusk and said it went; that beyond the bulge the bench opened as they had seen it; that for a walker Oxhollow was two days on.

"And a healer?" Cael said, because it was the sum.

Seln had it. He had asked at Thornwater about the thing the steading's woman had said: *the nearest walks a route and she's not due.* Oxhollow had no healer of its own. The nearest walked a circuit of the holds and was wherever on it she was. A man at Oxhollow in two days could send a carter along the void road to find her, and that was four days from any place on this mountain in any direction, counted generously.

"Four days," Seln said. "If she's near. First light tomorrow, I go."

"Tonight," said Lira.

"Tomorrow." Seln did not look at the leg. "The bench is new. I'll not walk it in the dark for a healer who isn't there."

That night the fire was small, lit with a flint, because the woman who lit fires was asleep against a box. Brom did the food, what had been in the packs and not the cart: a day and a half at the ford's rate for five, and Brom made it three at a rate he did not explain. Cael lay on his right side in the fin's lee with the box for a pillow and counted the breaths that stopped at the place, and did not take the log out, because he could not have held it and did not want to see the hand.

---

The next day was the one the mountain kept. Seln went at first light with the pack on and came back before the sun was over the gorge: the lip held a walker, and below the lip the fan the herd had taken down was still going, in slides, one every hundred breaths, and a man on it was a stone on it. "Tomorrow," he said, "or it's two of us." The day was rationing and the leg. Karis woke and could not light a lamp and did not try again. Lira's leg went hot under the plates by noon, and the tape came off and went back on wetted, and she said nothing about it, which was how Cael knew. He counted breaths, and the slides on the fan, and the gorge, and did not take the log out.

---

The third morning came up grey, and the sound of the gorge came up with it.

Seln had his pack on before the light. He stood at the fin's end looking along the bench toward the ledge's root, and did not go, and Cael, lying on the ribs with the day's first breath stopping where the others had, followed his eyes.

There was a mule on the bench.

It stood beyond the bulge, where the shelf opened again, small against the grey, panniers on it, and a rope from its head going to nothing, because the nothing was a woman on the lip. She came along it as Seln had, one hand on the mountain, at a walk that did not change at the narrow place or the wide. She came off the lip where the cart had stood, looked at the new shale and the fin and the five of them in its lee, and came on at the same walk.

Nobody reached for steel. Seln, who reached for it before anyone, looked at her hands and did not.

Late twenties. A square face, wind-marked; a carter's coat; a pack, and a leather roll under one arm. Her hands were the first thing he catalogued after the walk, because she came in with them out of her sleeves and open: square, short-nailed, the knuckles scarred over old, clean in a way nothing else on the mountain was clean. She looked at the five of them once, as he looked at a board. She did not ask who they were.

"Who's worst?"

Lira said "Me" as Brom pointed at her with the wrapped hand, and the woman was already on her knees at the leg with the roll going down beside her in the shale.

"Name's Oryn." She said it to the splint. "Tide. Healer." She had the cord off the plates and the tape off the ankle before anyone moved to help her. "A carter came into Oxhollow off the void road two days back at noon and said the shale-backs had come off the mountain onto the low road at a run, and the crew that was up on the traverse was overdue off it. I was at the ford." She looked up, at Lira. "When."

"Two days back. First light."

"Anyone touch this?"

"Straightened it," Seln said. "As far as it went."

"Then it went as far as it went. That's not straight." Her hands went on the leg, above the break and below, and were still, and it lasted the time it took Cael to draw one of the breaths that stopped. Seconds. He had watched assessors go still like that over a page; never a healer over a leg. Then her hands came off it.

"Tier," said Seln, from above her. The professional's question, in the professional's voice.

"Iron. Four, if anybody's keeping a book up here." She did not look at him. "Broken through. Both bones. Two days old. Somebody's ankle under it has been wrong for a month." She said it to Lira as a list. "I'm going to set it. That's the part you'll mind. Then I'm going to mend it, and you'll mind that less. Big man: knee. You" — Seln — "shoulders. Both hands."

Brom took the knee with the good hand and his weight. Seln took Lira's shoulders. Oryn took the foot and the shin in two hands and did not say *now*, and did the thing.

Lira did not scream. She made a sound he had not heard her make in four years, once, and bit it off; and the leg below Oryn's hands was a line again, with no angle in it, and Oryn held it there and did not let go.

"That's set. Don't move."

Then she mended it.

He had been under four healers in four years. A bone set was a bone set: hands and a splint and a season, and the healer's Path took a week off the season and charged for it. He had never seen a bone knit. It happened inside a leg over months, out of sight.

He watched it happen in a quarter of an hour.

She had both hands on the leg, one above the break and one below, and the nearest word he had was that she was pouring, without anything moving. Lira's face went a colour that was not white, and she said "Cold," not to anyone, and then "No. It's going along," and put her head back and shut her eyes. The swelling at the break went down, not as a swelling goes down over a night but as water goes out of a basin when the plug is pulled, in the time it takes to watch, and the skin over the place lay flat, and the two lines of Lira's shin, the wrong one and the right one, were one line, and stayed one line, and Oryn's hands came off it.

The leg was straight. From her knees going down in the shale to her hands coming off, he made it less than an hour.

Her face had gone the colour of the shale. Grey, wet at the temples in the cold, the mouth set as a mouth sets that is holding a count. She sat down on the bench, all at once, as Karis had at the eighth second — no. Not as Karis. Karis had dropped as a rope drops. This woman sat down as a carter sets down a load he knows the weight of to the pound, and will pick up again, and would rather not yet.

"That's one," she said. To nobody. She drank from a skin, a long time. Then, to Lira: "Weight on it tomorrow. Not today. The bone's whole; it's new. It'll ache for a season and hold for the rest of your life. The ankle under it I've not touched; I've not got it in me this morning, and it's not going to kill you. Somebody tape it. You'll know how."

"I know how," Lira said, with her eyes shut.

"Hands on. That's the only way it works." Oryn said it as a thing she said often, to a room, before anyone asked. "Two years on these roads and I've never done a thing to anyone from over there. And I can't do it to me." She got up, which took her two tries. "Arm."

---

Brom's arm took less. He sat with the forearm across his knee and the cloth off it, the wrist open in a mouth two fingers long, and she put her hands either side of the open place, and went still for the seconds, and said "Iron Skin," and Brom said "Yes," and she said "Then it should have closed in a month and hasn't, because you've pulled on it. Dogs?" and Brom said "Dog," and she asked nothing else. It took the time it takes to eat an apple. The mouth at the wrist closed under her hands as the leg had, and when she took them off, the arm from wrist to elbow was a raised dark line and nothing else, and Brom looked at it with the lock face and then, for once, without it.

"Don't pull on it for a week," she said. "It's not a dog's business now. It's yours."

She had gone greyer, and sat again, not long, and Cael counted that she had drunk half the skin.

Karis was awake, against the box. She had watched the leg and the arm with the look she gave a primary source, and said, when Oryn came to her, before Oryn had knelt: "There's nothing wrong with me."

"I'll be the one who says that." Oryn knelt anyway and took both the scarred hands in her own, palm up, and did not ask about the two raised lines across each, and went still. Seconds. She let the hands go.

"Ember," she said. "And nothing. You're empty. That's not a hurt; I can't put back what you spent, and I wouldn't if I could. Eat. Sleep. It fills." She looked at the black line the lattice had left across the face above the fin, thirty paces of scorched shale, and then at Karis, and there was no awe in it at all. "That's yours?"

"That's mine."

"Hm." The whole of the verdict. She stood.

He had the log out by then. He had got it out of the satchel with the right hand while she was at Brom's arm, and found that he could hold the pencil if he did not breathe past the place. He wrote one line. It was not his hand. It was a hand that hurt, and it leaned, and it was legible.

*Eightieth day. Lira's leg is straight. The boxes are ours. The cart is in the gorge. A healer came up the road because a carter said we were late.*

He closed it. She was standing over him.

---

"You," she said. "Ribs. I saw you breathe."

"Three. Left side. Low."

"I'll be the one who says that." She knelt in the shale at his side and did not ask him to move, and put both hands on him, one flat on the ribs where the shoulder had taken him, one on his back behind it, and went still.

Seconds. He counted them. He watched her face because it was in front of him: the face of a woman listening for a thing she had heard a thousand times, as a fighter listens for a step behind him.

She stopped.

Not as she had stopped at the leg or the arm, at the end of the seconds, with the finding ready. She stopped in the middle of them. Her hands came off him. She looked at them, both, palm and back, as a man looks at a tool that has done a thing it has never done. Then she put them back, exactly where they had been, and went still again, and held it longer, and her face did a thing.

He had watched that thing on faces for four years. At fourteen, for eleven seconds, from the inside. On assessors' faces at Ardenmere and at Greyvane, and on a panel in the academy above Ostrand, from the outside, over a page with his name on it and a bracket after it. It was the face of an instrument that has been put to a thing and has returned nothing, not a wrong number, no number, and is running itself again to be sure. He had never seen it on a healer. Healers did not read the page. Healers read the leg.

She took her hands off him and did not look at them this time. She looked at him.

"Your ribs are broken," she said. "I can feel that they're broken. I can't feel *you*."

Nobody moved. Lira's eyes had opened. Karis, against the box, did not move anything but her eyes.

"Whatever you are, I can't read it." It was said as the leg had been said: a list, exact, at the pace of a woman who did not hurry a finding because the finding was bad. "I've never said that to anyone. Tape them. They'll heal on their own." A breath. "They always have, haven't they?"

"Yes," he said.

"That's not an answer," Oryn said. "That's a *finding*."

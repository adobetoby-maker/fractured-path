# Chapter 13 — The Reading

Thornwater's gate came up out of the road at the end of the second day, and he counted the steading as he came through it, because he had counted it going out and wanted to know what five days had done to it.

The wall, timber on a stone footing, the same. The bowman on the gate-walk, the same man, who looked down at six where he had seen six go out and called nothing, which was his whole vocabulary and was enough. The yard, the well, the grain loft with its ladder, the house with two chimneys and one of them smoking. The steading's woman at the house door with her arms folded, counting them as they came in while he counted her steading, and arriving at the same number, and looking at Lira's arm slung across her chest, and at Karis's throat, and at the mule with its three boxes, and saying, "Six. Well," which was a ledger line.

Lira had walked the two days as Oryn had told her to and not as Lira walked — the taped right ankle put down flat at every stride, as if it were somebody else's — which was the first time in four years he had seen her take an order about her own body and keep it past the first mile. She stopped inside the gate, the foot turned out, and did not sit down until Oryn said she could.

He had walked the two days on the calf at nine on the rises and no count on the flat by the second noon, and had known by the second noon what that meant and had not said it.

---

Oryn said it for him, in the yard, before she went in to be fed.

"Tape," she said.

He got the shirt up. Twenty-three days of boiled linen round the ribs, hers, grey now, and she took the little knife from the leather roll and cut it at the back and unwound it, and the breath went all the way down for the first time since a shale-back's shoulder on a bench above a gorge. He let it go all the way down twice more, to be sure of the count, and she watched him do it.

"Three ribs, left side, low." She had her palm flat where they had been, not reading; looking, with the hand. "Twenty-three days. A rib is six weeks. These are done." She took the hand away. "Leg."

He got the trouser leg up and the binding off. The ford's line along the inside of the calf, opened on the rim's edge four days ago and washed and bound at the stone and not touched since, was a seam. Pink, closed its whole length, the black gone out of it. She put a thumb along it and pressed, and he did not move, and she pressed harder, and he did not move.

"Closed. Four days." She said it as she said everything: to the thing in front of her, without a verdict attached. "That should be a fortnight. I've said so four times and I'll not say it a fifth; it's on the page." She let the leg go and stood up, and gave him the look she had given the stone at the line: the look for a thing with a rule in it she had not been told. "Still and whole. Near enough that I'll say it. Tomorrow, after I've eaten and slept under a roof, which I've not done since this one. The long one."

"Yes," he said.

"You don't get a yes. You get told." She was already turning toward the house, where the woman was holding the door. "The back room. The door shut. Nobody in it but the two of us; she'll give me the room, she always does. You'll lie on the table on your side and you'll be still and you'll not talk and you'll not help."

"What does helping look like?"

"I'll know," Oryn said, and went in.

---

The loft, after the meal, the five of them and the lantern the woman had given them with a finger's width of oil in it, which he had learned to price.

Lira on the grain with her back to the sacks and the arm across her chest. Brom at the ladder's head, where he always sat when there was a ladder, with the crossbow across his knees and the right hand open on the stock, the four dark lines across the pads, the string untouched for two days because Oryn had said three. Karis on the middle box with the notebook open and the pencil up, and the dark under her jaw gone to yellow at the edges and green in the middle, and her mouth shut, because Oryn had said days and it had been two. Seln at the loft's one window, standing, with the yard behind him.

"I need the circle," Cael said. "Without her. It has to be without her, and I'll say why."

"I thought you might." Lira did not move. "Go on."

He had built the order on the road, because order was what he had, and he gave it as built.

"The long reading. Tomorrow, in the back room. Both hands, all of her, run through all of me, for minutes. She said twenty, at Oxhollow; she said it costs what a mending costs; she said I have to be still and whole, and she said in the yard that I am." He counted them off on the fingers of the hand he was not leaning on. "Sustained close engagement. Not a count of ten with her hands on a hurt. Twenty minutes of a Tide practitioner's whole current inside my architecture, at contact, on purpose. That's the first."

Nobody said anything. Seln, at the window, had turned his head.

"Stakes. I'm three days off the Ring. Everything I have went to nothing on that floor and rang again on the second morning and is still settling; I can feel it settle. And the person doing it is the only healer in a country, three mendings down in two days on a route that's late at the far end, spending a fourth on a patient who has given her nothing four times. That's real on both sides. That's the second."

"And the third's a fight," Brom said. It was not an objection. It was the weight being named.

"The third's earnest use. Ephram's said *earnest*. Ephram's said *non-hostile*. This isn't against me. It's on me. It's a woman doing the one thing she does, as well as she does it, to the one patient she's never once been able to do it to." He put the hand down. "That's the third, and I don't know if it counts. I've never had the conditions come together anywhere but a floor with somebody on the other side of it."

Karis had put the pencil down. She said it with the voice Oryn had told her not to use yet, and it came out in pieces, low and torn at the edges, and nobody told her to write it instead.

"Ephram fought you in earnest and the notice said non-hostile. This is earnest and it isn't a fight at all." She stopped to get breath past the throat. "The model doesn't say the engagement has to be a fight. It says it has to be real."

She wrote the same sentence under the spoken one, in the book, while they looked at her, and turned the book so it faced the lantern and not any of them, which was how she filed a thing.

"Real," Lira said. "So it might happen."

"It might happen."

"And you're asking us what."

"I'm not asking you whether. I'm telling you I've decided, and I'm telling you the price, and I'm asking whether I've got the price right. That's what the circle's for. It was for that in Ostrand and it's for that here."

---

The price took longer than the conditions, because the conditions were his and the price was hers.

"She doesn't know," he said. "What I am. What the nine are, or that there are nine, or what happens when the conditions come together. She's seen them work on the floor and she's said nothing, and she's not asked, and I've not told her. If it happens tomorrow, it happens inside a thing she's doing to help. She can't consent to it. Not because I won't ask. Because there's nothing she could be asked. You can't say yes to a thing nobody's told you exists."

"Then tell her," Lira said. "Tonight. Before."

He had known that was coming, because it was the honest question, and Lira asked the honest question first and let the rest of the room work out why.

"I've thought about it for two days. Here's where I got, and I don't like it." He looked at the lantern and not at her. "If I tell her tonight, she doesn't read me tomorrow. She tests a claim. A healer who goes in looking for a thing isn't running a reading, she's running a proof, and the one finding she's wanted since the mountain is gone whether it happens or not. And I don't know that it will happen. Four times she's had her hands on me and got nothing. It might be a fifth. So I'd be telling her a thing that might not be true, at the cost of the only thing she asked for." He stopped. "That's the argument. It's a good argument, and it's the argument of somebody who wants the thing. Both of those are true and I'm writing both down."

"That's not the reason," Lira said.

"No. The reason is I don't know what she'd say, and I'm not brave enough to find out before I've got a sentence. That goes in too."

Seln spoke from the window, to the yard.

"She asked for it. She's asked four times. Nobody asked Ephram to be measured; he asked to measure. Nobody asked the woman on the Ostrand road anything." A pause the length of a man checking a fact against a shelf. "That's not consent. It's the nearest thing to it the log's ever had, and it's still not it. I'd enter both."

"She asked to read you," Lira said. "She didn't ask for the rest. Those aren't the same sentence."

"No," Cael said. "They aren't."

Brom, at the ladder: "What's it cost her? Not the reading. The other thing."

That was the question he had not been able to finish on the road, and he gave Brom the part he had.

"I don't know. It's never cost the source anything I could see. Lira fights the same. Karis lights the same. Seln's still Seln." He did not look at Seln when he said it, and Seln did not look at him. "But I've never done it to somebody with their hands on me while they were spending themselves. If there's a cost to her, tomorrow's the first time it could show, and she'll be the one paying it, and she's the one person on this road who came up a mountain because a carter said we were late. If the architecture takes what she's using on me, it takes it from the one person who came to help. That's the cost. I can't price it. I can write that I can't."

"And after," Lira said.

"After, I tell her. All of it. The nine, the notices, the log, the covenant, what happened on her table, that I wrote this tonight and chose it. Whatever it costs. If she takes her hands off me for good, that's what it costs." He took the leather book out of the satchel and put it on his knee. "Before, not after. I'm writing it now. You can read it, the two of you who read it."

Karis wrote something short and did not turn the book. Lira got up, one-armed, off the sacks.

"Write it," she said. "I'll read it when it's dry."

---

He wrote it by the last of the woman's oil, with the loft asleep around him and Seln at the window still, and read it back once, and did not change a word, because a word changed after was a word written after.

*Ninety-ninth day. Thornwater, the loft. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Eighteen.*

*Written before. Tomorrow morning, the back room, the door shut. Oryn runs the long reading. Both hands, all of her, all of me, twenty minutes. I lie still. I'm still and whole for the first time in front of her; she said so in the yard, with the tape off.*

*The conditions, as I have them. Sustained: twenty minutes at contact is longer than any bout I've stood. Stakes: I'm three days off the Ring and the nine are still settling; she's three mendings down and her route's late at the far end and she's spending a fourth on a patient who's given her nothing four times. Earnest: she'll do the thing she does as well as she does it. Karis: the model says real, not fight. I don't know if the model's right. Nobody does. Tomorrow's the first time it could be tested and I'm the test.*

*The price. She doesn't know what I am. She can't consent to what she can't know about — not won't; can't. There's nothing I could put in front of her tonight that she could say yes or no to, because the thing doesn't have a name she's heard. I've decided not to tell her before. My reasons are good, and they're the reasons of somebody who wants it, and the real one is that I don't know what she'd say. Entered.*

*If it takes what she's using on me, it takes it from the one person on this road who came to help. I don't know what it costs a source. It's never shown. Tomorrow's the first time it could, and if it does, she pays, and I chose that for her.*

*After: I tell her. In full. On her table, before I'm off it. Whatever it costs me, including the route walking north without me on it.*

*I want it. Write that too.*

*Write it before, not after. That's the whole difference between me and the system.*

He put the pencil in the spine and the book under his hand and did not sleep well, and did not expect to.

---

The back room was the steading's, and then it was hers.

He catalogued it from the door, because she had said he would lie still and he wanted the room fixed before he did. Four paces by five. A window on the east wall, unshuttered, low, with the first sun coming through it flat along the floor and up the far wall to the height of his knee, and climbing. A table under the window, plank, long enough for a man, with a folded blanket on it where a head would go. A stool. A shelf with the woman's jars on it, pushed to one end to make room for the leather roll, open, its knife and its needles and its linen laid out in the order Oryn laid things out, which was the order she would want them. A door, plank, on a leather hinge, that shut.

She shut it.

"Shirt off. On your side, facing the window. Left side down." She had eaten and slept, and it showed where it showed on her — not in the face, which was the same square weathered face, but in the hands, which were steady and warm when she put one on his shoulder to turn him. "Your left hand under the blanket. Your right arm along your side, not on it. There." She moved the arm a finger's width. "You're going to want to breathe like a man being examined. Don't. Breathe like a man asleep."

He lay on his side on a plank table in a steading's back room with the sun on the wall in front of him, eighteen years old, nine fragments and one anomaly, and did what he was told.

Her hand came onto his sternum. Flat, the palm over the breastbone, the fingers spread toward the throat. The other came onto his back, between the shoulder blades, over the spine, so that the two hands had him between them like a thing held up to the light.

"Count of ten," she said. "You know this part."

He knew this part. He had watched it from outside on Lira's leg and Brom's arm and Karis's throat, and felt it from inside four times: her attention arriving, going down, going to the place, and not finding it. It went down now. He felt it go, as at Oxhollow's table — a pressure that was not a pressure, a hand that was not the hand, going under the hand — and felt it reach the place where on Brom it found *Iron Skin* and on Karis it found *Ember*, and find what it had found four times.

Ten, by her count.

"Nothing," Oryn said. "As before. That's the short one. Now the long one, and I'll talk, because I talk, and you'll not answer."

The count went on past ten and did not stop, and the thing under her hands changed.

---

He had no word for it and did not try to make one on the table. He catalogued it instead, because that was the thing he could do lying still.

It came in under the front hand first. Not cold. The idea of cold, without the temperature — the shape of a stream's edge on a wrist, arriving inside the chest where there was no wrist and no stream. It went where it was put. That was the first thing he knew about it and the thing he kept knowing: it did not push, and it did not search, and it did not go where the surface reading had gone, down to the hurt. It went *along*. Her current, in him, following his architecture the way water follows a channel it has been given, slow, at the pace of a woman walking a floor she has never walked and means to walk the whole of.

Nothing in him had been touched from outside before. He had been read from outside by instruments — assessors, panels, a sigil that had gone dark at eleven seconds and stayed dark — and none of them had come in. They had stood at the door. This came through it, and went along the first thing it found, which was Wind, faint, still ringing from the second morning, and he felt the current lie along Wind's whole length as a hand lies along a rope to learn its lay, and go on.

He could feel her deciding. That was the other thing. Her attention had a texture, and the texture was *patient*, and the patience had a count in it — he could feel her keeping time — she kept everything — so that the twenty minutes would be twenty and not nineteen said as twenty.

"I'm in," she said. Low, level, to the room. "Past the hurt-layer. There's a lot of you. I'd not expected that; the short one made you feel thin."

The current went from Wind to Pressure, and along Pressure, and he understood that she could not have told him those names — that what she was feeling was two movements, adjacent, and the names were his — and he lay still and did not give them to her.

"Movement. I'll say what I feel and not what it means, because I don't know what it means and I'll not learn it by saying." Her front hand shifted a finger's width, as it had on the calf at the stone, looking for a bank a finger over. "Movement, going. Not one. I'm on one and I've lost it, and I'm on — another. Or the same one round a corner. I can't tell yet."

Iron. Compression. The current lay along each of them, in turn, unhurried, and he felt the ones it had not reached yet ring faintly at the edges of where it was, strings on a bench when one is struck, and felt her attention go toward the ringing and stop, and go back.

"Everyone I've ever had my hands in, this is the part where I know." She said it as a finding about herself. "Iron Skin, you feel the banks first — dense, walled, everything held. Ember, the banks are a fire's, hot at the edge. Wind you barely feel the banks at all, the water's too fast. But there's always a bank. It's how you know which river." A pause, long, her current going along Ember and stopping at the ignition point and lying there as if it had found a stone in a bed. "I can't find one here. I keep putting my hand where the bank ought to be and there's more water."

Shadow. He felt her go along it, and felt it draw in as it always drew in, and felt her feel that — a movement that got quieter as she touched it — and go on, and say nothing, because she had said she would say what she felt and not what it meant, and a movement that hid was a meaning.

The sun on the wall had climbed to his shoulder's height. Ten minutes, near enough. There was no floor here to read the light on, as there had been at the stone; he counted by the wall.

---

Storm was where the reading changed for him.

Not for her. Her current went along Storm as it had gone along the seven before it, slow, learning the lay, and her hands did not move. But Storm was the loudest thing he owned, taken from a Gold-tier woman on a floor at survival intensity, and he felt her current go into it and find it *quiet*, three days off the Ring, settling, and lie along it as it had lain along Wind, and he understood something he had not put on the page the night before.

She was not looking for the thing that had frightened five people on a floor. She had watched him lead nine hounds across a line with everything he had lit, and three days later she had put her hands on him and gone in looking for how he was built. Not what he could do. How the water ran. It was the least frightened anyone had ever been of him with cause, and it was not courage. It was a trade, and a trade that had had its hands inside dying people did not keep a place in it for being impressed.

He lay still. It was the hardest still of the twenty.

Anchor, which since the Ostrand road had wanted doorways, wanted nothing under her hand at all, and he filed that. Blade. The current went along the ninth and slowed, and slowed, and he felt her feel the edge of it — not the edge it declared; the edge of *it*, the place where the ninth movement should have stopped and become bank — and felt her not find it.

"I've been the whole way round." Her voice had not changed, and the hands had not changed, and something under the voice had. "Or I think I have. I can't tell you I've been the whole way round, because I can't find the edge of the way."

The sun was at the top of the wall, going onto the ceiling. Twenty, by the wall.

She said it then, all of it, in the order she had found it, like one of Seln's reports.

"I can feel that there's movement. More than one. I can't count them — I can't find where one stops. There's no channel. Everyone has a channel; I feel the banks and I know the Path. I can't find the banks in you. I've looked for twenty minutes. It isn't that you're broken. It's that I can't find anything shaped like the thing I read."

The hands stayed. The current stayed, along whatever it was along — he had lost the count of which, for the first time; there were nine and it was on one of them and he did not know which, and the not-knowing was hers, arriving in him from her side.

Oryn said the next thing to the room, and not to him.

"Eleven years. I've never had my hands in something and not known what it was."

She stopped talking. She did not take her hands off.

---

The conditions the model required were not a thing he reasoned toward. They arrived, as findings arrived, whole, and he catalogued them because he was lying still and it was the thing he could do.

Sustained: twenty minutes of a Tide practitioner's whole current inside him at contact, the longest anyone had ever touched him, by nineteen minutes and fifty seconds. Stakes: hers, spent — he could feel the spending now, a thinness in the current where at the start there had been none, a route's fourth mending going out of her into a patient who had returned nothing; his, three days off the Ring, the nine settling, and the ones she had touched settling *differently*, as a string sits differently once a hand has been along it. Earnest: she had gone the whole way round a thing she could not read and had not once, in twenty minutes, tried to make it into a thing she could.

And long. That was the one the previous nine had never had. Every floor, every bout, every road cut had been minutes at most of a working architecture at contact in earnest, and the reaching had run on whatever it could gather in those minutes. This had been twenty. Whole. Nothing shouting over it. A working architecture, run through his own, slow, for long enough that his own attention — which had been cataloguing her the whole time, without his choosing to, because that was what it did — had the whole of it.

He felt it turn.

A count coming out even: a column he had not known he was adding arriving at its foot, and the foot being a number, and the number being *her* — how the current went where it was put; how it lay along a thing to learn it; how it went and did not push; how it kept time. The reaching was the interior action it had always been, on a yard's floor and in a sealed room and at a road cut and at a flag: attention turned wholly on a witnessed architecture and held there, open, without demand. It was running now on a plank table in a steading, with the witnessed architecture inside him, still, its hands on him, and it was going to complete.

He could stop it. He knew the shape of stopping it as he knew the shape of a burst's landing lock: take the attention off her, put it on the wall, on the sun, on the count. Say her name. Say *take your hands off*. Any of those.

Stopping her would be the lie. He had written the price the night before so that the price would be paid and not avoided, and the price was this, and it was arriving with her hands on him.

He reached. Directed. Open-eyed. Still.

It completed the way a knot completes when the last turn is drawn, and the drawing was hers: her current, going along whatever it had been along, and his architecture going along *it*, for the length of one of her breaths, learning the lay of the thing that was learning him.

The notice arrived while her hands were still on him. He read it on the wall, where the sun was, because that was where his eyes were:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Tide-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: Iron.
Note: current-perception component; architecture-reading component. Self and contact.
Contact range.
Acquisition: directed. Engagement: clinical — non-hostile.
```

Two firsts, counted on the page even now, lying still, because he was who he was.

The first notice whose engagement field said *clinical*. The grammar had bent again — it had bent for *directed* on a training-hall floor, for *non-hostile* at a flag — and it had bent now to describe a thing that was earnest and was help and had no other side to it at all. And the first fragment that did nothing in a fight. He read the note line twice to be sure. Perception. Reading. Self and contact. There was not a floor on the continent where it would move him a foot.

He counted a third and did not write it, and would not write it tonight, and knew as he did not write it that it was the one.

Ten. The tenth was Tide. The log had had a word in it for five years, from a session on a floor in Ardenmere with no Tide practitioner in the city, and the word was *Tide-adjacent*, and it had sat under three other words that said *patience*, and now there was a fragment with the same word on it, a real one, with a source, with hands. He did not know what that meant. He did not let himself put a verb after it. He filed it where it would keep.

Oryn took her hands off him.

He felt them go — the front one first, then the back — and felt the current go with them, out the way it had come, and felt, for the length of a breath, the *shape* of it going, from his side, as a thing with a lay to it, and then that was gone too and he was a man on a table in the sun.

She did not sit down. She stood beside the table with her hands in front of her, a little apart, palms up, and looked at them the way she had looked at them at the stone, and at the line, and on a road with the lantern out; and she went on looking at them for longer than she had looked at them anywhere.

"Something just changed," she said. "In you. While I was in there."

"Yes."

"Did I do that?"

He had written the answer the night before, so that he would give it and not a better one.

"No. I did." He did not get up. "I need to tell you what I am."

Oryn looked at her hands a moment longer, and then at him, and put the hands flat on the table's edge, one either side of his shoulder, and leaned on them.

"Then lie still while you do it. You've been still for twenty minutes. I want to see if you can manage twenty-one."

# Chapter 16 — Lira First

Thornwater's wall was timber on a stone footing, and the gate-walk ran along the inside of it at the height of a tall man's reach, and at dusk on the hundred and tenth day the bowman went off it to eat and left it to whoever wanted it, which was nobody, most evenings, and tonight was two.

Lira went up the ladder first, one-handed, because she had been going up ladders one-handed for two weeks and had stopped waiting to be watched. He went up after her. The walk was three planks wide with a rail on the yard side and nothing on the other but the top of the wall and the country past it, and he catalogued the country because he had not seen it from this height before: the void road going east out of the gate and over the first rise into the dark, toward a bend two days off that he could not see and could have drawn; the scrub going grey either side of it; the lower fields stripped to stubble, with the last cart's ruts still in them; a carter's fire a mile out, one point of orange, somebody walking the road quiet and camping short of the wall because the gate shut at dusk; the first star. She sat down with her back to the rail and her legs out along the planks and the arm across her chest, and looked at the road, and did not look at him, because she had said *later* on a road with her face turned away and this was later and she was giving him the same face to say it to.

He had built the order on the walk from the common room. It was short. He had had it since the stone and it had not grown, which he had noticed on the walk and filed as the only measurement of the thing he had: fifteen days, and a fact that had not got one sentence longer for being carried.

"At the line," he said. "The first day, when Karis marked it. Everyone's went out at the stride. Yours. Hers. Brom's, Seln's, Oryn's. I backed up with you and stepped over it with you and I felt nothing change. Not that day. Not in the ring, the whole afternoon. Not at the den. Not when I ran the pack out across the stone with everything lit." He was looking at the road too. "Mine's been dark since I was fourteen. Eleven seconds, and then a word, and then nothing, ever, from that day to this. There was nothing for the line to turn off. That's the whole of what I know."

"And the rest."

"The rest is that I used everything I have inside a place where none of you could use anything, and all of it worked, exactly as it works out here, and I don't know why. I've had fifteen days. I've got nothing I'd put in the log as a reason. I've got the fact, and I've got the not-knowing, and I told you *later* because I thought later I'd have a sentence." He stopped. "I don't. So this is later anyway."

Lira did not say anything for a while. The star had a second one near it now.

Then she said: "You told me when you knew. You don't know. So you told me *that*. Keep doing it."

It was a covenant six books old, made in a boarding-house room in Ardenmere by a girl who had not flinched, and it had been kept at a residence wall in Greyvane and on a bluff above Ostrand and on a road below it, and he had never once heard it said back to him in fewer words. He filed the count. Then he found that there was nothing to file it against, and let it stand.

"It scares you," Lira said.

She had not asked it. She had said it, to the road, as she said a measurement.

"Out there I was the thing the system couldn't hold." He had not known he was going to say it in that order until it came out in that order. "In there I was the only thing that *worked*. I don't know which one's worse."

Lira said, "The second one. You're allowed to say so."

He sat with that for the length of a third star. Then he said, "The second one," and she nodded, once, to the road, and moved her good hand along the plank until it was against his boot, and left it there, and that was the whole of what either of them did about it.

She asked the fighter's question after that, because she was a fighter and the other question had been asked.

"Inside. When you spent it all. Did it feel the same?"

"The same."

"Not louder. Not cheaper. Not — " she moved the good hand a little, looking for it — "not like it was the only thing in the room."

"It was the only thing in the room. It didn't feel like it. It felt like the ford. Two bursts, two locks, a dog in the second, the bill in the usual coin. I counted it the way I count it out here and the count came out the same." He looked at the carter's fire. "That's the part I keep turning over. If it had felt different I'd have a place to start. It felt like a Third-day."

"Then it's a Third-day in there," Lira said, "and a Third-day out here, and the only thing that changes at the line is everybody else." She let that sit. "That's not a sentence either. But it's a shape."

"It's a shape."

"The rest of them tonight," she said, after a while.

"Yes."

"Her too?"

"Her too. She had her hands in me for twenty minutes and stood at that line eight times on her own with nobody watching but me. She's the only one of us who's *tested* it." He looked at the road. "And she's going north in the morning. If I tell five and not six, I've kept something from the one who's leaving, and she'd know it by the time she reached the bend."

"Then six," Lira said, and got up one-handed, and went down the ladder first.

---

The common room, at night, with the fire lit for supper and the supper cleared and the woman's door shut on the other side of the house, because Karis had asked her and she had said *well*.

Six. He catalogued them, because it was the first time the six of them had sat at a table for this and he wanted the room fixed: Lira at the fire's end with the arm across her and the foot up. Brom on the bench beside her with the crossbow across his knees and his hands still on it, which meant he was listening. Karis across from them with the notebook open to the five findings and her hand flat on the page, keeping it open, which she had never in his life needed to do. Seln at the door end, where the two doors were, the case on his back, sitting as a man sits who has been told there will be a report and intends to hear it as one. Oryn beside Seln, on the end of the bench nearest the yard door, with her hands turned over on her knees, as at a fire at a bend when she had been a day late and about to be later. Himself at the table's head with the log shut in front of him, because this was not a thing he was going to read; it was a thing he was going to say.

He said it as he had said it to Lira, in the same order, because the order was true and he had not found a better one.

Nobody interrupted. When he stopped, Karis said, "The four who saw. Say what you saw. In order. I want it in the book as said and not as I remember it." And they did, because Karis had asked in the method voice and there was no refusing that voice at a table.

Brom, first, from the lip. "You came past me loud. Off the lane, north, across the scrub to the rut, everything lit — I've never once seen you loud. I've seen you *spend*, on floors, one thing at a time, with a count on it. That was all of it at once, in the dark, on ground where I'd just found out I was a big man with a hook." He looked at the crossbow. "Every head turned. Mine did."

Karis, from the ground. "I was under a dog. I saw your feet. I saw them go past me the wrong way, and I saw the grass go flat and white on both sides of them, and then I saw nothing, because it was gone toward the road with the pack after it, and then Brom had me." She wrote as she spoke, which she could do. "I heard it, more than saw it. The Wind. I've stood next to Lira's a hundred times. That was Lira's, inside a place where Lira had just made a step."

Seln, from the lane. "North to the rut. East along it, the wrong way, a hundred and twenty meters, with eight animals behind you and nothing in front. I called *back* when Lira was over. You turned on it." A pause the length of a fact. "You were loud the whole way so nothing looked south. It was correct. I'd have done it with a lamp. You did it with a Path."

Oryn, last, with her hands on her knees. "I had Lira's arm. I saw you go past the end of it, and I saw the grass, and I heard the dogs go, and I had nothing in my hands and you had everything in yours." She looked at him, and then at nobody. "That's what I saw. I'll say what I've got after she's said what she's got. Hers is the book."

"I've said mine," Lira said, from the fire's end. "On a road, with my face the other way. It's the same as theirs and I was closer."

Brom asked the thing Brom asked, which was about weight. "Inside. Did it cost more?"

"The same. To the count."

"And after. When you came out."

"Spent to the road. Same as Daeva. Same as any floor I've ever emptied on." He had the log shut under his hand and did not open it, because the numbers were in it and he knew them. "It didn't take anything extra going over the stone either way. I've looked. There's nothing in the bill that says *line*."

Brom nodded, once, and his hands stayed on the bow, and that was Brom finished.

Karis turned the notebook round and put it in the middle of the table, open to the five, and said them: the name; the failure; the strata, with the word she had used once and would not use again; the stakes; the observation with its interpretation stopped. She did not read the pages. She had them, and she said them at the table the way she had said them across the week, to whoever had been at the table, and when she had finished she took the book back and closed it on her pen.

"That's everything I can put a ruler against," she said. "It's on the page. I've a hypothesis and it isn't."

"Nor's mine," Cael said.

"I know. That's why I said it to you and not to the book."

Seln said the professional thing then, from the door end, to the table, in the voice for an inventory, and every word of it was a count.

"They know something turns Paths off. They *don't* know something doesn't turn off. Yet."

Nobody said anything for the length of a breath, because *yet* was the word with the weight in it, and Seln did not spend weight.

Oryn's turn.

"My hands stop at that line. Yours don't. I had my hands in you for twenty minutes and I can tell you what I didn't find, which is a Path. That's all I've got." She turned her hands over, and back. "I'm not going to guess past it."

That was the table, then. Six people who had between them four years of academies and a records hall and a circuit and fifteen years of a Compact posting and two years of a route and a boy's log, looking at the largest fact any of them had ever had on a table, and not one of them putting a verb after it. He watched them not do it. Brom looked at the bow. Lira looked at the fire. Karis looked at the closed book with her hand on it. Seln looked at the yard door and Oryn looked at her hands, and he sat at the head of the table and catalogued, because it was what he did, that the not-saying was not fear. Fear would have been a guess. This was six people who could each have made one and had each decided, separately and for their own reasons, that a guess was a thing you wrote over a true unknown and let the record carry forward, and that none of them would.

"So," Karis said. "What we have."

She had the carter's map out before she said it, unrolled on the table with the lantern on one corner and the notebook on the other, the void road running solid across it and bending three times, and she counted on the map with a finger, as she had counted the five sigils.

"One site. The Fallow Ring. Paced, measured, entered." Her finger on the first circle, the one with Seln's charcoal ring drawn over it. "Pike said *a handful*. His word. Thirty years on the roads and he's walked past more than one and never counted them, because you don't count what you walk round." Off the map, to the room. "The carter's map has three circles on it. Three blanks a carter drew because everybody knows the quiet ground, and one of them is the Ring, and two of them aren't." The finger to the second circle, and the third: one south, a day off the road; one further, east, past where the carter's copying had got careless. "And the contract was written inside. By somebody who knew there was nothing at the site, and wanted to know which crew would go in, and now knows." She put her hand flat on the map. "That's four things. None of them is a why. All of them are a where."

"The report's on a desk by now," Seln said. "The ferry went west eight days ago, with Pike's copy in the sack. Whoever wrote the slip has read that we came out, and is deciding what that's worth."

"Then we're on a clock," Lira said.

"We've been on a clock since the ninetieth day. I'm saying it's been wound." Seln did not look at the map. "It doesn't change the four things. It changes how long we have to be the only ones who know them."

Karis said, "We find the others. All of them. And we find out whether they're the same."

Brom said, "Yes," as a weight. Lira said, "Yes," to the fire. Seln said nothing, which was yes.

"Which first," Brom said.

"Whichever the board prices." Karis rolled the map. "We've a hundred and eight in hand and nothing owed and a mule that eats. Pike's got sixteen slips on a plank and one of them will be near a circle, because the circles are on the roads and the roads are where the work is. We go back to Lowmarch and we read the board the way he reads it" — a nod at Cael — "and we take the job that walks us past a blank, and we pace it, and we enter it. That's how you find a handful. One at a time, for money."

Cael looked at Oryn.

"North in the morning," Oryn said. It was not an answer to Karis; it was a fact about a route. "Oxhollow's seventeen days late and the old woman's knee doesn't care what's under a floor. I'll be on the road at first light and I'll be three days north by the time you're back at the board." She looked at him. "Thornwater's on my route. You know where that is now."

"Yes."

"Then that's where I'll be, when I'm there." She stood up, and put her hand on Seln's shoulder as she went past him, once, which nobody at the table had ever seen anyone do, and Seln did not move under it, which nobody had seen either. "Don't go in without me. Not because I'm any use inside. Because somebody should be outside the line who can put an arm back on."

The room let her go. It was the first time she had left a table of theirs without leaving the building.

---

Brom caught him at the loft ladder's foot, after, with the bow slung and the lantern in his mended hand, and stood in the way of the ladder without seeming to, which was a thing Brom could do with a doorway and had done with a lip of fused stone.

"The only one who worked," Brom said.

"Yes," Cael said.

"You said it like it was new." He turned the lantern down a thread, so that the yard door's draught would not have it. "It isn't. You were the only one who worked on the Ironyard's floor against a man two tiers up. You were the only one who worked in a hearing room with the best reader on the continent across the table. You were the only one who worked on a road below Ostrand with a woman who'd fixed the ground." He said it as a list of weights, in order, the way his father would have said what a load was. "Every room since I met you. The floor didn't make you the only one who worked. It just made it so nobody else did, for once, so you could see it."

Cael stood with his hand on the ladder.

"That's not comfort," Brom said. "I don't do that. It's a count. You like counts."

"I like counts."

"Then count it." He put the lantern in Cael's hand and went up the ladder ahead of him, one-handed, the other on the bow, and did not say anything else, because Brom said a thing once and let the weight of it do the rest.

---

Vastin received the broker's completion report as a courtesy copy, forwarded through a registry office that had no interest in any board east of the Line, in the second folder of the morning, marked *Fallow Ring: crew returned, instruments not recovered*. He read it twice — a broker's report from a town on no registry map, on his desk with no routing note — and did not know why it had reached him, and filed that, in the register of a man who had never once in forty years pretended to knowledge he lacked, as the whole of what he knew about it. He returned the copy to its folder unannotated.

---

She found him in the yard after, at the well, because the well was where he went when a room had had enough of him and he had not yet had enough of the day.

The hold was asleep. The mule was asleep. Oryn came out of the house with the leather roll already on her back by its strap, packed for first light, and stood a stride off in the cold with the light from the one window on the side of her face, and did not sit on the well's rim, which was how he knew this was not going to be about food.

"You can read people now," she said. "At contact. Me, Karis, Seln, anyone whose wrist you're holding. I read people for a living and I ask first, every time, even when they're dying. You will too."

"Yes," he said.

He said it at once, and meant it at once, and catalogued, because he could not help it, that he had sat at a table for two hours that night with Karis's wrist a foot from his right hand while she counted on a map, and Seln's shoulder under Oryn's palm across the table, and Lira's good hand against his boot on a wall at dusk, and that the tenth fragment had been awake for every one of them. Not wanting. It did not want; it listened. But it had been awake, and a thing that is awake at contact range in a room full of people you love is a thing that could be turned outward by a man who had spent four years learning to reach for what was in front of him, and had got good at it, and had never once until this month owned a reach that went *in*. That was the temptation, and it did not look like a temptation. It looked like a wrist a foot from his hand.

"You said it in the yard, with your hand on that well, on the second day. Say it in the log." She did not move. "I've seen what the log does to you."

He had the log. He had it under his arm because he had carried it out of the common room the way he carried it out of every room, and he sat down on the well's rim with it on his knee and opened it to the pencil, and she stood and watched him do it, which no one had done since a scarred table by a harbour, and he wrote it while she watched, in the plain hand, in full.

*The reading turned outward. I ask first. Every time. If I ever don't, that's the day I became the instrument.*

He turned the book so she could read it. She read it upside down, as she read everything of his, and nodded once.

"Good. That's the rule. Now the other thing." She put her hands in the coat's pockets, which he had never seen her do; her hands were always where she could use them. "You read yourself. The night of the birthday. You told the log and you didn't tell me, and the log said *owed*, and I read that upside down too, at the table, when you had it open at the wrong page."

"Yes. I'll tell you now."

"No." She said it as she said *lie still*. "Not yet. You've read it once. Once is a morning. I don't take a finding off one reading, from anybody, and I'm not going to take one off you, because you'll say it well and I'll believe it and then it'll be a finding, and it isn't. It's a morning." She looked at the road, north, where it went out of the gate into the dark. "Read it again. Twice more, at least, on different days, when you're not spent and not just off a table. Then tell me all of it at once, exactly, and I'll tell you what I think a healer thinks of it, which may be nothing. That's the order. The debt stands. I'm setting the day it's paid."

"You said you wanted to be the one I tell."

"I am the one you tell. I'm telling you when." Oryn took her hands out of the pockets. "Three days north. Thornwater's on the route. Come find me when you've got it three times."

"Yes," he said.

"And Cael." She was already turning toward the house, and the loft, and the four hours of dark before first light. "Don't read anyone on the road to find out whether you can. Not Lira. Not the carter. Not the man at the board. If you want to know whether it works on people, it does. I've told you. You don't need to check."

She went in. He sat on the well's rim in the cold with the log open on his knee and the rule on the page, and did not read anyone, and found that the not-doing had a shape, and that the shape was going to be there every day for the rest of his life, and wrote that down under the rule, smaller, so that it would be there in ten years when somebody opened the book at the wrong page.

*Under the rule: the rule is not for the days I want to. It's for the days I don't notice I've started. She read me for twenty minutes with my leave and could not find a bank. I could read her in twenty and find every bank she has, and she has walked past that line eleven times without stepping off the road and stepped over it eight times alone in the dark, and she is going north at first light with a route seventeen days late and my rule in her pocket. Every time. Written where the inventory goes, so that it gets read every time the inventory does.*

---

He finished the entry by the last of the window's light, after the window went dark, by nothing, because he knew where the lines went.

*Hundred and tenth day. Thornwater, the well. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Nineteen. Ten fragments. The tenth turned outward has a rule now, and the rule's above.*

*Told Lira. At the wall, at dusk, in the order I've had since the stone, which didn't get any longer for fifteen days of carrying it. She said the thing she said in Ardenmere, shorter. She said it scares me. She was right, and she said I'm allowed to say so, and I said so.*

*Told the six. Oryn included; my decision, no objection. The four who saw said what they saw, in order, as said. Karis said the five findings and closed the book on the sixth. Seln said they know something turns Paths off and don't know something doesn't turn off, yet. Oryn said her hands stop at the line and mine don't and she found no Path in me and won't guess past it. Nobody guessed. Six people at one table with the biggest thing any of us has ever had in front of us and not one verb between the facts. I've sat in a great many rooms. I've never once sat in that one.*

*What we have: one site. A handful, Pike says. Three circles on a carter's map. A contract written inside by somebody who now knows who went in. Four wheres and no why. Karis: we find the others, all of them, and find out whether they're the same. That's the second half of the year, decided at a table by six people who agreed they couldn't see the edges of the thing and decided what to do anyway. I've watched institutions decide things for four years. This was faster and it was honest and nobody wrote it down but me.*

*Oryn goes north at first light. Three days. Thornwater's on the route. She's set the day the debt's paid: three readings, on different days, then all of it at once. She's right. Once is a morning.*

*Nothing out here reads me. And at a line you can mark with a rock, every Path in this camp stops, and mine doesn't. Karis won't write her hypothesis. I won't write mine. We're going to find the other circles. Pike said nobody goes in twice. Tomorrow makes three.*

# Chapter 8 — Quiet Ground

Lowmarch on the ninetieth day, from the shelf above the ford, catalogued because it had been twenty days and because the meadow had turned over again while they were on a mountain.

He counted it before he read it. The river was lower than it had been on the sixtieth, and the ford's poles stood a hand farther out of it; the rope ferry was on the near bank, loading; the smith was working, which mattered, because six marks of what was in the satchel belonged to him. The upper inn had smoke. The lower inn had none. The meadow: forty tents and carts where there had been thirty-one, the grass gone to seed and yellow at the edges, the summer's fires fewer and smaller, and of the thirty-one he could find six. The ash was there. The carter with two mules was not — a woman with a hand-cart of hides had the place — and Brom looked at that, and at the ten paces on, where nothing was, and walked to the ten paces on and put the boxes down, one, two, three, with the right arm doing its share for the first time since the ford. The healer had said a week; Brom had given it seven days to the hour, and picked up the middle box with both hands on the eighth morning, and said nothing about it, and nobody else had either.

Six days from Oxhollow: a road, and a river, and a road, the ford knee-deep where Teague's crew had walked it quiet, and the five of them a day behind, walking it quiet after them, and not trying to catch them. Lira in two boots again: the right one a carter's, bought at Oxhollow's gate for two marks, a size wrong and packed with the healer's linen. The ankle under it taped every morning. The leg above it new, and aching the right amount, she said, when asked, which was once. Karis with the map and the book, the palms two pink lines each and the pen held as a pen. Seln ahead. And himself: the tape round his ribs where the breath stopped, twelve days old and hated by noon every day as she had said it would be; the calf bound in her cloth, and the count on it gone — past twenty on the flat it was a leg, and on the shelf's climb it had said nine, and it was the last thing on him that said anything.

Nine confirmed. One anomaly. No deployment since the ford. Eighteen.

They went down to the landing with three boxes and the look of people who had walked a long way round to arrive where they had left.

The board was under its roof, and Pike was under the roof with it.

---

He read the board before he read the man, because that was the order.

Seventeen slips. He read all seventeen twice and sorted, standing at the plank's far end, and the sort came out wrong, and he did it again, and it came out the same way, and he understood that the board had changed shape while they were on the mountain.

Six escorts. Two fence-lines. A wash recovery; a load out of a bog. Four hold contracts on good square paper with brands burned in the corner, each with HOLD in Pike's hand and a name written beside the word. Beside two of the four the name was Teague. Beside the other two — a fence-line at a hold on the east road whose brand was an axe-head, forty marks; a stretch of the north road to be walked ahead of a hold's carts before the autumn, fifty and fed — the name was not a name. It was five names, in a row, spelled as they had been said, in the same hand and the same size as the word beside them.

He read the five names four times. Then he read the rest of the board.

"Holds came to the ferry," Pike said, to the page. He had not looked up. "Asked who. I said you." The pen went on. "Axe-head's the fence you cleared cheap the first week. They've a longer one now and they asked by the names, because you've not given me a crew's name and I'll not make one up."

"When," said Lira.

"Fence a week ago. Road, four days." He finished his line. "They'll wait a fortnight or they'll take Teague. A hold doesn't mind which. That's what a hold is."

Cael put it where he kept things. Two slips. Forty and fifty. HOLD, and their names. Not a line on a wall — a hold, at the ferry, asking a one-armed man who, and the man saying five names. There was nothing on the shelf beside it that was the same shape.

"Traverse," Pike said.

Cael put the slip on the plank. Oxhollow's ford-keeper had marked it at the table under the lean-to with ink and rope on his hands, and had written across the brand in the corner the one word he had said aloud — *round* — and Pike drew it out from under the stone's edge and read it silently, and turned it to the light, and looked at the word.

"Witness is the ford."

"The man who keeps it."

"Right." He pinned it flat with the stone and did not write yet. "High traverse, Oxhollow to Thornwater. Mapped. Delivered at the ford. Carts go round." A pause, exactly as long as the pause he gave everything. "Cart lost. Crew of five, three hurt, none lost. Seventy." He looked up. "Paid seventy?"

"Paid seventy."

"Paid seventy." He wrote, and the five columns took it, and the fourth column took *carts go round* and *cart lost* in the same ink and the same size, and Cael watched them go in and did not look away from either. Then Pike stopped, with the pen up, and said the next thing to the page and not to any of them.

"Smith's cart."

"Six. I'll pay him today."

"Pay him. It's not his line. It's yours." He wrote again, at the end of the price column, smaller: *less six, the cart.* "You hired it and it went in a gorge. Seventy's what the ford paid. Sixty-four's what the job was worth. That's what goes on the wall." He read the whole line back aloud, word for word, from *High traverse* to *less six*, to the meadow, at the same size as everything he read, and when it was said he wiped the pen.

Cael had the arithmetic before the pen was wiped, because it was the arithmetic he would have done. A map had been asked for and a map had been made, and the map said no, and a map that said no was a map — the ford had paid seventy for it and would go round because of it; that was the credit, whole. A hired cart had gone into a gorge under their hands; that was the debit, whole, in the same column at the same size, and the wall would carry it as long as the wall carried them. He looked for the place in himself that wanted to argue the six, and found it empty. The bench had been a cart. Teague had said so at a ford and been right, and here it was in ink, and it was fair. He had been priced by better-made instruments than this one and never by a fairer.

"Fair," he said, because he had been asked nothing and it wanted saying anyway.

Pike looked at him for the length of one count of five, and let it go, and said, "Saying's tonight," and turned the ledger, with two fingers and one push, so that the page faced across the plank.

Seven lines under the heading now. He read them the right way up.

---

The posting was under the stone's far edge, where slips went that had been read and put back.

He would not have seen it from where he stood, except that Karis had gone round the plank to the map, as Karis went round anything to a map, and had put a hand on the stone to steady the sheet, and the corner of a thin paper had shown under the stone's edge, and he read papers. Thin: a broker's. A clerk's hand. He read it upside down from a yard off, out of habit and because it was there, and the first word stopped him.

*Recovery.*

He went round the plank. Pike watched him come and did not stop him. The slip had been under the stone long enough for the stone to have left a mark on it — a curve of dust where the weight had sat — and he drew it out and laid it flat and read it the right way up.

Broker's paper, the thin kind. A clerk's hand: even, schooled, the letters sitting on a line that had not been drawn. In the corner, pressed into a drop of dark wax, the broker's seal — a hook and a bar — which he had seen in ink on a third of the bales on the landing, because they were that broker's bales. *Recovery,* it said. *Surveyor's instruments abandoned at a site on the Fallow road.* Then the site: *east of Thornwater; the second day; where the road leaves its line at the rise and comes back to it.* Then the price, written twice, as brokers wrote it, once in figures and once in words. A hundred marks. *One hundred.* Then the seal, and nothing else — no crew's mark on it, no HOLD, no name beside a word, nothing to say who had taken it, because nobody had.

It was the best number on the board. The autumn road had been sixty and fed. This was a hundred, dry, for a walk and a carry.

He read it a second time for what it was. Item: a hundred marks for instruments, which meant the instruments were worth more than a hundred to whoever wanted them back, which meant they were real. Item: *abandoned,* not lost — a word with a hand in it; somebody had put them down. Item: the Fallow road, which he had seen on the map on this plank going east out of Thornwater's brand, and had not walked. Item: the dust-curve, which said the slip had been on this board longer than the two with his name beside them, and the soft corners, which said it had been picked up and put back by more than one hand.

"How long," he said.

"Fortnight." Pike had not looked at the slip; he had not needed to. "Broker's on the landing; his clerk wrote it. Every crew on the wall's read it. Every one of them put it back."

"Why?"

Pike put out his hand.

Cael gave him the slip. It was not a thing he did without deciding to; he had had paper taken out of his hands by better-dressed men for four years. He decided, and Pike took it in the one hand and held it to the light off the river and read it the way he read a slip before he wrote it — silently, once — and Cael, who watched faces at a plank, saw it happen.

The pen was in the same hand as the slip, between two fingers. It had been moving, a little, as a pen moves in a hand that is used to writing; it stopped. The face did the thing it had done for the man with eleven bales — did not change, and stopped moving — and then more than that: the shoulder that had a sleeve settled, a finger's width, as a load settles when the man under it has decided to carry it a while longer; and the eyes reached the place on the paper where the site was and stayed there, and did not go on to the price. He was not reading any longer. He was looking at a place.

Cael counted. He got to nine.

"That's the Fallow Ring," Pike said.

Karis's pen had come up off the map. Lira, who had been at the roof's edge, was not at the roof's edge. Brom had not moved. Seln was on the river side, and Cael did not look for him.

"That's quiet ground."

He said it as he said a price. Then he put the slip down on the plank, face up, and set the stone on it — on the corner with the wax, not on the words — and looked at the five of them, and Cael understood that the man had decided what he was going to say, and that it was the decision of a man who kept the only book out here that anybody could check.

"You've heard the words."

"Once," Karis said. Her voice had gone to the exact place she kept it for a source. "A carter's wife at Thornwater. Three sentences. Everybody knows the quiet ground. Nobody goes in."

"That's the whole of it," Pike said. "Near enough. I'll give you the rest, because you're going to read it off that slip whether I do or don't, and I'd sooner it came from me than from the ferry."

He said it plainly, in the order a carter says the fords on a road, and Cael took it down in his head as it came, because it came in the shape of a thing said many times and never once written.

"Quiet ground's where the sigil goes to sleep. Yours. Everyone's. You walk a road, and there's a place on it where your Arbiter stops — not slows; stops — and you walk on past and it's back. There's a handful of them. Along the void roads, all of them. Carters walk round them, and have as long as there've been carters. The dogs live in them. Stillhounds. Nobody's asked them why. Why any of it, nobody knows and nobody's needed to, because the rule's *don't go in*, and the rule's never once failed anybody who kept it." He stopped, and the stopping was part of it. "That's what a carter knows. That's what I know. There's not a man on the ferry knows a word more."

Nobody spoke. Karis was not writing. Cael marked that — Karis with a pen and a primary source and the pen not moving — and then she did write, three words, small, and he could not see which.

"Ring," Brom said. Not a question; the weight of the word, checked.

"Stones, some of them standing, in a ring. You see it off the road and you keep walking." His eyes had not left the slip. "I've walked past the Fallow Ring two hundred times. Never once stepped over the line. Thirty years on the roads and I've never met a practitioner who did it twice."

The river went by under the landing. The ferry's rope creaked once on its post.

"What's the line?" said Karis.

Pike looked at her, and at the pen, and at the healed hand holding it, and answered the question she had asked and not one she had not.

"You'll know," he said. "Your Arbiter tells you. It stops."

---

He paid the smith.

He did it then, before anything else, because six marks were owed and he had them. The smith took the six without stopping the hammer and said the cart had been a good cart and he had another, and would they be wanting it, and Cael said not this time, and the smith said that was what everyone said who had put one in a gorge.

A hundred and eight in hand.

When he came back under the roof Karis had the middle box open on the plank's landward end, with Pike's leave and without having asked for it, and had come out of it with a single thin page from the far end of a bundle and laid it on the plank beside the map. He knew the page. He had watched it go in. The letters were twice their size and leaned two ways, and there were three sentences on it, and under two words of the second sentence there were two lines.

She put a finger on the map at Thornwater's brand, and drew it east along the line that left the brand with days written beside it, slowly, a day, and stopped it where the line went out of true — bent, as a road bends round a bog — and came back. The second day. She held the finger there a moment. Then she took it off and looked at the page with the two lines under it instead.

"A location," she said. It was the first thing she had said since the plank. "And a contract."

"Karis."

"I'm not going to say anything else about it here." She did not look up. "I'm going to say it at a table, with a jug, to four people, once. I've had a rumor under a slip for a month. I'd like the next thing I say about it to be a sentence I've thought about."

She put the page back in the bundle and the bundle back in the box and closed the lid, and did not look at the stone with the slip under it, which Cael marked as the thing that cost her most, because he had watched her not-ask a carter's wife and this was the same shape, larger.

Lira had gone to the roof's edge again, on the road side. He went and stood beside her. She was looking at the road east, which went to Thornwater and past it, and her weight was on the mended leg and off the taped ankle, which was how she stood now.

"Two hundred times," she said. "He counted."

"He counts."

"So do you. I've never once heard you say never." She did not look at him. "Seln's not moved since the man said *dogs*."

He had not. Cael found him without turning his head, because he knew where the river side of the roof was, and Seln was on it, with his back to the post and his eyes on nothing, in the posture he kept for a road he had not walked yet.

---

The board's room, with the light gone off the water.

Six tables. The carters at the two by the road door, five of them, loud, and the hide-woman with them. The middle table: nobody. The best table by the fire: four chairs pushed in and no jug — Teague's crew out on the east road since dawn on the axe-head hold's other slip, the long fence, HOLD, Teague. Pike behind the book at the table by the fire, with a bowl, and the stone on the book. The sheet on the long wall on its four pegs. And the five of them at the table by the road door, because Brom could see both doors from it, with a jug and a loaf, and Karis's book open, and his own satchel flat on his knee.

He counted the sheet from where he sat before Pike opened the book. Thirteen lines. Theirs was the twelfth. He knew that and counted anyway.

Pike opened the book at the day's page and put two fingers on the line and read it to the room, not to them, the whole of it, *High traverse* to *less six, the cart*, and the carters stopped for it and started again after, and the hide-woman, who was new, looked at the five of them and at the wall and back, and that was a verdict from someone who did not know what she was pricing, and Cael filed it as one.

"That's said," Pike told the book.

Then he did the rest, as he had done it on the sixtieth day: took the four pegs out one-handed, let the sheet roll down into his hand, laid it under the stone, took a clean one, and wrote in the hand that did not hurry. He stopped at the ninth line this time, and looked at the old sheet, and wrote five names. Then three more lines under them, the mule last, and he pinned the new sheet in the same four holes and sat back down.

Tenth.

Two lines, for a map that said no and a cart in a gorge. He had been moved on walls by panels that had had a season to think about it, and never by a man who had thought about it for the length of time it took to say *sixty-four*, and had been right both times he had moved them, by a reckoning Cael could check.

"Teague's nine up," Pike said, to the book, asked by nobody. Then, still to the book, at the same size, as if it were the next line and not a different thing: "He read that slip yesterday. Read it twice, same as the boy did. Put it back under the stone and took the fence. I'd have done the same, and I told him, and he said he knew."

He picked up his bowl. That was the whole of it, unasked, and Cael put it on the shelf beside *he'll speak to you when you've cost him something* and found that the two sentences faced each other.

Karis closed her book on her finger.

"Now," she said.

---

She said it once, as she had promised, in the order she meant it.

"Everybody knows the quiet ground. Nobody goes in. I've had that under a slip since Thornwater, underlined, filed as a source and a bad one, because it was one woman on a cart and I couldn't ask her the next question. Today a man who has kept the only checkable record in this country for thirty years said the same two sentences, with a name in them, and a road, and a day-count, and a rule, and then a broker's slip put a price on it." She turned the book round on the table so that they could see the page, which was the three leaning sentences copied fair beneath the original in her own present hand, and under them, new: *The Fallow Ring. Fallow road, east of Thornwater, second day. Pike: the sigil stops at a line. The line: 'your Arbiter tells you.'* "That is a primary source — the first I have had on this side of the Line that is a place and not a rumor. I am going to go and stand at the line he says exists and find out whether my Arbiter does what he says it does. I would very much rather do that with the four of you than without. I've said that in the order I mean it, and I'll not say the last part twice."

She sat back. The pen was in the healed hand, and the hand was not quite steady, and she let it be seen.

Brom, who priced a thing by its weight before anything else, said: "A survey kit's a cart's worth. Brass. A level, a chain, the rule. Nobody puts a cart's worth down and walks off from it unless they walked off fast." He looked at the jug. "That's not the reason. That's what it is."

"The reason," Lira said, "is the number. It's a hundred because it's been under a stone a fortnight and the man who's nine lines up read it twice and put it back." She had a hand flat on the table, on the taped side, the way she put a hand down when she meant to stand and was not standing yet. "Two days past Thornwater. We've walked worse for forty. You've got three ribs and a calf; say that before he does." A nod, small, at the fire, where Pike was. "I'm saying it's a walk and a carry. I'm not saying it isn't the other thing."

"It pays," Cael said.

That was his voice at the table, and the four of them let it be, and he catalogued that they did.

Seln had the slip.

He had not been at the plank when it came out from under the stone and he had not asked for it; Pike had put it in his hand at the door, unasked, as Pike put things in hands, and he had brought it to the table and laid it flat by the jug and had been reading it since the saying, with the lamp on it — not the words; the paper. He turned it to the light now, as Pike turned a slip, and looked at the wax in the corner, and then at the hand, and then at the words, and then said the thing he said, which was the thing nobody else at the table could have said.

"Wax is right. Hook and bar; that's the landing broker. Hand's a clerk's — schooled, no drawn line; a broker's clerk writes on a line he can't see because he was taught to. That's right too." He put one finger on four words. "*Abandoned at a site.* That's not a broker's clerk. Not the letters — the words. A carter says *the place*, *the bend*, *the rise*. A broker's clerk writes what the carter said. Nobody out here says *site*. Nobody out here says *abandoned at*. That's a form. I've filled that form in." He took the finger off. "Somebody wrote this who learned to write inside."

Nobody said anything for the length of a breath. The carters by the door were laughing at something that was not this.

"Meaning," Karis said.

"I don't know." Flat; a road he had not walked. "The wax says the landing broker posted it. The words say whoever gave him the words to post learned them somewhere with a registry in it. That's two facts. I've not got a third and I'm not going to make one at a table." He pushed the slip a finger's width toward Cael, which was how he gave a thing to the log. "It goes in. Somebody looks at it later, when there's more."

"It goes in," Cael said.

"That's not a reason not to go," Seln said. "I want that said, because somebody's going to think it's what I've been sat here thinking. It's a reason to read the paper twice. I've read it twice." He looked at Karis, once. "Fifteen years I walked into rooms on paper that was wrong in a way I couldn't name. This is wrong in a way I can. I'd rather walk into this one on her account than any of theirs."

Karis did not write that down. It was the first thing all evening she had not written down, and Cael marked it, and would have put it in the log if it had been his.

Brom said, "Where does the Fallow road leave from."

"Thornwater's east gate." Karis had the map out — Pike's, copied in the last of the light at the plank onto the back of her own sheet, the Fallow road drawn on with its days and its bend. "Two days from here to the steading. Two on from it to the rise. Four."

"The ninety-first tomorrow," Brom said. "Thornwater the ninety-third."

Karis's pen stopped.

Cael had the count before she said it, because he had had the count since Oxhollow's gate, and had carried it six days without taking it out. Nine days from the eighty-fourth. The ninety-third. Thornwater on her route, and the mule at the gate, and a woman who had said *come find me* at a walk that did not look back.

"The ninth day," Karis said. "The day she said."

Nobody said the sentence.

He watched four people not say it, and it was the most careful thing he had seen any of them do at a table. Lira's hand stayed flat. Brom looked at the jug. Seln looked at the door. Karis looked at the map, at the brand of the steading where a healer would be on the ninety-third with a table and a line of people who were hers, and did not say *bring her*, and neither did he, who had had the sentence at a gate and catalogued not saying it, and now watched four other people arrive at the same gate and do the same thing, each for a reason he could guess and would not write. She had seven holds and a child three stops on and a road she owned. You did not say *bring her* about a person like that. You said where you would be.

Karis wrote. He read it upside down as it went in, small, under the day-count.

*Thornwater, ninety-third. The healer.*

That was all. She underlined nothing.

"Then that's the walk," Lira said.

"That's the walk." Brom.

Cael took the slip from where Seln had pushed it, folded it once along the dust-curve, and put it in the satchel against the leather book, where a thing went that would be taken out later. Then he got up, and went to the fire, and stood at the table by it until Pike looked up from the bowl, and said, "The recovery. The Fallow road. We'll mark it."

Pike looked at him for one count of five.

"Mark it in the morning," he said. "At the plank, in front of the meadow, so it's seen." He went back to the bowl. "I'll write HOLD by it if you like. There's nobody else to write."

"Our names."

"Your names. It's the only crew's name you've got." The spoon. "Whoever marks it at the other end marks it. That's the board."

He went back to the table by the road door. Seln was watching the landing door. Lira had taken her hand off the table. Brom had poured the last of the jug into Karis's cup without saying anything about it, and she had not noticed yet.

He took out the log and wrote the thing he had been carrying since a man's face stopped over a piece of thin paper, in the plain hand, because it was the end of something and he wrote the ends of things plainly.

*Ninetieth day. Lowmarch. The lower inn, the table by the road door. A hundred and eight in hand; the smith paid; the wall says tenth.*

*Twelve weeks. Seven contracts. Pike says there's ground out here where the Arbiter goes quiet, and nobody goes in twice. Karis has been waiting her whole life for a sentence like that. Seln says the contract was written by someone from inside. I say it pays. Two days east, through Thornwater, on the day the healer said. For twelve weeks nothing out here has read me. I'd like to see what a place that reads nothing does with that.*

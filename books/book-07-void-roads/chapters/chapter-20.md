# Chapter 20 — The Stair's Floor

Teague's crew held the rim in the morning without anybody asking them to, and he catalogued that from the stone before he catalogued anything else, because it was the first thing on the ground that had not been there the day before.

Four of them, at the four quarters of the bowl, on the rim's lip where the hooded six had stood — the tall woman with the spear on the south, above the road's far cut; the broad man with the crossbow and the limp on the east; the young one on the north, above the stone; Teague on the west, where the last of the six had stopped and looked back. They had walked up in the dark and taken the rim as a crew takes a job, and they had not come down to the road to say so, and they were not going to. Teague had said *if you're late back, we'll come look*, and had come, and had looked, and the looking had turned into holding, and a captain who holds a rim for a crew ten lines under him does not want it entered anywhere but the ground.

"He's outside the line," Karis said, beside him, with the lamp already lit and the sun not yet on the floor. "All four. He's read the stone."

"He's read the stone," Cael said, and they went down.

---

Six over, in the order Seln gave, and Oryn checked her hands at the stride and said "Gone," and Brom crossed with the box on his good shoulder and the bad one carried the way a man carries a thing that has been mended and told not to be used: still, and slightly forward, and not mentioned. Nobody asked him to leave the box. Nobody was going to.

Cael crossed with nothing in his hands and the rim's bill still in his legs. The suite had gone out of him on a lip the evening before to the last framework, and it rang faint this morning, as it did on second mornings, all ten with a hollow in each, and his legs had stopped having opinions and had not yet started having the other thing; he had slept in a rut with a stone at his back and had eaten what Brom put in his hand, and the unit had looked at him at the stone and decided, without a word, that he would carry no box today, and he had let them decide it, because a man who has spent everything on a rim owes the people who watched him do it the courtesy of being seen to be spent.

The bowl in the morning was the bowl of the morning before, and it was not, because the six of them walked across it under four people who were theirs and not six who were not, and the difference was in the scrub, which was only scrub, and in the floor, which took the first of the sun and went white along its whole width while they were still on the road's cut, and in the hole at the floor's middle, which was a hole and not a door somebody had been waiting for them to run to.

Teague said one thing, from the west rim, before they went down, in the voice a captain uses across a bowl: "We'll hold it till you're up." It was not an offer. It was the shape of the day, stated once. Cael said "Noted," up the slope, and Teague turned his shoulder to the bowl the way he turned it to a fire, and that was the whole of the conversation the two crews had that day, and it was, Cael thought, going down, exactly as much as either of them wanted.

They went down the forty steps with two lamps.

The air changed at the twentieth step. He had not marked it the day before, with a bolt-wound man ahead of him and six crossbows behind; he marked it now: cold, still, dry, with nothing in it — no earth, no water, no root, none of the smells a hole in the ground has because holes in the ground are made of ground — and the two lamps burned in it straight up, without a flicker, as if the chamber had never once had a draught in it, which, he thought, catalogued as a fact and not a reason, it had not.

---

He catalogued the chamber by lamp, properly, because the day before he had catalogued it in the length of a breath with a bolt-wound man beside him, and a count taken in a breath is a count.

Square, or near it: ten strides by ten, walked. Fused stone, floor and walls and ceiling, and the ceiling was the floor — the underside of the plate the six of them had run across in the morning and the bowl's rim had watched all afternoon — one piece of grey from the stair's foot to the far wall, a hand above Brom's head, no join in it anywhere, so that the chamber was not a room under a floor but the floor's *other side*, and he stood under it and put his palm flat on the ceiling, a man touching a ceiling, with nothing running in him but the hollow the rim had left — and it was one piece to the hand as it was to the eye, cold, dry, no join under his palm anywhere, and he did not write the word for that either.

The walls. Carved floor to ceiling in the lines — not in bands round uprights, as at the Ring, but in courses along the walls, the depth of a fingernail, the width of a knife's back, regular as a rule, the same kind of line and never the same line, and more of them than the Ring's eleven uprights had carried by a factor he could not count and Karis could. She had her charcoal on the first wall before the second lamp was lit, and a sheet against it, and she had said nothing, and she was not going to say anything for six hours, because Karis at a primary source did not spend breath on speech.

Oryn, at the stair's foot, watched the first sheet fill, and said, to nobody, "What are they," in the voice she used for a distance, and Karis, without turning, said, "Lines," and that was the last thing either of them said to the other until the walls were flat on the floor. He filed it as the two of them settling a thing: the healer had asked the researcher's question once, and got the researcher's answer, and had not asked it again, because she had recognised the answer as one of her own.

The floor of the chamber, level to the eye and to her cord, seamless, lipped at the walls' foot like the floors above at their edges — a shallow basin's rim turned inward, the height of a hand — so that the chamber was a floor with a wall stood on its lip and another floor laid on the wall.

And at the middle, where he had seen shapes by a lantern held close to a man's shoulder, the shapes.

---

A registry survey kit.

He had never held one. He had stood in front of a great many, and he stood in front of this one for the length of a breath before he let himself go to it, because it was a registry object in a place the registry had drawn as a hole, and it had the mark on it, and every registry object he had ever been near had been on the far side of a counter or a seal or a man's back. This one was on a floor, open, with its owner five years gone. It was theirs by the plain arithmetic of a contract, and it was the first thing with the registry's mark on it that had ever been *theirs*, and he catalogued that once and put it away, because the object was more interesting than the feeling.

He knew it as an object before he knew it as a fact, because he had stood at the corner of every printed sheet the registry had ever put in front of him and had seen, on the wall behind the clerk, the same case: leather over a frame, brass at the corners, a strap for the shoulder, a lid with the registry's mark burned into it under the scale of five winters. The case was open. That was the first thing, and the thing that made the rest of it what it was. Not shut and left. Open, with its lid back on the stone, and its contents out of it, in the order a surveyor takes them out, and not in the order he puts them back.

A pacing cord, laid out. Knotted at the fives, as Karis's was, because there was one way to knot a pacing cord and the registry had taught it to her before it had taught it to anyone with a stake; it lay across the chamber's floor from the middle toward the east wall, its far end at the wall's foot and its near end by the case, straight, taut once and slack now, a line somebody had measured along and had not coiled.

A stake. Iron, the twin of the two on the perimeter, numbered — thirty-two, and the small mark, the stroke and the bar — lying on its side at the cord's near end with its point bright. Bright, where a mallet had struck the head and the point had been driven at the floor, and beside it the mallet, and under the point, where a stake driven at earth goes in to the hand, the stone. Unmarked. Karis, when she came to it, put her thumb on the bright point, and looked at the floor under it, and said the only thing she said before the copying took her: "He tried to drive it. Twice, by the head. It wouldn't go. Nothing goes into this." Then she went back to the wall.

And a field ledger, open on the stone.

Registry stock, bound, the size of Karis's notebook; the cover turned back; the pages that had been written on turned past, and the page it lay open at half-full, in a hand that had been writing across it in the cipher-shorthand the registry taught its surveyors and nobody else, line under line, and had stopped. Not at a line's end. In the middle of one. The pen's last stroke went down and did not come up, and there was a mark beside it on the stone where the pen had been set down or dropped, and no pen.

"Five years," Seln said. He had come to it as he came to everything, along the wall, reading the ground, and he stood over the open book with his hands at his sides. "By the scale on the case. By the stakes. He was working. He stopped in the middle of a word and put the pen down and left the book open and the cord out and the case's lid on the floor, and he went up forty steps, and he didn't come back for any of it." He did not kneel. "Nobody leaves a registry kit. It's signed out. You don't get another."

"Then something made him leave it," Lira said, from the stair's foot, with her arm across her chest.

"Something made him leave it in a hurry." Seln knelt. "I'm not going to say what. There's nothing on the floor that says what. There's a book that says he was here, and what he did, and where he stopped."

---

Seln read it.

He read it as he had read the contract on Pike's plank: all of it, where it lay, without picking it up, the whole of the open page and the pages before it turned back one at a time with a knife's flat so that his fingers did not touch them, and he read the cipher aloud as far as it could be read, in the voice for an inventory, and Karis's charcoal did not stop on the wall while he did, but her head turned.

"Distances." A finger over a column, not on it. "Paced. Cord and stride, as she does it — the registry taught her the registry's way and this is the registry's way. He walked the perimeter in cords and marked the stakes where he'd measured to. Sixty-two cords and a piece." He turned a page back with the knife. "A radius. He walked in from the stake to the middle, as she had, and wrote what he got. Two hundred meters." He did not look at Karis. "Their own hand. Their own cord. The number she got with hers. It's theirs, five years before it was hers."

Karis wrote something on the sheet's margin that was not a carving.

"A designation." The finger over a line near the page's top, where a group of the cipher's marks stood apart from the columns, with a stroke and a bar beside them — the mark on the second stake. "The site's got a name in something. Not a word; an entry. It's in an index. He doesn't say which index and he doesn't say what the entry means, because he wasn't writing for anybody who didn't already know." Seln looked at the group of marks a while longer. "I carried maps with holes in them. I never saw an index. I don't know what this is in, and I'm not going to say."

Nobody theorized. He watched them not, because he had watched them not at a table with the biggest fact in the book on it, and this was the same six people and the same discipline and a smaller fact, and it held.

"And the last line." Seln put the knife's flat under the open page's edge and did not turn it, because there was nothing past it. "He's finished the perimeter. He's finished the radius. He's written the designation. And he starts a line — " the finger over the last marks, the ones that stopped — "*third site confirmed on bearing* — and there's a mark, and it's the start of a number or the start of a word, and it isn't either, because he put the pen down." Seln took his hand back. "*Bearing.* That's the last thing a registry surveyor wrote in this room."

Karis's charcoal stopped on the wall.

He saw it, because he was watching her, because watching Karis at a source was a thing he did without deciding to, like reading a room. She did not turn round. She stood with the charcoal against the sheet and the sheet against the carved stone and her whole body changed, the way a body changes when a word arrives that it has been built to receive: the shoulders, the hand, the set of the head. *Bearing.* A line with a direction. A thing a straightedge was for. She had had a straightedge in the middle box since Ternhall and had used it on masonry and on maps and had never once had a thing on a continent to lay it along, and a man five years dead or gone had written the word she had been waiting for and had not finished the sentence.

"Third," she said, to the wall. "He says *third*. This is the second. The Ring's the first, by his stakes — thirty-one there, thirty-two here. He'd found a third." She started the charcoal again. "He'd found it on a bearing from these two. He wrote *bearing* and he stopped, and I'm not going to finish his sentence for him. I'm going to copy this wall."

She copied the wall.

---

Six hours, by the square of grey at the stair's top, which went white and stayed white and went grey, and by the two lamps, which Brom refilled twice from the oil they had brought down for it, and by Karis, who did not sit, or eat, or speak, except to say "Sheet" when a sheet was full, and Lira, one-armed, gave her a sheet.

He catalogued the six hours because cataloguing was what he had, and because the six hours were, he understood by the second, the thing the day was for. Oryn sat at the stair's foot with her back to the wall's carved courses and her hands in her lap, and did not read anyone, because nobody was hurt and there was nothing to read with, and watched Karis work with the look she gave things that had a rule in them. Brom held the lamp where she pointed and did not once change hands on it, because one of his hands was a hand and the other was a mended shoulder's. Seln sat by the ledger and read it again, the whole of it, three times, and then once more with the knife's flat, and then closed it, and put it inside his coat, against the case's strap, and Cael watched him do it and did not say anything, because Seln keeping a ledger was Seln keeping a ledger.

Lira gave sheets. She did it one-handed, off the middle box's stack, the arm across her chest and the taped foot flat on the fused floor, and she did it before Karis said "Sheet" nearly every time, because she had watched the charcoal reach a sheet's edge twenty-eight times and had learned the length of a wall's course as she had learned the length of a floor: by standing on it. Brom refilled the lamps. Seln read. Oryn watched. Cael catalogued the four of them doing the four things, and found that it was a unit at work with no Path in any of them and nothing to fight, and that it looked exactly like a unit at work.

He counted them. Twenty-nine, off the middle box's paper, every one of them the height of the wall's courses and cut to it, and Karis worked along the four walls in order — east, south, west, north — and at the end of each wall she stopped and laid the sheets on the floor in the order she had taken them, edge to edge, so that the wall lay flat on the chamber's floor as the ring had lain flat on Thornwater's table, and stood over it with the lamp, and counted.

He knew a tally when he saw one. She had the second sheets out — the ones from the week of tables, the rows of marks that were the *kinds* — and she was going along the copied courses with a pencil and the tally, and putting a stroke by a kind every time it came, and not by a kind when it did not, and her mouth was moving, and it was not moving on words.

At the end of the fourth wall she put the pencil down.

"Forty-one," she said.

She said it to the floor, to the four walls laid flat on it, and then she turned round and said it to him, because he was the one who had asked her, at Thornwater, how many kinds, and had been told she would not say until she had a second site.

"Forty-one kinds of line. The Ring has forty-one. I counted them for four days and wouldn't say the number, because a count from one place is a count. The Stair has forty-one." She picked up the tally and held it so he could see the strokes, which he could not read and did not need to. "Every kind that's on those eleven uprights is on these four walls. Every one. Not one kind here that isn't there, and not one there that isn't here — except one."

She went to the east wall's sheets and knelt, one-kneed, and put the pencil's end on a line.

"This one. It's not on the Ring. I looked for it for four days and it isn't on any upright. It's here, on every wall, and it's not in the courses with the rest — it's *between* them. On its own. Here." The pencil moved along the sheet to the next one, and the next. "And here. And here. I've paced the spacing because I pace things. It comes every — " she looked at the notebook, where she had written it, and read it out as a number she had checked twice — "every ten cords and a piece. Two hundred meters and a stride, to the stride. It recurs, on the wall, at the perimeter's radius."

Nobody said anything.

"The floor was carved by somebody who knew the circle's size," Karis said. "That's the finding. I'm entering it as that, and I'm entering it as nothing else." She stood up. "A measurement is a fact about a ruler, not about the hand that held it. I've a measurement. I've got the same number three times now — the cord's, the ledger's, the wall's — and three's a fact, and the fact is *two hundred meters*, and the fact stops there. Whose ruler, and why, and what the other forty-one are saying — I don't know, and I'm not going to know from a wall, and I'm not going to write a guess in the small hand over a true unknown and let the record carry it forward." She began, without hurry, to pick up the sheets in the order she had laid them down. "Twenty-nine sheets. Two sites. One line that measures the circle from the inside. Entered."

He wrote it in the log at the stair's foot, while she rolled the sheets, in the plain hand, the finding and not one word past it, because that was the discipline and he had learned it from her.

---

The kit went up the stair on Brom's shoulder.

Nobody had discussed whether it would go up at all, because there had never been a question. The contract said *recovery*, and the kit was the instruments, and the crew had walked into two floors for it and had a man shot on the second, and the question of what a crew does with a thing it was hired to recover, when the man who hired it was the man who had built the trap on the rim, had been answered at a plank at Lowmarch by a defector with a finger on a slip, and had not needed to be asked again in a chamber.

Karis packed it. She did it as she packed the middle box, in the order it came out: the stake in its loop, the cord coiled at the fives, the mallet, the lid down on the case and the strap through its buckle. She left the ledger out, because the ledger was inside Seln's coat and she had watched it go there and had said nothing, because a ledger with a bearing in it was the one object in the chamber that was going to be read again, by lamp, on a road, and Seln was the one who could read it.

Brom took the case.

He took it by the strap with his good hand and put it on his good shoulder, and Lira said, "I've got a hand," and Brom said, "No," and Oryn said, "That's a mended shoulder," and Brom said, "It's the other one," and picked up the middle box as well, by its strap, in the same hand, so that he went up forty steps of fused stone one man wide with a survey kit and a field archive on his left side and his right side carried still and forward, a man carrying a thing he has been told not to use, and refused help twice more on the way, once from Cael at the twentieth step and once from Lira at the top, who had a hand out again and had it looked at.

The bowl was in full sun. Four of Teague's crew on the rim, exactly where they had been at dawn. Teague, on the west, looked down at six people coming up out of a hole in a floor with a registry case on a big man's shoulder, and did not come down, and said nothing, and Cael filed the nothing where he filed Teague's things, which was a shelf that had got longer in two days than it had in a season. The tall woman on the south rim lifted the spear off her shoulder and grounded it, once, on the stone, which was a thing done for the rim and not for the road, and the young one on the north went back to watching the country, and the broad man on the east shifted his weight off the limp and stayed. Four people holding a rim for a crew that had nothing to give them for it. He did not have a column for that either, and did not try to build one.

---

He wrote the log at the rim, at the stone, with the slack meter of scrub either side of it and the road going south out of the bowl toward a bearing nobody had drawn, and Karis beside him with the middle box open on the road and the straightedge already out of it, brass, folding, laid across the carter's map, not yet touching anything.

*Hundred and fifteenth day. The Long Stair, the rim, at the stone. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; three hundred entered as taken and never to be paid. Nineteen. Ten. Brom's shoulder mended and not used. Lira's arm closed and not used. Seln's got a ledger. Karis has twenty-nine sheets and a number, and the number is forty-one.*

*Instruments recovered. The contract's paid, and the man who posted it is never going to see them, because Seln's keeping the ledger and I'm keeping the kit. Someone measured this place five years ago and wrote bearing and stopped. Karis has a straightedge. She's been waiting her whole life to use it. The Ring, the Stair, and whatever's on that bearing. Pike said a handful. We have two. The kit says three. We're going to go look.*
